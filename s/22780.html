Moving Forward Instead of Moving On: Parallel Entrepreneurial Realities in Peripheral Urban Renewal

SIM
George Redhead (University of East Anglia); Zografia Bika (University of East Anglia)
Urban renewal is often promoted as a response to urban challenges, yet it can unevenly exacerbate deprivation. Research to date overlooks non-core regions and their residents, focusing instead on popular cities/tourist destinations. Addressing gaps in theory and evidence through a case study in Ipswich, UK, we ask: how do everyday entrepreneurs in peripheral urban areas experience uneven urban renewal? Findings reveal how micro-level agency underpins entrepreneurial self-gentrification and self-renovating neighbourhoods, broadening entrepreneurship theory and informing regional policy. We reinforce that entrepreneurship is no panacea for regional development and its effectiveness is further undermined when urban renewal marginalises parallel entrepreneurial realities.

Who Rewards Hybrids: The Role of Familiarity and Stigmatization in Crowdfunding Social Ventures

SIM
Anaïs Périlleux; Zineb AOUNI (ICN Business School); Marek Hudon
Little is known about how potential funders’ preconceptions about hybrid organizations shape funding outcomes. Drawing on an audience-based approach to categorization and a quasi-experimental design on crowdfunding platforms, this paper investigates how stereotypes about the commercial and social categories, along with familiarity, influence investment in hybrid venture, that combine social and commercial logics. The results show that negative perceptions (stereotypes) of either social or commercial category actually increase support for hybrids. Surprisingly, however, the perceived coherence between the two categories does not influence investment decisions. With respect to familiarity, funders who are more familiar with commercial enterprises tends to increase their support for hybridity, whereas familiarity with non-profit organizations does not. Paradoxically, the results provide empirical evidence that negative stereotypes can make hybrids more attractive to certain funders who typically avoid either purely commercial or purely social organizations. Results also show that hybrid ventures may strategically benefit from targeting funders familiar with commercial enterprises.

Founding Speed and Performance in Social Enterprises

SIM
Yoo Na Youm (Loyola University Chicago); In Hyeock Ian Lee (Loyola University Chicago)
Social enterprises are hybrid organizations pursuing a social mission with entrepreneurial activities. However, their founding speed and performance remain understudied. Our study examines how entrepreneurs’ prosocial identity may affect the founding speed of new social ventures, and in turn, impact firm performance, both of which could be conditioned on the founders’ entrepreneurial and commercial approaches. Results from social enterprises in South Korea during 2021 to 2022 are three-fold: (1) the prosocial identity of entrepreneurs delays the founding speed of their social enterprises; (2) the entrepreneurial orientation of entrepreneurs expedites the founding speed; and (3) although the speedy founding of social enterprises does not translate to their better firm performance, entrepreneurs’ prior startup failure experience and their external network with commercial enterprises may boost the performance. The study concludes with theoretical contributions to the social enterprises literature and practical and policy implications.

Algorithm and Ancestry: Multi-Level Insights into AI Implementation in Indigenous Tourism

SIM
XUAN MINH DAM (James Cook University); Kim-Lim Tan (James Cook University); Manisha Agarwal (James Cook University); Emiel L. Eijdenberg (HU University of Applied Sciences Utrecht)
Adoption theories suggest that technological and organizational readiness drive AI use, yet in culturally grounded contexts, adoption often falters. This study explores how service providers in Vietnam’s Indigenous tourism interpret and implement AI. Based on 21 semi-structured interviews across frontline, managerial, and institutional roles, we apply the Technological-Organizational-People (TOP) and Technological-Organizational-Environment (TOE) frameworks to trace multi-level dynamics. Findings reveal that cultural alignment, the fit between AI applications and Indigenous value systems, is both a necessary condition for transforming readiness into legitimate use and a mediating mechanism linking policy and resources to daily practices. AI adoption unfolds through a translation process: frontline actors experiment and adapt, managers verify cultural compatibility, and institutional actors formalize cautious routines. Implementation weakens when technological-cultural misalignment and operational constraints persist. The study extends TOP and TOE by defining when readiness becomes effective and theorizes a multi-level implementation mechanism that legitimizes AI use. These insights guide the design and governance of AI that aligns with Indigenous values while enabling effective service delivery.
