Strategies for Coping with Big Tech Dominance: Regulation, Reform, or Revolution?

STR
Anne Jacqueminet (ESSEC Business School); Dovev Lavie (Bocconi University); Tommaso Valletti (Imperial College London)
The Big Tech firms have captured substantial economic value through their dominant digital platforms, often at the expense of stakeholders and society. However, the field of strategy remains largely focused on studying their value creation, leaving remedies to other disciplines. In this study, we examine three approaches to addressing the consequences of Big Tech dominance, highlighting their merits and limitations: regulation through the judicial system, voluntary reform of corporate purpose, and revolution through the design of alternative platforms that prioritize societal values. We discuss how these approaches can be complementary and encourage strategy scholars to actively engage in this debate by revisiting core assumptions, theorizing, and testing new business models for digital platforms that can challenge the Big Tech firms’ hegemony.

The Influence of President Trump’s Twitter Sensegiving in Shaping Global Economic Order

STR
Ting Xiao (University of California Santa Barbara)
How do political leaders reshape firm environments and the broader global economic order when they communicate directly with markets through digital platforms? While research on stakeholder theory and nonmarket strategy predominantly emphasizes firm–stakeholder relationships and proactive corporate political action, it has paid limited attention to how exogenous political actors influence firms by simultaneously reshaping the behavior and coordination of multiple stakeholder groups. Drawing on stakeholder theory and sensegiving perspectives, we develop the concept of digitally mediated political sensegiving to explain how political leaders’ social media communications generate inter-stakeholder response asymmetries that alter firms’ strategic environments beyond managerial control. Using a difference-in-differences design and hand-collected data on firm-specific Twitter communications by the U.S. President between 2017 and 2019, we examine responses by public investors, corporate insiders, media organizations, and financial analysts. We find that political tweets trigger surges in public trading activity, reductions in insider buying, heightened media coverage with sentiment divergence, and decreased analyst coverage accompanied by selective optimism. These findings reveal how political sensegiving disrupts stakeholder alignment and reallocates interpretive authority across market actors. Our study advances stakeholder theory by moving beyond dyadic models to theorize inter-stakeholder dynamics under political uncertainty and contributes to strategy research on nonmarket influence, digital platforms, and uncertainty in the global economic order.

Political Entrepreneurs: Are Entrepreneurs in Politics Entrepreneurial in Policymaking?

STR
Joyce (Heqiao) Ma (Duke University); Aaron Chatterji (Duke University); Jorge Guzman (Columbia Business School); Ryan McDevitt (Duke University)
Business interests shape public policy through lobbying, campaign contributions, and political connections, yet we know far less about how policy changes when entrepreneurs themselves enter public office. This paper examines how entrepreneurial experience influences legislative behavior after business owners are elected. We construct a novel dataset linking state legislators’ personal financial disclosures to detailed records of bill sponsorship and legislative text across 26 U.S. states from 2009 to 2023. Using text-based classification, we distinguish between general pro-business bills and pro-entry legislation that lowers barriers to market entry. We show that entrepreneurial representation varies mainly across states and is negatively associated with legislative professionalism and state-level entrepreneurial activity. Once in office, entrepreneurs are not more prolific sponsors overall, but are significantly more likely to act as first primary sponsors. Rather than broadly representing business interests, they selectively promote pro-entry policies - particularly antitrust and deregulation - while showing no greater emphasis on innovation or access-to-capital initiatives. These findings reveal an insider channel of non-market strategy and demonstrate how entrepreneurial experience shapes legislative agendas and institutional change.

Revolving Doors on Corporate Boards: A Comparative Study of Seven European Countries

STR
CARMEN CARDENAS ELVIRA (ESADE Business School); Angel Saz-Carranza (ESADE Business School - Ramon Llull University); Ruth V. Aguilera (Northeastern University)
The movement of individuals between public office and private corporations, commonly known as the revolving doors phenomenon, has important implications for firm behavior, state-business relations and the well-functioning of democratic institutions. Prior research on the presence of such individuals in corporate boards has produced mixed findings and has largely focused on single-country analyses. In this paper, we use a comparative approach to examine how the value political / bureaucratic experience is relative to the institutional context where the firm operates. Using an original, hand-coded dataset of listed firms in seven European countries (Austria, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Switzerland and United Kingdom) and their respective board members, we identify the directors with prior experience in the public sector across multiple dimensions and compare its prevalence in 2019 and 2023. Our preliminary findings indicate a generalized increase in the presence of revolvers across countries. Moreover, through the lens of administrative traditions, the results also suggest that firms prioritize different types of political/bureaucratic background depending on the institutional context they are in. Overall, the study provides an exploratory mapping of board political capital across different European countries and highlights future lines of research regarding the implications at firm-level.

Political Leadership and Grand Societal Challenges: The Role of Management Skills

STR
Gustavo Cordeiro; Sandro Cabral (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute)
While prior research has examined the role of managerial skills among entrepreneurs and corporate managers and their effects on firm performance, little is known about whether such skills matter when held by elected political leaders and about their broader societal consequences. Leveraging close mayoral elections in Brazil between 2004 and 2020, we employ a Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) and a comprehensive dataset covering more than 28 million births across all 5,570 municipalities to estimate the causal impact of mayors with formal management training on the incidence of low birthweight (LBW), a key public health outcome. Our results show that mayors with formal management training significantly reduce LBW. Mechanism analysis indicates that these effects are driven by increased efforts to obtain financial resources through federal transfers, higher investments in primary healthcare, and the expansion of local healthcare capacity, including physicians, nurses, and community health agents. We further find that mayors without formal management training partially offset performance gaps by appointing subordinates with management expertise to strategically important positions. Responding to recent calls to extend strategic management theory beyond firms, this study demonstrates that management skills held by elected political leaders and their high-ranking subordinates function as strategic assets that enable the mobilization and effective allocation of resources to address global challenges and create public value.
