Justice or Just a Signal? Distributive Justice Under Ambiguity and Talent Self-Identification

IM
Tamiko Kasahara (Waseda University)
Global talent management (GTM) practices in multinational corporations (MNCs) often rely on strategically ambiguous criteria for identifying high-potential and high-performing individuals. Challenging uniformity assumptions in GTM, ambiguity makes distributive justice the primary lens for interpreting talent signals, especially when cues align with individuals’ talent self-identification. Drawing on fairness heuristic theory, distributive justice is conceptualized as the dominant heuristic under ambiguity. Evidence from a two-wave survey of 271 managers and professionals in U.S.-based subsidiaries of East Asian MNCs and U.S.-based headquarters supports this account: greater ambiguity heightens reliance on distributive justice, shaping psychological contract fulfillment, knowledge sharing, and acceptance of increased performance demands. The findings clarify when and why distributive justice overrides other justice dimensions and show that GTM interpretations depend on signal clarity and identity.

Whose Time Counts? When and How Meeting Time Centralization Shapes HCN Employees’ Identification

IM
Qihan Qin (Korea University); ChangHan LEE (Korea University); Ji-Won Song (The University of Edinburgh); DuckJung Shin (Thompson Rivers University)
In multinational enterprises, online meeting scheduling has become a routine coordination practice in global work and is often anchored in headquarters’ working hours. Yet prior studies have largely emphasized efficiency outcomes, offering little insight into how scheduling practices communicate whose time really counts. This study examines how meeting time centralization (MTC)—the extent to which meeting schedules systematically privilege the headquarters time zones—shapes subsidiary host-country national (HCN) employees’ perceived procedural justice and identification in cross-unit work. Drawing on a justice-signaling perspective, we conceptualize MTC as a temporal governance practice through which headquarters enact coordination authority, thereby shaping perceived procedural justice. Procedural justice, in turn, influences two distinct forms of identification: dual organizational identification (DOI; entity-based) and transnational role identification (TRI, role-based identification in boundary-spanning work). We further theorize that these effects vary with employees’ hierarchical orientation (power distance orientation) and their temporal resources for coping with scheduling demands (work time flexibility). Using two-wave survey data from 251 subsidiary HCN employees across multiple countries, we find that MTC is directly related to DOI, and indirectly related to both DOI and TRI via perceived procedural justice. These indirect effects are weaker for employees with a higher power distance orientation and greater work time flexibility. Overall, this study advances global virtual work research by showing that temporal coordination routines function as micro-level governance mechanisms that communicate legitimacy and authority, with consequential implications for employees’ fairness perceptions and identification.

(Non-)Convergence of Fairness Perceptions Across Cultures

IM
Markus Pudelko (University of Tübingen); Joerg Bueechl; Bradley L. Kirkman (North Carolina State University)
We employ an exploratory qualitative study to enrich the organizational justice literature with insights from cultural identity negotiation theory by developing an evidence-based model illuminating whether and how culturally diverse subordinates and supervisors can achieve shared fairness perceptions through their cross-cultural interactions. Our analysis is based on a complex research design using a total of 133 interviews in China and Germany from Chinese subordinates of German supervisors, German subordinates of Chinese supervisors, Chinese supervisors of German subordinates, and German supervisors of Chinese subordinates. As a result of our empirically induced theorizing, we highlight the centrality of inter-linked cultural identity negotiation processes. Only those subordinates and supervisors willing and able to engage in such processes, once they encountered the other respective party, succeeded in arriving at an approximation of previously more distinct fairness perceptions and ultimately at a partially shared understanding of fairness. We also found moderators influencing cultural identity negotiation processes. Of these, cultural preservation (i.e., the degree to which the subordinates and supervisors cling to their original cultural identity) was by far the most important one.

Beyond Assimilation: Structured Moral Dialogue and Ethical Hybridization in Senegal and Cameroon (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Hae-jung Hong (NEOMA Business School); Hyemi Shin (Royal Holloway, University of London)
The increasing complexity of ethical challenges in global business necessitates a deeper understanding of how moral traditions interact in cross-cultural settings. Existing business ethics research often frames ethical adaptation as passive cultural assimilation or individual moral adjustment, overlooking the role of structured moral dialogue in shaping ethical transformation. This study challenges these assumptions by demonstrating that ethical adaptation is an ongoing negotiation between diverse ethical frameworks rather than a unilateral process. Using a multi-method qualitative fieldwork approach—including 24 in-depth interviews with Korean business owners, missionaries, and NGO workers, alongside 20 interviews with locals, field observations and secondary data in Senegal and Cameroon—this study examines how moral dialogues across Confucian, Christian, and indigenous African communalist ethics (specifically Senegalese Teranga and Cameroonian Njangi) facilitate ethical trust-building, hybridization, and governance transformation. Findings reveal three key dimensions of ethical adaptation: (1) Cultural memory and temporality, where professionals reconcile past ethical commitments with present realities; (2) Hybridization of ethical practices, where structured moral engagement and faith-based mediation foster new, hybrid ethical models; and (3) Ethical legacy and institutional change, where hybridized ethics become embedded in governance structures. By positioning inter-tradition dialogue as the mechanism that transitions ethical adaptation from individual negotiation to institutional transformation, this study contributes to business ethics by demonstrating that moral traditions engage in structured dialogue to create new ethical models, ethical hybridization influences governance frameworks, and trust emerges through sustained moral dialogue rather than merely contractual or relational mechanisms.
