Beyond Stringency: How Reform Characteristics Shape Firms' Environmental Innovation Strategies

TIM
Tian Feng (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
Do regulatory reforms facilitate or impede firms’ engagement in environmental innovation? The current study aims to address this puzzle that has not been addressed by previous studies. Based on institutional theory, this study investigates how regulatory reforms affect corporate environmental innovation. Results obtained from Poisson regressions with a sample of 1,791 publicly listed firms in China indicate that regulatory reforms at the provincial level facilitate firms’ adoption of environmental innovation. This empirical result was further validated by demonstrating that research and development intensity and analyst attention serve as channels through which regulatory reforms affect firms’ environmental innovation. Local officials’ hometown ties strengthen the effect of regulatory reforms on environmental innovation, while these officials’ tenure weakens this relationship. These findings offer insights into institutional theory by explaining how regulatory reforms affect environmental innovation.

Organizing Mission-Oriented Collaborative Innovation: Evidence from Innovation Consortiums

TIM
Jin Chen (Tsinghua University); Lili Zhang (Tsinghua University)
Collaborative innovation has become central to addressing complex technological challenges that exceed the capabilities of individual organizations. While prior research in technology and innovation management has examined alliances, ecosystems, and other interorganizational forms, it has largely treated collaboration as voluntary and market-mediated. Less is known about how collaborative innovation is organized when innovation activities are explicitly mission-driven and deeply embedded in public policy. This study develops a process-oriented understanding of mission-oriented collaborative innovation through a comparative case analysis of two innovation consortiums operating under the same policy environment but characterized by different mission structures. Drawing on extensive documentary evidence and comparative process tracing, the study examines how mission orientation and policy embedding shape governance design, coordination mechanisms, and innovation processes. The analysis reveals that differences in mission specificity give rise to distinct organizational pathways. Application-oriented missions are associated with centralized authority, iterative coordination, and deployment-based learning, facilitating rapid technology-to-application translation. In contrast, system-oriented missions rely on more formalized and layered governance arrangements, supporting long-term exploration and cumulative capability building. Synthesizing these findings, the study proposes a comparative process model that explains how mission orientation functions as an internal organizing principle shaping collaborative innovation. By theorizing innovation consortiums as a policy-embedded form of collaborative innovation, this study contributes to research on collaborative innovation, governance design, and mission-oriented technology development.

University-Orchestrated Regulatory Responsiveness: Strategic Ambidexterity

TIM
Thinh Gia Hoang (RMIT University); Umair Tanveer (University of Exeter); Shamaila Ishaq (University of Derby); Muhammad Mustafa Kamal (University of Exeter)
Regulators increasingly evaluate novel technologies under uncertainty, while universities assume hybrid roles spanning innovation, expertise, and public oversight. This study examines how universities, through collaboration with firms and regulatory agencies, contribute to regulatory responsiveness within national innovation systems. Using a longitudinal multiple-case design of three leading universities in developing-country contexts (37 interviews and archival documents), the analysis shows universities enact strategic ambidexterity by coupling legitimacy-oriented regulatory engagement with experimentation-oriented innovation work. Three mechanisms underpin this role: strategic positioning in politically sensitive or high-uncertainty domains, translation of exploratory knowledge into decision-relevant regulatory evidence, and structural coordination across cross-sector interfaces. The study extends Strategic Ambidexterity Theory into regulatory science by theorising ambidexterity as an institutional configuration shaping regulatory judgement, and reframes universities as epistemic intermediaries that condition regulatory adaptation in innovation governance.

When Intellectual Capital Fuels Radical Innovation Under Dual Turbulence

TIM
Chi-Cheng Wu (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Chunhsien Wang (National Chiayi University); Chin-Chia Ou (Asia University)
How can firms convert intellectual capital into radical innovation under turbulent conditions? Grounded in resource orchestration theory, we propose that ambidextrous innovation simultaneously pursuing exploratory and exploitative innovation translates intellectual capital into radical innovation. We further argue that this conversion is contingent on dual turbulence: technological turbulence and market turbulence jointly heighten the need to reconfigure knowledge and routines, strengthening the link between intellectual capital and ambidextrous innovation and, in turn, the indirect effect on radical innovation. Survey data from 323 Taiwanese firms across multiple industries support the model. Intellectual capital is positively related to ambidextrous innovation, which predicts radical innovation. The intellectual capital ambidextrous innovation relationship is strongest when both technological and market turbulence are high, producing a strengthened indirect effect on radical innovation. These findings clarify when intangible resources generate breakthrough outcomes, extend ambidexterity research by identifying dual turbulence as a key boundary condition, and offer guidance on aligning intellectual capital investments with exploration exploitation practices in volatile technology and customer environments.
