Shaping Entepreneuring – Monogamous and Polygamous Family Configurations in Tanzania

OMT
Maren Iwastschenko (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz University of Hanover); Christiana Weber (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz University of Hanover); Thomas Wrona (Hamburg University of Technology)
Entrepreneurship research increasingly recognizes the social embeddedness of entrepreneurial strategizing and organizing. However, less attention has been given to how different family configurations shape the everyday enactment of entrepreneurship. Drawing on 22 focus group discussions with 189 coffee entrepreneurs in rural Tanzania, we develop a relational-practice perspective that explains how family configurations shape entrepreneurial life and vice versa. Our analysis uncovers two generative mechanisms, resource embeddedness and social order and power, that account for the co-evolution of configurations and practices across monogamous and polygamous families. We advance Entrepreneurship-as-Practice research through a meso-level perspective on family configurations. By conceptualizing the family as a constitutive site where entrepreneurship and social order are continuously negotiated, we contribute to entrepreneurship literature. Finally, we broaden the geography of practice theory by showing how entrepreneuring unfolds within kinship-based arrangements in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Navigating Uncertainty: A Market Topology of Entrepreneurial Strategy

OMT
Nenad Apostoloski (INSEAD); Peer Fiss (University of Southern California)
Entrepreneurial strategy unfolds across markets that vary not only in overall uncertainty but also in the internal heterogeneity of their information environments. To explain how entrepreneurs navigate such complexity, we introduce a market topology framework grounded in information entropy and complexity science. We conceptualize markets as dynamic information landscapes comprising three coexisting zones—allocation, discovery, and creation— each characterized by distinct levels of information uncertainty. Entrepreneurs deploy three corresponding strategic postures: positioning (in low-entropy allocation zones), adapting (in medium-entropy discovery zones), and shaping (in high-entropy creation zones). By aligning posture with local entropy conditions, ventures can more effectively pursue, create, and reshape opportunities. Our framework clarifies when and why different strategic logics are effective, specifies zone-contingent pivot mechanisms, and shows how entrepreneurial agency drives market evolution by managing knowledge flows across zones. In doing so, we move beyond dichotomies of discovery versus creation and planning versus action to propose a dynamic, multilevel theory of entrepreneurial strategy. We also identify how misalignments between posture and zone shape survival outcomes and suggest how ecosystems can sustain adaptability by preserving entropy-generating creation zones. Our perspective offers theoretical precision and practical guidance for entrepreneurs, scholars, and policymakers navigating complex market systems.

The Evolving Impact of Founders on Startup Employee Retention

OMT
Minjae Kim (Yale University); J. Daniel Kim
Employee retention is a persistent challenge for startups, but how does the founder impact this outcome? While founders’ influence on employee retention may diminish as organizations develop, it may also grow as founder-employee complementarities deepen over time. Using matched employer–employee data from the U.S. Census and premature founder deaths as an identification strategy, we find that losing a founder significantly increases employee turnover. Moreover, this effect is stronger in more mature startups. Consistently, individual-level analyses show heightened turnover among employees with longer shared tenure or who share the founder’s gender. These findings suggest that employees develop founder-specific complementarities over time, thereby deepening founders’ influence on retention as startups evolve.

Multimodal Optimal Distinctiveness: Evidence from Crowdfunding Campaigns

OMT
Rui Wang (Tsinghua University)
How do entrepreneurs convey the distinctiveness of their ventures without compromising categorical comprehension? Drawing on optimal distinctiveness and multimodality studies, we introduce the concept of “multimodal optimal distinctiveness” and theorize how visual and verbal modes serve complementary functions that jointly help entrepreneurs achieve optimal distinctiveness. Analyzing data from over 140,000 Kickstarter campaigns and employing state-of-the-art foundation models, we find that visual distinctiveness (conformity) negatively (positively) relates to funding outcomes, while verbal distinctiveness positively relates to funding outcomes. Moreover, the positive effect of verbal distinctiveness is strongest when visual distinctiveness is low. Our findings advance a multimodal view of optimal distinctiveness in which different modalities are not interchangeable but complementary in satisfying the dual needs for fitting in and standing out. Our study suggests that the conformity-distinctiveness trade-off need not be a balancing act but rather a design problem that can be resolved through strategic deployment of cultural means across modalities.

Evading Entrepreneurship: How Social Capital Directs Financial Resources Among Marginalized Individuals

OMT
Inara Tareque (Columbia Business School)
Can entrepreneurship be an unintended spillover effect of public financial assistance awarded to socially disadvantaged groups? I develop and test theory linking financial constraints and social capital to entrepreneurship, arguing that while public financial assistance increases entrepreneurial entry, this effect is contingent on the quality of social ties an individual has access to. I leverage the quasi-random placement of refugees across U.S. states and metropolitan areas from 1996 to 2020, and exploit variation in state- level financial assistance with variation in access to co-national communities. Using data on over 75,000 refugees, I find that greater public financial assistance increases the likelihood of entrepreneurial entry. However, this positive effect is significantly attenuated in areas with greater access to co-nationals. Mechanism tests show that dense co-national networks constrain entrepreneurship by limiting exposure to diverse information and by providing access to alternative wage-employment opportunities. These findings contribute to research on entrepreneurship, social capital, and policy spillovers by showing how financial and social constraints jointly shape occupational choice among marginalized populations.
