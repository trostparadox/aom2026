Coopetition Centrality and Disruptive Innovation: The Role of AI Adoption & Relationship Structure

TIM
Wei Wang (Zhengzhou University); Zhiyang Li (Zhengzhou University)
Disruptive innovation is critical to industrial transformation, yet how it emerges from firms’ embeddedness in complex interorganizational networks remains unclear. Drawing on network embeddedness and dynamic capability theory, this study examines how coopetitive centrality, reflecting a firm’s position within overlapping cooperative and competitive relationships, influences disruptive innovation. To capture both internal capability and external relational contingencies, we further examine artificial intelligence adoption and coopetitive relationship configurations as moderating conditions. The results show that coopetitive centrality enhances firms’ access to heterogeneous knowledge and facilitates the recombination of technological pathways, and this effect is stronger for firms with higher levels of artificial intelligence adoption, which strengthens their ability to process and reconfigure complex external knowledge. Moreover, coopetitive relationships are classified into four configurations based on the relative dominance of cooperation and competition, namely cooperation-dominant, competition-dominant, strong equilibrium, and weak equilibrium. The findings indicate that cooperation-dominant and weak equilibrium configurations strengthen the positive effect of coopetitive centrality on disruptive innovation, whereas competition-dominant and strong equilibrium configurations weaken it. Overall, the results underscore the joint roles of network position, relational structure, and internal technological capabilities in shaping the emergence of disruptive innovation.

How Policy Mix Differentiation Drives Regional Breakthrough Innovation: A Thematic Perspective

TIM
Guannan Xu (Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications); Hongyan Liu (Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications); Yuan Zhou (Tsinghua University)
Breakthrough innovation, characterized by its high uncertainty, long gestation periods, and transformative potential, inherently requires guidance from policy mixes rather than isolated interventions. Existing studies have examined the effects of digital transformation policy mixes on innovation, yet they have paid insufficient attention to the policy mixes and breakthrough innovation. To address this gap, this paper proposes a two-dimensional framework, analyzing the horizontal differentiation (across regions) and vertical differentiation (from central guidelines) of policy mixes through a thematic lens; based on this framework, we develop a conceptual model linking policy mix differentiation to regional breakthrough innovation. The empirical analysis draws on data from Chinese regions between 2003 and 2021, employing natural language processing to extract digital policy themes and their differentiation, while measuring regional breakthrough innovation through an index capturing both ex-ante path-breaking novelty and ex-post impact. The results show that both horizontal and vertical differentiation positively contribute to regional breakthrough innovation, while the positive effect of policy differentiation is weaker in regions with higher levels of public data openness, indicating a negative moderating role. This study contributes to the literature by deepening the understanding of how the thematic characteristics of digital policy mixes drive breakthrough innovation, extending the measurement framework of breakthrough innovation by incorporating both ex-ante and ex-post attributes. It also provides important implications for designing differentiated digital innovation policies in the digital era.

Firm and Group Network Dynamism in Radical Innovation

TIM
Andres Velez-Calle (EAFIT University); Jessica Salmon; Salma Zaman; Emine Beyza Satoglu; Fernando Sanchez Henriquez (Universidad de Chile)
This study examines the impact of ego-network dynamism at the firm and group levels on radical innovation performance. While prior research has examined the role of network structure in innovation, less attention has been paid to how firms dynamically reconfigure their network ties over time to balance knowledge exploration and exploitation. Drawing on the Knowledge-Based View (KBV) and Global Innovation Systems (GIS) perspective, we argue that ego-network dynamism enables firms to integrate diverse knowledge sources across multiple levels, enhancing radical innovation. Using a multilevel dataset of 184 Nordic firms spanning 1998–2023, we find that both firm- and group-level ego-network dynamism are positively associated with radical innovation. Our results also reveal a substitutive effect between firm and group dynamism, suggesting that firms in highly dynamic group networks may not need to engage in extensive firm-level network reconfiguration to achieve similar innovation benefits. These findings contribute to the KBV literature by conceptualizing network churn as a mechanism for knowledge recombination in global innovation ecosystems and extend the GIS framework by demonstrating how firms navigate between local and global knowledge complementarities through network adjustments. From a managerial perspective, firms should find a balance between internal and external network shifts to enhance their ability to drive radical innovation effectively.

Reliance on Disruptive Science and the Translation of Science into Technology

TIM
Seongwoo Jang (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology); Dawoon Jeong (University of Chicago); Seolmin Yang (University of Virginia); Seungryul (Ryan) Shin (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology)
The outcome of translating scientific research into a technological invention is largely shaped by the body of science the scientists build upon. While prior studies have mainly focused on the impact of technological inventions based on the extent to which they are closely tied to science through citation links, this paper examines which science scientists rely on, particularly regarding the disruptiveness of the target science. We analyze a dataset of over 200,000 patent-paper pairs, which provides a unique opportunity to observe the value of resulting inventions in relation to the type of science that scientists built upon during the research process. We find that scientists’ reliance on disruptive science is negatively associated with the value of patented inventions, as measured by the number of forward citations they receive. Further analysis suggests two possible explanations for this pattern: (i) disruptive science may hinder the novel application of scientific knowledge in technology, and (ii) disruptive science may impede the integration of technological components. To strengthen the causal interpretation, we instrument reliance on disruptive science using scientists’ differential exposure to paper retractions, based on the historical contributions of scientists in nearby geographical areas to the journals where the retractions occurred. Our findings highlight the often-overlooked value of non-disruptive, i.e., consolidating, science in the successful translation of science into impactful technology.
