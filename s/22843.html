When Social Challenges Become Market Opportunities: A Review of Sustainability-Oriented Coopetition

SIM
Angélica Rodríguez (University of Mondragón); Valery Chistov (University of Deusto); Orlando Rodriguez (Universidad Tecnológica de Pereira - UTP)
While Social Issues in Management research has emphasized firms’ responsibilities toward stakeholders, less attention has been paid to how competitors collectively organize to transform shared sustainability challenges into new customer value and markets. This study addresses this gap through a systematic literature review of research on sustainability-oriented coopetition in business networks. Integrating normative and strategic perspectives—most notably Stakeholder Theory and the Resource-Based View—we identify five persistent gaps in existing explanations. We reconceptualize these gaps into four analytically distinct but interrelated dimensions: industry-specific sustainability challenges as endogenous drivers, collective governance structures, collaborative value-creation dynamics, and new customer value and market creation as outcomes. By articulating theoretically grounded expectations across these dimensions, the study advances SIM scholarship by shifting attention from firm-level responsibility to collective action among competitors, and by offering a structured agenda for future research on how markets emerge from shared social and sustainability concerns.

Business Ethics as Resistance

SIM
Samuel Alexander Mortimer (Kyoto University)
The most influential theories of business ethics focus on the ethics of managerial decision-making. An assumption underlying these approaches is that leaders have significant discretionary control over business organizations. In this paper, I explore the possibilities and limitations of leader control. I argue that leaders have far less control over the ethical performance of business organizations than often assumed. On this basis, I propose an alternative approach to business ethics, which I term ‘ethical resistance’. This approach changes the focus of ethical theorizing from top leaders to any member of the organization—including the rank and file—and from decision-making to the imaginative, opportunistic, and strategic ways that people exercise ethical influence over organizational routines and systems.

When Stigma Meets Scarcity: Resource Mobilization in Core-Stigmatized Social Enterprises

SIM
Pradeep Kumar Hota (Indian Institute of Management Udaipur)
Stigmatized organizations are considered fundamentally flawed by one or more groups of stakeholders, leading to a lack of resource support for the organization. Existing research on the management of stigmatization has looked at stigma management approaches of either for-profit or not-for-profit organizations. So, we do not understand how hybrid organizations might address stigmatization challenges. Using a revelatory case approach, we develop a grounded resource management framework on how stigmatized social enterprises respond to stigmatization challenges and successfully mobilize resources. Our study particularly highlights six different stigma management strategies used by the organization to address stigmatization challenges, and hence, it adds to the literature on organizational stigma. Further, by proposing a resource mobilization framework, our study contributes to the social entrepreneurship literature.

Sustainability in Higher Education: From Institutional Commitment to Individual Accountability

SIM
Neeti Ingole
Sustainability has become a prominent commitment within higher education institutions (HEIs), manifested through governance structures, reporting practices, and sustainability-oriented curricula. Yet, despite this institutionalization, sustainability efforts often remain weakly connected to sustained individual behavior. In this paper, we argue that this gap reflects a conceptual limitation in existing scholarship, which has largely framed sustainability as an institutional or educational endeavor rather than as a domain of individual responsibility. Drawing on accountability theory, we advance a conceptual framework that reconceptualizes sustainability in HEIs as a multi-level accountability process. We position individual-level felt accountability—the perception that one’s actions are visible, evaluable, and consequential—as the central mechanism through which institutional sustainability initiatives translate into meaningful outcomes. By theorizing HEIs as formative accountability environments, the framework highlights how sustainability outcomes, including reductions in carbon emissions and sustained engagement in responsible practices, emerge from the interaction between institutional design and individual moral agency. This paper contributes to research on sustainability, ethics, and governance by shifting attention from awareness and symbolic adoption toward responsibility and accountability-oriented sustainability adoption.

Some are More “Special" Than Others: Editorial Conflicts of Interest in Special Issues

SIM
Dermot Breslin (Queen's University Belfast); Declan French
Special issues (SI) play a key role in the advancement of knowledge in business and management research, promoting innovation, inclusion and journal citations. However, there is an under-researched dark side to SIs related to the quality and ethics of the peer review process, including guest editorial conflicts of interest. This paper sets out to explore three key conflicts of interests that impact SI publication, namely guest editor authorship, authorship by former guest editor coauthors, and reciprocal guest editor-author arrangements. Examining SI publications over the past 15 years, we find that the majority of SIs do not engage in any of these behaviors. However, in a significant minority of cases, such practices do prevail, being more prevalent in certain journals and disciplinary areas. We explore the ethical implications of these conflicts of interest, and propose a number of recommendations targeted at policing and governing such practices.
