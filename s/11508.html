Session overview

“In theory, the network residue from yesterday should be irrelevant to market behavior tomorrow” (Burt, 2000: 348): decisions in the market should be made based on current information. In practice, however, organizational actors (people as well as organizations) rarely start from a blank slate. Instead, they rely on the networks they have built in the past and what happened within them, especially in ambiguous times (Burt, 2000). An implication is that professionals must recognize how past networks shape current decisions and strategically build and maintain connections to leverage or counter these historical patterns. For management scholars, this should make yesterday’s network impossible to ignore when trying to understand how networks function. However, despite progress in understanding network dynamics – i.e., the regularities by which networks form, evolve, and dissolve over time (e.g., Chen, Mehra, Tasselli, & Borgatti, 2022; Jacobsen, Stea, & Soda, 2022), – Granovetter’s observation from more than thirty years ago, that “treating relations and structures of relations as if they had no history that shapes the present situation” still broadly holds true today (1992: 34). Notably, a handful of studies has begun to incorporate history into organizational network research. Soda, Usai, and Zaheer (2004), studying the Italian television production industry, found that past closure in project teams has stronger effects on performance than current closure and that the benefits of past structural holes in external networks decayed rapidly. Extending insights on this decay assumption, McEvily, Jaffee, and Tortoriello (2012), examining partner networks and co-employment ties, demonstrated that the founding conditions under which bridging ties were formed can leave lasting imprints that generate persistent brokerage advantages over time. Explaining why some bridges decay while others persist, Quintane & Carnabuci (2016) show how brokers use different strategies by using ”tertius gaudens“ (keeping parties separated) in new, short-term connections where a joint history is absent but switching to ”tertius iungens” (facilitating direct connections) in relationships that have a rich history. More recently, Burt and Opper (2024) demonstrated that historically developed Guanxi relationships, which gain strength through shared experiences and significant life events rather than through common third-party connections, can function as “strong bridges” that span structural holes without decaying. Thus, even without structural embedding, network ties can still remain valuable by creating lasting structural benefits through time and shared experiences. Finally, in a recent conceptual paper that builds upon these studies and focuses on the history of ties rather than networks overall, Brennecke, Ertug and Carnabuci (2025) distinguish between founding conditions, trajectories, and critical events, emphasizing how these factors shape the significance, capacity, and future development of network relationships. Overall, the above studies approach network history by conceptualizing ties and networks as temporally embedded and shaped by their own past. To extend these seminal insights and aiming to spur the emerging conversation on network history, the proposed symposium aims to bring together leading scholars in organizational network research to ask how our understanding of social networks within and between organizations and their implications changes, when we start to systematically take network history into account. Hence, the symposium’s main objective is to challenge the prevailing ahistorical view that still dominates most organizational network research and advance a shared understanding of how and why network history matters. We suggest that accounting for network and relationship history opens new theoretical and methodological avenues for understanding present structures and future possibilities. Specifically, the symposium has three main intentions. First, it seeks to start discussions on re-theorizing classic network constructs through a network history lens, revisiting foundational ideas such as brokerage (Burt, 1992) and tie strength (Granovetter, 1973) as temporally situated phenomena. For instance, while structurally identical brokerage positions may look the same, a manager connecting two department heads who previously built trust through shared crisis experiences may achieve brokerage benefits far more effectively than when connecting two people without that relational history (Brennecke et al., 2025). Thus, a historic perspective has the potential to challenge static views of structural advantage by emphasizing how the legacy of past interactions can create enduring sources of social and organizational value. Second, the symposium aims to identify methodological opportunities to capture the history of networks and ties, such as reconstructing collaboration patterns from archival data and interviews or tracing evolving interaction networks using digital trace data (e.g., Quintane & Carnabuci, 2016). Third, we seek to discuss implications for management practice. Specifically, the symposium will discuss how organizations and practitioners can identify what kinds of historical network information are most valuable and how such insights can be effectively used in managerial decision-making to improve social capital and performance.
