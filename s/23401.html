Session overview

What happens when generative AI enters not just the classroom, but the institution itself? This session examines management education at the meso and macro levels, exploring how universities make sense of AI's challenge to their core purpose, how accountability arrangements shift under AI-mediated decision-making, and whether AI is already a valid pedagogical instrument by established validity standards. Drawing on institutional theory, sensemaking, and sociomateriality, these papers reveal that the deepest challenges of AI in education are not technological but organizational and normative.

Papers (5)

Making Sense of the Purpose of Higher Education in the Generative Artificial Intelligence Era

MED
Olli-Jaakko Kupiainen (Haaga-Helia University of Applied Sciences); Katja Wirenius; Eija Kjelin (Haaga-Helia University of Applied Sciences)
In the age of generative artificial intelligence, it has been argued that this technology can either undermine or enhance teaching and learning in higher education. This qualitative study explores how management, lecturers, and students use their technological frames of generative artificial intelligence to make sense of the purpose of higher education in a business school context. Based on interviews, we develop a hierarchical model of the purpose of higher education in the era of generative artificial intelligence. Although generative artificial intelligence is integrated into teaching and learning, participants constructed clear boundaries on what it cannot replace, reflecting broader academic, cultural, and ethical values. The study contributes to the management education literature by showing how organizational members frame new technology in relation to teaching and learning and linking those frames to the purpose of higher education.

Fluid Accountability and Risk-Calibrated Delegation in Generative AI Decision-Making

MED
Najla Abdullah Ahmed Albannai (University of Sharjah); Muhammad Mustafa Raziq (University of Sharjah); John Lewis Rice (University of Sharjah)
This study examines how Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) is reshaping decision-making processes and accountability arrangements within higher education institutions over time. Drawing on a six-month longitudinal qualitative design, we conducted two rounds of semi-structured interviews with 20 senior administrative leaders across four universities in the United Arab Emirates. Guided by sociotechnical systems theory and hybrid intelligence perspectives, we employed longitudinal thematic analysis to capture evolving interpretations, practices, and governance responses to GAI adoption. Our findings reveal five interrelated GAI-enabled constructs that emerged and evolved over time: GAI as a cognitive partner, dynamic accountability, delegation by design, strategic unpreparedness, and organizational unpreparedness. Building on these insights, we advance two theoretical contributions—fluid accountability and risk-calibrated delegation—which explain how GAI destabilizes conventional notions of authorship, ethical responsibility, and institutional trust in decision-making contexts. The study further demonstrates how sustained exposure to GAI intensified leaders’ concerns regarding ethical boundaries, governance structures, and capability gaps, prompting increasing calls for formal AI policies, ethical guidelines, and AI-related education and training. By revealing how accountability and delegation logics adapt over time in response to GAI, this research contributes to emerging debates on AI governance, decision-making, and hybrid intelligence in higher education and knowledge-intensive organizations.

Designing Human–AI Collaboration as a Learnable Capability

MED
Ricardo Laverde (Insper)
Generative AI is diffusing into organizations faster than complementary managerial routines can adapt. While tool focused training proliferates, organizations struggle with a deeper problem: designing reliable human AI collaboration in workflows marked by rapid technological change, ambiguous task boundaries, and shifting risk profiles. We develop a conceptual framework for management education that treats human AI collaboration as a teachable capability grounded in a scientific approach to decision making: theorization, experimentation, and disciplined updating. The framework highlights two core challenges for learners. First, effective collaboration requires calibration, meaning accurate judgment about when to rely on AI versus when to override it, so performance depends on inference and behavior, not just technical skill. Second, short run productivity gains can mask longer run costs to learning, skill development, connection, engagement, and professional voice, making it essential to evaluate AI use through a multi dimensional value lens and to revisit decisions over time. We formalize Human AI Collaboration Capability as a bundle of task theory, coordination, and evaluation and governance sub capabilities; specify boundary conditions; and derive design principles for course architecture and assessment. We conclude with a research agenda for theory driven classroom experiments, illustrated with a jigsaw puzzle protocol, that can generate cumulative evidence on how to teach effective human AI collaboration under technological turbulence.

Perceptions and Readiness for AI Pedagogical Agents in Higher Education

MED
Muhammad Saqib Iqbal (National University of Sciences and Technology); Zulhasni Abdul Rahim; Muhammad Fawad Khan (National University of Sciences & Technology (NUST) Islamabad); Mommin Arshad Durrani (National University of Sciences & Technology (NUST) Islamabad); Hamza Iftikhar (National University of Sciences & Technology (NUST) Islamabad)
The rapid diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI) in higher education has intensified interest in AI-enabled pedagogical agents as tools for personalised adaptive learning. However, empirical understanding of institutional readiness to adopt such technologies remains limited, particularly in the Global South. This study examines the readiness of Pakistani higher-education institutions to integrate PLAs by developing a context-sensitive framework based on systematic evidence and expert consensus. Adopting a sequential mixed qualitative design, this study first conducts a PRISMA-based systematic literature review of 62 peer-reviewed studies published between 2018 and 2025 to synthesise the dominant themes related to pedagogical value, ethical concerns, faculty agency, institutional capacity, and equity. Building on these insights, a two-round Delphi study with 18 experts from academia, educational technology, and higher education governance in Pakistan was employed to refine and prioritise the readiness dimensions. The Delphi process yielded a statistically significant consensus (Kendall’s W = 0.61, p < .001) on ten critical dimensions, with ethical governance, faculty AI competence, and digital infrastructure readiness emerging as the highest priorities. The resulting layered readiness framework positions governance and values as the guiding foundations, supported by human and pedagogical capacities, and underpinned by technical and infrastructural enablers. The findings highlight that the responsible adoption of PLAs in Pakistan depends not only on technological availability but also on the strategic alignment of ethical safeguards, human capacity building, and institutional leadership. This study contributes to the AI-in-education scholarship by extending the readiness theory to a Global South context and offering practical guidance for university leaders and policymakers seeking to harness AI for pedagogical innovation while safeguarding academic integrity and equity.

Is AI Already A Valid Tool In Business Education?

MED
Nan Zhang (California State University-Stanislaus); David Zhu (California State University-Stanislaus); SIJING ZONG; Liancheng Fu
This study examines whether generative artificial intelligence (AI) can be considered a valid tool for business education. Moving beyond adoption-focused research, we evaluate AI-generated outputs using a multi-dimensional validity framework encompassing face, content, and criterion validity. Drawing on authentic instructional cases from various disciplines in the business school, we compare AI outputs against instructor expectations, disciplinary standards, and authoritative external benchmarks. Across cases, AI outputs frequently exhibit high face validity but fail to demonstrate adequate content and criterion validity, particularly for tasks requiring relational reasoning, structural accuracy, or verifiable scholarly grounding. Fabricated citations, conceptual misalignments, and structural errors are common despite fluent presentation. These findings suggest that surface plausibility can mask substantive deficiencies, increasing the risk of uncritical student reliance. The study advances a validity-based perspective on AI in management education and offers implications for pedagogy, governance, and assurance-of-learning practices.
