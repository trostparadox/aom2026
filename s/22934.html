The Lock-In of Family Equity, Patience in Ownership and Firms’ Artificial Intelligence Adoption

OMT
Xiaogang He (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Xinyu Jiang (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); WenXin Ni (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics)
While artificial intelligence (AI) offers substantial opportunities for organizational transformation, family firms remain comparatively reluctant to adopt this technology. This study examines whether structural tools—formal family governance mechanisms that lock family equity into legally separate vehicles—can facilitate AI adoption in family firms. We theorize that structural tools institutionally stabilize family socioemotional wealth (SEW) by reducing restricted-SEW anxieties and enhancing extended-SEW, thereby increasing patience in ownership and encouraging long-term technological investment. Using data on Chinese listed family firms from 2008 to 2022, we find that firms employing structural tools exhibit significantly higher levels of AI adoption, and that this effect is mediated by increased ownership patience. A series of robustness tests, including instrumental variable analysis, parallel trends test, and the Heckman two-step method, support our hypothesis. Moreover, the positive effect of structural tools persists under conditions of crisis, political uncertainty, sanctions, and performance pressure. This suggests that structural tools constitute a family governance mechanism with enduring resilience. Our findings primarily contribute to the literatures on family governance by highlighting how ownership-level institutional arrangements can reshape family priorities and enable strategic investment in AI.

How Information Infrastructures Influence Beliefs Formation

OMT
Alexandra Valencia Zapata; Erik Reimer Larsen (Aarhus University); Dorthe Doejbak Haakonsson (Aarhus University); Kyosuke Tanaka (Aarhus University)
Collaborative organizations using natural resource commons often face tensions among actors with differing beliefs about cooperation, despite their shared goal of sustainability. Although beliefs can be challenging to change, these organizations can implement information infrastructures to influence actors’ beliefs. This study investigated how individuals update their beliefs about the extent to which other members of their community would cooperate over time within a hypothetical energy community. It evaluated responses under a two-condition, between-subjects experimental design in which participants received either aggregated feedback (i.e., information on individual electricity needs, requests, and allocation, and the community’s total requested electricity) or disaggregated feedback (i.e., each individual’s requested and allocated electricity). We conducted two experiments (N = 403 participants): a laboratory experiment with university undergraduates and a lab-in-the-field experiment with residents from an island in the Caribbean. Experimental results revealed that participants who received disaggregated feedback showed stronger belief persistence in their prior beliefs than those who received aggregated feedback. Specifically, this disaggregated information led to slower belief updating, suggesting that detailed information about others’ decisions reinforces prior beliefs. Therefore, collaborative organizations should carefully design their information infrastructures, as the feedback they provide can significantly shape beliefs about the consequences of cooperation.

Management as Rules: Evidence from an Adaptive Bayesian Questionnaire

OMT
Tom Schwantje (Bocconi University)
Most organisational practices are communicated and understood through rules, stable ``if–then'' decision policies, but such rules have traditionally been difficult to measure well. This paper develops a novel Bayesian survey method to empirically measure the use of managerial rules within organisations. The method implements an adaptive survey, incorporating Bayesian optimisation, to first understand decision-making in a potentially high-dimensional space. It uses the information from this method to then directly ask about the rules driving patterns in these decisions. I apply this tool to measure the rules used by senior HR managers at Ethiopian firms. Descriptively, I find that this measure robustly, positively correlates with firm performance and helps explain variation in performance across firms, above and beyond more traditional measures of firm management. Using quasi-exogenous variation from firms' exposure to COVID-19, I further find that while managerial rules enhance firm performance, they also reduce resilience to economic shocks, suggesting a trade-off in the use of more rigid practices between performance and flexibility. The methods and findings in this paper motivate further research into the causal mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of managerial rules, and into understanding the factors influencing the heterogeneous adoption of such rules across organisations.

Testing the Related Acquisitions Following Unrelated Spinoffs (RAFUS) Mechanism

OMT
James Alexander Profestas (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Pursey Heugens (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Emilio Marti; Taco Reus (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
This study revisits the Related Acquisitions Following Unrelated Spinoffs (RAFUS) framework as a dynamic organizational design mechanism through which firms rebalance autonomy and coordination under changing temporal and macro-financial conditions. Using RAFUS as an empirically tractable exemplar, we examine when and why autonomy–coordination rebalancing persists or weakens across changing coordination and financial conditions. We then extend the framework in two directions. First, a temporal extension tests whether the RAFUS mechanism persists across decades (1974 – 2024) of evolving coordination technologies and modular governance. Second, a monetary-policy extension incorporates the Federal Funds Rate (FFR) as a macro boundary condition that alters the cost of coordination and thus the feasibility of recomposition. Results show that the RAFUS relationship weakens in more digitally integrated eras and varies systematically across monetary regimes, consistent with RAFUS as a conditional rather than universal design mechanism. Importantly, monetary policy shapes the scale of post-spinoff recomposition rather than the likelihood of acquisition, reinforcing RAFUS as a conditional design mechanism rather than a universal restructuring pattern. Notably, tight credit environments favor focused, related acquisitions, while low-rate conditions are associated with greater diversification in the scale and mix of post-spinoff acquisitions. These findings refine RAFUS as a conditional design mechanism, clarifying the limits of its generalizability and situating it within an ODT framework of dynamic reconfiguration. The study contributes to theory-building by identifying when and how sequential corporate restructuring functions as a non-random organizational adaptation mechanism.

Navigating Multiple Logics in Hybrid Spaces: Symbolic Management in Corporate Venture Capital Units

OMT
Tobias Gutmann (EBS University of Business and Law); Magnus Schückes (University of Mannheim); Markus Perkmann (Imperial College London)
Organizations establish hybrid spaces – organizational subunits operating under distinct institutional logics – to extend their reach to additional stakeholders and pursue goals beyond their primary activities. Such spaces are notoriously difficult to manage. Prior research has shown how structural solutions – specific practices and processes – can help in productively managing the institutional complexity arising in these spaces. Yet structural solutions may be insufficient as they do not directly address the legitimacy deficit that hybrid spaces face within their own organization. We explore how actors in a hybrid space generate the necessary internal legitimacy for it and its activities. Through a three-year ethnographic study of a corporate venture capital unit in a multinational corporation, we find symbolic management plays a key role. We identify specific symbolic tactics – masking, reconciling, and staging – that are used to foster the legitimacy of the hybrid space towards internal stakeholders. Adding to prior research that has emphasized structural solutions, our study demonstrates that symbolic management is a distinct and necessary means of addressing institutional complexity in hybrid spaces.
