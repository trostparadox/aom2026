A Quality of Mercy is Not Trained: Practice vs. Representation of Care in Artificial Initelligence

HCM
Anand Bhardwaj (McGill University); Samer Faraj (McGill University)
Organizations increasingly turn to artificial intelligence (AI) to improve complex, knowledge-intensive work, yet systems with strong technical performance often fail to become meaningfully embedded in complex, multi-occupational work processes, such as in healthcare. This study examines this puzzle by analyzing how AI development interacts with the situated forms of knowing that underpin complex clinical-administrative work, where different knowledge bases and local goals are negotiated to deliver care. Drawing on a two-year qualitative field study at a major tertiary-care hospital, the analysis contrasts operating room (OR) scheduling-in-practice with OR scheduling-as-represented during the local development of an AI-based tool intended to augment scheduling. The study advances two key contributions: First, it foregrounds scheduling-in-practice as sustained through ongoing negotiation among distinct regimes of knowing, as surgeons, OR nurses, and programming clerks reconcile different knowledge bases and local goals through judgment, discretion, and compromise. Second, it highlights how developing process-specialized AI tends to focus on core tasks, selectively stabilizing the perspectives of knowers who are more amenable to mobilization and alignment, and forms of knowing that are more readily articulated and represented in data structures, model logic, and interface categories. data structures, model logic, and interface categories. Together, these insights explain why technically excellent AI systems often struggle to take hold in contexts such as healthcare: because care is sustained through negotiation among multiple ground truths, and AI systems are rarely designed to negotiate.

Healthcare Responsibility for Social & Digital Determinants: Leader Perspectives in the U.S.

HCM
Pavani Rangachari (University of New Haven); Alisha Thapa (University of New Haven); Naimitha Balireddy (University of New Haven); Vandana Vijaykumar Maurya (University of New Haven)
Background: U.S. health systems are increasingly expected to address social and digital determinants of health (SD-DOH) as part of equity-oriented transformation. While prior research documents growth in SD-DOH initiatives, less is known about how leaders and stakeholders frame healthcare organizations’ responsibilities for this work and whether those responsibility perspectives align with equity-oriented initiatives. Methods: We conducted a PRISMA-ScR–guided scoping review with qualitative evidence synthesis of U.S.-based peer-reviewed and gray literature published January 2020–June 2025 (N = 94; PubMed, Scopus, ProQuest, CINAHL). We integrated two conceptual lenses: (1) four SD-DOH strategic levers used to characterize equity-oriented initiatives: enabling services (downstream), community partnerships (midstream), policy advocacy (upstream), and direct community investment (upstream); and (2) responsibility perspectives grounded in the National Academy of Medicine’s 5A Social Care Framework (Awareness, Assistance, Adjustment, Alignment, Advocacy), extended by this study to include a sixth domain: system alignment & accountability. Descriptive synthesis characterized initiatives; reflexive thematic analysis examined responsibility perspectives and alignment with strategic levers via cross-tabulation. Results: Equity-oriented action concentrated in enabling services (86%) and community partnerships (70%), while policy advocacy (34%) and direct community investment (9%) were less common. Responsibility perspectives were multi-dimensional, with Alignment (83%) most prevalent, followed by Assistance (68%), Adjustment (65%), Awareness (61%), and system alignment & accountability (60%); Advocacy appeared least often (32%). Alignment patterns were strong: 100% of community partnerships and 100% of direct community investment initiatives reflected Alignment (and so did 83% of enabling service initiatives). Assistance and Adjustment were tightly coupled with enabling services (98% each). By contrast, Advocacy was concentrated in upstream action: 75% of policy advocacy initiatives (vs. 25% of enabling services initiatives). System alignment & accountability functioned as a bridge across levers, appearing in 100% of direct community investment initiatives, 75% of policy advocacy initiatives, 56% of community partnerships and 54% of enabling service initiatives. Conclusions: Responsibility framing consistently aligned with equity action across U.S. health systems. Although policy advocacy and direct community investment remain limited, the widespread presence of system alignment & accountability suggests the field may be at an inflection point. Institutionalizing equity through governance, measurement, and durable partnerships is essential, with policy advocacy representing a feasible near-term upstream pathway for systems with foundational alignment capacity. By empirically linking responsibility framing with equity action, this study advances theory on organizational responsibility for health equity and offers a foundation for more intentional and accountable equity-centered transformation.

Experience With AI Algorithms And Algorithm Aversion In AI-Supported Medical Care

HCM
Luigi Preti (Bocconi University); Amelia Compagni (Bocconi University); Giulia Cappellaro (Bocconi University); Bart Vanneste (University College London)
Despite the growing deployment of AI-based decision support systems in professional settings, experts frequently override algorithmic recommendations, particularly under conditions of high uncertainty. Prior research offers mixed evidence on whether experience with AI-based algorithms mitigates such algorithm aversion and provides limited insight into how different forms of experience matter over time. We address this gap by examining how experience with an AI-based algorithm shapes experts’ acceptance of algorithmic advice across decision contexts characterized by varying levels of uncertainty. Leveraging a unique longitudinal dataset of more than 600,000 real-world treatment recommendations generated by a certified AI system used in nephrology care, we study nephrologists’ acceptance of algorithmic recommendations in therapeutic decisions involving substantial clinical discretion and risk. We distinguish between time-based experience and volume-based experience with the algorithm. Our analyses show that time-based experience—rather than the sheer volume of interactions— increases acceptance of algorithmic recommendations, and that this effect is concentrated in decision contexts marked by the highest uncertainty. We further show that time-based experience is associated with reduced therapeutic inertia and greater alignment with the algorithm’s treatment optimization logic, accompanied by improved clinical performance.

Redefining the Patient in the Digital Twin Age: Conceptual Model for Inclusive Healthcare System

HCM
Atantra Das Gupta (Management Development Institute Gurgaon)
The emergence of the Digital Twin (DT) in healthcare signifies a revolutionary change in how patients are represented, managed, and governed within AI-driven systems. Traditionally, healthcare relied on episodic data and static diagnoses; now, the Digital Twin develops a dynamic, continuously learning digital replica of the patient—merging biological identity, data infrastructure, and algorithmic analysis. Although increasingly important in precision medicine and clinical simulations, the theoretical foundations and ethical oversight of Digital Twins are still underdeveloped. This paper provides a scoping review and conceptual integration of 76 peer-reviewed studies (2000–2025) that define the Digital Twin as both a technological system and a cyber-physical entity. Using the PRISMA-ScR methodology, the review identifies key gaps in knowledge, governance, and inclusivity across three intersecting areas— conceptual/theoretical, empirical/technical, and policy/ethical research. Six themes emerged: trust and transparency, cultural inclusivity, data agency, participatory governance, AI personalization, and moral regulation. Building on these findings, the study proposes a three-dimensional framework that redefines the patient concept in the Digital Twin era along the axes of identity, agency, and participation, and adaptive governance. This framework underscores that ethical AI in healthcare requires reflexive, participatory, and equitable governance models that can evolve alongside technology. By viewing the Digital Twin as a socio-technical ecosystem rather than just a computational tool, this paper promotes a policy approach for responsible, transparent, and human-centered innovation in the field of intelligent medicine.

Generative AI and Patient Interaction in Online Health Communities

HCM
Changqing Zhuo (Renmin University of China); Jiamin Yin (Renmin University of China); Kanliang Wang (Renmin University of China)
Online health communities (OHCs) generate substantial value for patients and platforms. Recent advances in generative AI (GenAI) enable patients to obtain on-demand support through private, conversational interfaces. However, how GenAI affects patient-to-patient interaction in OHCs remains an open empirical question. GenAI may facilitate subsequent peer exchange by helping patients articulate concerns, or it may substitute for peer interaction by absorbing inquiries that would otherwise be shared within the community. Drawing on coping theory and social support theory, we develop a theoretical framework that theorizes the impact of GenAI on patients’ posting and commenting behaviors in OHCs. Leveraging a natural experiment on a large Chinese OHC, we use a difference-in-differences design to examine GenAI’s effects on patients’ behaviors. Results show that the introduction of GenAI is associated with fewer problem-focused posts and information-support comments, alongside more emotion-focused posts and emotion-support comments, particularly among problem-focused patients and informational support providers. These findings contribute to the literature on GenAI in healthcare and online health communities by illuminating how GenAI influences patient interaction in OHCs and offer practical guidance for OHC platforms for integrating GenAI into their platforms.
