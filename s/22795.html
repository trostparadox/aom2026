Multiple Pathways to Corporate Philanthropic Disaster Response in Japan: A Configurational Analysis

SIM
Kentaro Azuma (Ritsumeikan University); Nicolas Dahan (Seton Hall University)
This study investigates corporate philanthropic disaster response (CPDR) through a configurational approach. Using fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) on 232 Japanese listed firms after the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake, we identify three sufficient pathways for the decision to donate and four for high donation amounts. Results show that pragmatic and cognitive legitimacy provide the most consistent foundations of CPDR, while moral legitimacy plays a more selective role. The analysis reconciles prior contradictory findings by demonstrating that determinants such as embeddedness, social engagement, and legacy matter in contingent ways, whereas consumer orientation, profitability, and firm size contribute only through their presence. The study advances research on CPDR and legitimacy by showing how Suchman's tripartite framework can be systematically applied in a large-N configurational design.

From Celebrity to Philanthropist: How CEO Celebrities Influence Firm Donations?

SIM
Yiling Chen (Henan Normal University); Yuqing Cui (Henan Normal University); yanqi yang (Henan Normal University); Zhigang Zhang (Henan Normal University)
Corporate philanthropic donation is a key strategy for organizations to acquire legitimacy and social capital. Drawing on insights from upper echelons theory and signaling theory, this study addresses a significant gap in the literature by shifting the focus from organizational-level antecedents of corporate philanthropy to the role of a specific type of strategic leader: the celebrity CEO. We theorize that the salient social identity and heightened reputational capital of a celebrity CEO serve as a distinct driver of corporate philanthropic engagement. Using data from Chinese A-share privately listed firms on the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges from 2010 to 2022, we construct an ordinal measure of CEO celebrity for empirical testing. The findings indicate that CEO celebrity has a significantly positive impact on corporate philanthropic donation. Further analysis shows that CEO power, investor attention, and the development of digital financial inclusion positively moderate this relationship. Specifically, the positive effect of CEO celebrity on donation is stronger when the CEO has greater internal decision-making power, external market attention is higher, and the regional digital financial environment is more developed. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that this effect is more pronounced for firms in their start-up stage and when the CEO is male or lacks an overseas background. By systematically introducing the mechanisms of "social visibility" and "reputational capital" into the upper echelons analytical framework, this study unveils how managers' social identity shapes corporate non-market strategy and clarifies its contingent conditions, thereby extending the research boundaries on strategic leadership and corporate social responsibility.

Measuring the Good: Advancing Social Return on Investment

SIM
Tahniyath Fatima (Qatar University); Manoj A. Thomas (University of Sydney Business School)
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) research has made significant progress in examining organizational-level and stakeholder-specific outcomes of socially responsible initiatives. However, CSR literature remains limited in its ability to capture broader societal impact, particularly in contexts where organizational interventions are intended to contribute to long-term development outcomes. This paper advances Social Return on Investment (SROI) as a Theory of Change-based valuation framework capable of bridging this gap. Drawing on CSR and SROI literatures, we identify the limitations of SROI framework and propose best practices based on the principles of SROI. The paper contributes to the literature on social issues in management by improving effective social impact evaluation, highlighting the role of continuous learning in sustainability-oriented methodological innovations, and clarifying the value of SROI as a conceptual bridge between organizational CSR activities and macro-level development goals.

Escape After Wealth: Executive Emigration and Corporate Charitable Giving

SIM
Xuerong Peng (Zhejiang Gongshang University); Pei Fang (Zhejiang Gongshang University); Seoki Lee (The Pennsylvania State University)
Drawing on upper echelons theory and institutional theory, this study investigates the direct impact of executive emigration on corporate social responsibility (CSR), specifically corporate giving, among hospitality and tourism (H&T) firms and further explores the moderating roles of firm and executive visibility in this relationship. We argue that executive emigration significantly inhibits the CSR efforts of associated domestic H&T firms due to executives' reduced demands for domestic individual legitimacy. However, increased firm and executive visibility mitigates this negative effect by exerting informal institutional legitimacy pressure on CSR engagement among H&T firms to uphold domestic corporate growth. Hypotheses are tested using longitudinal data comprising 652 firm-year observations from 70 publicly listed Chinese H&T firms from 2001 to 2021. Our findings indicate that executive emigration negatively affects corporate giving, and this effect is mitigated when either emigrated executives or affiliated firms have greater visibility. This work enhances our understanding of the interplay between internal executive cognitive preferences and external informal institutional pressures in predicting CSR in the H&T industry.

Nationalist Talk, Philanthropic Action: Evidence from China

SIM
Iris Chenyue Yang (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
In an era of rising nationalism, organizations increasingly adopt nationalist rhetoric as a strategic tool for legitimacy. However, how this rhetoric interacts with other corporate strategies remains unclear. This study examines the link between rhetorical nationalism and corporate philanthropy among Chinese listed firms. It is found that while nationalist rhetoric does not increase the likelihood of donating, it is associated with larger donations among firms that do contribute. These effects are moderated by directors’ overseas experience and the presence of women in senior management. This research extends the literature on organizational nationalism by exploring its interactions with other non-market strategies.
