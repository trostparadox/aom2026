From Campus to Career: How Students’ Growth Mindset Influences Their Job Search

CAR
Samira Fallah (University of Mary Washington); Kanchan Deosthali (University of Mary Washington); Azadeh Sabz (Missouri State University)
Searching for a job is an effort-intensive and stressful process for college students. Their limited experience and smaller professional networks exacerbate the challenges. While prior research has highlighted the role of personal attributes in shaping job search behaviors, less is known about how students’ mindsets influence their search strategies. This study examines the role of growth mindset about job search (GMJS), the belief that job search skills can be developed through effort and learning, in shaping students’ search behaviors. Our findings show that GMJS affects job search intensity and haphazard job search primarily through two sequential mediation pathways: a bolstering pathway involving psychological capital and job search self-efficacy, and a buffering pathway involving psychological capital and job search stress. We also find a marginally significant negative direct effect of GMJS on both job search intensity and haphazard job search, suggesting that students with higher GMJS tend to engage in a more strategic and purposeful job search rather than frequent but uncoordinated activities. Together, these findings extend growth mindset theory into the job search domain and identify GMJS as a key antecedent of students’ job search behaviors and psychological capital, also offering practical insights for career development programs in higher education.

The Path Not Chosen: Psychological Costs of Career Indecision in Early Career Stages

CAR
Fauzia Syed (Rabat Business School, International University of Rabat); Muhammad Waheed Akhtar (Rabat Business School, International University of Rabat); Muhammad Usman (University of Sharjah); Qaisra Zaheer (International Islamic University)
ddCareer indecision represents a significant psychological burden, especially among young professionals in the service sector who frequently face occupational instability, emotional labor, and limited career development opportunities. Despite its prevalence, limited research has examined the downstream emotional and behavioral consequences of unresolved career choices during this formative stage. Drawing upon regret regulation theory (Zeelenberg & Pieters, 2007), this research elucidates the emotional and cognitive consequences of career indecision by examining its associations with emotional exhaustion, psychological withdrawal, and career distress. Across two multi-study designs, findings establish that self-blame and career regrets mediate the relationship between career indecision and these negative outcomes, while self-regulation failure serves as a critical moderator, exacerbating regret intensity among individuals with reduced regulatory capacities. Importantly, the study introduces a theoretically novel three-way interaction, demonstrating that the interplay between career indecision and self-regulation failure on self-blame career regrets is more pronounced among early-career individuals than their late-career counterparts. This developmental contingency suggests that early-career individuals, due to limited experiential resources and a heightened future time perspective, are more susceptible to maladaptive emotional responses when confronted with career uncertainty. These insights extend the application of regret regulation theory to vocational contexts, emphasizing the need for career stage-sensitive interventions, including personalized counseling and self-regulation training, to mitigate the adverse psychological effects of career indecision.

Curvilinear Relationship Between Future Work Self Salience and Career Inaction: A Dual-Path Model

CAR
Huiru Lu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Whereas future work self salience (FWSS) is typically regarded as a motivational asset that fosters proactive career behaviors, we propose a curvilinear relationship with career inaction (i.e., the failure to act over time on a career desire). Drawing on regulatory focus theory, we hypothesize an inverted U-shaped relationship between FWSS and career inaction that is mediated by two distinct pathways: a promotion-focused path via career self-efficacy and a prevention-focused path via career rumination. Results from three-wave survey study in China (N = 144) support our model. At low-to-moderate levels, FWSS is positively related to career inaction through career rumination. At moderate-to-high levels, FWSS is negatively related to career inaction through increasing career self-efficacy and decreasing career rumination. Furthermore, career wisdom buffers the indirect effect of FWSS on career inaction via career rumination at low-to-moderate FWSS levels. This research also identifies career wisdom as a critical resource to mitigate career inaction at low-to-moderate levels of FWSS.

Formal or Workplace Education Builds Sustainable Entrepreneurs? The Mediating Role of Soft Skills

CAR
Aleksandra Janeska-Iliev (Ss Cyril and Methodius University in Skopje, Faculty of Economics); Stojan Debarliev (Ss. Cyril and Methodius University in Skopje); Bojan Kitanovikj (Ss Cyril and Methodius University in Skopje, Faculty of Economics)
This study investigates how formal-education approaches and workplace training approaches relate to human-capital interaction outcomes in entrepreneurial firms across the Western Balkans (Serbia, North Macedonia, Bosnia & Herzegovina, and Albania). Drawing on human capital and dynamic capabilities perspectives, we test a parallel mediation in which Developed soft skills transmit the effects of education and workplace practices to organizational results. Using cross-sectional survey data and mediation regression analysis, we find that both approaches have significant positive direct effects on human-capital interaction outcomes and significant indirect effects via developed soft skills, indicating partial mediation. Effects are consistently stronger for workplace training approaches, suggesting that situated learning consolidates soft-skill behaviors into day-to-day routines, while formal education provides complementary conceptual foundations—particularly when it embeds experiential components. The model explains substantial variance in human-capital interaction outcomes, offering a mechanism-based account that connects entrepreneurship education to firm-level benefits. We discuss implications for curriculum design, enterprise training strategy, and policy instruments that align higher education with workplace development in transitional economies.
