Self-Awareness as a Meta-Capacity for Ethical and Spiritual Agency: Dimensions, Processes, & Effects

MSR
Ajoy Kumar Dey (IILM UNIVERSITY, GURUGRAM); Sachin Tyagi (Goa Institute of Management); Shreya Mishra (Birla Institute of Management Technology)
Self-awareness is widely acknowledged as central to ethical agency, leadership discernment, and personal development, yet its conceptual and empirical treatment remains fragmented across management, psychology, and spirituality research. This integrative review synthesizes 68 rigorously selected studies to reconceptualize self-awareness as an organized meta-capacity that coordinates attentional, evaluative, regulatory, and developmental processes, enabling individuals to interpret self-relevant experience and translate it into value-congruent action over time. The framework specifies constituent dimensions, enabling conditions, mediating mechanisms, and boundary conditions, clarifying why self-awareness can both support moral agency, authentic leadership, and meaning-making, and also amplify rumination, defensiveness, or ethical distortion. By situating self-awareness as a process-oriented foundation for ethical and spiritual agency, the study advances theory at the intersection of strategy, leadership, ethics, and spirituality, providing a disciplined platform for cumulative and context-sensitive research on inner awareness in organizational life.

Leadership Development from the Vedic Tradition

MSR
Anil K. Maheshwari (Maharishi International University); Poonam Purohit (Chanakya University)
Despite the proliferation of leadership theories, models, and frameworks, contemporary organizations continue to experience high levels of stress, burnout, ethical erosion, and declining mental well-being. If prevailing leadership paradigms were genuinely holistic, such crises of integrity and inner imbalance would not persist. Drawing on classical Vedic texts, including the Bhagavad Gita, Upanishads, and Patanjali Yoga Sutras, etc., we argue that effective leadership arises not primarily through additive skill acquisition but through subtractive inner purification. Leadership development is conceptualized as a dynamic balance between Niv?tti (inward purification work involving the reduction of mental impurities) and Prav?tti (ethical and responsible outward expansion of engagement). An exploratory quantitative study employed two instruments: a Ten-Impurity Index (e.g., pride, anger, greed, jealousy) and a Twenty-Six-Virtue Index (e.g., compassion, humility, steadfastness). Data analysis showed that compassion, humility, and steadfast faith emerged as the strongest positive predictors, while greed was the most detrimental impurity. These results highlight the necessity of integrating inner purification with outward leadership behaviors, pointing toward a sustainable, consciousness-based model of leadership development.

Leading with Spirit: Curbing Knowledge Sabotage via Empathy and Moral Identity in the Hotel Industry

MSR
Muhammad Muavia; Muhammad Usman (University of Sharjah); Muhammad Shahid (The University of Lahore); Rabeeya Raoof
Purpose – This study aims to explore how spiritual leadership can serve as a strategic deterrent to knowledge sabotage—a covert, counterproductive behavior that undermines organizational learning and performance. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, it examines the emotional and moral mechanisms through which spiritual leadership influences employee behavior. Specifically, it investigates the mediating role of empathic concern and the moderating role of moral identity in shaping this relationship within the hospitality sector. Design/methodology/approach – A time-lagged, multi-source survey was conducted among 368 hotel employees and their supervisors across three major cities in Pakistan. Structural equation modeling and moderated mediation analysis were employed to test the hypothesized relationships. Findings – Spiritual leadership significantly reduces knowledge sabotage. Empathic concern mediates this relationship, while moral identity strengthens the negative association between empathic concern and sabotage. The conditional indirect effect confirms that high moral identity amplifies the protective influence of spiritual leadership. Originality/value – This study introduces spiritual leadership as a novel antecedent to knowledge sabotage and highlights the emotional and moral pathways that buffer deviant knowledge behavior. It offers actionable insights for leadership development and ethical climate enhancement in high-contact service environments.

Spiritual Leadership as Moral Infrastructure: Greenwashing as Anathema in Sri Lankan Banks

MSR
Amali Shanika De Silva; Louis W. Fry (Texas A&M University-Central Texas)
As scrutiny of corporate sustainability intensifies, organizations face growing pressure to demonstrate the authenticity of their Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) commitments. Although Spiritual Leadership Theory (SLT) has been tested primarily through quantitative hypothesis-driven studies of individual and organizational outcomes, little qualitative research has examined how leaders’ inner moral worlds function as the foundational infrastructure shaping authentic sustainability practice—particularly in highly regulated sectors within emerging economies. This exploratory qualitative study investigates how chief executive officers (CEOs) of Sri Lanka’s licensed local commercial banks construct and enact spiritual leadership through inner moral reasoning and spiritual self-regulation. Drawing on near-census elite interviews with 11 CEOs (85% of the population), the analysis reveals a moral process model linking inner-life cultivation, moral self-regulation, resistance to greenwashing, stewardship, and legacy orientation, and institutional defence of values. Participants framed greenwashing not as technical misreporting but as moral drift arising from weakened inner discipline. Together, these findings reposition spiritual leadership as an inner moral infrastructure that safeguards corporate sustainability under intense institutional pressure.

Implications of Multifaith Fasting on Organizations

MSR
Hussein Al Jardali (American University of Beirut); Rima M. Bizri (Canadian University Dubai); Amr Kebbi (American University of Beirut)
This conceptual paper argues that fasting, as a spiritual practice rooted in delayed gratification, can enhance organizational functioning, particularly in multifaith workplaces. Building on self-regulation theory and the concept of delayed gratification, we propose that fasting cultivates patience, self-control, empathy, and shared discipline, which positively influence teamwork, leadership, and organizational culture. The Lebanese context in 2025 offers a unique case in which Christians and Muslims fasted during overlapping periods, fostering a dynamic of shared spiritual discipline across faiths. Using this real-world occurrence, the paper develops a model that links fasting-induced self-regulation to organizational culture, including enhanced trust, group cohesion, task performance, and inclusive, values-based leadership. Traditional self-regulation research views impulse control as an internal trait. At the same time, our model posits that, through employee-synchronized multigroup fasting, self-regulation becomes socially reinforced, creating an external environment that strengthens internal regulation and demonstrates that it can be cultivated and magnified through cultural practices embedded in organizational life. By integrating insights from spiritual leadership, workplace spirituality, and entrepreneurial behaviour research, this study contributes to conversations on workplace spirituality, inclusive HR practices, and behavioural antecedents of productivity. It fills a critical gap by connecting a profound human discipline to measurable organizational outcomes within faith-sensitive environments.
