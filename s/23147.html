Framing Generative Art: Public Evaluation of Machine Creativity

CTO
Milad Saeedi (Ivey School of Business); Ning Su (University of Western Ontario); Robert D Austin (Ivey School of Business)
Generative art (artwork created autonomously or semi-autonomously by digital technologies) has gained increasing public attention as advances in generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) promise new forms of creativity, innovation, and artistic production. Despite this growing visibility, generative art remains largely absent from the management and organization literature, leaving a limited understanding of how this emerging technology is interpreted, evaluated, and legitimized in the public domain. Public discourse plays a critical role by providing frames through which actors make sense of novel technologies and coordinate collective evaluation. Drawing on framing and legitimacy theories, this study examines how generative art is evaluated in news media discourse. Through qualitative analysis of public news articles, we identify five distinct frames that differ systematically in their purpose, focus, temporal orientation, sentiment, and legitimacy logic. These frames portray generative art as (1) augmented human creativity, (2) ethical and moral transgression, (3) democratized but devalued production, (4) an emerging cultural institution, and (5) a data-driven and opaque infrastructure. Together, these framings reveal how generative art is simultaneously normalized, contested, and stabilized through competing evaluative logics. Our findings show that legitimacy is negotiated across moral, cognitive, and pragmatic dimensions and anchored in different temporal horizons, shaping whether generative art is treated as an ongoing ethical problem, an evolving creative practice, or a settled cultural form. This study contributes to research on framing, legitimacy, and creativity and highlights why debates surrounding AI-enabled creativity remain unresolved despite growing institutional and market adoption.

When Machines Express Ethical Concerns: Examining Employee Reactions to AI Moral Advice

CTO
Yangchun YAN (Central South University); Yueru Ma (Central South University); Tina Rongwen Jia (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
As artificial intelligence (AI) takes on communicative roles in organizations, they are emerging as potential agents of expressing ethical concerns. However, little is known about how employees perceive and respond to AI-generated moral advice. Drawing on the theory of discourse ethics, this research examines employee reactions to two types of AI-generated moral advice: AI’s promotive moral advice, i.e., advocating moral good, and AI’s prohibitive moral advice, i.e., warning against ethical violations. Across three studies—a survey (Study 1) and two scenario-based experiments (Study 2 and 3)—we find that AI’s promotive moral advice induces humanity threat and thus decreases advice endorsement; in contrast, AI’s prohibitive moral advice promotes moral reflection and facilitates endorsement. Study 3 further examines a moderating role of AI explainability. Results showed that, while individuals tends to endorse prohibitive moral advice of explainable AI due to the increases of moral reflection, they are less likely to take promotive advice of explainable AI because of intensified humanity threat. The findings highlight the distinct mechanisms underlying employee reactions to different types of AI-generated moral advice and offer a nuanced view of explainable AI in shaping legitimacy of its moral guidance.

Pluralistic Evaluative Logics in Public Perception of Artificial Intelligence

CTO
Necdet Gurkan (University of Missouri-Saint Louis)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in organizational and societal decision-making, yet public evaluation of AI remains contested. Prior research typically models public perceptions as aggregate attitudes or demographic differences, obscuring how disagreement is structured by shared evaluative frameworks. We develop a consensus-based account of AI evaluation that conceptualizes public judgment as organized around multiple coexisting evaluative logics—internally coherent ways of coordinating sociotechnical values such as trust, risk, fairness, accountability, and autonomy. Using nationally representative U.S. survey data from 2021, 2023, and 2024, we extend Cultural Consensus Theory to infer latent evaluative logics and characterize how values are coordinated within each logic. Across all waves, public evaluation exhibits persistent pluralism rather than convergence toward a single framework. Evaluative logics differ not only in the emphasis placed on specific values but also in their underlying trade-off structures. Entropy-based analyses further show that logic membership cuts across demographic categories, indicating that disagreement reflects shared evaluative frameworks rather than demographic polarization. These findings highlight the need for AI governance and decision support systems that operate under conditions of enduring normative pluralism.

Algorithmic Agency in Organizations: Reconceptualizing AI as Organizational Actor

CTO
Fayyaz Khan (Al Yamamah University)
Organizations increasingly rely on algorithmic systems to make consequential decisions about workers, resources, and strategy. Existing research bifurcates into human-AI collaboration (HAIC), which examines how humans use AI as tools, and algorithmic management (AM), which examines how AI constrains human agency. Both streams treat algorithms instrumentally rather than as organizational actors. This paper challenges the instrumental view by theorizing algorithmic agency: the capacity of algorithmic systems to autonomously initiate, execute, and adapt organizational decisions independent of human oversight. Drawing on organizational theory, I develop a dimensional framework identifying four constitutive dimensions (autonomy, scope, temporality, opacity) and theorize how their configurations shape organizational outcomes. This configurational approach explains why similar algorithmic properties produce different worker experiences, for example, comprehensive opacity creates an "invisible cage" on Upwork but "information asymmetry" on Uber. This reconceptualization has implications for organizational theory's treatment of hierarchy, accountability, governance, and membership boundaries.

Workplace Surveillance and Agency Perceptions With Generative AI

CTO
Joel Jose Rivera Jiménez (Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore); Giovanni Gatti; Marco Marabelli (Bentley University); Jeffrey Proudfot (Bentley University)
While enterprise GAI platforms such as Microsoft 365 Copilot offer secure, organization-controlled environments and promise substantial productivity gains, they also introduce emerging risks for employees. In this paper, we focus on workplace surveillance as a critical yet underexplored consequence of enterprise GAI adoption. Drawing on a qualitative case study of an organization implementing Microsoft 365 Copilot, we examine how employees perceive and reconcile the productivity benefits of GAI with concerns about surveillance and monitoring. Our findings reveal that employees perceive enterprise GAI as an additional, abstract surveillance agent capable of both enabling and constraining their work. Employees respond to this perceived surveillance in differentiated ways depending on their organizational roles. We contribute to the workplace surveillance literature by reconceptualizing surveillance as a triadic relationship involving human actors and GAI platforms, and to research on algorithmic surveillance by showing how enterprise GAI extends algorithmic monitoring beyond platform-mediated work into conventional organizational settings. Finally, we highlight important implications for organizations, researchers, and policymakers concerning the governance and ethical risks of workplace surveillance in the age of enterprise GAI.
