Designing Digital Systems That Sustain Clinical Judgment: Cognitive Sustainability in Healthcare

CTO
Curtis Merriweather (Duke University); Michael Richard Cauley (Vanderbilt University)
Digital systems increasingly shape how professionals exercise judgment in complex, high-stakes work. In healthcare, electronic health record (EHR) systems have become the primary organizational platform through which clinicians access information, coordinate across roles, and make diagnostic and treatment decisions. Yet many digital health systems are designed to optimize documentation, compliance, and data capture rather than the cognitive conditions required for high-quality decision-making. Drawing on a qualitative study of interprofessional healthcare teams, this paper examines how EHR systems influence clinical judgement and introduces the concept of cognitive sustainability: the capacity of digital systems to support sustained, accurate, and contextually grounded decision-making over time. Our findings show that cognitive overload, poor interoperability, and fragmented communication structures remain attentive to patient needs. We synthesize these insights into a Decision-Making Axis that highlights how technology design choices shape cognitive load, sensemaking, and relational coordination in digitally mediated work. The paper contributes to the CTO Division by reframing judgment under complexity. We argue that cognitive sustainability should be treated as a core design and governance criterion for digital platforms, particularly as organizations integrate advanced analytics and artificial intelligence (AI) into mission-critical processes. Keywords: cognitive load, cognitive sustainability, decision-making, electronic health records (EHRs), human factors, sociotechnical systems

Does AI Exacerbate the Green Technology Innovation Opportunism? Evidence from a Mixed-Method Study

CTO
Keyu Chen (Zhejiang University); Jingyi Li (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics)
Against the backdrop of intensifying global environmental constraints, green technology innovation has become a core pathway for achieving sustainable development. Meanwhile, Artificial Intelligence (AI), as a disruptive technology characterized by both general applicability and pervasiveness, is regarded as a new driver for stimulating green innovation. However, AI-enabled green innovation does not unidimensionally "improve quality and efficiency." Under the mechanisms of dual externalities and indirect benefits, it may also induce a decoupling tendency within firms. Existing research largely focuses on the promoting effects of AI while overlooking its potential institutional distortions and decoupling risks. Based on decoupling theory, this study proposes that firms may leverage AI to excessively reinforce explicit and visible technological efforts in green innovation to achieve legitimacy and reputational gains, rather than focusing on substantive technological improvements. Utilizing a process-embedded mixed-method research design, which combines grounded theory analysis of central energy SOEs with empirical testing of data from 597 AI-equipped Chinese listed companies between 2018 and 2024, the results indicate the following: AI capability significantly reinforces a firm's tendency toward green technology innovation decoupling characterized by low cost and high speed, and it indirectly leads to this effect by promoting digital-real technology integration. Conversely, common institutional ownership, acting as an external monitoring force embedded in firms, effectively inhibits this process. This study challenges the mainstream view that AI promotes green technology innovation and reveals its potential decoupling tendency. Furthermore, it extends the applicable boundaries of decoupling theory in the context of green innovation, providing theoretical and practical implications for firms to conduct substantive innovation using AI and for policymakers to optimize effective green innovation incentive mechanisms.

Discord to Novelty: How Digital Intelligence Reveals Hidden Opportunities and Catalyzes Innovation

CTO
Jihun Choi (Korea University); Jongwoon Han (Korea University); Young-Kyu Kim (Korea University)
Digital intelligence (DI) is widely viewed as important for innovation, yet prior studies disagree on whether it mainly reinforces efficiency or enables exploration. We argue that this ambiguity reflects organizational context. Building on the behavioral theory of the firm and the resource-based view, we theorize that internal fragmentation across coalitions reduces firms’ share of exploration by weakening perceived synergistic resources for innovation— members’ recognition that dispersed expertise and ideas can be recombined for novel outcomes. We further propose that DI mitigates this negative indirect effect by increasing internal visibility and enabling the discovery and redeployment of latent resources. Using the 2022 Korean Innovation Survey of manufacturing firms, results support the proposed mediated relationship and show that DI significantly attenuates the negative indirect effect, suggesting that internal fragmentation need not be merely a liability but can become a source of exploration when DI helps surface and recombine internally dispersed resources.

Inside Out: When Joy Doesn’t Help. Incidental Emotions and Peer Similarity in Social Evaluations

CTO
Chang Wa Huynh (ESCP Business School); Arnaud Cudennec
Why do individuals form social connections, and with whom? While research consistently documents homophily, we know little about how incidental emotions—emotions unrelated to the connection decision itself— affect these choices. Drawing on the Appraisal Tendency Framework, we theorize that incidental emotions influence social connection through two pathways. An activity pathway shapes whether incidental emotions encourage or discourage connection formation, while a selection pathway shapes how similarity between individuals impacts the effect of incidental emotions on connection formation. We test this model using proprietary panel data from a large online movie ratings platform, combining behavioral data on connection formation with transformer-based language model measures of emotional exposure. Consistent with our theory, exposure to anger and surprise increases connection likelihood, whereas exposure to joy decreases it. However, the similarity between individuals modulates these effects: a greater similarity amplifies the effect of joy on connection formation while it negatively moderates the effect of surprise. These findings demonstrate that incidental emotions—operating through action readiness and certainty rather than valence—can shape peer group composition and the informational environments underlying social evaluations.

Is Remote Reporting Oversight Effective? Early Evidence from Supervisory Board Meetings

CTO
Lino Ballof (Technical University of Munich); Michael Stich (Technical University of Munich); Maria Stich (Technical University of Munich)
This study provides evidence whether the meeting policy – either face-to-face or remote – of the supervisory board of German firms affects oversight effectiveness of executive board’s decision-making. To provide empirical evidence from a major western economy, we use unique data provided in accordance with the disclosure recommendation of the German Corporate Governance Code. Controlling for other governance mechanisms and the endogenous nature of governance characteristics, we find that the average level of earnings management increases with the percentage of remote plenary meetings of the supervisory board. More specifically, we document a U-shape relation where the average level of earnings management is lowest when about one quarter of all plenary meetings are held remotely. Taken together, we take our findings as early evidence that firms should carefully weigh and combine different meeting types to most effectively constrain executive board’s earnings management rather than establishing monotonous meeting policies.
