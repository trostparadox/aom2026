Plowing Ahead or Sowing Seeds: Navigating Mental Health Stigma in Occupations

MOC
Devin Rapp (San Diego State University); Robert Monnot (Rockhurst University); Matt Hughey (University of Nebraska-Lincoln); Glen E. Kreiner (The University of Utah); Dane Cannon (The University of Utah)
Mental health stigma is one of the most salient and costly forces preventing individuals from seeking help and improving well-being. Drawing on 101 semi-structured interviews with farmers and agricultural workers, we identify the forces promoting and diminishing mental health stigma and consider promising tactics implemented by workers that reframe traditional occupational values to reduce stigma and promote healthier approaches to work and mental health. Our study contributes to the stigma literature by illuminating how occupations can serve as amplifiers of stigma. Our multi-level analysis demonstrates how stigma operates and manifests simultaneously at self, social, and structural levels, offering a novel integrative perspective. We also showcase novel, occupation-based pathways for stigma reduction. Our findings highlight how farmers successfully reframe traditional occupational values—values like self-reliance, resilience, and strength that have often served as sources of mental health stigma—transforming them from barriers into pathways for reducing stigma and supporting mental well-being. Finally, we offer practical implications aimed at reducing stigma and promoting mental wellness in rural settings and beyond, calling on researchers and practitioners to address this significant but under-studied source of suffering and economic loss.

Unpacking the Architecture of Workplace Authenticity: Strategic Drivers and Their Dynamics

MOC
Jayasankar MA (Indian Institute of Technology Guwahati); Abraham Cyril Issac (Indian Institute of Technology Guwahati)
Authenticity is a strong foundational construct to ensure health, well-being, job engagement, mindfulness, and emotional intelligence in the work environment. It is characterized by a worker's sense of being true to themselves and loyal to their values. This study identifies and analyzes the interrelationships among various enablers or strategic factors (SFs) of workplace authenticity, specifically in the context of the Information Technology industry. To date, no studies have been conducted to unravel the key enablers of workplace authenticity in the IT industry. To address this knowledge deficit, we employ Total Interpretive Structural Modeling (TISM) to develop a comprehensive model of workplace authenticity, specifically tailored to the IT industry. Utilizing the Cross-Impact Matrix Multiplication Applied to Classification (MICMAC) method, we further categorize the enablers into four quadrants: autonomous, dependent, linkage, and driving/independent based on their driving power and dependence power. A total of eleven factors have been identified for the study, grounded on an extensive literature review, brainstorming, and expert ratings from the IT industry associated with academia. Autonomy, opportunities for learning and development, and supervisory support exert strong driving power and weak dependence power and are placed at the lowest level of the TISM hierarchy. The findings further demonstrate the role of job resources in shaping psychological capabilities and interpersonal dynamics of employees. Policymakers and top management must emphasize those factors that have a strong driving power for the successful nurturing and empowerment of authenticity within IT organizations, which can ensure employee engagement, retention, and overall performance.

Turning Mistakes into Performance Gains: The Role of Error Management Culture

MOC
Ying-Ni Cheng; Hsu-Chu Wu (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Tian-Jyun Gong (National Chung Cheng University)
Making mistakes is an unavoidable part of organizational operations, and effectively managing employees' errors is a core concern for organizations and academia. Traditional approaches to error handling often focus on preventing employees from making errors. However, some scholars have proposed that organizations can adopt an "Error Management Culture" (EMC), which aims to minimize the negative impact of mistakes, quickly control damage, and reduce the likelihood of the same mistake occurring again in the future, thereby allowing for learning and growth. This culture can influence employees' tendencies toward problem-solving or blaming, leading to different levels of work performance—an area that warrants further exploration. Additionally, since awareness of errors is relevant with work stressors, this study further examines the potential moderating effect of trait conscientiousness on the relationship between two mediators (i.e. problem-solving and blaming) and performance. Data were collected from 517 employees through a prominent market research firm in three phases. The contribution of this study is to clarify two opposing mediation paths between error management culture and work performance. The practical implication is to provide organizations with concrete management recommendations to shape a more effective error-handling culture and to plan management training programs, which can ultimately lead to the positive consequences of mistakes.

When Value Congruence Undermines Pride:Prestige Motivation & Emotional Pathway to Voice & Engagement

MOC
Md Kamrul Hasan (Washington State University); Hana Johnson (Washington State University)
Does alignment between employees’ values and those of their organization always facilitate greater engagement and voice? Although person–organization value congruence is often assumed to promote positive attitudes and proactive contribution via cognitive or relational mechanisms, we argue that its effects may also derive from emotional processes. Drawing on the group engagement model, we propose that pride is a central self-conscious emotion linking value congruence to employee engagement and voice. We predict that value congruence increases pride, which in turn motivates engagement and voice. Then, based on the contingencies-of-self-worth perspective, we further theorize that prestige motivation moderates the effect of value congruence on pride. We predict that the positive effect of value congruence on pride is weaker for individuals whose self-worth is more strongly contingent on external status and recognition (i.e., prestige motivation). Feelings of pride then drive voice behavior and job engagement. Results from an online scenario experiment with 397 full-time employees in the United States largely support this model. Our findings reveal that the emotional consequences of person–organization value congruence are contingent rather than constant, clarifying when value congruence elicits pride and subsequently promotes engagement and voice.
