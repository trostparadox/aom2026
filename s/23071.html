When Frugal Healthcare Innovations Stall: Institutional Constraints in an Emerging Market

HCM
Evertom Waltrick da Silva (Universidade do Vale do Itajaí - Univali); André Moraes dos Santos (Universidade do Vale do Itajaí - Univali)
Frugal innovations are often developed in emerging markets to address severe resource constraints, institutional voids, and limited access to essential services, yet many struggle to diffuse beyond localized pilots. Existing diffusion models centered on perceived innovation attributes underexplain these outcomes because they insufficiently account for institutional restrictions characteristic of resource-constrained environments. This study examines how institutional mechanisms shape the diffusion process of frugal healthcare innovations in emerging markets by integrating Diffusion of Innovation theory with Institutional Theory. We develop a process account of the diffusion trajectory of tilapia-skin biocurative dressing for burn treatment within Brazil’s healthcare system—an emerging-market context marked by regulatory rigidity, public procurement constraints, and professional hierarchies. The study draws on twelve semi-structured interviews with physicians, nurses, hospital managers, procurement officers, and health-technology specialists across public and private organizations in two Brazilian regions, triangulated with regulatory documents, scientific publications, and media materials. The findings show that diffusion in resource-constrained environments is shaped less by technical superiority than by legitimacy dynamics and institutional restrictions. Mimetic mechanisms stimulate early interest as organizations benchmark “reference hospitals” under uncertainty, but normative pressures embedded in professional dominance limit experimentation and cross-occupational influence. Coercive mechanisms—particularly regulatory approval processes and public procurement routines—constrain trialability and delay scale-up, producing a disconnect between high symbolic visibility and low operational adoption. We further identify the role of intermediaries and commercialization infrastructure in mitigating institutional frictions by enhancing observability, routinization, and legitimacy. We theorize a frugal diffusion process model that explains how institutional pressures in emerging markets condition whether technical advantages translate into sustained adoption under conditions of scarcity.

When Letting Go is Not an Option: How Residual Commitment Sustains Failing Initiatives

HCM
Celine Hendriks (Erasmus Univeristy, Rotterdam); Maike Vanessa Tietschert (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Frank Eijkenaar (Erasmus Univeristy, Rotterdam); Daniëlle Cattel (Erasmus Univeristy, Rotterdam)
Organizations are remarkably effective at launching initiatives yet surprisingly ineffective at terminating those that underperform. This paper examines the phenomenon of persistence under failure—why organizations continue with a failing initiative despite a progressive breakdown of the very resources sustaining it. We introduce the concept of “residual commitment” to describe this dynamic. Our study draws on a case of a stalled healthcare provider– insurer partnership that collaboratively launched an innovative payment model to address structural shortcomings in lung care. Despite mounting resource erosion and operational decline, stakeholders not only maintained but at times intensified their commitment. We identify two interrelated mechanisms that sustain this commitment: (1) framing mechanisms that reinforced stakeholders’ belief in the initiative and its underlying purpose, and (2) enabling mechanisms—structural and organizational arrangements that made continued involvement possible. Based on these findings, we develop a process model explaining how, over time, stakeholders became residually committed. Our research extends theories of persistence under failure and indecision by theorizing a concept at their intersection: how stakeholders make decisions yet lack the resources to enact them.

Exploring the Changing Role of Leadership in the Transition from Implementing to Sustaining EBPs

HCM
Shannon Schwarz Kenyon (Michael E. DeBakey VA Medical Center)
Leadership plays a pivotal role in the sustainability of evidence-based practices (EBPs) within healthcare systems, yet empirical research on leadership during post-implementation or sustainment phases remains limited. This study explores the nuanced relationship between leadership behaviors and the successful sustainment of EBPs, drawing upon qualitative data from leaders involved in EBP implementation within the Veterans Health Administration (VHA). Using the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment (EPIS) framework as a theoretical lens, the study examines leadership actions across different phases of sustainment. Findings reveal that effective leadership behaviors vary depending on the phase of sustainment, with vision and motivational leadership being critical during early implementation, while empowering leadership becomes essential during early sustainment. The study also identifies "guided sustainment" as a dynamic process where the EBP continues to evolve over time, challenging the traditional view of sustainment as a static state. Strengths and limitations of the study are discussed, along with suggestions for future research, emphasizing the importance of tailored leadership approaches and longitudinal studies to enhance our understanding of sustainment processes in healthcare settings. Overall, the study underscores the significance of leadership in facilitating the adoption and long-term sustainability of EBPs, offering valuable insights for healthcare organizations aiming to improve EBP implementation and sustainment efforts.

Multiple Paths To Blood Pressure Control: An Organizational Configurational Analysis

HCM
Mahdieh Zare; Larry R. Hearld (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Kristine Ria Hearld (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Elizabeth Jackson; Andrea Cherrington
Background: Understanding what organizational conditions foster better performance is a central, long-standing topic in health care management research. Contingency theorists argue that there is no single best way to design and manage organizations; rather, performance is determined by the alignment and fit between an organization’s structure, strategy, and external environment. Theoretical arguments about alignment and fit, however, have outpaced empirical tests of these conditions. Methods: We analyzed data from 47 primary care clinics participating in a statewide practice facilitation program aimed at improving hypertension management. Using Qualitative Comparative Analysis, we identified configurations of organizational (e.g., FQHC status, clinic size), contextual (e.g., medically underserved area (MUA) status), and implementation support conditions (e.g., readiness to implement the facilitation program, practice facilitation) associated with clinic-level hypertension control rates. Outcomes included hypertension control rate improvement from baseline to the end of intervention (12 months) and sustainment from intervention completion to six months post-intervention. Results: Multiple pathways to hypertension control improvement and sustainment were identified, demonstrating equifinality across organizational settings. Large clinics located in MUAs with high organizational readiness achieved substantial BP improvement during the intervention period (62% coverage). However, pathways to sustainment differed from those supporting initial improvement. Smaller non-FQHC clinics with high readiness were more likely to sustain gains, whereas large FQHCs in underserved areas with low readiness struggled to maintain improvements. Paradoxically, practices with limited reporting infrastructure and briefer facilitation interactions achieved better outcomes than those with more sophisticated systems or intensive engagement, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Conclusions: Hypertension control improvement and sustainability depend on specific configurations of organizational conditions rather than individual factors or uniform implementation strategies. The findings challenge conventional assumptions that greater infrastructure or more intensive facilitation necessarily lead to better outcomes, highlighting the role of organizational readiness and context. Tailoring practice facilitation strategies to organizational conditions may enhance both implementation and long-term sustainability of clinical improvements. Keywords: Primary care clinics, hypertension, organizational performance, organizational configurations, configuration theory, fuzzy set Qualitative Comparative Analysis

Navigating Mandate and Reality: Strategies for Government Mandated Technology Implementation

HCM
Teresa Janel Paulk (East Tennessee State University)
Healthcare organizations implementing government mandated technology (GMT) encounter significant challenges and could benefit from empirical and theoretical research to understand effective implementation strategies. This study investigates how a highly distributed Human Health Services organization (HHS Inc.) navigated the challenges of implementing GMT through the lens of the Technology, Organization, and Environment framework. The research investigates how the healthcare organization overcame barriers to implementation. Data was collected through semi-structured interviews with twenty employees across multiple organizational levels and three states, complemented by secondary sources including field observation notes, meeting documentation, system utilization metrics, and implementation timeline records. The findings of this study 1) outline effective strategies for healthcare organizations to overcome implementation barriers, such as feedback mechanisms and phased rollout approaches and 2) provide a tool to determine the degree of assimilation of GMT into healthcare delivery and operational practices. The insights gained from this research offer valuable guidance for organizations and practitioners leading GMT implementation projects and provide actionable recommendations to facilitate the successful assimilation of GMT in complex healthcare organizational settings.
