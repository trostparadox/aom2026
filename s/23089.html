The Complex Effects of Algorithmic Human Resource Management on Job Performance: A SDT Perspective

HR
zhang zijian (Shandong University); Dongqian Yang (Shandong University); yuan zhang (Shandong University); Yu Chen (Carlos III University of Madrid); Anan Peng (Shandong University); Chenghao Men (Shandong University)
With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, algorithms are increasingly embedded in human resource management, making algorithmic human resource management (AHRM) a salient organizational phenomenon. Drawing on self-determination theory, this study investigates how AHRM influences employee job performance through basic psychological needs. We propose that AHRM has dual effects, enhancing competence need satisfaction and thereby improving job performance while undermining autonomy and relatedness needs and thus impairing performance. We further argue that empowering leadership moderates these effects by amplifying AHRM’s positive consequences and attenuating its negative ones. Using three-wave, multi-source data from 223 employee–supervisor dyads in a Chinese firm, we find support for the mediating role of psychological need satisfaction and for the moderating role of empowering leadership in the indirect relationship between AHRM and job performance via autonomy and competence need satisfaction. Together, these findings reveal the ambivalent nature of AHRM and highlight empowering leadership as critical for unlocking its human potential.

Shall I A(I)pply to This Job Ad? Effects of Ad Content, AI Disclosure, and AI Attitudes

HR
Tobias Marc Härtel (Osnabrück University of Applied Sciences); Alina Langenberg (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management)
Generative AI adoption for job advertisement optimization has outpaced theoretical and empirical understanding, motivating a three-phase study examining the impact of AI-optimized job ads on application intentions: (1) using a combined top-down/bottom-up approach to identify six theoretically and practically relevant optimization strategies (AIDA, signaling, DEI, P-E-fit, humor, financial benefits), (2) optimizing ads via ChatGPT-4o, and (3) testing them against a human-written baseline. In a 2 (ad source: human vs. AI) × 6 (strategy) × 2 (disclosure) mixed experimental design, 330 participants evaluated both the baseline and one randomly assigned AI-optimized version (660 evaluations total), with AI authorship disclosed to half the sample. Multilevel models revealed no general AI advantage but content-dependent effects: financial benefits (d = 0.34), P-E-fit (d = 0.31), and AIDA (d = 0.25) enhanced intentions relative to other optimization strategies, while humor substantially reduced them (d = -1.00). AI disclosure showed no effects, yet applicants’ AI attitudes moderated responses to AI-optimized ads, suggesting implicit instead of explicit AI detection. Findings reposition AI as strategy executor rather than universal quality enhancer and reveal dual processing pathways: content-based evaluation of recruitment signals (what is communicated) and source-based evaluation of AI legitimacy (how applicants perceive algorithmic organizational actors).

Psychological Contracts in Algorithmic Workplaces

HR
Ronan Carbery (University College Cork); Maral Khanjani (University of Minnesota)
The use of algorithmic management systems are increasingly being embedded in people management processes, yet theory offers limited guidance on how organizations can deploy these systems while preserving the psychological contracts that underpin effective employment relationships. We develop a framework proposing that three design principles enable organizations to implement algorithmic HRM while maintaining psychological contract integrity. Drawing on psychological contract theory, organizational justice theory, and algorithmic fairness scholarship, we argue that algorithmic fairness supports distributive and procedural justice by ensuring equitable outcomes and consistent processes; transparency supports informational justice by enabling employees to understand how algorithmic systems function and why decisions are made; and preserved autonomy supports interpersonal justice by maintaining human agency and dignity within algorithmically mediated work. We distinguish our framework from platform work contexts where workers form multi-party psychological contracts with algorithms as distinct parties, arguing that traditional organizations retain structural features that enable bilateral contract maintenance through intentional design choices. The framework contributes to psychological contract theory by specifying context-dependent effects of algorithmic management, advances organizational justice theory by translating principles into concrete design requirements, and bridges algorithmic fairness scholarship with HRM theory. We develop a number of propositions to guide empirical research and offer practical guidance for HRM professionals tasked with deploying algorithmic systems.

AI Feedback and Employee Green Behavior: Effects of Disclosure Mode and Perceived Control

HR
Wei Zhang (Sichuan University); Yili Yu; Min Wu (Sichuan University); Zihan Hu (Sichuan University)
With the widespread application of AI technology in corporate management, exploring its impact on employee green behavior has become a research hotspot. Based on the framework of Feedback Intervention Theory (FIT), this study analyzes the effects of AI feedback on employee green behavior in two disclosure scenarios: private feedback and public feedback, as well as the underlying mechanisms. We conducted a four-week field experiment with 360 employees from a large manufacturing company in Southwest China. The results show that, in the private feedback scenario, AI feedback had a significant negative impact on employees' green behavior, while in the public feedback scenario, AI feedback significantly enhanced green behavior. Moreover, perceived behavioral control played a partial mediating role in the relationship between AI feedback and green behavior. This study enriches Feedback Intervention Theory by revealing the moderating effect of feedback disclosure on the effectiveness of AI feedback and provides practical recommendations for companies to effectively use AI feedback in green human resource management.

Navigating Loss with Purpose: Understanding Gig Workers’ Compliance with Algorithmic Management

HR
Jiaojiao Qu (Huaqiao University); Hao Zeng (Jiangxi Science and Technology Normal University); Cherrie J. Zhu (Monash University); Xia Cao; Xin Chen (Huaqiao University); Xiushen Hu (Huaqiao University)
The rapid expansion of the gig economy has brought algorithmic management to the forefront of contemporary labor processes. While existing research typically dichotomizes worker responses into either active resistance or proactive adaptation, our sequential mixed-methods inquiry uncovers a third, underexplored reaction: instrumental compliance—characterized by emotional neutrality coupled with strategic behavioral adherence. Grounded in Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and the family motivation literature, we explore why certain workers, specifically those with prior business failure, adopt this compliance strategy. Our studies were conducted among food delivery riders in China. In Study 1, inductive analyses of in-depth interviews reveal that these riders reframe algorithmic control not as an oppressive mechanism, but as a source of predictable certainty essential for resource restoration, particularly through the activation of family responsibilities and the urgency to rebuild household capital. Building on these insights, Study 2 utilizes three-wave, time-lagged survey data from 307 food delivery riders to deductively test the underlying mechanisms. The results confirm that prior business failure indirectly promotes compliance through heightened family-oriented motivation. Furthermore, family burden salience acts as a critical boundary condition. Crucially, our analysis reveals a sign-reversal effect: while high family burden strengthens the compliance pathway, low family burden reverses it, associating prior failure with reduced compliance. This finding suggests that the autonomy-seeking orientation inherent in former entrepreneurs resurfaces when financial urgency is low. Overall, our findings challenge traditional labor process perspectives by revealing compliance as a conditional, purpose-driven strategy to navigate resource loss. This research advances the understanding of worker agency by highlighting how biographical histories and family anchors shape responses to algorithmic regimes.
