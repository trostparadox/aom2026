Public Power, Private Gains: Digital Platform Organizations and New Strategic Responses

OMT
Brigitte Dekker (Radboud University Nijmegen); Stephanie Koornneef (Radboud University Nijmegen); Hans Van Kranenburg (Radboud University Nijmegen)
This paper examines how digital platform organizations engage in strategic behaviors to shape and interact with their institutional environment in ways that extend beyond established theoretical frameworks of strategic responses. Building on institutional theory, we conduct a qualitative case study of three extreme cases, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta, using content analysis of 961 media articles from The New York Times, Financial Times, and The Economic Times. Focusing on these three extreme cases, we identify three novel forms of strategic behavior: dependency creation, surveillance, and public function substitution. These strategies allow digital platform organizations to bypass formal institutional pathways and instead gain influence through infrastructural integration, information asymmetry and the delivery of public functions respectively. Our findings reveal a shift from symbolic to systemic power, whereby digital platform organizations become functionally indispensable to public, private, and societal institutions. Theoretically, this study extends institutional response literature by conceptualizing new forms of institutional responses. Practically, our study underscores the urgency of reevaluating governance mechanisms for digital platform organizations that now function as quasi-public institutions.

How Engaging Risk-Bearing Stakeholders Shapes Lobbying Effectiveness

OMT
Jue Wang (The Pennsylvania State University)
Firms seeking favorable regulations for new technologies have traditionally built political support by emphasizing benefits and mobilizing stakeholders who stand to gain, which is a strate-gy that proved effective when regulators relied primarily on expert input and closed-door lobbying. As regulatory processes become more participatory, however, risk-bearing stakeholders gain direct access to regulators and shape problem definitions, such that benefit-focused lobbying becomes insufficient when firms cannot credibly address the risks these stakeholders foreground. Drawing on research in the social construction of technology and problem definition in policymaking, this paper theorizes that firms can sustain regulatory influence in participatory settings by stabilizing early problem definitions around stakeholder-defined risks that their technologies can credibly ad-dress. By anchoring regulatory discourse to these risks, firms establish evaluative reference points that shape how regulators assess subsequent policy proposals, with anchoring proving most effec-tive when firms possess strong reputational legitimacy and closely align with risk-bearing stake-holder framings. Evidence from drone firms’ regulatory narratives during FAA rulemaking (2014-2020) supports this framework. The findings show how participatory regulation reshapes political influence by shifting firms’ nonmarket strategies from benefit-focused advocacy toward early-stage problem definition centered on risk-bearing stakeholders.

Who Gets the Crowd? Organizational Status and Conspicuous Evaluation

OMT
Balazs Kovacs (Yale University); Qingyang (Iris) Wang (Yale University)
Crowdsourced review platforms promise to democratize organizational evaluation, yet audiences reproduce status hierarchies through participation choices: They selectively review high-status organizations to signal elite position, access, and taste—a process we call conspicuous evaluation. Matching Yelp reviews to geolocated foot traffic for 39,369 restaurants, we estimate reviews per visit by organizational status, proxied by restaurants’ self-selected price tiers. Higher-status venues receive more reviews per visit, controlling for business characteristics, actual spending, and review history. This disparity intensifies when venues are locally higher-status and attenuates among socioeconomically advantaged audiences. Within organizations, review rates decline as venues accumulate reviews and appear more mainstream. High-status venues also attract more status-signaling language and dormant (versus habitual) reviewers. This unequal attention compounds advantages for high-status organizations and reproduces status hierarchies.

Complementor Strategies for Platform Selection and Expansion: An Institutional Perspective

OMT
Daria Demyanova (University of Bologna); Mariachiara Colucci (University of Bologna); Leonardo Corbo (University of Bologna)
Platform ecosystems depend on complementors’ ongoing engagement decisions, from initial affiliation to deepening, reallocating activity, and exiting. Despite substantial attention to governance and adaptation costs, the institutional underpinnings of these decisions and the temporal pathway linking early entry to subsequent shifts remain underexplored. This study investigates how complementors engage with platforms over time by externalizing responses to competing institutional logics. Drawing on inductive research in the context of Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), we develop a process model that unfolds a three-phase sequence: initial platform selection, emergence of tensions due to internal and external triggers, and resolution through differentiated platform engagement. Our findings reveal that complementors’ decisions are shaped by tensions between market, professional, state, and community logics. To resolve these tensions, complementors adopt distinct strategies – external compartmentalization, integration, decoupling, and deprioritization – allocating specific logics across platform relationships. This research offers three key contributions. First, it extends platform literature by theorizing complementors’ platform engagement as a dynamic, institutional process shaped by evolving logics. Additionally, it repositions complementors as agentic and reflexive actors, thereby challenging platform-centric views that frame them as merely reactive participants. Second, by introducing the concept of “external compartmentalization”, it shows how organizations externalize the management of institutional complexity to interorganizational infrastructures, shifting logic resolution beyond organizational boundaries. Third, it conceptualizes digital platforms as institutional arenas that complementors strategically use to externalize and manage incompatible logics.

From Economic Incentive to Social Norm: How Hybrid Governance Evolves in Platforms to Sustain Growth

OMT
Yaomin Zhang (Queen's University Belfast); Jonatan Pinkse (King's College London)
Digital platforms govern user behavior through both market-based mechanisms (e.g., price signals) and community-based mechanisms (e.g., social norms). This hybrid governance enables platforms to co-create value with users but also introduces tensions that may limit growth. Despite their widespread combined use, little is known about how these institutionally distinct mechanisms are integrated into effective governance configurations over time. Assuming that sustainable platform growth requires a dynamic “search-for-fit” across developmental stages, this study identifies six high-performing pathways of hybrid governance and reveals four structural conditions that shape the extent to which market and community mechanisms can be sustainably combined. By bridging organizational design with evolutionary perspectives, the study explains effective hybrid governance as the outcome of continuous design evolution while remaining constrained by early-stage conditions.
