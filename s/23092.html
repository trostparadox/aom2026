“Sitting in a Boring Room?” Hybrid Work and Skilled Migrants’ Paradoxes of Acculturation

HR
Valentina Rillo (University of Neuchâtel); Cinzia Dal Zotto (University of Neuchâtel); Luca Carollo (University of Bergamo); Anne Keegan (University College Dublin)
Hybrid work (HBW) is increasingly deployed in international HRM to attract and retain skilled migrants (SMs), yet its implications for acculturation and integration success remain unclear. Drawing on paradox theory, we conceptualize acculturation as inherently tension-laden and examine how HBW renders contradictory yet interdependent demands salient for SMs. Based on 30 in-depth interviews with international academics in Switzerland, we identify three connection-centered acculturation paradoxes: the familiar connections paradox (host-country home vs. home-country ties), the productive connections paradox (office as social hub vs. “lonely castle”), and the flexible connections paradox (structured sociability vs. autonomous interaction). We further show how HBW both triggers these paradoxes and enables coping through three strategies (embedding in the host country, focusing on socialization, and adapting work schedules) for managing the tensions they produce. In so doing, we theorize HBW as a bottom-up form of organizational support that, through autonomy, can simultaneously facilitate and strain SMs’ acculturation and requires a strategy for continuous surfacing of and coping with the tensions pervading the working lives of SMs. Our study extends research on skilled migration, coping, and HBW and offers HRM guidance for supporting SMs without undermining belonging, career capital, or retention.

The Ups and Downs of Business Travel: How Social and Temporal Resources Shape Performance

HR
Ryan Terry (Wichita State University); Jeffrey E McGee (The University of Texas at Arlington); Deniz Gevrek (Texas A&M University-Corpus Christi)
Business travel is a defining feature of contemporary work, yet its performance consequences remain poorly understood. Prior research has largely emphasized strain and well-being outcomes, offering limited insight into how travel shapes cognitive performance over time or how social and temporal contexts condition these effects. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, the job demands–resources model, and social capital perspectives, we develop a relational framework that conceptualizes business travel as a dynamic resource process embedded in social and situational contexts. We test this framework using nine seasons of Major League Baseball data (2015–2023), examining over 20,000 home-plate umpire assignments with objective, pitch-level measures of decision accuracy. Results reveal a curvilinear (inverted-U) relationship between accumulated travel and cognitive performance: moderate travel enhances accuracy, whereas extensive travel undermines it. This relationship is contingent on the relational and temporal structure of travel. Colleague familiarity and destination duration each buffer travel-related resource depletion, flattening the performance curve and delaying the onset of decline. Moreover, these resources interact synergistically, such that performance is most resilient when umpires work with familiar colleagues and have sufficient time to acclimate at destinations. By integrating resource, relational, and temporal dynamics, this study advances theory on mobile work and demonstrates that the performance consequences of business travel depend on the configuration of social and situational resources, not travel volume alone.

Weaving Intention: Gender, Culture, and Women’s Entrepreneurial Agency

HR
Felix Akinwumi Orole (The University of Queensland); Modupe Ogunleye (Australian Catholic University); Emmanuel Mogaji (Keele University); Bernard Joseph McKenna (The University of Queensland)
This study examines how women entrepreneurs in Nigeria navigate and resist gendered cultural expectations through their business ventures. Drawing on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and feminist critiques, the paper reconceptualizes entrepreneurial intention not simply as a psychological process, but as a culturally embedded negotiation of identity, agency, and power. Based on semi-structured interviews with 33 women entrepreneurs across sectors, four themes emerged: entrepreneurial self-efficacy, attitudinal shifts toward autonomy and purpose, experiential learning, and the reworking of cultural norms. These themes reveal how participants weave together duty, ambition, and constraint to create new patterns of economic and symbolic participation. The findings extend TPB by showing how subjective norms can be repurposed as sites of resistance and empowerment, particularly when viewed through a feminist lens. This study contributes to critical organizational scholarship by situating entrepreneurial intention within the gendered cultural context of the Global South, offering a thick and grounded understanding of how women challenge patriarchal tradition.

A Resource Perspective on Migrant Worker Turnover Intentions

HR
Lianghui Lei (Guangdong Polytechnic Normal University); Qihai Huang; Jianjun Zhang (Peking University); Yite Zhu (Tsinghua University)
Hundreds of millions of migrant workers in emerging economies occupy lower-skilled jobs, yet turnover remains persistently high. Research has identified personal, relational, and organizational antecedents of migrant turnover, but evidence is often fragmented and lacks an integrative framework for explaining how these conditions operate jointly. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we conceptualize turnover intentions as an evaluation of whether a viable resource ecology is sustained through co-occurring personal and relational resources and the organizational conditions that support them. Using fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) on large-scale, multi-source survey data from China, we identify multiple sufficient combinations of resource caravans (self-efficacy and happiness; supervisor and coworker support) and organizational passageways (training; firm-provided community infrastructure) associated with low turnover intentions, as well as distinct combinations associated with high turnover intentions. The results demonstrate equifinality and causal asymmetry: pathways to staying differ from pathways to leaving. We contribute by extending COR’s caravans and passageways to migrant-worker turnover, clarifying how organizational supports function as passageways that sustain or undermine resource patterns, and offering actionable guidance for employers and policymakers seeking retention in resource-constrained migrant workforces.
