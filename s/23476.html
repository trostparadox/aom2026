Beyond Static Profiles: Investigating the Perception and Impact of AI Personas in Design Thinking

TIM
Julian Ruf (Helmut Schmidt University - University of the Federal Armed Forces of Hamburg)
User research is foundational to Design Thinking (DT), yet practitioners frequently struggle with the resource-intensive nature of traditional persona development. While Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) research suggests that Artificial Intelligence (AI) can automate and dynamize user representation, empirical evidence regarding how these AI personas influence social interaction and collaboration in real-world innovation contexts remains scarce. This paper explores how participants perceive and experience AI personas within DT workshops. We employ an exploratory qualitative multiple-case study of three Design Thinking workshops (one pilot and two main cases) conducted in Germany, Switzerland, and Canada. Our findings reveal a dual-natured perception: participants viewed AI personas as socio-emotional actors, triggering fascination and engagement through "quasi-social" interaction, and as sparring partners who accelerate perspective-shifting and provide a third point of view to depersonalize group conflict. However, these benefits are tempered by technical friction and a situational trust dynamic that may increase susceptibility to AI hallucinations. This study bridges human-computer interaction literature on AI persona development with innovation management's focus on user centricity and collaborative sensemaking in organizational innovation work. We thereby offer an interaction-focused account of AI personas that highlights both collaborative value and emerging risks in innovation practice.

How Generative AI Reshapes Creative Work Across Innovation Processes

TIM
Marcos Cerqueira Lima (SKEMA Business School); Ludmila Striukova; Philippe Wagner (Independent Researcher)
This study examines how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) reshapes creative work across stages of an organizational innovation process. Drawing on a longitudinal mixed-methods study of 38 middle managers engaged in Design Thinking–based innovation projects within a large aerospace multinational, we analyze how GenAI influences distinct dimensions of creativity—fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration—over time. The findings show that GenAI does not uniformly enhance creativity. Instead, its contribution is stage-contingent and functionally differentiated. GenAI strongly supports fluency, flexibility, and elaboration by expanding search spaces, accelerating synthesis, and lowering execution barriers, particularly during prototyping and convergence phases. In contrast, its perceived contribution to originality declines as innovation work shifts toward strategic commitment and value-based selection, reflecting limits rooted in the absence of intentionality. The study reveals a hierarchical division of creative labor in which humans retain authority over framing, judgment, and meaning, while GenAI functions as a combinatorial and execution-oriented cognitive scaffold. By conceptualizing AI-enabled creativity as a process-specific reconfiguration of creative labor, the study advances research on human–AI collaboration in technology and innovation management.

Integrating AI Late in the Work Process is Perceived as More Creative

TIM
Joshua Mahaney (ESSCA Aix-en-Provence); Rajarshi Majumder (Grenoble Ecole de Management); Jareef Martuza (Norwegian School of Economics)
Does the timing of integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into creative work affect how much credit is given to the creators? A creator’s decision to use AI for broad ideation at the beginning or for precise editing at the end of the creative process reflects strategic reasoning for its use and not a deficiency in their creative depth. Nevertheless, across 3 pre-registered experiments (N = 940), we find that evaluators systematically perceive creators as less creative when integrating AI at the beginning of the creative process (compared to the end). Two parallel psychological pathways explain this gap: evaluators perceive creators as less effortful and less authentic when integrating AI recommendations at the beginning, resulting in lower perceived creativity. However, signaling that the creator has elaborated on rather than directly implemented the AI recommendations substantially attenuates the effect. Consequently, consumers place a significantly lower economic value on products created with early AI integration. Our findings indicate that evaluators’ judgments of human creativity depend on when and how creators characterize their interactions with AI, with implications for creativity and innovation, human-AI collaboration and consumer judgements.

Augmented (or Not): The Human–GenAI Tango in Generating Creative Ideas

TIM
Jafar Sabbah (City - University of London); Aneesh Banerjee (Bayes Business School); Feng Li (City St George's - University of London)
This study addresses a burgeoning question: under what conditions does human–GenAI collaboration expand the solution space and produce higher-quality ideas in terms of novelty, utility, and feasibility, and what do humans still add to this process? This question is becoming increasingly important as recent studies on human–GenAI collaboration in creative and innovation processes show that the desired effect of augmentation is far from automatic. Adopting a search-based view of creativity, we address this question through an experimental study (n = 350) and focus on how different configurations of human–GenAI collaboration shape that search. The findings show that simply involving GPT-4 in idea generation, without changing how search is seeded or structured, does not improve idea quality in terms of novelty, utility or feasibility. Providing participants with an initial list of ideas generated by GPT-4 increases the perceived utility of final ideas but does not enhance novelty or feasibility. By contrast, seeding the search with GPT-4 ideas generated under transformational creativity principles significantly boosts both novelty and utility, at the cost of feasibility. Semantic similarity analyses further indicate that utility is predominantly shaped by GenAI’s initial suggestions, whereas divergence from these seeds reflects human contributions that matter more for novelty and, to a lesser extent, feasibility. Together, these results show that GenAI augments ideation mainly when its role in seeding and structuring joint search is deliberately designed, and they clarify the distinctive, complementary roles of humans and GenAI in shaping creative outcomes.
