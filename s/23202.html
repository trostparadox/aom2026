Funding Her Future: How Female Investors Drive Early Growth and Success in Female-Led Startups

ENT
Jennie Jiongni Mao (University of St. Gallen); Valerio Pelucco (Luiss university)
Female entrepreneurs face challenges when accessing venture capital. While support from other women is often proposed, we argue that the effectiveness of this same gender support depends on whether evaluative norms shift—specifically, whether investors’ default frames are recalibrated. Rather than viewing this peer support as an isolated individual-level factor, this paper applies structuration theory to investigate how the industry-level representation of female venture capitalists improves the disadvantaged situations of female-led startups in their financing performance, subsequent growth and chances of success. Our results show that, in industries where a larger share of deals is led by female VC partners, female-led startups raise more capital, generate higher annual revenues, and have higher success rates than in industries with lower female VC partner representation. Overall, these findings suggest that greater female representation among evaluators can drive structural change that helps women entrepreneurs overcome persistent barriers and perform better over time.

Intergroup Threat Perception: Impact of Gender Subgroup Configuration on Startup Funding Outcome

ENT
Abu Rehan Abbasi (Indian Institute of Management Kashipur); Ipsu Khadka (University of South Carolina); Dalhia Mani (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Previous research suggests mixed-gender teams perform better in complex contexts, but it overlooks how gender subgroup configurations influence team dynamics. On a continuum where higher diversity indicates greater female representation, existing work predicts a unicuspidal (inverted-U-shaped) relationship with outcomes. While acknowledging the value of representation and mixed-gender teams, we draw on identity threat and subgroup theories to argue that rising minority representation—regardless of focal gender—can heighten perceived threat, increasing conflict and hindering information processing. We propose that gender-unbalanced teams, with a clear majority, perform better due to lower threat perception, particularly when the lead founder’s gender aligns with the team majority. Using data on Indian technology startups (1990–2015), we find a bicuspid (M-shaped) relationship between team gender composition and funding outcomes: increasing from all-male to majority-male teams, dipping at equi-gender, rising again at majority-female, and falling at all-female teams. This research enhances our understanding of how subgroup configuration impacts the diversity-performance relationship. It also nuances the research on the male-leader premium using an identity-threat lens, showing that both male- and female-led teams have an advantage if the leader’s gender matches the majority gender of the team.

When Constraint Becomes Capability: Patriarchy and Females’ Entrepreneurial Skills Development

ENT
Augustine Awuah Peprah (University of Southampton); Bede Akorige Atarah
Drawing on in-depth qualitative evidence from female entrepreneurs in Ghana, this study theorises how entrepreneurial capabilities emerge within patriarchal institutional environments. We show that patriarchy, commonly conceptualised as a constraint on women’s economic participation, also operates as an informal learning system that inadvertently cultivates entrepreneurial skills. Through early and sustained socialisation in nuclear families, extended kinship networks, and local communities, women acquire patterned gendered behaviours - domestic submissiveness, domestic judgement, and domestic bricolage. These behaviours foster distinct but interrelated entrepreneurial capabilities, including assertive skills that balance deference and agency, imperious skills that enable evaluative decision-making without overt authority, and contentment skills grounded in creativity, improvisation, and negotiation under scarcity. Although developed within domestic and communal spheres, these capabilities later shape women’s ability to recognise, evaluate, and exploit entrepreneurial opportunities. Our findings further reveal that the expression and transferability of these behaviours and skills are contingent on women’s marital and caregiving positions, such as marriage, motherhood, divorce, and widowhood, underscoring the life-course dynamics of learning under constraint. By conceptualising patriarchy as a paradoxical informal institution that simultaneously restricts and enables capability formation, this study advances feminist and social role theories by revaluing reproductive labour as a source of economic learning and extends entrepreneurial learning theory by locating skill acquisition beyond formal entrepreneurial settings.

When Women Lead: Activist Homophily and the Rise of Female Entrepreneurship

ENT
Xing Li (Utrecht University); Jiaxing You; Qian Liu (Xiamen University)
While prior research has long recognized the benefits of female representation in politics for gender equality in general, little is known about its implications for female entrepreneurship. Building on the perspective of activist homophily, we examine whether, how, and under which conditions, female officials—a disadvantaged group in politics—enact activist-based homophilic preferences to intentionally support female entrepreneurship. Using data from Chinese prefecture-level cities between 1992 and 2020, we find that cities with female officials serving as mayors or municipal party secretaries have higher levels of female entrepreneurial activity. This relationship is explained by female officials’ engagement in activist homophily, which operates through two underlying mechanisms: reshaping supportive formal policies and influencing informal social norms on gender equality. Our findings further suggest that female officials are more likely to engage in activist homophily for supporting female entrepreneurship in cities with weak financial, legal, and digital economy development, and in high-revenue industries where women are particularly underrepresented. By focusing on female officials and demonstrating both the presence of activist homophily and the mechanisms and conditions under which it operates, this study extends theory on same-gender support for closing the gender gap in entrepreneurship and identifies a new channel for promoting female entrepreneurship.
