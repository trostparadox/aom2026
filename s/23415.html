Frequency or Severity: Firms’ Exposure to Climate Change-related Natural Disasters and Mitigation

ONE
Soolim Park (NEOMA Business School); Jorge Rivera (The George Washington University)
This study aims to investigate whether and how the geographic exposure to climate change (CC)-related natural disasters at headquarters (HQ) and facilities is associated with changes in firms’ greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Using data on large GHG emitters in the United States, our findings reveal that frequent disaster exposure at a company’s HQ county is significantly associated with reduced GHG emissions over time. We also found that exposure to more costly disasters at a company’s facility counties is associated with greater reductions in GHG emissions. Additionally, our results suggest that HQ and facility exposure interact to intensify each other’s impact on GHG reduction efforts. These findings contribute to the literature on business sustainability by illustrating how more frequent CC-related natural disasters at HQ and more costly disasters at facility locations influence managers’ attention dynamics, potentially reshaping the long-term collective action required for climate strategies, such as GHG emission reductions.

When do City Networks Cover Regions Prone to Hot Summer Extremes? The ICLEI Network Case

ONE
Lars Feuerlein (University of Hamburg); Daniel Gotthardt (University of Hamburg); Leonard Borchert (University of Hamburg); Henrik Wallenhorst (University of Hamburg); Leonie Wolf; Jana Sillmann (University of Hamburg); Achim Oberg (University of Hamburg)
Cities are increasingly at the forefront of climate change impacts, particularly as extreme heat intensifies and spreads across the globe. At the same time, transnational city networks such as ICLEI have emerged as key actors in urban climate governance, yet it remains unclear how environmental risk, economic capacity, and historical connectivity shape participation in these networks. This paper examines the global diffusion of ICLEI membership between 1990 and 2020 in relation to extreme summer heat, urbanization, and the socio-economic landscape of cities. Using ERA5 reanalysis data on hot summer extremes alongside global population density, ICLEI member city locations, and World Bank GDP data, we analyze spatial and temporal patterns of network developments. We find that early ICLEI membership was concentrated in economically resourced and historically connected cities in Europe and North America, while later expansion increasingly reached cities in regions experiencing high absolute and intensifying hot summer extremes, including parts of West and Southern Africa. Our results further show that regional clustering and local diffusion play a central role in network expansion, with membership often spreading from early adopters to neighboring cities. Overall, the findings highlight how transnational urban climate governance emerges at the intersection of climate exposure, economic resources, and existing relationships.

Regenerating Ecologically Embedded Global Production Networks in Crisis

ONE
Christopher R Whynacht (University of Massachusetts Boston)
Since publication of the Tragedy of the Commons in 1968, stakeholders in organizations, civil society, and government have worked to avoid the type of ecological collapse Hardin forecasted. Unfortunately, these efforts have not always been successful and globally we see that environmental disruptions are on the rise. This paper studies patterns of response within ecologically embedded Global Production Networks (GPNs) that are adapting to environmental degradation. I study the case of the Atlantic cod fishery through a focus on Gloucester Massachusetts. Beyond economic implications, declining natural resources threatened the community’s cultural, political, and institutional foundations. Rather than treating the environment as a replaceable external element, this case reveals how actors in crisis responded by recommitting to regenerate productive relationships among diverse actors within the socioecological system. This inclusive approach highlights strategies which promote durable positive outcomes for communities, organizations, and the natural environment.

The Disappearance of Hyperlocal News Infrastructure and Extreme Pollution Growth

ONE
Sanghyun Joo (Rutgers University); Petra Christmann (Rutgers University)
This study examines how the disappearance of hyperlocal news infrastructure affects firms’ environmental behavior. We argue that hyperlocal news outlets function as visibility infrastructure that constrains extreme deviations in facility-level pollution. When this infrastructure collapses, facilities face weaker informal accountability, enabling greater growth in extreme toxic emissions. Using facility-level emissions data combined with county-level measures of hyperlocal news presence, we analyze two-year changes in toxic air emissions following transitions into news deserts. Quantile regression analyses focusing on the upper tail of the emission growth distribution show that facilities located in counties newly exposed to news deserts exhibit significantly higher extreme pollution growth. We further examine the role of regulatory capacity and ownership structure, finding that the constraining influence of regulatory capacity weakens in newly exposed news deserts and that foreign-owned facilities display higher extreme emission growth following the complete disappearance of hyperlocal news. These findings highlight the role of hyperlocal news infrastructure in enabling social and regulatory constraints and demonstrate how the erosion of local visibility alters firms’ extreme environmental behavior.
