Mind the Gap: Internal and External Frames in Scaling Entrepreneurial Firms

ENT
Odhrán McIntaggart (IE Business School); Christina Kyprianou (IE University); Kieran Michael Conroy (Queen's University Belfast)
Literature predominantly conceptualizes scaling as one with structural and operational challenges, while largely neglecting the socio- cognitive friction that accompanies high growth. Using an inductive qualitative design in the Spanish digital ecosystem, we examine how internal actors (founders and executives) and external actors (investors and advisors) frame the challenges of scaling. We identify six scaling challenges that cluster into three core tensions: agility versus cohesion, exploration versus focus, and autonomy versus discipline. Furthermore, we uncover a systematic “framing gap”: internal actors privilege relational and cultural frames, whereas external actors stress professionalization, governance, and structural frames. Our findings highlight the potential for misaligned interpretations to produce organizational challenges during scaling. Ultimately, we advance scaling literature by introducing the "liability of misalignment," demonstrating that successful scaling requires founders to actively navigate competing logics as the entrepreneurial firm scales.

Professionalizing the Entrepreneurial Firm: Grafting a Management System onto the Founder Imprint

ENT
Laurens Vandeweghe (ETH Zürich); Nelson Phillips (University of California Santa Barbara)
In this paper, we draw on an in-depth case study of the implementation of a Stage Gate system for innovation at a FinTech scale-up to examine professionalization. Inspired by our data, we view professionalization as the implementation of a managerial blueprint against the backdrop of a founder imprint. We find that successful professionalization requires professional managers to ‘graft’ the formal, corporate-like management system onto the founder blueprint, and that such ‘imprint grafting’ involves a ‘vascular’ coupling of the management system with the founder imprint following an organic implementation process. Our findings allow us to make two key contributions. First, we provide insights into the mechanisms of successful professionalization of entrepreneurial firms. Second, we bring together two different views on imprints by showing that some of the features of an imprint are explicit – overt and easy to communicate – while others are tacit – covert and difficult to convey.

The Slow Hunch: Strategy Formation and the Emergence of the Entrepreneur's Vision

ENT
Monique Boddington (University of Cambridge); Stelios Kavadias
This paper focuses on how entrepreneurial vision-making influences and drives strategy formation in early-stage ventures. The success of a venture depends on entrepreneurs balancing their vision of an entrepreneurial opportunity with external feedback. Based on a longitudinal field study of six entrepreneurial firms, this paper proposes that entrepreneurs engage in an interplay between vision-making and learning through experimentation and external feedback. We document how vision, rather than a single epiphany, develops over time through a gradual process akin to a 'slow hunch', evolving and influenced by multiple factors. This process involves selective signal filtering within a process of temporal abduction. Through testing and iteration, entrepreneurs strengthen their vision, which increasingly acts as a guiding light for future decisions. Over time, the vision takes primacy, and strategy formation is increasingly directed by the vision rather than external signals. Ultimately, the concept of the slow hunch acts as a bridge between creating an imagined future state of the world and adapting to it.

Towards a Theory of the Entrepreneurial Professional Service Firm

ENT
Atul Nerkar (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill); Amit Narendula (Kenan-Flagler Business School, University of North Carolina Chapel Hill)
Entrepreneurship research has developed rich theory about opportunity discovery and exploitation, yet it has paid limited attention to a widespread form of firm formation: licensed professionals founding independent practices, which we term the Entrepreneurial Professional Service Firm (EPSF). We develop a knowledge-based theory of when EPSFs emerge and persist across professional fields and why their prevalence varies across professions. Our core claim is that EPSF formation depends on how professional knowledge is structured: its tacitness, articulability/standardizability, rate of change, and systemic interdependence. These properties shape whether expertise can be effectively owned and governed at the level of an individual-led practice or is more efficiently organized within larger firms, chains, or institutional providers. We contribute by identifying EPSFs as an under-theorized site of entrepreneurship and extending entrepreneurship’s domain to professional service firm formation.

When Knowledge Constrains: Interdependence, Business Relatedness, and Employee Entrepreneurship

ENT
Luyao Wang (Communication University of China); Shunfang Ruan (Beijing Institute of Technology); Alina Efimova (Beijing Institute of Technology); Guannan Qu (Beijing Institute of Technology); Chengxia Lan (Beijing Institute of Technology)
We theorize and empirically examine how firm-specific knowledge (FSK) influences employee entrepreneurship. In a knowledge-intensive industry, we define the “entrepreneur-parent” knowledge interdependence as the structural linkage between the founder’s knowledge base and the parent firm’s FSK ecosystem. This interdependence shapes the new venture’s strategic direction and value capture ability. Using data from China’s patent law firms, we find that higher knowledge interdependences leads to higher business relatedness in the new venture. This effect is weaker when the entrepreneurial team comes from the same parent firm. Moreover, greater knowledge interdependence generally reduces entrepreneurial performance, although this disadvantage is mitigated when the team is homogeneously sourced from the same parent organization. Our findings highlight knowledge interdependence as a key mechanism through which FSK exerts influence beyond firm boundaries, offering important implications for managing spinout strategies and entrepreneurial risks.
