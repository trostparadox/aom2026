The Fit-Friction Frontier: Dynamic Alignment, Poaching Externalities, and the Drivers of Performance

STR
Stefan Maric (Bentley University); Robert M Wiseman (Michigan State University)
Why does firm performance heterogeneity persist and even amplify during turbulence? We develop a dynamic equilibrium model that integrates real options, search frictions with on-the-job search, and resource complementarity to characterize the ``Fit--Friction Frontier.'' Firms face a trade-off between the Adaptation Imperative (realigning leadership as contexts evolve) and the Protection Imperative (shielding valuable complementarities from poaching externalities). The option to replace leadership makes firm value inherently convex, and this convexity interacting with market dynamics yields three results. First, greater uncertainty raises the option value of waiting, so rational boards tolerate more misalignment (Strategic Mismatch Persistence). Second, we identify a Paradox of Efficiency: higher market fluidity speeds adaptation but also intensifies poaching; in turbulent environments, losses of high-quality matches can outweigh gains from faster realignment, so excessive fluidity can reduce aggregate value. Third, this implies a Contingent Value of Protective Frictions: governance mechanisms that selectively deter poaching (rather than merely raising replacement costs) improve efficiency when the Protection Imperative dominates. The model unifies microfoundations for strategic inertia, contingent governance design, and the dynamics of performance dispersion.

Strategic Power of Outgroup Anger: Promoting Ingroup Cooperation for Competitive Advantage (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Xiaoxiao Duan (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Eric Zhao (University of Oxford); Zhiyang Liu; Qiaobing WU
As entrepreneurial activities migrate into digital spaces, an emerging question is whether emotionally charged group-based expressions—especially outgroup anger—can be strategically leveraged to mobilize online communities and secure favorable competitive outcomes. Drawing on intergroup emotion theory, we argue that entrepreneurs’ displays of outgroup anger enhance competitive performance by activating ingroup collaboration and escalating intergroup conflict among followers. We further propose that the strength of these mechanisms depends on how severely entrepreneurs frame the competitive context as a crisis. To test our framework, we applied state-of-the-art machine learning techniques to a rich multimodal dataset consisting of 306,518 video frames, 180,665 utterances, 56,016 voice segments, and 379,433 records of viewer comments, collected from 475 live competition sessions (PK battles) hosted by the same top streamer on Douyin. Our analyses reveal that outgroup anger significantly improves competitive financial performance and that this relationship is partially mediated by heightened ingroup cooperation. Contrary to our expectations, crisis severity attenuates—rather than strengthens—the positive effect of outgroup anger on ingroup cooperation in the absence of severe crisis framing. This study offers one of the first empirical demonstrations that entrepreneurs can mobilize digital communities by strategically expressing outgroup anger, thereby generating collective action that translates into superior competitive performance.

Caught in the Middle: How Does Supply Chain Dependence Shape the Competitive Repertoire?

STR
JIURUI HE (Wuhan University); Xinming Deng (Wuhan University); Lilong Huang; Xiaofei Zhao (South-Central University for Nationalities); Guohua Liu (Shanghai International Studies University)
As competition among enterprises increasingly evolves into competition among supply chains, how the concentration of supply chains affects the competitive behavior of enterprises has become a key issue. Based on the resource dependence theory, this study uses the listed companies in China's home appliance industry from 2013 to 2023 as a sample to empirically test the impact of supply chain concentration on the quantity and complexity of competitive actions, and examines the moderating role of the board network position (centrality and structural hole richness). The study finds that the concentration of supply chains has an inverted U-shaped relationship with both the quantity and complexity of competitive actions; the centrality of the board network can weaken the inverted U-shaped curve between concentration and the quantity of actions; the richness of structural holes weakens the inverted U-shaped curve between concentration and the complexity of actions. This study connects supply chain management with dynamic competition theory, providing management insights for enterprises to optimize their supply chain structure and construct competitive action combinations.

Promoting or Inhibiting: Product Market Competition and Employee Overtime Behavior

STR
zhaohu wang (Nanjing University)
This study examines the effect of product market competition (PMC) on employee overtime behavior using data from Chinese listed companies between 2011 and 2024. The findings reveal that PMC inhibits employee overtime by promoting the adoption of artificial intelligence technologies and optimizing human capital structures—specifically by decreasing reliance on conventional low-skilled labor and increasing the use of unconventional high-skilled labor. Contextual analysis shows that this effect is more pronounced in non-state-owned enterprises, firms with fewer redundant resources, those with higher operational efficiency, and in regions with a rich talent pool. Additionally, the study finds that while employee overtime may enhance corporate performance in the short term, it ultimately undermines firms’ long-term competitive advantage. Overall, this study leverages large language models (LLMs) and satellite night-time light data to effectively measure both PMC and employee overtime, contributing to alleviating the ongoing debate on how market competition influences corporate ESG practices.

Corporate Governance and the Feasibility of Smart Growth

STR
Paul Ferreira (Getulio Vargas Foundation - São Paulo School of Business Administration); Andre Aroldo Freitas De Moura (Getulio Vargas Foundation - São Paulo School of Business Administration); Olivier Bertrand (FGV EBAPE)
We study when corporate governance expands or constrains firms’ ability to grow competitively and sustainably. We model growth as a firm-specific corridor bounded by a rivalry-based competitive benchmark (RCB) and a sustainable capacity ceiling (SCC), classifying firm–quarters into Smart Growth, Excessive Growth, Sub-Growth, and Smart-growth Infeasible states. Using a quarterly panel of Brazilian listed firms in B3’s stepped governance tiers (2010– 2019), we relate corridor states to governance tier residence and within-firm governance upgrades. Residence in the most stringent tier is associated with less Sub-Growth but more infeasibility and less Smart Growth. Upgrades are associated with fewer infeasible quarters and more Smart Growth, especially when earnings are volatile. The results suggest governance shapes whether smart growth is feasible, not only whether it is realized.
