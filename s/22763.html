Backlash Toward Gender Counter-Stereotypical Communication During a Major Organizational Shock as a

OB
Wenchao Dong (Max Planck Institute for Security and Privacy); Karandeep Singh; Meeyoung Cha (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology); Meredith Lauren Woehler (Purdue University); Narjes Yousefi (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Giuseppe Labianca (University of Massachusetts Amherst)
Gender wage gaps persist across all major economies, and no single explanation fully accounts for their endurance. Wage growth often unfolds in a nonlinear fashion over a career, marked by bursts of change during major organizational shocks such as mergers and acquisitions (M&A). Understanding how wage growth differs for men and women during these periods—and how backlash toward gendered communication behaviors might influence this process—can reveal important underlying mechanisms. This study examines a large-scale M&A between two rival firms, leveraging a unique dataset of 183,212 anonymized internal emails exchanged during the post-merger integration period. We coded the email content along two theoretically-established linguistic dimensions – agency and communion – and matched it to pre-post wage outcomes. Our findings reveal that women who used agentic, stereotype-inconsistent language experienced smaller pay increases during the organizational shock. This backlash effect was most pronounced among younger women and those in non-managerial positions, while it diminished for women in managerial roles and disappeared entirely for women over the age of 53. These results suggest that organizational and societal role expectations shape the extent to which counter-stereotypical behavior is penalized through backlash effects.

A Gender-Contingent Process of How Daily Loneliness Fuels Task Proficiency and Creativity

OB
Xingyou Bu; Li Guo (University of International Business and Economics); Min Cui (University of International Business and Economics)
While prevailing wisdom depicts workplace loneliness as a “social toxin” that depletes resources and impairs performance, we adopt an affect-as-information perspective to challenge this consensus. We propose that daily loneliness functions not merely as a deficit state, but as an informational signal that triggers systematic cognitive processing to resolve the problematic situation. Specifically, we theorize that this affective signal motivates task reflection, which subsequently enhances two distinct performance domains: task proficiency and creativity. Testing our model using a daily diary study, we find that daily fluctuations in loneliness are positively associated with task reflection, which in turn improves same-day task proficiency and creativity. Crucially, we identify gender as a key boundary condition. The translation of loneliness into task reflection is significant for men, but not for women. Our findings reveal when and for whom the pain of social disconnection can be paradoxically transmuted into productivity.

Gendered Judgment In Financial Misconduct: Evidence from Sec Enforcement Cases

OB
Ranjabati Boral (Imperial Business School, Imperial London); Celia Moore
Research on gender and punishment yields mixed evidence on whether women are sanctioned more or less harshly than men following misconduct. We argue that these inconsistencies arise from treating punishment as monolithic, and that differentiating the forms of penalties imposed for similar violations helps explain these inconsistencies. Drawing on 1,811 U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) enforcement actions against individual defendants from 1995–2025, we examine how gender shapes the structure of punishment. We show that gender differences in penalties for financial misconduct are limited to certain types of sanctions. In general, gender does not affect the form and severity of penalties imposed for violations matched by type and severity. We observed no gender differences monetary penalties or sentence length for men and women, when these penalties were imposed. However, we found that women are more likely than men to receive penalties that suppress their agency (e.g., custodial and supervisory penalties), even for comparable offenses. These findings suggest that institutional punishment selectively constrains agency based on the perpetrator’s gender. We conceptualize this pattern as agentic suppression – a form of punishment that limits autonomy rather than extracts financial cost.

When Advice Feels Useful: Gender, Team Climate, and Role-Differentiated Advice Evaluations

OB
Tongtong Zhao (University of Groningen); Ying Zhang (University of International Business and Economics)
Advice interactions are key pathways through which employees access knowledge and build informal influence, yet gender inequality research has emphasized “gateway” decisions more than how everyday advice exchanges are interpreted. We examine advice evaluation—perceived usefulness of advice received and given—as a subtle mechanism sustaining gendered dynamics. Drawing on social role perspectives, we argue that advice roles carry asymmetric identity implications: receiving advice can signal dependence (more threatening for men under agency norms), whereas giving advice signals competence and influence (potentially costlier for women under modesty and communion norms). We further propose that team climate cues shape these evaluations. Using time-lagged survey data from 289 employees in 74 teams, we test gender differences in advice usefulness and moderation by psychological safety and knowledge exchange norms. Results show no overall gender difference in evaluations of advice received, but women rate the advice they give as less useful than men. Psychological safety increases men’s perceived usefulness of advice received, whereas knowledge exchange norms increase women’s perceived usefulness of advice given. These findings highlight advice evaluation as a psychological pathway through which gendered participation and influence may accumulate and point to climate-focused interventions that legitimize men’s advice receiving and validate women’s advice giving.

Organizational Gender Composition and the Relational Roots of Employee Voice Climate

OB
Yoon Hyuk Jeong (London Business School); Sun Young Lee (UCL - University of London); Selin Kesebir (London Business School)
Although organizations increasingly implement diversity initiatives, many workplaces remain male-dominated. Existing research largely focuses on how such environments disadvantage women. We broaden this scope, proposing that organizational gender composition shapes relational dynamics for all organizational members. Drawing on gender socialization theory, we argue that male-dominated organizations exhibit a more hierarchical culture and lower interpersonal trust, which consequently undermines voice climate. We test this argument using two large-scale field studies, using a nationally representative multi-source dataset from the United Kingdom (Study 1) and a longitudinal organization-employee linked panel from South Korea (Study 2). Results consistently show that organizations with a higher proportion of male employees demonstrate a more negative voice climate, for both male and female employees. Furthermore, we find that this relationship is mediated, in tandem, by perceptions of a more hierarchical culture and lower interpersonal trust. Our research reframes organizational gender composition as a structural antecedent of relational dynamics beyond its effects on numerical minorities. These findings suggest that increasing gender balance extends beyond merely meeting diversity benchmarks, and may help organizations cultivate environments where employees feel empowered to offer critical upward suggestions.
