Optimizing New Venture Teams in the Era of Diverse Work Modes: Applications of Teamwork Science

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 2:30PM–3:30PM
Philadelphia Marriott Downtown — Poster Wall 3
ENT

People

Author: Tsuyoshi Aburai

Session overview

When remote work became widespread during the COVID-19 pandemic, interviews with several companies about what they prioritized in implementing remote work and maintaining team performance revealed a common answer: “trust.” Previous studies have suggested that trust is fostered through natural face-to-face interactions in the office, such as casual conversations during breaks or after work. However, such opportunities are difficult to achieve in remote work settings. Therefore, this study aims to clarify whether trust—traditionally cultivated through in-person interactions—can also be developed in a hybrid Project-Based Learning (PBL) environment that combines face-to-face and online collaboration, and if so, under what conditions. A review of the literature on trust and interviews with companies confirmed that, in remote work, “mutual trust” is particularly emphasized. In 2022 and 2023, students from the author’s seminar participated in Japan’s largest product development competition, “S-College (Student Innovation College),” as part of a PBL project. The project lasted eight months, from April to December, with six teams from the seminar each year, totaling twelve teams. During the project, three online workshops were conducted, and dynamic data were collected to measure the state of teamwork. The dynamic data quantified four elements: “amount of speech,” “nodding,” “smiling,” and “silent pauses.” Additionally, mutual trust among team members was assessed through surveys and interviews in August and December. At the end of the project, variations in trust levels among teams were observed. To identify which workshop elements influenced the development of mutual trust and to clarify causal relationships, a Bayesian network model was employed. The causal diagram included parameters such as “dynamic data (workshops),” “mutual trust,” “personality traits” (e.g., extraversion and agreeableness, quantified through surveys), and “idea value” (external evaluations of team proposals presented in December and members’ satisfaction scores). Sensitivity analysis revealed that mutual trust was influenced by factors such as: “When team members effectively fulfill their roles, the originality of ideas increases,” and “Teams with highly open members, frequent nodding and smiling during workshops, and high idea satisfaction tend to provide mutual support when needed.” These findings indicate that, under certain conditions, trust can indeed be fostered in hybrid PBL environments.
