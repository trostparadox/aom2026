Beyond Making Do: Relational Resource Orchestration in Institutional Voids

ENT
Soumodip Sarkar (University of Evora); Suresh Bhagavatula (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Informal entrepreneurs often build ventures without the institutional supports that conventional theory treats as prerequisites for durable exchange. We examine how manufacturing entrepreneurs in three Mumbai informal settlements—Dharavi, Mandala, and Kaulabander—sustain repeat transactions and extend trade credit despite unenforceable contracts, insecure property rights, and limited access to courts and bank finance. Drawing on inductive qualitative analysis of 50 ventures using a Gioia-informed approach, we identify a consistent process we term Relational Resource Orchestration (RRO): the deliberate structuring, bundling, and leveraging of social ties as governance infrastructure substituting for formal institutions. RRO operates through three interlinked mechanisms. Spatial commitment substitutes for documented collateral by tying creditworthiness to observable residence, place-based investments, and costly exit. Network embedding substitutes for formal verification by mobilizing shared ties, reputation channels, and staged vetting routines to screen partners and discipline opportunism. Continuous calibration substitutes for contractual enforcement through ongoing monitoring, synchronized settlement calendars, renegotiation practices, and graduated sanctions that stabilize expectations and recover arrears. These patterned practices are strikingly similar across cases, challenging accounts that explain informal durability primarily as bricolage-driven improvisation. We clarify how credit, verification, and enforcement are rebuilt through relationships over time. We extend resource orchestration theory by showing how orchestration can rely on relational governance when formal scaffolding is absent, and we advance institutional voids scholarship by specifying micro-level mechanisms that perform institutional functions. We also identify boundary conditions: dense relational systems that enable coordination can propagate shocks under severe stress. These insights redirect attention from scarcity toward governance capacity in practice.

Entrepreneurship as a Collective Practice: Peer Collaboration and Sharing in the Informal Sector

ENT
Anne Thérèse Méno Tamno (Université catholique de Louvain)
Informal entrepreneurship is central to economic activity in many developing countries, providing jobs, income, and essential services to urban populations. In Cameroon, for example, informal mechanics and carpenters operate small garages and workshops that are deeply embedded in local communities yet remain largely outside formal regulatory, financial, and institutional support systems. While prior research on informal entrepreneurship has emphasized survival strategies and institutional voids, it has paid limited attention to the everyday collaborative and sharing practices through which informal entrepreneurs collectively sustain their work. This study addresses this gap by examining how informal mechanics and carpenters collaborate and share resources in their daily activities. Drawing on social capital and practice-based theory, and based on an inductive qualitative case study, we identify five key domains of sharing (knowledge, workspace, tools, labor, and financial resources) and analyze them through a Why–How–Barriers framework. We show that collaboration is not merely a moral obligation or a survival reflex, but a situated entrepreneurial practice shaped by interdependence, shared professional identities, and pragmatic problem-solving. These collaboration and sharing practices are governed by informal rules, relational work, and material arrangements, and are constrained by trust breakdowns, opportunism, coordination challenges, and structural inequalities within informal networks.

How Much Does Context Matter? A Multilevel Decomposition of Informal Entrepreneurship

ENT
Mark Mallon (Florida Atlantic University); John Yue Zhang (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Daniel Andrews (Georgia State University)
Studies of informal entrepreneurship typically focus on institutional or individual antecedents, leaving unexplained which level of analysis accounts for more of the variation in whether entrepreneurs operate formally or informally. We address this tension by decomposing entrepreneurs’ registration decisions using a sample of 26,847 new ventures across 166 countries and 1,095 cities. We find a substantial concentration of explanatory power at higher institutional levels: country-level institutions explain 24.5% of the variance and city-level institutions explain 12.5% of the variance, whereas industry effects account for less than 1.0%. Our results suggest new avenues of research of informal entrepreneurship including multilevel analyses, attention to specific contexts related to informal institutions, and more focus on the influence of cities.

Understanding Informal Entrepreneurs’ Intentions to Formalize: An Emerging Market Perspective

ENT
Everlyne Misati (The George Washington University); Fred Ochieng Walumbwa (Florida International University)
Research on institutions and entrepreneurship suggests that imperfections in formal institutions, such as laws and regulations, provide the impetus for informal firms and that the higher the quality of these formal institutions, the more likely informal entrepreneurs are to formalize their firms. This presents an interesting puzzle: in emerging markets, where informal firms are more prevalent, the imperfections in formal institutions, which engender informality, tend to persist. Yet, some entrepreneurs in these contexts still transition from informality to formality. Thus, a salient question remains: what influences informal entrepreneurs’ intentions and decisions to formalize in emerging markets? We employ a mixed methods approach, drawing on primary interview and survey data, and find that informal entrepreneurs exert their agency, via bricolage, i.e., making do with the scarce resources at hand, and network relationships, to grow their firms. This initial growth then serves as a signal of viability, which incentivizes them to formalize, to facilitate broader access to resources and markets. Our study contributes in two key ways. First, it highlights an understudied yet important context of informal firms, which provide a source of livelihood for nearly two-thirds of the global workforce, and significantly contribute to the world economy. Second, it shows how, when operating in contexts with sustained imperfections in formal institutions, rather than wait perennially for conditions to improve, entrepreneurs turn lemons into lemonade. These entrepreneurs act: they leverage what they have (resources), what they can do (capabilities), and who they know (networks), to formalize and thus conceivably contribute to poverty alleviation, frugal innovation, and sustainability.

Uneven Contributions: Informal Entrepreneurship and Sustainability in the Global South

ENT
Tariq Ahmed (Rabat Business School)
Informal entrepreneurship is a pervasive yet contested form of economic activity across the Global South, raising critical questions about its contribution to sustainable development. While informal enterprises are widely recognised for generating income and employment, their broader social and environmental implications remain insufficiently understood. Drawing on the Triple Bottom Line (TBL) framework, this study examines the economic, social, and environmental sustainability effects of informal entrepreneurship across twelve developing countries from 2005 to 2020. Using panel data from the World Bank Group Entrepreneurship Survey, the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor, and the World Development Indicators, we construct composite sustainability indices aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals and analyse their relationship with informal entrepreneurial activity. Fixed and random effects models with Driscoll–Kraay robust standard errors are employed to address heteroskedasticity, serial correlation, and cross-sectional dependence. The findings reveal a clear duality. Informal entrepreneurship positively contributes to economic sustainability by expanding employment and income-generating opportunities, particularly in institutionally fragile contexts. However, these economic gains are accompanied by notable social and environmental trade-offs, including weaker labour protections, limited social inclusion, and increased environmental degradation. By extending the TBL framework to informal entrepreneurial settings, this study advances sustainability research beyond its traditional corporate focus and underscores the need for context-sensitive policy approaches that balance economic benefits with social and environmental safeguards.
