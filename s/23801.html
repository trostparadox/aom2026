The Double-Edged Ethics of Algorithmic Management: Status Motives and Managerial Objectification

OB
Ping Liu (Hunan University); Ling Yuan; Zhihong Tan (Chongqing Normal University); Elfi Baillien (KU Leuven)
Algorithmic management (AM) is reshaping how organizations allocate tasks, monitor performance, and deliver feedback, yet its ethical implications remain contested. Prior work has largely documented employees’ experiences of alienation and dehumanization under algorithmic control, but has paid less attention to a more proximal ethical process: when managers enforce algorithmic rules in everyday supervision and evaluation, how does AM shape whether they treat employees as instruments or as subjects? Drawing on the evolutionary social psychological theory of status, which distinguishes dominance-versus prestige-based influence routes, we develop and test a moderated parallel mediation model. We conceptualize perceived AM intensity (the extent to which algorithms are involved in monitoring, task control, and evaluation/decision making) as a status-relevant situational cue. Higher AM intensity can, on the one hand, heighten pressure for executional consistency and outcome controllability, thereby strengthening dominance-based status motives centered on control and obedience and increasing managerial objectification of employees. On the other hand, it can provide structured opportunities to use data for diagnosis and performance improvement, thereby strengthening prestige-based status motives grounded in demonstrating competence and contribution and reducing objectification. We further theorize algorithmic transparency as a key boundary condition: when decision rules and rationales are more transparent, managers are more likely to use AM as a tool for explaining standards, providing feedback, and facilitating improvement, rather than as a mere enforcement mechanism, thereby weakening the dominance pathway while strengthening the prestige pathway. Two complementary studies—a three-wave manager–employee dyadic survey in Chinese firms and a 2 × 2 scenario experiment—support these hypothesis: under low transparency, AM is more strongly associated with higher objectification via dominance motives, whereas under high transparency, it is more strongly associated with lower objectification via prestige motives. Overall, our results show that the ethical direction of AM depends on the status motives it activates and can be systematically shaped through design features such as transparency.
