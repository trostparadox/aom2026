A Tale of Three Cities: The Influence of Small Towns, Suburbs, and Big Cities on Entrepreneurship

ENT
Jeffrey T Cavanaugh (University of Wyoming); Varkey Titus (University of Nebraska-Lincoln); Cameron Jay Borgholthaus (University of Wyoming)
Entrepreneurship is the fundamental result of (A) an opportunity, (B) resources, and (C) an entrepreneur. The community-level mechanisms that provide resources and opportunities for entrepreneurs may conflict with each other, as communities with abundant resources improve not only opportunities for nascent entrepreneurs, but also increase the opportunity cost of pursuing entrepreneurship, as other career alternatives provide robust income and stability. Thus, we examine the influence of a continuous notion of rurality on the new venture formation rate, whereby the most rural and the most urban communities see the highest rates of entrepreneurship and suburbs see relatively lower rates of entrepreneurship. While the most rural communities lack resource munificence, because of a dearth of well-paying careers, the opportunity cost of pursuing entrepreneurship is much lower, thereby pushing entrepreneurs into business. In the most urban communities, despite exceptional alternative career opportunities, environmental resource munificence pulls entrepreneurs into business. We test our hypotheses with fifteen years’ worth of U.S. County-level data and find robust support for our hypotheses, thereby reframing the literature’s notion of entrepreneurial opportunity cost as it relates to resources and opportunities.

How Cultural Diversity Shapes Partial and Full Foreign Exit Decisions in Family and Non-Family Firms

ENT
Evelyn Owie (West Virginia University); Alex Brinkmeier (Coastal Carolina University)
Multinational enterprises (MNEs) increasingly face pressure to exit foreign markets as competitive intensity and uncertainty rise. While prior research has identified numerous antecedents of foreign market exit, relatively little work adopts a real options perspective to explain how uncertainty shapes exit mode choices, or how ownership structures condition these decisions. Drawing on Real Options Theory (ROT), this study examines how cultural diversity between home and host countries, as a source of uncertainty, influences MNEs’ likelihood of pursuing partial versus total foreign exits, and how this relationship differs between family and non-family firms. ROT suggests that under high uncertainty, firms may delay irreversible actions by maintaining flexibility through partial exit rather than full abandonment. We argue that cultural diversity heightens uncertainty in foreign operations, increasing the attractiveness of partial exit as a real option. Integrating the socioemotional wealth (SEW) perspective, we further theorize that family firm governance amplifies this effect. We test our hypotheses using panel data from Planet Retail on 92 international retailers operating in 108 countries between 1998 and 2004, comprising 2,522 firm-year and branch-level observations. Using a two-step probit model and Cox hazard estimations, we find that cultural diversity increases the likelihood of partial exit relative to total exit, with this effect significantly stronger for family firms. This study advances research on foreign market exit by distinguishing exit modes and highlighting the joint roles of cultural uncertainty and family governance in shaping MNE exit decisions.

Immobility as Infrastructure: Mechanisms of Entrepreneur Survival in Rome’s Bangladeshi Enclave

ENT
Iftekhar Ul Karim (BRAC University); Kerem Gurses
Entrepreneurship research has long exhibited a mobility bias; this study shows how immigrant entrepreneurs can build successful businesses by staying in one place. Based on two years of ethnography in Rome’s Bangladeshi enclave, we conceptualize immobility as an entrepreneurial infrastructure and identify four social mechanisms: subscriptions, timings, supply chains, and investments. These mechanisms constitute a system of socialized governance that stabilizes outlooks, synchronizes daily routines, and facilitates the continuous flow of resources within the enclave. Our study shows that community infrastructures sustain immigrant entrepreneurship more than mobility does. Rootedness functions as a form of agency that generates collective viability. Immobility, therefore, is not a constraint but a productive condition through which marginalized entrepreneurs create stability, belonging, and durable economic life.

Navigating Crime: Entrepreneurs' Spatial Protection Strategies in an Informal Economy

ENT
Mohammed Bendaanane (Technical University of Munich); Robert Nason (McGill University); Siddharth Vedula (Miami University); Andrew Charman
Extant research often argues that the environment of the informal economy entrepreneurs is inherently dangerous and strongly associated with crime. In this study, we seek to move beyond crime as a generalized characteristic and draw on environmental criminology to explore variations in crime victimization. We adopt methodological bricolage and leverage a unique 'small area census' dataset of entrepreneurs in a South African township. Using geographic information systems (GIS) data and geospatial analyses, we first visualize and identify geographically bound hot and cold spots of crime within the township. Next, in probing factors that influence the variation in crime victimization, we find that taxi ranks were a particular protective spatial feature. In subsequent regression analysis, we find that taxi ranks play a role in enabling cold spots or providing shelter within hot spots and are particularly protective for more marginalized actors (foreigners). We theorize that taxi ranks serve as form of spatial safe haven for operating businesses. However, we find that the protective influence of taxi ranks has a limited geographic reach; the range of protective coverage varies across taxi ranks and in particular is much broader in cold spots compared to hot spots. To explore this, we zoom in to explore the micro-geography of each type of rank. By visually examining each’s evolution over time and through satellite imagery and field observation, we identify three apparent protective strategies: 1) defensive co-location (vendors setting up near the rank over time), 2) spatial integration (between rank and vendors) and 3) spatial authority (singular vs. multiple land claimants) as important factors that link defensive co-location to the physical range of protection from crime. Our work builds generative links across literatures of entrepreneurship, micro-geography, and environmental criminology which extends understanding of co-location patterns and enriches understanding of how crime relates to informal economy entrepreneurship.

To Stay or to Leave Smart Cities? Family and Non-Family SMEs Location in Entrepreneurial Ecosystems

ENT
Filippo Marchesani (Gabriele d'Annunzio University); Andrea Bikfalvi; Pilar Marques-Gou (University of Girona)
This study explores how smart city advancement influences the localization and persistence of family and non-family SMEs within urban entrepreneurial ecosystems. Building on embeddedness and agglomeration theories, we use a panel of 38 Italian cities (2010–2023) to test for curvilinear effects across ownership types. Results show that while both firm types initially benefit, non-family SMEs tend to exit at higher advancement levels, whereas family SMEs persist due to stronger territorial embeddedness. These findings highlight the differentiated impact of digital transformation on firm geography and call for inclusive, ownership-sensitive strategies to sustain entrepreneurial ecosystems within smart city transitions
