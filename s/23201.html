Beyond Engagement: A Theory of Gender Congruity in Women Entrepreneurs’ Online Social Support

ENT
Xian Cao (Illinois State University); Frederic Delmar (emlyon business school); Dan K. Hsu (North Dakota State University); Hongfei Li (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Qingyu XU (City University of Hong Kong)
Digital platforms are increasingly important spaces where entrepreneurs vent frustrations and seek social support. Yet women entrepreneurs often report a paradox: despite receiving substantial engagement when venting online, they frequently do not feel supported. We address this disconnect by developing a dynamic, interactional theory of gender congruity grounded in identity management. Rather than focusing on whether social support is offered, we examine how different forms of social support, such as emotional versus informational, carry distinct identity implications. Using large-scale observational data from Reddit, we show that women entrepreneurs’ venting elicits systematic variation in social support provision depending on venting topic and listener gender. When women vent about business difficulties, they receive more emotional support overall, largely from female listeners, but less informational support, especially from male listeners, despite informational support being most strongly associated with feeling supported in this context. When women vent about work-life balance, emotional support declines while informational support increases, driven primarily by female listeners. Overall, perceived supportedness depends on whether social support aligns with the identity meaning of the vented issue, explaining why engagement alone is an incomplete indicator of social support in online entrepreneurial communities.

Entrepreneurial Well-Being as Relational Process

ENT
Shanon Yang (Syracuse University)
Despite extensive research documenting entrepreneurial strain, a persistent puzzle remains: why do similar levels of business performance yield markedly different well-being outcomes across entrepreneurs? This paper addresses this question by developing a relational, process-oriented framework that moves beyond direct performance-outcome assumptions to explain how entrepreneurial well-being emerges through resource negotiations embedded within family systems and structured by gendered expectations. Integrating Conservation of Resources theory and Social Role Theory, we theorize that business performance influences entrepreneurial well-being indirectly through perceived role conflict—the subjective experience of incompatible demands between work and family domains. We further propose that this relationship is contingent upon two critical moderators: differentiated forms of family support (instrumental versus emotional) and gender-based role expectations. Our framework advances entrepreneurship scholarship in four ways. First, it identifies perceived role conflict as the psychological mechanism translating performance fluctuations into well-being outcomes, moving beyond descriptive symptom catalogs toward explanatory process models. Second, it resolves inconsistent findings regarding family support by distinguishing between instrumental support (direct resource replenishment through task assistance) and emotional support (indirect psychological reframing through validation), revealing their differential pathways in mitigating role conflict. Third, it demonstrates that gender fundamentally moderates the performance-conflict relationship itself, not merely well-being outcomes, illuminating how socially structured role obligations create asymmetric vulnerabilities to resource depletion. Fourth, it extends both theoretical foundations by revealing that resources operate paradoxically in entrepreneurial contexts—simultaneously buffering strain and generating new demands—and that role conflict emerges dynamically from resource fluctuations rather than static role incompatibilities. These insights reframe entrepreneurial well-being as a relational achievement shaped by boundary processes and structural conditions, offering actionable leverage points for entrepreneurs, families, support organizations, and policymakers seeking to sustain well-being in increasingly demanding entrepreneurial environments.

The Dark Side of Support: How Social Support Becomes Detrimental in Gendered Contexts

ENT
Golshan Javadian (Morgan State University); Fateme Kolasangiani (University of Tehran); Kambiz Talbei (University of Tehran); Zahra Arasti (University of Tehran)
Women entrepreneurs often operate under persistent resource constraints that extend beyond material shortages to affect psychological functioning and overall well-being. While prior research has documented the negative consequences of resource constraints for entrepreneurial outcomes, less is known about the mechanisms through which these constraints shape well-being or the conditions under which social support alleviates—or exacerbates—their effects. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and feminist perspectives on social support, this study examines how resource constraints influence women entrepreneurs’ well-being through psychological capital and how different forms of social support moderate this process. Using survey data from 198 women entrepreneurs in Iran, a highly resource-constrained and gendered context, we test a moderated mediation model in which psychological capital (hope, optimism, self-efficacy, and resilience) mediates the relationship between resource constraints and well-being, and emotional and instrumental social support serve as moderators. The results show that resource constraints are negatively associated with well-being both directly and indirectly through reduced psychological capital. Contrary to dominant assumptions, emotional social support strengthens the negative indirect effect of resource constraints on well-being, amplifying psychological resource depletion under conditions of scarcity. Instrumental social support, however, does not significantly moderate this relationship. These findings challenge universalistic views of social support as inherently protective and highlight its context-dependent and gendered nature. By integrating resource constraints, psychological capital, and differentiated forms of social support, this study contributes to entrepreneurship research on well-being, advances non-WEIRD theorizing, and underscores the importance of relational and institutional context in shaping women entrepreneurs’ lived experiences.

When Does Community Support Spur or Suppress Nascent Venture Innovativeness?

ENT
Kurian George (Tilburg University); Hooman Seyed Abootorabi (Towson University)
Community support has been gaining traction in entrepreneurship research as nascent ventures, i..e, new and emerging businesses in the early stages of development, are increasingly viewed as embedded in local social environments. Yet we lack a clear understanding of how community support influences nascent ventures’ innovativeness and why it can both enable and constrain novelty. In this paper, drawing on Self-Categorization Theory, we hypothesize that community support functions as a categorization cue that shifts entrepreneurs’ identity salience between personal and collective identities, generating nonlinear effects on innovativeness. We further argue that government support moderates these processes by reducing reliance on community-based identity cues. To test these hypotheses, we construct a unique longitudinal, and multi-level data on nascent ventures by integrating multiple datasets. Our findings suggest a U-shaped relationship between community support and innovativeness—higher innovativeness at low and high levels of support and lower innovativeness at moderate levels. They also show that government support dampens this curvilinear effect. This study advances research on community by showing that its influence on nascent venture innovativeness is non-linear and identity-based rather than uniform and resource-based. Our findings also advance research on new venture innovativeness, extends Self-Categorization Theory to entrepreneurial ecosystems, and offers practical implications for designing community and policy support that fosters innovation without suppressing experimentation.

Women Entrepreneurs’ Well-Being: Systematic Literature Review and Research Agenda

ENT
Magdalena Markowska (King's College London); Ute Stephan (King's College London)
This paper takes stock of existing knowledge on women entrepreneurs’ well-being, a topic often overlooked in entrepreneurial well-being research despite its centrality to human functioning. Through a systematic review (SLR) of 204 studies published between 1983 and 2024, we find that women entrepreneurs’ well-being is conceptualized broadly, encompassing work-family balance and emotional, social and spiritual dimensions, operating as a multi-level construct spanning individual, family, and community contexts. Compared to men, women’s well-being is influenced more by non-monetary goals, particularly self-fulfillment. Our synthesis introduces a practice perspective, identifying practices such as introspecting, self-improving, balancing, (re)sourcing and empowering as generative of well-being. Consequences extend beyond the individual, benefitting families and communities. This review reframes entrepreneurial well-being as both an experience and an ongoing, socially embedded practice. We propose a research agenda emphasizing longitudinal designs and contextualized approaches to advance theory and practice.
