Evolutionary Complementarity in Platform Ecosystems

STR
Jiaming Yang (Zhejiang University of Finance and Economics); Zhengyao Kang (University of Bristol); Sihan Li (INSEAD)
Established firms, incumbents with deep-rooted positions and pre-existing commitments in physical marketplaces, are increasingly joining digital platform ecosystems as complementors. However, their ecosystem participation requires navigating a fundamental alignment-autonomy tension: to enjoy broader digital marketplaces, they must align closely with the digital platform owner, yet such alignment often necessitates ecosystem-specific investments that can compromise their autonomy and erode their traditional domains. Drawing on an in-depth case study of Eifini, a leading women’s apparel merchant that has participated in China’s largest e-commerce platform, Tmall, since 2011, we identify complementarity renewal as a key mechanism through which established firms address this tension and sustain growth. Unlike prior studies that portrays digital platforms as disruptors of physical marketplaces, we identify a symbiotic co-evolution pattern: Established firms leverage digital platforms to expand digitally while simultaneously reinforcing their physical market positions. Our study highlights the dynamic and evolving nature of complementarity, extending the prevailing view in ecosystem literature that treats complementarity as static and ex ante. By focusing on established firms and tracing their ecosystem participation trajectory, we contribute to the growing body of research on complementor-centric strategies in digital platform ecosystems.

Architectural Competition: How EV Startups Disrupted Automotive Incumbents

STR
Erkko Autio (Imperial College London); Herve Legenvre
How do firms capture value during radical industry reconfigurations? Traditional frame-works focusing on complementary assets struggle to explain competition when industry boundaries are in flux. We introduce Architectural Rents Theory (ART), positing that sustainable prof-its derive from Architectural Agency—the active reshaping of industry structures. Through a longitudinal analysis of Tesla, BYD, and Toyota, we identify four mechanisms of rent appropriation: Bottleneck Control, Parallel Play, Regulatory Shaping, and Ecosystem Governance. We demonstrate that successful entrants capture value by constructing new systemic bottlenecks, while incumbents struggle as legacy advantages erode. This study defines architectural strategy as the capacity to architect the industry itself.

Value Creation in Ecosystems: Bottlenecks, Resource Allocation, and System-Level Tradeoffs

STR
Sungho Kim (Southern Illinois University, Edwardsville); Kyu-Min Lee (Chungbuk National University)
A growing body of work emphasizes that firm performance depends not only on firm-level strategies but also on coordination among interdependent actors within business ecosystems. Yet, existing studies primarily examine firm-level actions or focal actors’ incentive designs, leaving limited understanding of how ecosystems themselves can act strategically to optimize collective value creation. We address this gap by conceptualizing ecosystem-level strategy as a problem of coordinated resource allocation under interdependence. Among coordination of activities in ecosystems, we consider bottleneck strategies, how the ecosystem as a whole manages bottlenecks, to be the main coordinated action of our interest. We develop a computational model to examine how resource allocation between bottlenecks and non-bottlenecks impacts ecosystem value creation. We analyze alternative ecosystem-level bottleneck strategies, including resource concentration on bottlenecks, equal allocation across ecosystem participants, and coordinated reallocation that dynamically aligns bottlenecks and non-bottlenecks over time. Our experiments and analyses yield three core insights. First, ecosystem performance exhibits a nonlinear relationship with bottleneck investment, peaking at intermediate levels of resource concentration rather than increasing monotonically. Second, ecosystem-level value creation displays threshold effects, improving discontinuously once coordination surpasses a critical level. Third, early allocation decisions generate strong path dependence, imprinting persistent constraints on later coordination efforts. Together, we extend the ecosystem-as-structure view toward an ecosystem-as-system view. Findings reconceptualize bottlenecks as endogenous design variables and underscore that ecosystem coordination is fundamentally a system-level design problem, not a simple extension of firm-level optimization.

Structural Decoupling in Ecosystems: A Process Theory of How Value Capture Undermines Value Creation

STR
WENTING LI; Luiz F. Mesquita (Arizona State University); Thomas Y Choi (Arizona State University); Dale Stewart Rogers (Arizona State University)
Supplier ecosystems are commonly assumed to be self-disciplining: competitive pressure among firms is expected to align value capture with value creation over time through resistance, renegotiation, or exit. We challenge this assumption by theorizing how modular ecosystem architectures—the very structures that enable specialization and legitimate unequal value distribution based on differential contributions—can paradoxically enable value capture to transition from disciplined rivalry to blind extraction. This transition diminishes collective innovation capacity and undermines the synergistic advantage that makes ecosystems greater than the sum of their parts. We develop a three-stage process theory explaining: (1) how myopic bargaining—locally rational decisions that optimize immediate value capture without visibility into systemic effects—creates a liquidity extraction trap through three reinforcing mechanisms; (2) how distributive alignment frameworks can diagnostically distinguish functional from distortive value capture; and (3) how architectural governance mechanisms—synchrony, traceability, and proportionality—can restore coupling between value creation and value capture. This decoupling remains difficult for focal firms to detect as its consequences unfold unevenly across tiers and over time. Our theory contributes to research on ecosystems and coopetition by identifying the conditions and mechanisms through which value-capture approaches in supplier ecosystems transition from supporting joint value-creation to being self-undermining.

Guarding Value or Growing the Pie? Interlacedness and Co-Inventon Timing in Enabling Technologies

STR
Tejas Ramdas (Cornell University); Gautam Ahuja (Cornell University)
Enabling technologies are broadly applicable, but their productivity depends on complementary inventions. Adoption is often slow and uneven because firms must build application-specific complements under uncertainty with imperfectly contractible investments. The timing of a firm’s first complementary invention is inherently relational: early complements reshape what other potential inventors can see, interpret, and attempt. We formalize this with technological interlacedness, defined as a firm’s embeddedness in the enabling-technology-specific input–output knowledge network, and derive competing logics. A value capture logic predicts that highly interlaced first movers strategically design complements to contain spillovers and raise rivals’ cognitive, learning, and implementation costs, which delays others’ first complementary inventions. A generative advantage logic predicts the opposite: because early complements help a first mover claim and navigate the technology’s generative space, interlaced firms have incentives to build exploratory, robust, and more general complements that expand optionality. These choices also accelerate others’ first complementary inventions. Using the universe of U.S. utility patents (1983–2024), we identify enabling technologies and measure each firm’s time to first complementary invention, defined as its first citing patent within 15 years. For each enabling technology, we build a firm knowledge network from input–output citation ties and train a graph neural network to predict adoption times. We then run counterfactuals that vary which firm goes first and how interlaced it is, while holding the technology fixed. Results support generative advantage: more interlaced initial inventors speed later complementary invention. Each additional knowledge tie predicts roughly a 9–10% reduction in average time to first complement relative to the fastest adopter.
