Renewable Energy Readiness and Investment Gaps in the USA.: ML and Spatial Analysis

ONE
Syed Anees Haider Zaidi (University of Warsaw); Ulviyya Abasova (University of Warsaw); Piotr Wojcik (University of Warsaw); Rana Umair Ashraf (University of Warsaw)
Regional disparities threaten the effectiveness and equity of renewable energy transitions in the United States (US), yet policymakers lack granular tools to evaluate subnational readiness and guide targeted investment. To address this gap, this study develops a comprehensive, data-driven framework to evaluate and forecast renewable energy readiness across U.S. states from 2015 to 2023, with explicit consideration of structural disruptions. A novel Regional Renewable Readiness Index (RRI) is constructed by integrating economic, societal, energy, and environmental indicators. Using machine learning algorithms (LASSO, Random Forest, XGBoost) alongside interpretability tools (Shapley Additive Explanations), the analysis predicts adoption patterns while uncovering key readiness drivers. Spatial econometric models, including Spatial Error Models, capture geographic interdependencies and spillover effects. Results indicate substantial regional disparities, pandemic-induced shifts in renewable trajectories, and statistically significant spatial spillovers, with readiness gaps exceeding around 83% between leading and lagging states. These findings offer a roadmap for geographically tailored, evidence-based renewable investment strategies that can enhance both equity and resilience in the post-pandemic energy transition.

Green Is Credit: Government-Led Environmental Credit Ratings and Trade Credit

ONE
Shihao Yin (Tianjin University); HUIBIN DU; Helen Feng Liang (Western Kentucky University); Shouyu Yao (Tianjin University); Zhongguo Lin (Tianjin University)
In emerging markets, the credibility of ESG signals is often compromised by greenwashing and high verification costs. We examine whether state-mediated signaling can resolve this friction to unlock trade credit. Exploiting China’s mandatory environmental credit rating system as a natural experiment and a staggered difference-in-differences (DID) design on 28,623 firm-year observations (2009-2021), we document that firms with high government-audited ratings (excellent/good) secure significantly more trade credit. The effect is concentrated among non-SOEs, financially constrained firms, and in weak institutional environments, supporting the hypothesis that government backing acts as a critical substitute for private screening trust. Mechanism tests validate two pathways: a Perception Effect, where ratings bolster corporate reputation and lower perceived risk (evidenced by reduced penalties, increased subsidies, and positive media coverage); and an Information Effect, where ratings mitigate asymmetry and verification costs (reflected in lower supplier concentration, greater analyst attention, and improved earnings quality). Our findings demonstrate that state-backed systems effectively transform unverifiable environmental data into actionable credit inputs, providing a scalable blueprint for integrating sustainability into inter-firm financing.

Corporate Mitigation Strategy: Real Option Approach

ONE
Seonghoon Kim (The Hang Seng University of Hong Kong); Johan Sulaeman (National University of Singapore); Wang Haoxu (ISEG Lisbon School of Economics and Management, University of Lisbon); Qingwei Li (The Hang Seng University of Hong Kong)
In response to pressure to mitigate environmental impacts (e.g., GHG emissions), firms face the dual challenge of meeting environmental requirements while maintaining financial profitability through their chosen mitigation strategy. To meet both goals, firms can choose more or less substantive actions—those that permanently mitigate environmental impacts through operational or technological innovation, which we encapsulate as “abatement”—or symbolic actions—those that meet the goal through offsetting via credit purchases, encapsulated as “offset.” While abatement is socially preferable, the firm-level calculus often favors offsetting as a lower-risk option, leading to a socially sub-optimal outcome. Motivated by the need to encourage substantive corporate action, this research examines the conditions under which firms choose abatement over offset. We develop a financial model using a real options approach, supported by scenario analysis and empirical validation. Our findings emphasize that the choice of abatement depends on favorable firm- and institution-specific conditions, particularly a firm’s high technological confidence and the presence of a high (or stringent) environmental tax rate.

Do Environmental Externalities Influence a Firm’s Zero Leverage Propensity?

ONE
Antonio Renzi (Sapienza University of Rome); Paolo Saona (University of Valladolid); Pietro Taragoni (Sapienza University of Rome); Gianluca Vagnani (Sapienza University of Rome)
The persistent presence of zero-leverage firms remains a notable puzzle in corporate finance. Conventional explanations typically emphasize firm-level demand factors, such as financial flexibility, or focus on credit supply constraints; however, empirical investigations often conflate these two elements. This research introduces an innovative supply-side mechanism that links a firm’s environmental externalities to its access to debt capital. Based on the well-documented "brown premium” observed in equity markets, we contend that creditors with short-term horizons tend to favor firms with high negative externalities because of their robust current cash flows, whereas they discount sustainable firms that incur substantial initial costs. This dynamic establishes a supply-side constraint that fosters zero leverage among environmentally friendly firms. To distinguish between demand and supply effects, we employ a bivariate probit model with partial observability, applied to a comprehensive sample of 1,022 firms across 54 countries from 2011 to 2022. Our findings indicate that environmental impact notably reduces the likelihood of debt availability—an outcome that conventional univariate models fail to detect. In particular, firms with lower environmental externalities are more likely to be credit-constrained, resulting in zero leverage. Additionally, we observe that this effect is moderated by the firm’s stage of development and that firm value exerts asymmetric effects: it negatively affects demand while positively affecting supply. Methodologically, we demonstrate that the joint estimation of debt demand and supply is essential to prevent biased conclusions. These results contribute to the theoretical understanding of the zero-leverage phenomenon, identify a critical friction in sustainable strategies, and suggest that capital markets may unintentionally penalize green pioneers through debt rationing.

The Impact of Environmental Protection Fee-to-Tax Reform on the Cost of Equity

ONE
Baoxuan Yan (Sun Yat-Sen University); JINGSONG TAN (Sun Yat-Sen University); YUE YAN (Sun Yat-Sen University); Jingxin Zhang (South China Agricultural University)
We uses the implementation of the Environmental Protection Tax Law of China as a quasi-natural experiment to explore the impact of the legalization transformation of the environmental governance system on the resource allocation of the capital market. The study found that the environmental fee-to-tax reform policy increases the cost of equity capital (COE) financing for manufacturing enterprises, which implies an increase in risk for the enterprise and a decrease in the efficiency of market-oriented allocation of resources. Nevertheless, companies facing environmental regulatory pressure can effectively mitigate the impact of the fee-to-tax policy on corporate risks through market-based environmental management strategies, including strengthening green information disclosure, increasing green investment, or avoiding taxes through off-site subsidiaries. Cross-sectional analysis shows that this policy has a more significant impact on the COE of companies facing heavy external regulatory pressure, strong legitimacy needs, and industries with fierce competition. This study provides theoretical insights for environmental regulations and the green-oriented function of the capital market, and decision support for green tax systems.
