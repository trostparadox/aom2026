When Moralizing Intrinsic Motivation Backfires: The Role of Leader Bottom-Line Mentality

OB
Linkun Han (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Yujie Shi (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Li Guo (University of International Business and Economics)
Leaders often attach moral value to intrinsic motivation. However, this widely endorsed normative moral stance may not always yield positive outcomes. This research explores when and why leader moralization of intrinsic motivation might lead to detrimental team consequences, by identifying leader bottom-line mentality (BLM) as a critical boundary condition. Drawing on attribution theory, we propose that leader BLM shapes how leader moralization of intrinsic motivation is interpreted, such that under high leader BLM, leader moralization of intrinsic motivation is more likely to be associated with a team-shared attribution of leader selfishness. This shared attribution, in turn, is negatively related to team performance and positively related to team turnover rate. Results from a multi-source field study in the insurance industry supported the hypotheses. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings and outline directions for future research.

None of My Business: How Supervisor Performance Pressure Spills Over to Subordinate Expediency

OB
Ning Chen (Chengdu University of Technology); Jingyu Dong (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Diwan LI (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Hui Lv (Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University)
Drawing on the exploitation perspective in labor relations, we developed and tested a model that explains the spillover process by which supervisor performance pressure leads to subordinate expediency through supervisor exploitation of subordinates, contingent on supervisor benevolence. Using two multi-source and time-lagged field studies conducted in two firms (a real estate agency and a commercial bank) located in southwest China, we demonstrated that supervisor performance pressure heightened supervisor exploitation of subordinates, which in turn promoted subordinates’ expedient behavior. Furthermore, the positive effect of supervisor performance pressure on exploitation was buffered when the supervisor showed benevolence, thus leading to lower expediency in subordinates. Our findings contribute to the literature on performance pressure and add to our knowledge of how supervisor performance pressure, in interaction with their traits, shapes the exploitation and subordinates’ subsequent behavioral responses.

When Profits Become Moral Imperatives: Bottom-Line Mentality and Leaders’ Ethical Reasoning

OB
Zaid Alqhaiwi (RMIT University); Sen Sendjaya (RMIT University); Hamidreza Harati (RMIT University); Sehrish Shahid (RMIT University)
Prior research has documented the consequences of leader bottom-line mentality (BLM), yet less is known about how such leaders pursue desired outcomes. Drawing on motivated moral reasoning theory, we examine whether BLM activates leaders’ moral disengagement, thereby increasing unethical pro-organizational behavior (UPB) and encouragement of follower immorality. We also explore relational accountability as a potential boundary condition. Across a daily diary field study, a laboratory experiment, and an online experiment, results consistently show that BLM heightens moral disengagement, which in turn promotes UPB and immorality encouragement. Together, these findings illuminate how bottom-line–focused leaders morally rationalize questionable means to achieve organizational goals.
