Perceived Colleague Secrecy and Knowledge Sabotage: A Moderated Mediation Model

HR
SIJIA ZHAO; Chao Ma (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Rebecca Kechen Dong (University of Technology, Sydney)
Integrating stress appraisal theory and workplace aggression literature, we examine why and when perceived colleague secrecy leads to knowledge sabotage in the workplace. We hypothesize that perceived colleague secrecy increases employees’ threat appraisal, and this relationship is strengthened when leaders fail to adhere to justice rules. We further hypothesize that such threat appraisal drives employees to engage in knowledge sabotage behavior as a typical form of work-based violence. We test our framework through two field studies. The first study analyzes 256 employees and confirms the positive relationship between perceived colleague secrecy and threat appraisal, moderated by leaders’ justice rule adherence. The second study, using data from 327 employees and their supervisors, replicates these findings and validates the full model. We conclude with a comprehensive discussion of both theoretical and practical implications of the present research.

Consistency Matters: How Variability in Job Resources Shapes Discretionary Behavior

HR
Patrick Downes (University of Kansas); Sasha Pustovit (Towson University); Miguel Caldas (The University of Texas at Tyler)
The job demands–resources (JD–R) model has been central to understanding how job resources (e.g., job autonomy and social support) shape employee motivation and behavior. However, prior research has largely conceptualized these resources as either stable between-person characteristics or momentary within-person fluctuations with short-lived effects. Integrating these between- and within-person perspectives, we propose that job resource variability (i.e., the degree of within-person variability in an individual’s job resources) might have between-person effects on extra-role behaviors. Using experience-sampling data collected across ten workdays from full-time employees, results show that between-person variability in job autonomy and social support predicts between-person organizational citizenship behavior, creativity, and counterproductive work behavior above and beyond mean levels of job autonomy and social support. These effects operate through distinct psychological pathways, rather than a uniform mediational process. Social support variability primarily operates through burnout, whereas autonomy variability undermines experienced meaningfulness and exhibits direct relationships with creativity and counterproductive behavior, suggesting distinct underlying mechanisms. These findings extend JD–R and conservation of resources theories by demonstrating that how consistently job resources are experienced matters, alongside the average levels of job resources employees have. More broadly, this study highlights the importance of moving beyond static and momentary conceptualizations of work design to incorporate resource dynamics as a durable influence on discretionary behavior, including how motivation, self-regulation, and behavioral opportunity unfold over time.

A Double-Edged Sword? A JD-R Perspective on How SRHRM Shapes Employee Job Performance

HR
zhang zijian (Shandong University); Yixuan Jing (Shandong University); Yu Chen (Carlos III University of Madrid); yuan zhang (Shandong University); Dongqian Yang (Shandong University); Anan Peng (Shandong University); Chenghao Men (Shandong University)
As socially responsible human resource management (SRHRM) gains prominence as a means of promoting fairness, well-being, and organizational sustainability, questions remain regarding its consequences for employee performance. Drawing on the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model, we propose that SRHRM triggers two competing psychological processes that jointly shape job performance. On one hand, SRHRM provides critical job resource that enhances employees’ work meaningfulness, thereby improving performance. On the other hand, SRHRM may provide job demand by elevating employees’ role overload, which in turn undermines performance. We further theorize that leader support serves as a key boundary condition that strengthens the positive indirect effect of work meaningfulness, while attenuating the negative indirect effect through role overload. A multi-wave field study supported our hypotheses. Our research contributes to the SRHRM literature. We also discuss the limitations and future research directions.

Organizational Citizenship Behavior Breadth: Predictors and Consequences in Team Diversity Contexts

HR
Hanbo Shim (The University of Texas at Arlington); Joo Hun Han (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology); Andrea Kim (Sungkyunkwan University); Xueqing Fan (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Drawing on theories and research on OCB motive and team diversity, we investigate how and when individuals with unique OCB motives perform OCB to other team members (OCB-I) broadly above and beyond the level of OCB. In so doing, we propose a novel OCB-I breadth con-cept representing how broadly an individual performs OCB above and beyond the level of OCB-I. We also introduce the novel motive for OCB, or personal growth motive that reflects the focal in-dividual’s need for growth in one’s job or career. We further theorize that the team diversity types (i.e., task-oriented and relation-oriented team diversity) will serve as the key contexts for one’s OCB motives related to the focal individual’s advancement potential rating via OCB-I breadth above and beyond the OCB-I level. We tested our study model using two-wave survey data over four months from 481 team members in 117 team leaders at the headquarters of a South Korean manufacturing company. Taken together, our findings not only reveal a novel condition for the link between OCB motives and advancement potential but also suggest a breadth-based mechanism and possible conditions that can deliver positive impacts to other team members, as well as the benefits of focal individuals, simultaneously within their teams.
