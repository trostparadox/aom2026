Family Control, Board Gender Diversity, and Social Performance Across Cultural Contexts

ENT
Ratan Dheer (Eastern Michigan University); Kai Xu (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Len J. Trevino (Florida Atlantic University)
We integrate the organizational identity and identification perspectives with gender socialization and cross-cultural theory to advance knowledge of international differences in the corporate social performance (CSP) of family and nonfamily firms. Based on a 15-year panel dataset of 8,327 family and nonfamily firms from 44 nations, we find that although family firms exhibit greater CSP than nonfamily firms, the impact is contingent on their board gender composition and national cultural conditions. Specifically, family firms with a higher proportion of women on corporate boards exhibit higher CSP than other firms. Furthermore, our three-way interaction analysis suggests that culture creates unique boundary conditions, as cultural differences in individualism, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity significantly contribute to explaining CSP, even among family firms with similar board gender compositions. Our study offers meaningful theoretical contributions and practical implications for the international corporate social performance and family business literature.

Labor Productivity in Family Firms: An HR-Centered Perspective

ENT
Fabio Arena (University of St. Gallen); Jeroen Neckebrouck (IESE Business School); Thomas Markus Zellweger (University of St. Gallen)
The productivity of family firms remains a central yet unresolved issue in management research. While prior studies report both productivity advantages and disadvantages of family firms, we know relatively little about the human resource (HR) mechanisms that condition these outcomes. This study adopts an HR perspective to examine how employment-related contingencies shape labor productivity in family versus nonfamily firms. Using a unique matched panel dataset from a Belgian payroll provider linked to firm-level registry and administrative data, we analyze almost 35,000 firms over multiple years. Using between-effects panel regressions, we find productivity advantages for family firms only under specific HR conditions. Higher wages raise productivity for family and nonfamily firms, but they matter significantly less in family firms. Job security raises productivity equally across firm types, indicating a universal performance benefit rather than a family-firm-specific mechanism. In contrast, family firms benefit disproportionately from managerial professionalization, but exhibit greater productivity declines under employee turnover shocks and extended emoployee tenure. These findings highlight the importance of workforce configuration as a mechanism through which family firm status affects firm productivity.

Navigating Tensions Between Caregiving and Financial Goals During Algorithm Adoption in Family Firms

ENT
Isabel Aufricht (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Nadine Kammerlander (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management)
As organizations increasingly integrate algorithms into their strategic and operational processes, questions arise about the influence of organizational goals on algorithm adoption. Algorithms often privilege efficiency, which can create tensions with non-financial goals such as caregiving. However, we know little about how decision-makers make sense of and manage this emerging goal tension in the context of algorithm adoption. Family firms offer a revealing context, as they are continuously required to balance financial and non-financial goals. Thus, drawing on sensemaking theory, we investigate how organizational goals shape algorithm adoption behavior in family firms. This qualitative study draws on 47 interviews with decision-makers from family firms in the manufacturing industry, focusing on how they navigate tensions between financial performance and caregiving goals. Our study shows that all family firm decision-makers perceive goal tensions in algorithm adoption and rely on the same three sensemaking mechanisms: empowering, compensating, and justifying. However, they interpret and apply these mechanisms differently depending on their goal orientation. This results in three distinct algorithm adoption behaviors: avoiding, conscious acting, and pushing. Our study contributes to existing research by advancing the debate on the interplay between organizational goals and algorithm adoption. Furthermore, we expand family firm research by theorizing goal-laden sensemaking in technology-driven tensions.

The Diffusion of Conflict in Family Firms

ENT
Sunny P.H. Wang (London Business School); Freek Vermeulen (London Business School)
Although scholars have linked family involvement in publicly listed firms to conformity with stakeholder expectations and harmonious stakeholder relationships, they often assume family unity and overlook a widely prevalent yet potentially detrimental cost of family involvement: family conflict. Drawing on emotional contagion theory, we theorize that negative emotions experienced by controlling family members as a result of severe family conflicts can spread to the firm. Once negative emotions have diffused into the firm, they lead to internal conflicts between the firm and employees, and to conflicts with external stakeholders. We test these notions using data on publicly listed family firms and court judgment records in Taiwan. Our findings reveal that family firms controlled by families with severe conflicts are more likely to become involved in lawsuits against employees and with external stakeholders, such as suppliers, customers, and government agencies, even when the feuding family members hold no formal positions in the firm.
