Isolation in Remote and Hybrid Work: A Conceptual Review

Authors: Julia Eisenberg Pace University; Jennifer Gibbs University of California Santa Barbara; Elisa Mattarelli San Jose State University; Anu Sivunen University of Jyväskylä

Session overview

As remote and hybrid work arrangements become increasingly prevalent, concerns about work isolation have emerged, encompassing physical, social, and professional dimensions. Our study systematically reviews empirical literature on isolation in remote and hybrid work, drawing from management, psychology, communication, and information systems research published between 1999 and 2025. The review suggests that isolation is not a singular experience but a multi-dimensional phenomenon, with implications that vary across individual, team, organizational, and societal levels. Isolation is often viewed as detrimental because it may hinder knowledge transfer, organizational commitment, and foster loneliness. However, it may also offer benefits, such as increased flexibility and autonomy for experts, digital nomads, and marginalized groups. Technological advances have further complicated the relationship between physical presence and social or professional connectivity. Our study presents a conceptual model integrating antecedents and outcomes of both perceived and objective isolation. It calls for a nuanced understanding of isolation, recognizing its diverse manifestations and impacts across different worker populations and organizational contexts. The findings underscore the need for organizations to design remote and hybrid work environments that balance flexibility and autonomy with opportunities for meaningful connection and knowledge sharing. We conclude with a future research agenda aimed at informing workplace design and fostering inclusive, engaging climates for all employees, regardless of location.
