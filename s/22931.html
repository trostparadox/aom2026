Corporate Philanthropy as a Strategic Tool: Navigating Market Liberalization and State Intervention

OMT
Weiwei Yang; Linda Hui Shi; Diego Coraiola (University of Victoria)
Research has uncovered how corporate philanthropy works in democratic societies ruled by a market logic imperative. In these contexts, philanthropy is a strategic tool for corporations to signal goodwill and enhance their reputation and legitimacy. In contrast, recent studies indicate that corporate philanthropy may take a different shape in non-democratic, state-capitalist regimes characterized by a complex institutional environment. In these contexts, we argue, philanthropy functions as a strategic non-market signal that allows firms to reconcile state expectations with market imperatives. We theorize how institutional complexity shapes the effectiveness of that signal based on an analysis of corporate philanthropy in China. We argue that stringent state regulations substitute for philanthropic legitimacy-building, while market liberalization enhances its credibility as a voluntary alignment mechanism. In addition, we show how firm ownership conditions these relationships. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) use philanthropy to signal alignment with state goals under regulation, whereas private firms deploy it more flexibly to strengthen their position under market liberalization. Our research advances the literature on corporate philanthropy in three ways. First, we explain how firms leverage philanthropy to navigate tensions between market and state logics in a non-democratic, socialist market economy. Second, we detail how state-based regulations reduce the effectiveness of corporate philanthropy. Third, we uncover how ownership moderates firms’ strategic use of philanthropy to garner legitimacy in complex institutional environments.

Turning Founder Know-How into Organizational Capability: Evidence from Egyptian Family Enterprises

OMT
Maged Elshenawy (City - University of London); Ajay Bhalla; Aneesh Banerjee (Bayes Business School)
Founder-led family businesses rely heavily on founders’ tacit know-how yet struggle to convert it into organizational capability as they professionalize. In this study, we ask how founder know-how crosses family and role boundaries and becomes embedded in routines that endure beyond the founder. Drawing on an inductive study of ten Egyptian founder-led family firms, we show that founder knowledge initially flows through selective, relational exposure that preserves control but creates bottlenecks during succession and growth. Dependence declines when firms develop internal absorptive-capacity routines that allow non-family managers to assimilate founder know-how. These routines are enabled or constrained by trust, participative leadership, and governance choices that balance openness with protection. We synthesize these insights into a stock-and-flow framework that builds on the idea of absorptive capacity in founder-led businesses as an internally directed process of translating founder know-how into organizational capabilities.

Status-Based Theory of Firm Growth: Why High-Status Firms Expand Scope and Raise Prices (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Jusang Lee (Università della Svizzera Italiana)
How do high-status firms grow when the exclusivity that sustains their status appears to constrain expansion? This paper develops a status-based theory of firm growth by arguing that high-status firms do not grow by overcoming exclusivity, but by leveraging it. Because elite consumers have exceptional purchasing power and a heightened willingness to pay for premium offerings, high-status firms can expand revenues by intensifying value extraction within the elite clientele rather than broadening access. I theorize three growth strategies consistent with elite-oriented growth logic: related diversification into complementary offerings, geographic diversification that tracks elite consumers across locations, and growth through margins rather than volume. Using panel data on U.S. law firms from 2012 to 2022, I find that high-status firms prioritize these two forms of diversification and margin expansion, while relying less on volume-based growth through increased case intake or new client acquisition. Together, these findings show that exclusivity, when anchored in elite demand, becomes a basis for growth rather than a constraint.

Organizational Embeddedness and Agency: The Influence of Field Position on Organizational Actions

OMT
Dimitry Jacob; Harsh Kumar Jha; Mark Palmer
This study shows how MNEs holding distinct field positions differ in managing political and economic risks in Emerging Markets. Based on comparative case analysis of nine subsidiaries of foreign pharmaceutical MNEs operating in Russia in the aftermath of the annexation of Crimea from Ukraine in 2014, we identify a typology of strategies with which local subsidiaries counter effects of Western sanctions imposed on the Russian market and the rising economic nationalism in Russia, notably demonstrating corporate loyalty, multinationality straddling, politicking, and leveraging expert status. We further find that MNE subsidiaries holding central, intermediate, and peripheral field positions deploy strategies correspondingly in unique, yet overlapping, combinations: the Dominant expert, the Machiavellian expert, and the Loyalist. The findings of our study contribute to existing literature, illuminating the role of field positions as largely overlooked in understanding organizational institutional agency.

Against the Odds: Rule Disruption and Underdog Victories in Pro Beach Volleyball

OMT
David Lucas (George Mason University)
Competitive advantage depends not only on firm capabilities but also on the institutions that structure competitive interaction. We study how changes to the “rules of the game” can redistribute advantage to enable underdog victories. We exploit a natural experiment in the context of professional beach volleyball, wherein the main U.S. tour introduced a rule that changed endgame scoring—lengthening matches and altering risk payoffs. Leveraging other professional tours as counterfactuals, we find that the rule change increased the incidence of upsets, especially among women’s teams and in tightly contested matches, and enhanced upward mobility for low ranked teams. The findings reveal how institutional design can empower underdogs, highlighting the role of rules in the dynamics of competitive strategy and as drivers of performance heterogeneity.
