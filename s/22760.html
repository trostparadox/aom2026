When and Why Ghosters Re-Engage: An Actor’s Perspective of Workplace Ghosting

OB
Faye Schlake (The University of Connecticut)
Scholars increasingly observe ghosting in workplace communication, yet theory has largely overlooked the behavior from the ghoster’s perspective. In this paper, I situate workplace ghosting within the broader withdrawal literature and advance an actor-centered account of its consequences. Using emotion regulation theory, I argue that two emotional pathways following a ghosting episode—guilt and relief—shape whether the ghoster fully re-engages, partially re-engages, or does not return. Two conditions structure these pathways: an individual’s orientation and task interdependence. Orientation predisposes which emotion emerges following ghosting, whereas task interdependence determines how emotions translate into behavior by shaping demands. I advance propositions suggesting that when task interdependence is high, guilt promotes full re-engagement, while relief promotes partial re-engagement. When task interdependence is low, guilt promotes partial re-engagement, while relief sustains nonreturn. I further theorize feedback loops in which relief paired with nonreturn reinforces future ghosting, whereas guilt paired with full ghostee re-engagement dampens future ghosting. This paper contributes to theory by conceptualizing workplace ghosting as a form of relational withdrawal, clarifying its cyclical dynamics, and foregrounding the actor’s perspective. Finally, I outline an agenda for future research that examines network-level dynamics of workplace ghosting, variation in ghosting intensity, and ghostee responses to re-engagement.

Leaders’ Family Experiences and Employees' Unethical Behavior

OB
Muhammad Usman (National University of Sciences and Technology); Imran Shafique
This study examines how leaders’ experiences of exclusion within their families shape ethical outcomes at work. Drawing on the Conservation of Resources theory and the work–home resources model, we argue that leader family ostracism depletes emotional and cognitive resources. This resource loss increases leaders’ self-serving cognition, which fosters ethical ambiguity among employees and, in turn, encourages unethical behavior. We further examine two boundary conditions. Leader trait resilience weakens the relationship between family ostracism and self-serving cognition, while employee moral awareness attenuates the effect of leader self-serving cognition on employee ethical ambiguity. We test the proposed model using two studies, including an experiment and a multi-source, time-lagged field study. The results support a serial mediation process linking leader family ostracism to employee unethical behavior through leader self-serving cognition and employee ethical ambiguity. The moderation effects are also supported. These findings demonstrate how leaders’ family experiences spill over into the workplace and erode ethical conduct. The study contributes to research on ostracism, leadership ethics, and work– family spillover, and offers practical insights into strengthening leader resilience and employee moral awareness to safeguard ethical behavior in organizations.

Employee Approval and Corporate Misconduct: The Double-Edged Role of Internal Legitimacy

OB
Aqib Hossain (RWTH Aachen University)
Are firms with high or low CEO employee approval more likely to engage in corporate misconduct? This study explains how CEO employee approval shapes corporate misconduct by influencing CEO behavior through two competing forces—constraint and pressure. Building on Tetlock’s social contingency model, we argue that employee approval heightens accountability expectations and curbs misconduct. We test our theory using 1,866 firm-year observations from 2007–2022 on S&P 500 firms with misconduct cases recorded across 50 countries. Our results show that higher CEO approval is associated with lower corporate misconduct. However, this constraining effect is conditional and reverses for female CEOs. In contrast, CEO power amplifies the constraining effect of approval, leading to sharper reductions in misconduct among powerful CEOs. Our findings contribute to research on corporate misconduct by clarifying how internal legitimacy operates as an organizational source of accountability. As employee evaluations become increasingly visible and consequential, our study offers a caution against treating CEO employee approval as a uniformly positive signal.

Always Online, Morally Offline? Work Connectivity Behavior After Hours and Deviant Behavior

OB
Xie Julan (Central South University); Jiyuan Mei (Central South University); Ya Luo (Central South University)
In today’s always-on work environment, employees increasingly engage in work connectivity behavior after-hours (WCBA), responding to emails, messages, and tasks outside formal work hours. While prior research has documented both benefits and costs of WCBA, little is known about its potential influence on employees’ extra-role, unethical behaviors. Drawing on moral licensing theory, we propose that WCBA can be construed as morally commendable, generating moral credits that may license subsequent workplace deviance. We further argue that this process is contingent on ethical judgment (which amplifies moral credit accumulation) and organizational justice (which constrains credit-driven deviance). Across four studies (three experiments and a multi-wave field study), we first establish that WCBA is indeed perceived as morally laudable rather than merely a source of strain and employees with higher ethical judgment are more likely to convert WCBA into moral credits. High moral credits predict deviant behaviors, but only when organizational justice is low. Finally, a three-wave employee-supervisor study confirms the full moderated mediation model in real organizations, ruling out alternative explanations such as fatigue, negative affect, and work-family conflict. This research integrates WCBA and moral licensing literature, offering new insights into how “good deeds” at work may backfire.

When Leader Anger at Incompetence Fuels Subordinate Cheating: Anger at Unethicality as a Moderator

OB
Minjun Yoo (Seoul National University); Jeong Hoon Seol (ROK Naval Academy); Dongman Sun (Seoul National University); Soohyeok Im (Seoul National University); Seokhwa Yun (Seoul National University)
While leader anger toward incompetence is often seen as a performance-enhancing tactic, its potential to induce unethical behavior (i.e. cheating) by influencing subordinates’ moral cognition remains understudied. Moreover, little attention has been paid to how other forms of leader anger (i.e., anger toward unethicality) may simultaneously shape such outcomes. Drawing on Emotions as Social Information (EASI) theory, this study examined the indirect effect of leader anger toward incompetence on cheating via subordinates’ perceived leader moral decoupling, as well as the moderating role of leader anger toward unethicality. Across two time-lagged, multi-source survey studies (South Korea: N = 125; China: N = 196), results showed that leader anger toward incompetence increased subordinate cheating by heightening perceived leader moral decoupling. Moreover, this indirect effect was attenuated when leaders consistently expressed anger not only toward incompetence but also toward unethicality. These findings suggest that leader anger toward incompetence may undermine subordinates’ moral judgment, while also indicating that the unintended unethical consequences of leader anger can be reduced when social information about both performance and morality is provided together.
