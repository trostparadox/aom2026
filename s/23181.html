Meta-Work as a Communicative Practice in the Context of Hybrid Work

CTO
Iida Kukka-Maaria Rekonen (University of Jyväskylä); Anu Sivunen (University of Jyväskylä)
Research on remote and hybrid work has typically focused on work processes and outcomes related to workers who work from a distance to others outside the office. As hybrid work is becoming more salient, this study focuses on hybrid work from the perspective of employees working at the office as well as on the communicative practices they engage in to prepare and enable the hybrid mode of working. We used meta-work as a theoretical concept to analyze data from observations and semi-structured interviews from 15 hybrid workers while they worked at the office at the company headquarters. We found three types of meta-work, visibility work, articulation work, and compensation work, that hybrid workers engage in to facilitate the actual work. The findings expand prior research by demonstrating that hybrid work has changed work at the office and that hybrid workers engage in meta-work to make themselves visible, communicate their availability, and to compensate the shortcomings of hybrid work to enable smooth collaboration with colleagues both on-site and remotely.

Embeddedness, Distributed Voice and Employee Retention in Remote-First Organizations

CTO
Lichen Zhen (Pennsylvania State U); Marlon DeMarcie Twyman (University of Southern California)
Distributed systems used at work, such as Git for software development, play a crucial role in organizational communication through facilitating collaborative project management. In addition to their functions that allow users to complete technical tasks at work, such systems also help create new spaces for employees to channel and integrate their interpersonal communication into work artifacts. These communicative spaces, created intentionally or unintentionally, are particularly important in remote work, where informal conversations are limited. The promising features of these tools raise the question of the extent to which employees are likely to utilize them to exercise their voice within the organization and whether such employee participation brings positive outcomes for organizations. We explore these questions by studying the technology company, GitLab, and its employees’ digital contribution records to their company’s employee handbook hosted on a Git repository. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to predict employee turnover using variables coded from 391,983 contribution records in the handbook. Results show that employees embedded in cohesive subgroups (higher k-core) are less likely to leave, while those with redundant ties (higher constraint) are more likely to leave. Voice behaviors positively predict turnover, and interaction analyses reveal that voice behavior moderates embeddedness: employees with high constraint yet actively voice concerns are more likely to stay than those who remain silent. We call both scholarly and practitioners’ attention to employees’ adoption of distributed collaborative systems in the workplace to communicate opinions and engage in discussions about management issues.

Knowledge Management Challenges in Remote Work: The Threat of Supervision to Knowledge Sharing

CTO
Rui Yan (xinjiang university); Yaxi Zhang (xinjiang university); Yan Meng
Remote work has intensified organizations’ knowledge management challenges, as increased social distance often undermines employees’ willingness to share knowledge. However, little is known about how routine managerial practices in remote work unintentionally create new barriers to knowledge flow. Drawing on the threat–rigidity framework and adopting a defensive motivation perspective, this study investigates how organizational supervision may unintentionally hinder knowledge sharing among remote employees, as well as the boundary conditions of this effect. Using three-wave data from 448 remote administrative employees recruited via an online platform, the results indicate that organizational supervision is a significant antecedent of reduced knowledge sharing, with employee bottom-line mentality serving as a key mediating mechanism. Task visibility is identified as a critical boundary condition. Under low task visibility, organizational supervision elicits a stronger bottom-line mentality, further suppresses knowledge sharing, and amplifies the mediating effect of bottom-line mentality. These findings contribute to the literature by identifying a novel antecedent of inhibited knowledge sharing in remote work, uncovering the defensive motivational mechanism and contextual boundary through which organizational supervision impairs knowledge sharing, and offering important insights into the unintended consequences of managerial control and strategies to mitigate knowledge-sharing decline in remote work contexts.

Can’t Unplug From Work? Right to Disconnect Laws, Firm Profitability and Employee Work-Life Balance

CTO
Mark (Shuai) Ma (University of Pittsburgh); Yuye Ding (University of Pittsburgh); Zhuoying Niu (University of Pittsburgh)
Telecommuting technologies blur boundaries between work and life, prompting many countries to adopt right-to-disconnect (RTD) that allow employees to disengage from work-related communications. Using a sample of firms in OECD countries and a difference-in-differences design, we find a significant increase in firm profitability after RTD laws adoption, driven by higher employee productivity. The impact is moderated by enforcement mechanisms and labor market conditions. Evidence from Glassdoor reviews by Canadian employees further shows improved employee work-life balance after Ontario’s RTD law adoption. Our findings highlight that RTD laws benefit both firms and employees, contributing to policy debates on remote work.

Investigating Increasing Employees Loneliness During a Return to Office Event

CTO
Eric Lawrence Demirjian (Florida Institute of Technology); Jennifer Gibbs (University of California Santa Barbara); Kate A. Hill; Yuxin Jin (University of California Santa Barbara)
Research on post-pandemic work arrangements has increasingly emphasized the organizational challenges associated with accelerated return-to-office (RTO) transitions. This convergent mixed-methods study examines how employees experience the rapid shift from remote and hybrid arrangements to fully in-person work environments, with particular attention to how RTO events impact employee loneliness. Drawing on the collective empirical literature and validated measurement tools, this research investigates why organizations continue to struggle with reducing loneliness despite renewed emphasis on RTO mandates and in-office collaboration. The researchers investigate how RTO events contribute to employee loneliness. Using two waves of survey data from telecommunications employees (January: N1=393; July: N2=367), complemented by qualitative insights, this study reveals that the percentage of lonely employees went up by 9.52% in the six months after an RTO mandate, with lonely employees now outnumbering non-lonely employees. The repeated cross-sectional study identifies key mediators of work status change, including organizational identification, a supportive teaming environment, and a psychologically safe communication climate. The data demonstrate that a change in work status alone is not associated with higher employee loneliness. These findings generate actionable recommendations for organizational leaders to lower employee loneliness during RTO events by strengthening organizational identification, fostering a supportive teaming environment, and promoting a psychologically safe communication climate. By situating employee loneliness within the broader context of post-pandemic organizational change, this article contributes to ongoing research on RTO mandates. The study advances theoretical understanding of workplace loneliness and offers evidence-based guidance for cultivating more connected, resilient, and productive workforces.
