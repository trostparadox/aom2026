Paradox Navigation Across Field Positions: A Case of Environmental Peacebuilding in Israel-Palestine

OMT
Ambreen Ben-Shmuel (University of Michigan); Katharina Hug (Leuphana University of Lüneburg)
Paradox theory has not adequately theorized how field positions shape actors’ experience of tensions and response capacity under structural asymmetries. Integrating Bourdieu’s field theory with paradox theory, we show how actors hold different forms of capital (economic, cultural, social) in varying social fields creating asymmetric but reciprocal dependencies. Cooperation emerges through capital exchange enabled by position-conscious organizational practices. Through five years of ethnographic research in an environmental peacebuilding organization operating across Israel and Palestine, we examine cooperation-separation tensions and identify three position-conscious practices enabling intercommunal organizational work: recognition (creating protected spaces for articulating power asymmetries), multiplicity (enabling divergent interpretations through parallel structures), and distancing (legitimizing temporary withdrawal). These findings advance paradox theory by showing how field positions shape navigation capabilities under power asymmetry, revealing the limits of universal both/and prescriptions in contexts of structural inequality.

From Interpersonal Trust to Interorganizational Trust: The Evolution of a Chemical Industry Alliance

OMT
Ranjay Gulati (Harvard Business School); David Shin (Harvard Business School); Emily Tedards
As global challenges such as climate change increasingly demand collaboration among firms, understanding the mechanisms that sustain and expand interorganizational trust has become imperative for scholars and practitioners alike. Thus, we ask: How does interorganizational trust emerge and become institutionalized over time, such that it can expand as multilateral alliances grow in scale and scope? Building on calls for multi-level, longitudinal, qualitative research, we address our question through a study of Together for Sustainability (TfS), a global chemical industry alliance dedicated to improving supply chain sustainability. Drawing on our analysis of extensive archival materials and interviews with chemical industry professionals involved in TfS, we conceptualize interorganizational trust as a dynamic process that unfolds through a set of four trust institutionalization practices: identity maintenance, deliberative governance, role personalization, and cultivating commitment. In contributing to the literature on interorganizational trust, we specify the processes through which interorganizational trust sustains and grows over time, even as the interpersonal bases of cooperation and the structures through which collaboration unfolds change. We also advance research on the micro-macro dynamics of trust by showing how interpersonal interactions produce outcomes that are consequential for interorganizational trust.

Time Does Not Heal All Wounds: Sustained Nature of Trust Erosion Between Main Street and Wall Street

OMT
Basak Yakis-Douglas; Kerem Gurses; Ferran Giones (University of Stuttgart)
We investigate the persistent nature of public trust gaps in corporate institutions, focusing on the structural conditions that sustain distrust over time. Drawing on public opinion data and 65 interviews with business leaders conducted in five waves between 2011 and 2024, we examine the enduring divergence between societal expectations and corporate perceptions of trustworthiness. Our analysis reveals that while some breaches of trust are amenable to repair, those involving legally permissible but ethically contentious practices such as corporate tax avoidance become entrenched. We introduce a framework that explains how trust deficits persist when organizational priorities such as shareholder value and legal compliance conflict with societal norms, transparency, and social responsibility. This misalignment is especially salient in cases where firms rationalize tax avoidance as financial optimization, while the public views it as a moral failure. The resulting trust gap is not just a communication failure but a reflection of deeper normative disjunctures. By foregrounding controversial practices as key mechanisms in the erosion of public trust, our findings offer new insight into why firms struggle to reconcile their actions. We contribute to ongoing debates on organizational legitimacy by theorizing how trust gaps endure despite reputational risks and reform efforts.

A Theory of System Work: How Multiple Types of Actors Co-Enact Transitions Towards Sustainability

OMT
Natalia Lyly (LUT University); Oana Branzei (Ivey Business School); Laura Albareda (LUT University)
Tracing the transition of an urban mobility system in the capital region of Finland as it is sought to be changed from mobility as a good to mobility as a service, we theorize how different types of actors co-enact system transitions towards sustainability. We abductively develop the concept of system work to explain how the government, public and private actors “work” interdependently and interactively to revise the foundations, structures and substances of this system. Our process model informs the broader puzzle of organized system change by foregrounding the intentional efforts diverse actors make to 1) periodically reinterpret and reconfigure core elements around emergent roles (system foundation work), 2) derive new patterns of inclusion and interaction (system structure work), and 3) dynamically interconnect one another’s positions through a recursive cycle of acting-reacting and escalating (system substance work). Our four-stage model contributes directly to the mid-range literature on system sustainability transitions by defining and describing system work as a form of inter- and multi-actor agency that unfolds “among” rather than “within” actors. We show how these three types of system work create and connect feedforward loops that disrupt the system with feedback loops that periodically “settle” it into tentative and temporary states.

An Aesthetics of Organising Perspective of Co-Creation Encounters

OMT
Marianna Rolbina (Manchester University); Tonny Kukeera (Not Associated)
Co-creation encounters, i.e. episodes where co-creating actors interact with each other, are the building blocks that make up success or failure of the entire co-creation process. Yet our knowledge of encounter dynamics remains fragmented. This conceptual paper uses aesthetics of organising as a lens to review existing insights from the co-creation literature and propose a dynamic model of co-creation encounter. We theorise that co-creation encounters unfold across three stages (preparation, nexus, and closure) and are shaped by systematic shifts of actors’ attention from artefacts toward actors, and finally toward a balanced focus on outcomes and relationships. We further show that these dynamics require the focal firm to shift its role from facilitator to orchestrator to gatekeeper. By articulating testable propositions, the paper advances understanding of how co-creation processes unfolds and provides a process perspective with direct implications for designing and managing effective stakeholder interactions.
