Parent Firm Perceived Institutional Uncertainty and Overseas Subsidiaries Green Innovation (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Shan Wu (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Na Yang (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
Drawing on dynamic capability theory, we examine the impact of parent perceived institutional environment uncertainty on its overseas subsidiaries’ green innovation. We employ natural language processing models, Word2Vec, to construct a firm-level institutional environment uncertainty index. Using a manually collected dataset of US parent firms and their Chinese subsidiaries, we find a positive relationship between parent-perceived institutional environment uncertainty and subsidiaries’ green innovation. Furthermore, the positive relationship is more pronounced when the parent company shows more proactivity and the subsidiaries own more localized experience, whereas asset diversification weakens this positive relationship. Overall, our findings provide insights into understanding the intra-organizational mechanisms through which MNEs translate environmental uncertainty into subsidiary-level strategic responses that are explicitly oriented toward environmental responsibility. Implications for executives of MNEs and policymakers are discussed.

How Firms’ Informal Legacy Influence Environmental Sustainability Practices

IM
Yanhui Yu (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Yaqun Yi (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
Environmental sustainability has become an international management issue, yet firms across countries vary widely in their environmental behaviors. This study investigates how formalized firms with informal legacy influence their environmental sustainability practices. We argue that firms that originated informally but later formalized are more likely to engage in environmental sustainability practices than those that are formally created. Based on the legitimacy perspective and stakeholder theory, we reason that formalized firms with informal legacy (1) suffer from potential environmental legitimacy liability because of their informal origins; and (2) subsequently strive to build environmentally responsible identities after formalization, to restore legitimacy and respond to stakeholder expectations. Using the World Bank’s Enterprise Surveys data on 3,483 firms across 86 countries from 2022 to 2025, we reveal a positive relationship between informal entrepreneurship and firms’ environmental sustainability practices. In international comparisons, this relationship is more pronounced in countries with large environmental-economic discrepancy and among firms that exhibit high export intensity. This study offers novel theoretical insights into informal entrepreneurship and environmental sustainability practices within an international management context.

Parent Firms’ Perceived Institutional Uncertainty and Subsidiary Environmental Impact

IM
Shan Wu (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Na Yang (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
Drawing on dynamic capability theory, we examine how parent firms’ perceived institutional uncertainty is associated with the environmental impact of their overseas subsidiaries. We employ a Word2Vec-based natural language processing approach to construct a firm-level measure of perceived institutional uncertainty from managerial disclosures. Using a manually matched panel dataset of U.S. MNEs and their Chinese subsidiaries from 2011 to 2016, we find that higher levels of parent-perceived institutional uncertainty are associated with lower subsidiary environmental impact, proxied by resource consumption. We further show that this negative relationship is contingent on multi-level conditions. Specifically, the effect is stronger when parent firms exhibit higher proactivity, when subsidiaries operate as non-wholly owned entities, when they are engaged in non-manufacturing industries, and when they are located in regions with higher levels of marketization. Overall, our findings highlight how parent firms’ uncertainty perceptions are systematically associated with heterogeneous environmental consequences in host countries. The study offers implications for multinational executives and policymakers concerned with environmental sustainability under uncertainty.

Coordinating Sustainability in Multinationals: An Interpretive Alignment Perspective

IM
Junghoon Park (Loyola Marymount University); Hyeongjung Kim (Yonsei University); Yeonwoo Lee
Sustainability has become central to multinationals’ strategy and operations as they face increasing pressure to reduce their negative impacts while enhancing positive contributions to society. As sustainability disclosure shifts from voluntary commitment to regulatory mandate, multinationals’ headquarters face the challenge of coordinating environmental, social, and governance (ESG) disclosures across subsidiaries embedded in heterogeneous institutional environments. We argue that rather than viewing cross-border coordination as a technical process of data standardization or compliance enforcement, it needs to be understood as an institutional translation process because ESG concepts are meaning-laden and normatively charged, interpreted differently across institutional contexts. Drawing on institutional translation, we develop a theoretical framework for achieving interpretive alignment on sustainability, organized around four headquarters-level coordination tasks: (1) recognition—diagnosing how subsidiaries interpret ESG concepts differently; (2) translation—jointly interpreting what ESG concepts mean across contexts; (3) communication—building relational infrastructure that enables ongoing interpretation; and (4) institutionalization—embedding interpretive alignment through governance structures. We introduce concepts that capture the distinctive challenges of ESG coordination—interpretive mapping, the standardization trap, the distance-control paradox, and interpretive authority—and identify practical mechanisms including interpretive dialogue, joint interpretation, boundary-spanning roles, polycentric governance, and reverse knowledge flows. Our central insight: the challenge of ESG coordination in multinationals is not merely ensuring subsidiary compliance but achieving shared understanding of what ESG concepts mean across diverse institutional contexts.
