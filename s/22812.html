Formal and Informal Leaders in Organizations: A Nomological Investigation

OB
Michael Paik (Southern Utah University); Gang Wang (Florida State University)
While prior research has focused predominantly on formal leadership, informal leaders—those without formal authority who nevertheless exert influence— also play a critical role in organizational functioning. This study examines the interaction between formal and informal leaders in the context of informal leadership, a relatively underexplored area in the leadership literature. Drawing on person-environment (P-E) fit theory and dominance complementarity theory, we develop and test a model that explains how power value congruence and dominance complementarity between formal and informal leaders may jointly influence interpersonal conflict (i.e., task, process, and relationship conflict) and subsequent outcomes for both formal and informal leaders, as well as teams. Using multi-wave survey data, the findings reveal that power value congruence reduces conflict, and this effect is moderated by the degree of dominance complementarity. This study extends the leadership and conflict literatures by highlighting the unique power dynamics between coexisting formal and informal leaders. It provides an integrative framework for understanding how supplementary and complementary fit influence these interactions and outcomes.

Examining Follower Responses to Needed and Received Servant Leadership from a Fit Perspective

OB
Anthony Perrier (TBS Business School); Assâad El Akremi (Toulouse School of Management); Robert C. Liden (University of Illinois at Chicago); Mathieu Molines (KEDGE Business School)
Extant research on servant leadership predominantly highlights its positive implications for employees and organizations, with substantial empirical evidence linking it to enhanced job performance. Consequently, the dominant assumption within the literature is that employees exhibit uniformly favorable reactions when supervisors demonstrate higher levels of servant leadership. Drawing on the person–environment fit (P–E Fit) perspective, we challenge this assumption by examining how congruence between the level of servant leadership that employees need and that which they perceive from their supervisors predicts variations in task performance, adaptivity, and proactivity. We further explore the underlying mechanisms through which this fit influences employee outcomes. Across two studies (a daily within-person examination and an experiment), results indicate that emotional exhaustion and resource depletion are lower, and that felt trust is higher when received servant leadership fits followers’ needs. We also found that emotional exhaustion, resource depletion, and felt trust mediates the effects of the fit between needed and received servant leadership on followers’ task performance, adaptivity, and proactivity. Implications for theory and practice are discussed.

Just Bragging? When Leader Capitalization Incongruence Triggers Supervisor-Directed CWB

OB
Stefanie Marx-Fleck (Goethe University Frankfurt am Main); Tobias Dennerlein (Purdue University); Jana Kühnel (Goethe University Frankfurt am Main)
Supervisors are often encouraged to share their work-related positive events to inspire and motivate employees, a behavior we define as leader capitalization. Although capitalization research in the realm of intimate relationships highlights benefits of capitalization, emerging research from workplace relationships suggests that such disclosures can backfire. Integrating person-supervisor fit theory and impression management theory, we propose that a misfit (i.e., incongruence) between leader capitalization wanted and received accounts for negative reactions of employees. Specifically, we argue that leader capitalization incongruence elicits perceptions of leader conceit, which, in turn, fuels supervisor-directed counterproductive work behavior. We test our model in a multi-wave field study with N = 369 employees, using polynomial regression and response surface analyses. Results indicate a positive relationship between leader capitalization incongruence and leader conceit. However, contrary to our expectations, over- and undercapitalization do not differentially relate to leader conceit. Moreover, incongruent leader capitalization indirectly increased supervisor-directed CWB through leader conceit.

How Supervisor and Coworker Undermining Incongruence Affects Employee Deviance

OB
Michael S. Cole (Texas Christian University); Shannon G. Taylor (University of Central Florida); Nikos Dimotakis (Oklahoma State University); Artemis Boulamatsi (Texas Christian University); Xin Zhao; Xinxin Lu (Hunan University)
Although studies have shown that employee well-being and work behavior are affected by mistreatment over time, from a single perpetrator, and across targets, there is little research examining the effects of incongruent treatment across sources (i.e., perpetrators). This raises novel and important questions about how and why supervisors and coworkers, as co-conspirators of mistreatment, jointly influence employee well-being and behavior. Drawing on theory and research on ambivalence, we propose that undermining incongruence across sources—i.e., the divergence between the levels of undermining employees experience from their supervisor and coworkers—creates a sense of unsureness that influences employee well-being and behavior. We test our predictions in two field studies. In Study 1, we first demonstrate that the effects of supervisor-coworker undermining incongruence on employee CWB are transmitted by reduced feelings of thriving (or languishing). In Study 2, we extend these findings by explicitly incorporating felt ambivalence as a micro-mechanism linking supervisor-coworker mistreatment incongruence to thriving. Implications for theory and research are discussed.

The Consequences of Supervisor-Subordinate Motivation (In)Congruence

OB
Manish Saini (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Nishtha Arora (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Varun Sharma; Prabhat Kumar (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow)
In this three-wave study, we draw upon self-determination theory and person-environment fit theory and utilize a matched sample of 301 supervisor-subordinate dyads to examine the consequences of congruence and incongruence between supervisor and subordinate motivation for subordinates’ work outcomes (job performance, work engagement, emotional exhaustion, turnover intentions), mediated through their basic psychological need satisfaction and frustration. We find that (in)congruence in motivation has significant and varied consequences for subordinates’ needs and work outcomes, depending on the type (autonomous and controlled) and level (low vs. high) of motivation. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings and advocate that future research examining the consequence of motivation should take supervisors into account.
