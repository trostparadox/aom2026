LGBTQ+ at Work: Visibility, Mistreatment, and Belonging

Session overview

These papers focus on LGBTQ+ employees' experiences of visibility, stigma, microaggressions, identity management, and inclusion. Across studies of coping, meta-stereotypes, concealment motives, same-gender relationships, and sexual minority leadership appointments, the session highlights the complex dynamics of belonging and exposure at work. Together, the papers deepen the understanding of sexual orientation as a consequential dimension of organizational diversity.

Stand Up for Self: The Coping Strategies of Microaggressions & Identity Threat among LGBTQ Employee

DEI
Chien-Chung (Jordan) Kao (National Sun Yat-Sen University)
Previous research on identity management strategies has primarily focused on whether employees conceal or reveal their stigmatized identities. However, minority individuals may leverage their social identities as an extension of coping strategies, further, prior studies remain unclear how LGBTQ employees cope with everyday microaggressions and through what psychological mechanisms these coping responses emerge. In Study 1, data were collected from 212 LGBTQ employees using the critical incident technique to capture their responses to workplace microaggressions. Exploratory factor analysis identified nine distinct coping dimensions organized into two higher-order constructs: approach and avoidance. In Study 2, a three-wave survey of 208 LGBTQ employees was conducted to examine the underlying psychological processes. Relying on stigma-induced identity threat model, the results demonstrate that identity threat serves as a key mediator linking microaggressions to both approach and avoidance coping behaviors. Moreover, LGBTQ policy function as an important boundary condition the relationship between microaggressions and identity threat. These findings contribute to research on microaggressions, identity threat, and coping strategies, and offer practical implications for organizations seeking to foster more inclusive and supportive work environments.

Measuring Sexual Orientation Meta-Stereotypes in Organizations: Scale Development and Validation

DEI
Tunahan Sari (University of Groningen); Ngoc Han Nguyen (University of Groningen); Hendrik Johan Van De Brake (University of Groningen); Laetitia Mulder
Meta-stereotypes are individuals’ stereotypical beliefs about how others view them. These beliefs may play an important role in shaping social interactions within organizations. Yet previous research has not fully addressed how to empirically measure these beliefs, particularly regarding sexual orientation. This paper develops and validates sexual-orientation-related meta-stereotype measures for heterosexual, bisexual, and gay employees in organizational contexts. Across three studies (N = 1,136), we developed psychometrically reliable scales capturing both negative and positive meta-stereotypes for underrepresented (LGB) and majority (heterosexual) employees. In Study 1, we identified the most common and recurrent meta-stereotypes for all groups. In Study 2, we conducted exploratory factor analyses, which revealed two clusters of meta-stereotypes: negative and positive. In Study 3a, we tested convergent validity and conducted confirmatory factor analysis, and in Study 3b, we examined predictive validity. Taken together, these studies resulted in three validated and reliable measures tailored to each group. This work advances the meta-stereotype literature by simultaneously considering both negative and positive perceptions across different sexual orientation groups. It also provides researchers with empirical tools for future investigation of intergroup dynamics and offers practical implications for organizations seeking to foster more inclusive workplaces.

Cost of Vigilance: Concealment Motive Linking Heterosexist Mistreatment to Engagement and Turnover

DEI
Chris Zhang (University of Houston-Downtown); Shujie Zhang (York University); Eva (Binhua) Huang (Algoma University); Xinyao Zhou (Ontario Tech University); Linli Wang
As a group with concealable stigmatized identities, sexual minority employees often face workplace heterosexist mistreatment with important consequences for work engagement and turnover intention, yet the psychological mechanisms through which such mistreatment exerts its effects and the structural conditions that can attenuate these harms remain underspecified. Drawing on Minority Stress Theory and the Psychological Mediation Framework, we argue that a central cost of mistreatment is the vigilance it elicits and the vigilance-driven concealment motive it activates: a self-protective goal orientation marked by persistent anticipatory monitoring and prioritization of safety in identity regulation. We theorize two distinct behavioral pathways through which this motive operates: reduced revealing behaviors (less proactive authenticity at work) and intensified concealing (more active information management), and we examine whether their downstream effects are asymmetric. We further identify task interdependence as a job-design buffer that weakens the mistreatment– motive link by fostering necessary collaboration. Using three-wave data from 447 LGB employees in China and structural equation modeling, we find that heterosexist mistreatment is associated with stronger vigilance-driven concealment motives, which in turn predict both reduced revealing and intensified concealing. Critically, however, only reduced revealing carries the indirect association with lower engagement and higher turnover intention; parallel paths via concealing were not significant. Task interdependence buffered the mistreatment–motive association, weakening the indirect effect on engagement (with weaker evidence for turnover intention). These findings suggest that the harms of mistreatment arise less from concealment acts than from sustained vigilance and foregone opportunities to build authenticity-based resources at work, highlighting task interdependence as a tangible lever for mitigating such harms.

Work-Family Integration Among Employees in Same Gender Presenting Romantic Relationships

DEI
Joseph Regina (Rutgers University-Camden); Tammy D. Allen (University of South Florida); Soner Dumani (Human Resources Research Organization)
Work-family scholarship has long examined how individuals navigate their professional and personal lives, focusing largely on heterosexual employees. However, recent research and minority stress theory suggest LGBTQIA+ individuals experience unique challenges related to integrating one’s romantic partner into their work life. Building on this and informed by signaling theory, we examine perceived availability of work-romantic partner integration supplies (WRPIS), or the degree individuals perceive it permissible to integrate their partner into their work environment. We posit formal and informal signals of LGBTQIA+ inclusion as predictors of WRPIS, and job satisfaction, romantic relationship satisfaction, and emotional exhaustion as outcomes of WRPIS; moreover, one’s preference for work-family integration was posited as a moderator of the latter relationships. Hypotheses were tested using a three-timepoint survey with a sample of 172 full-time employed individuals who were involved in a committed same gender presenting romantic relationship. Results support informal signals of inclusion relate to WRPIS and WRPIS relates to romantic relationship satisfaction. This suggests greater perceptions of the opportunity to integrate one’s romantic partner into their work life is beneficial for this population. Implications for how formal organizational policies and informal organizational norms can impact work-family management, careers, and wellbeing among LGBTQIA+ employees are discussed.
