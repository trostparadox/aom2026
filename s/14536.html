Session overview

Leader–member exchange (LMX) has long been recognized as one of the most influential relationship-based leadership theories, capturing how differentiated leader–follower relationships shape employee attitudes, behaviors, and performance (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995; Liden, Sparrowe, & Wayne, 1997). While decades of work have shown that LMX quality matters for a wide range of individual and team outcomes, emerging research increasingly reveals that LMX operates within broader social environments, comparative frames, and multi-level relational contexts. The main goal of this symposium is to advance this next generation of LMX scholarship by showcasing research that moves beyond simple leader–follower dyadic quality to examine how LMX is interpreted, compared, signaled, and embedded within the social systems employees inhabit. Taken together, these papers highlight that the meaning and impact of LMX depend not simply on its absolute quality, but on who is comparing, to whom, and under what social context, offering a more dynamic and socially embedded perspective on leader–member relationships. The symposium begins with a brief introduction outlining the goals of the session and format, followed by presentations of five papers highly relevant to this evolving perspective. Paper 1 reveals why leaders and followers so frequently fail to “see eye to eye” about their relationship quality, identifying biased assimilation and attitude extremity as key roots of LMX disagreement. Paper 2 extends the LMX lens outward, demonstrating how leaders’ relationships both with followers (LMX) and with their own supervisors (LLX) convey status signals that shape group identification and discretionary behaviors through respect and pride. Paper 3 examines how LMX serves as a powerful third-party signal that determines whether employees’ attempts to influence peers—using dominance or prestige strategies—are accepted, rejected, or sustained. Paper 4 shows that employees view LMX differentiation as fair only at moderate levels—neither too low nor too high—with such merit-based perceptions boosting performance, especially when leaders clearly see members’ contributions. Paper 5 expands the comparative boundaries of LMX by showing that employees evaluate their standing not only relative to coworkers inside their team but also against peers in other teams, with internal–external congruence shaping emotions and downstream workplace behaviors. Finally, the symposium will conclude with a summary and discussion by Dr. Fadel Matta, a distinguished scholar widely recognized for his influential contributions to LMX research and leader–member relationship dynamics.
