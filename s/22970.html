Going into Overdrive? Structural Positions and Prosocial Behavior of Reviewers in Online Communities

OMT
Yeaji Kim (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Olga Khessina (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Samira Reis (Carlos III University of Madrid)
By having become a popular repository and reference on product evaluations, online product review communities play an increasingly central role in shaping consumer purchasing decisions and, as a result, impact organizational performance. While the literature has established that characteristics of products, producers, and reviewers all shape product evaluations in online communities, it has treated reviewers as “undersocialized” or structurally independent evaluators. Yet, online review communities tend to develop social networks in which reviewers are structurally embedded. In this paper, we develop a theory explaining how a structural position of a reviewer in a community’s social network shapes their reviewing behavior. We argue that as a reviewer’s centrality in the community’s social network rises up to a threshold, driven by increasing social pressures the reviewer starts writing more product reviews that highlight values prioritized by the community but decreases commitment to this behavior. The dynamics reverses past a threshold when a reviewer’s centrality becomes very high, and they acquire autonomy to resist pressures to engage in value-based reviewing, but when they do choose to do it, they become more committed to it. This dynamics should be more pronounced in those local network clusters in a community that have stronger value-orientation. We find support to our theory in longitudinal analyses of all reviews posted by all users in the online tea review community Steepster.com, 2009-2022. These findings show that evaluation processes are shaped by users’ embeddedness in online community networks, highlighting how structural position shapes online value-based reviewing.

The Long Shadow of Brokerage: How Brokers Recreate Advantages Through Effective Communication

OMT
Dana Jongyoun Baek (Not Associated)
Does the advantage of brokerage survive the loss of the network? While structuralist theory suggests brokerage benefits vanish when individuals leave their positions, we argue that the social capital of brokerage can transform into human capital —thereby conferring advantages to brokers independent of continued access to their existing social contacts. Specifically, we propose that individuals who bridge structural holes adopt a distinct conversational style: they explicitly inquire about the counterpart’s network ties, which helps them access resources more efficiently in unfamiliar contexts. Testing this through a randomized, anonymized network simulation that strips participants of their actual ties, we find that real-world brokers utilize network awareness communication style (i.e., explicit referencing of third-party connections) to find information and allocate resources significantly more effectively than non-brokers. Our findings demonstrate that individuals’ positions in the social structure carry important differences in communication effectiveness that persists across contexts. We contribute to network theory by decoupling structure from agency to reveal communication style as an important source of network advantage. We conclude by discussing the implications of how brokerage positions and specific communication styles mutually reinforce each other and shape portable network advantage.

Triadic Closure as a Tug of War Between Brokers and Alters

OMT
Xiaoou Bai (Georgia State University); Wei Xia (Rutgers Business School); Florian Ellsaesser; Eric W. K. Tsang (The University of Texas at Dallas)
Research on triads (consisting of one broker and two alter firms), which are building units of networks, adopts either a broker-centric or an alter-centric perspective, yielding inconsistent findings about triadic closure. We propose a firm-status-based framework that jointly models broker status-driven and alter status-driven mechanisms. We treat status as a signal of output quality grounded in capabilities. Using computational modeling, we study closure probabilities across all possible open triad configurations, whereby the broker firm and the two alter firms may each be high or low status. When the status– capability correlation becomes moderately strong (i.e., stronger than about 0.43), triads with a high-status broker and two high-status alters (HHH) are most likely to remain open, whereas those with a low-status broker and two high-status alters (LHH) are most likely to close.

Relational Appreciation in the Workplace: Conceptualizing a New Type of Network Tie

OMT
David Bender (Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg); Julia Brennecke (Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg); James Coutinho (University of Melbourne)
Prior research has primarily examined appreciation as a feeling that individuals hold and studied its positive consequences for employees and organizations. This approach overlooks its interpersonal nature: appreciation is a directed exchange between an appreciator and an appreciatee. In this article, we conceptualize relational appreciation as a directed network tie. We argue that appreciation ties comprise affective, behavioral, and cognitive dimensions that are not necessarily all present at the same time. We take the smallest unit of a network, the dyad, and examine how appreciation is negotiated between partners to create a stable relationship in the workplace. Moving beyond the dyad, we draw on social network theory and argue that the emergence of appreciation ties is shaped by structuring principles such as reciprocity, multiplexity, preferential attachment and clustering that lead to distinct patterns characterizing appreciation networks. These patterns and the principles behind them explain how appreciation is distributed across organizational members, who becomes appreciated or may be overlooked, and how informal hierarchies of appreciation emerge. Our study makes two contributions: (1) we advance a unified concept of relational appreciation that integrates fragmented definitions from prior research into a multidimensional conceptualization, and (2) we develop a network perspective that explains not only how appreciation operates, but also where it comes from and how it structures organizational life.

Family-Professional Coalitions and Career Advancement in Family Business Groups

OMT
Sea Jin Chang; Young-Choon Kim (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology); Sangchan Park (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology)
This study examines how coalition participation shapes career outcomes in professionalized family firms, where formal managerial hierarchies coexist with enduring family influence. Integrating family business research with coalition theory, we reconceptualize family-professional relations as inter-group coalitions whose career value depends on the bases of coalition ties and partners’ positional attributes, rather than as fixed role conflicts or unilateral family dominance. Using longitudinal panel data on executives from Korea’s large business groups, we show that professional managers’ careers are primarily advanced through task-based, co-working coalitions with family members, which robustly predict promotion across ranks, especially when ties involve higher-positional-power and older-generation family actors. In contrast, family managers’ advancement relies more heavily on socially embedded coalitions, such as college-based ties with professional managers, and on co-working ties with same-generation professionals at higher ranks. Moreover, family disputes selectively amplify the career returns to coalition participation for professional managers, while leaving family managers’ coalition benefits largely unchanged. These findings reframe family-professional relations as dynamic coalitions under dual authority and extend coalition theory by clarifying when inter-group coalition ties translate into observable career outcomes.
