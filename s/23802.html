Protecting Work Engagement Amid Insecurity: Organizational Support vs. Institutional Trust

OB
Milton Paredes (Escuela Superior Politécnica del Litoral); Andrea Alatamirano-Estrella; Kety Jauregui; Raul Barriga Medina
Public insecurity represents an increasingly salient extra-organizational context that may shape employees’ motivation to work. Drawing on the Conservation of Resources theory and the Job Demands–Resources model, this study examines whether security-related resources originating inside organizations (perceived organizational support) or outside organizations (trust in security institutions) are more closely associated with work engagement under acute insecurity salience. We distinguish between affective threat appraisals (fear of personal harm) and cognitive threat appraisals (perceived risk of victimization) and assess whether these relationships vary by work modality. Survey data were collected in Ecuador immediately after a highly visible security incident occurred. Using PLS-SEM, the results show that organizational support is the strongest positive correlate of work engagement, while trust in security institutions also contributes positively. Fear of personal harm is negatively associated with engagement, whereas the perceived risk of victimization shows no direct effect. Moderation and multigroup analyses indicate that these relationships are more pronounced among face-to-face workers than among remote or hybrid workers. The findings highlight the primacy of organizational support as a proximal resource for sustaining engagement in insecure environments.
