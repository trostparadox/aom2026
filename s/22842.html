Who Has (No) Close Friends at Work? Workplace Friendship Profiles from a Social Convoy Perspective

OB
Konstanze Arnoldussen (Friedrich-Alexander University of Erlangen-Nuremberg); Mark Stemmler (Friedrich-Alexander University of Erlangen-Nuremberg); Sabine Hommelhoff (LMU Munich School of Management)
Workplace friendships have attracted growing research and media attention. This study adds a long-term, person-centered perspective, framed by the social convoy model, which addresses close personal networks across life. Using panel data from over 4,000 employees (2018–2022), we conducted latent profile and transition analyses. Five profiles emerged—coined the work-oriented, moderate, disconnected from work, socially connected, and fewest friends— differing in the number of close friends overall and at work, as well as in job satisfaction and sense of accomplishment. Overall, close workplace friends were rare. For example, the work-oriented profile, the largest and most stable (over two thirds of employees), had about four close friends in general, few or no close workplace friends, high levels of accomplishment, job satisfaction, and also well-being and health a year later. A small socially connected profile (around 4%) reported three to four close workplace friends and similar subsequent well-being and health. The smallest profile, fewest friends, showed the least favorable outcomes and fewest friendships overall. No profile combined many close workplace friends with poor outcomes. Further analyses indicated that both job and private life changes may impact profile transitions. We interpret findings through the lens of the convoy model and discuss implications.

Task Conflict and Employee Behaviour: A Cognitive Perspective

OB
Amna Yousaf (Swinburne University of Technology)
The current research contributes to our understanding of why and how task conflict leads to improved task performance of employees. We propose an alternative framework of exploratory learning as a mediation mechanism to understand what happens to employees' cognition when faced with task conflict and how the nuanced link between task conflict and employee’s task performance is executed. Furthermore, we examine the role of employees' cognitive flexibility and argue that it interacts with task conflict to influence employees’ exploratory learning, which subsequently leads to positive employee’s task performance. : Collecting multi-source, multiperiod data in three waves from a random sample of 412 employees and their 39 line managers from a large bank located in Pakistan, we tested the relationships using hierarchical linear modelling. The study found that task conflict was positively related to employees' task performance, and this relationship was mediated by exploratory learning. Furthermore, cognitive flexibility moderated the influence of task conflict on task performance mediated by exploratory learning, such that the relationship became stronger when cognitive flexibility was high. The research contributes to the literature on conflict management by integrating literature from employee learning and cognitive perspective which has been unexplored so far.

To Accept or Deflect Praise? Examining the Social Consequences of Compliment Responses

OB
Emily Prinsloo (Rice University); Alice Moon (Georgetown University)
Compliments can benefit recipients, givers, and their relationships, but responding to them can be surprisingly complex. Across four preregistered studies (N = 2,056 adults online and in the lab), we demonstrated that deflecting (vs. accepting) compliments reliably produced negative consequences for how recipients were perceived. Though deflecting had mixed effects on perceived humility relative to accepting—at times, decreasing, increasing, or having no effect on humility)—recipients who deflected (vs. accepted) compliments were consistently seen as less warm, less competent, and less likable both across recalled real interactions (Study 1) and controlled scenarios (Studies 2-3). These effects were mediated by lower perceived relational receptivity: Compliment givers saw recipients as less aligned with their positive view, which in turn led to these negative social evaluations (Study 3). Subsequently, these negative social perceptions resulted in behavioral consequences: Recipients who deflected were less likely to be chosen as future work partners relative to those who accepted (Study 4). Together, these findings reveal the negative social consequences of deflecting praise and identify relational receptivity as a key mechanism through which everyday conversational responses shape social evaluation.

Relational Energy In Social Context: A Socially Dynamic, Embedded, And Comparative Phenomenon

OB
Alexis Lee (University of Mississippi); Kristin Cullen-Lester (University of Mississippi)
Relational energy is a valuable psychological resource generated through workplace interactions, yet existing theory largely treats it as stable, dyadic, and isolated in the broader social context. These assumptions limit understanding of why relational energy fluctuates across interactions, why similar exchanges produce different experiences and outcomes across employees, and when energizing interactions extend beyond focal relationships to shape collective consequences. In this paper, we introduce a socially situated framework that repositions relational energy as a dynamic, embedded, and comparative phenomenon. We identify three lenses on social context - relational history, relational embeddedness, and relational signals - that together explain how relational energy is experienced and interpreted. The relational history lens highlights how prior interactions shape the experience and consequences of relational energy over time. The relational embeddedness lens explains when and how relational energy extends beyond dyads through patterns of connection. The relational signals lens clarifies how employees interpret relational energy comparatively within shared social environments. By integrating these lenses, our framework moves relational energy research beyond static, dyadic, and absolute accounts and provides a more precise explanation of when and why relational energy varies, circulates, and matters for individual and collective outcomes at work.
