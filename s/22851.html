Inclusive Leadership and Employee Voice

OB
Ali Ahmad (Radboud University Nijmegen); Muhammad Sarwar (Not Associated); Merium Naz; Rabeea Ishaq (Riphah International University)
While previous research has identified a wide range of mediators and explanations for the relationship between inclusive leadership and employee voice, the overall picture remains fragmented. Studies rely on different conceptual frameworks without a unifying theoretical lens. To address the fragmentation in the current literature, the present study aimed to identify the key mechanisms that explain the relationship between inclusive leadership and employee voice. Drawing on Morrison’s (2014) framework, it was theorized that inclusive leadership promotes employee voice through two distinct pathways: prosocial motivation and the expected utility calculus. These mechanisms were represented in this study by felt responsibility for constructive change (FRCC) and psychological safety, respectively. Using cross-sectional survey data from 197 employees in the Netherlands, the results confirm that inclusive leadership is positively related to both promotive and prohibitive voice. Furthermore, both FRCC and psychological safety significantly mediate these relationships. These findings provide a straightforward yet comprehensive explanation for why inclusive leadership fosters employee voice.

When Empowering Leadership Backfires

OB
Michael S. Cole (Texas Christian University); Shannon G. Taylor (University of Central Florida); Min Z. Carter (Southern Illinois University, Carbondale); Xin Zhao
Empowering leadership is widely regarded as a uniformly beneficial influence strategy. This assumption has, however, recently been challenged by emerging research suggesting that empowering leadership is more likely a “mixed blessing.” As this is still a relatively novel idea, we seek to more fully understand how, when, and why empowering leadership can backfire. We offer theoretical refinement by distinguishing two interdependent facets of empowering leadership as experienced by followers: (a) the frequency of empowering behaviors and (b) the perceived authenticity of those behaviors. This blend of empowering and authentic behavior allows for meaningful conceptual differences in empowering leadership that is perceived as genuine versus inauthentic. Drawing on expectancy violations theory, we propose that incongruence between frequency and authenticity—whether empowering behaviors are authentic but infrequent or frequent but inauthentic—elicits follower paranoia. In turn, paranoia heightens follower silence, which subsequently fosters follower cheating behavior and leader abuse. Findings from a time-lagged field study support our predictions. We discuss implications for theory, practice, and future research on empowering leadership and its perceived authenticity.

How Paternalistic Leadership Transforms Team Conflict into Team Performance via Team Voice

OB
ChungJen Chien; Thi Hao Nguyen (Yuan Ze University); Emil Velinov (RISEBA University of Applied Sciences)
While diversity is heralded as a key driver of team innovation, it inevitably breeds conflict. In the Asian context, characterized by high power distance and relational harmony, transforming this conflict into performance presents a "double-bind" paradox: the interpersonal risk of speaking up (silence) and the coordination costs of diverse opinions (inefficiency). Drawing on the indigenous theory of Paternalistic Leadership (PL), we propose a team-level moderated mediation model to resolve this paradox. We argue that Benevolent Leadership (BL) and Authoritarian Leadership (AL)—often viewed as contradictory— function sequentially as a "safety valve" and a "structuring force." Analyzing multi-source data from 92 retail teams in Taiwan, we find that BL facilitates the transformation of task conflict into team voice by fostering psychological safety (Input Stage), while AL amplifies the translation of voice into team performance by providing execution structure (Output Stage). The indirect effect of conflict on performance is strongest when leaders exhibit a "dual-high" style. This study contributes to conflict theory and indigenous leadership research by advancing a processual, functional view of the complementarity between benevolence and authority, offering a culturally nuanced solution to the conflict-performance puzzle.

Voice as a Mirror: How Subordinate Voice Shapes Inclusive Leadership via Supervisor Self-Reflection

OB
Yiyong Zhou; Lei Lu (Peking University); Shiyao Jiang (Nankai University); HAO NENG (Peking University)
While inclusive leadership is critical for organizational success, the impact of subordinates' behaviors on supervisors' inclusive leadership remains underexplored. Drawing on followership framework and social information processing theory, this study employs a mixed-method approach, including two vignette experiments (i.e., Study 1a, n = 193; Study 1b, n = 191) and a three-wave field survey (Study 2) with data collected monthly from 400 supervisor-subordinate dyads. The findings reveal distinct curvilinear mechanisms: subordinates’ promotive voice exhibits an inverted J-shaped effect (diminishing returns), while prohibitive voice exhibits a J-shaped effect (accelerating returns) on supervisors’ inclusive leadership via supervisor self-reflection. Furthermore, we find that supervisor comparison orientation specifically moderates the impact of prohibitive voice: high comparison orientation attenuates the accelerating benefits of prohibitive voice on self-reflection, whereas low comparison orientation strengthens them. Theoretical and practical implications regarding the complex, bottom-up dynamics of leadership development are discussed.

When External Brokerage Meets Internal Control: The Downside of Leader Boundary Spanning on Voice

OB
Yejin Jeon (Seoul National University); Sunghyuck (Ryan) (Ryan) Mah (Seoul National University)
Prior research on leader boundary spanning has primarily emphasized its role in facilitating access to external resources and its associated positive outcomes. However, little is known about its potential downsides. As leaders engage more extensively in boundary spanning, the concentration of resources in the leader may strengthen leader power, raising concerns that subordinates become overly dependent on it. Building on power-dependence theory, we propose that leader boundary spanning can inadvertently increase subordinates’ dependent decision making, which in turn diminishes constructive upward voice. We further argue that this process is amplified by power-oriented internal leader behaviors, particularly leader close monitoring. Using three-wave, multi-source data from 129 leader-subordinate dyads in Korea, we found that leader boundary spanning increased subordinates’ dependent decision making only under high levels of close monitoring, and that dependent decision making was negatively associated with voice. Together, these findings highlight that while leaders may span boundaries to secure valuable resources, how those resources are managed internally is critical for preventing excessive subordinate dependence.
