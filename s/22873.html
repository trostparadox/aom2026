Symbolic Voice Solicitation as a Hierarchy-Maintenance Strategy

OB
Adrienne Mishel Kafka (Duke University); Aaron Kay (Duke University)
The management literature largely portrays employee voice, or upward communication of input intended to improve organizational functioning, as central to organizational effectiveness. Implicit in this view is the assumption that managerial solicitation of voice is itself predominantly prosocial and beneficial. In this paper, we complicate this assumption by showing that voice solicitation can operate both as a tool for inclusion and improvement and as a mechanism for preserving the status quo. Drawing on research on hierarchy maintenance, we propose that managers may use symbolic voice solicitation—requesting input with no genuine intention of using it, but with ulterior motives such as pacification—as a hierarchy-preserving strategy that reduces resistance through signals that employees are heard and the system is fair. We argue that symbolic voice solicitation is theoretically distinct and empirically distinguishable from authentic voice solicitation, which reflects genuine intent to consider employee input, as a function of distinct underlying drivers. Four studies support our theorizing. Study 1 provides descriptive evidence that symbolic voice solicitation is a construct managers resonate with. Studies 2-4 provide complementary correlational and causal evidence that two well-established drivers of hierarchy-maintenance motives—one ideological (social dominance orientation) and one situational (hierarchy fragility)—differentially predict symbolic versus authentic voice solicitation. Across these studies, symbolic voice solicitation was positively predicted by both social dominance orientation and hierarchy fragility, whereas authentic voice solicitation showed opposite or null associations with these variables. Our findings highlight the importance of asking not only whether leaders invite input, but why they do so.

Unveiling the Impacts of Territorial Behaviors on Job Performance

OB
Shen Ye (University of Electronic Science and Technology of China); Lu Chen (University of Electronic Science and Technology of China); Yuanmei (Elly) Qu (Rowan University); Song Zhang (University of Electronic Science and Technology of China)
Drawing on costly signaling theory and the prestige/dominance framework of social rank, we show that territory can be regarded as social resource (e.g., social status). In particular, territorial expanding helps enhance actor’s prestige status, thereby promoting interaction and communication with colleagues, fostering workplace friendship, and ultimately contributing to improved job performance. Conversely, territorial defending demonstrates evident exclusionary and defensive tendencies, thus leading to dominance status. Dominance status further impedes the formation of workplace friendship, which in turn negatively affects job performance. We examined these hypotheses in a scenario experimental study and a multisource, time-lagged field survey. Both studies indicated that the positive relationship of territorial expanding with job performance was sequentially mediated by prestige status and workplace friendship, and the negative relationship of territorial defending with job performance was sequentially mediated by dominance status and workplace friendship. The second study further showed that perceived team competitive climate moderated the aforementioned serial mediations by attenuating the positive indirect effect of territorial expanding and amplifying the negative indirect effect of territorial defending. These findings offer both theoretical and practical implications.

Authenticity Matters:The Interplay of Virtue, Competence, and Dominance in Shaping Taking Charge

OB
Rui Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Mengting Zhu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Feng Bai (University of Macau); Xingshan Zheng (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
In today’s dynamic business environment, organizations rely on employees to take charge—voluntarily challenging routines and implementing constructive change. Because high status employees possess visibility and influence, they are often expected to spearhead such efforts. Yet prior research offers mixed insights: some suggests status motivates initiative, while others argue it restrains risk taking. To resolve this puzzle, we distinguish among three routes to status—virtue, competence, and dominance—and theorize their distinct intrapersonal consequences. Building on status attainment theory, we propose that these routes jointly shape employees’ sense of authenticity, which in turn determines their willingness to take charge. In a multi source, multi wave field study conducted in China, we find that competence based status interacts with virtue and dominance based status to enhance authenticity and, consequently, taking charge behavior. A preregistered experiment with a U.S. sample further establishes the causal role of experienced authenticity in motivating constructive change. By revealing how virtue , competence , and dominance based status jointly convert social standing into self congruence and proactivity, this research links status attainment to the psychological foundations of constructive change and advances understanding of when—and why—high status employees take charge.

Who Experiences Greater Job Insecurity in Collaborative Task Automation? The Role of Perceived Power

OB
Li Ji (Zhejiang University); Yanran Fang (Zhejiang University); Yingjie Qian (Zhejiang University); Junqi Shi (Zhejiang University)
The introduction of organizational automation technologies is profoundly reshaping how employees collaborate on task execution. Drawing on cognitive appraisal theory of stress, we propose that collaborative task automation increases employees’ job insecurity, with perceived power as a key boundary condition. Using a three-wave survey data from 3,226 employees in China, we found that collaborative task automation was positively associated with job insecurity, and this association was stronger for employees with higher perceived power. Social identity characteristics, including age, team tenure, gender, and migrant status, indirectly moderated this relationship by shaping employees’ perceived power. In addition, job insecurity brought by collaborative task automation was positively related to work stress and turnover intention. Our research offers implications for understanding the impact of task automation from the perspective of collaboration and uncovers the differential effects of collaborative task automation on job insecurity across employee groups with varying levels of perceived power.
