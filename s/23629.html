Making Open Strategy Work: Turning Symbolic Inclusion into Participatory Action

MC
María-Ángeles Rastrollo-Horrillo (University of Málaga); Françoise Goter-Grivot
This article examines how organizations can turn employee involvement in strategy from sy into action. Many firms now invite employees into strategic conversations, yet implementation often remains top-down and opaque. Drawing on ten years of socio-economic intervention-research experience conducted across 25 medium-sized European firms—illustrated through two longitudinal cases— we develop an evidence-based understanding of how negotiated participation connects everyday work with strategic objectives. Based on intervention-research settings in which organizational dysfunctions are diagnosed, solutions are implemented, and their effects observed over time, we identify a set of practices that transform openness into coordinated action through an Open Strategy Implementation Cycle. The article shifts attention from who participates to how participation is organized, paced, and sustained through structured intervention mechanisms. Beyond its theoretical contribution to open strategy, the study offers actionable insights for managers and management consultants by providing a process-oriented roadmap for turning multi-voiced strategic reflection into routinized and inclusive implementation.

Freedom at a Cost: Strategic Autonomy in Turbulence

MC
Dexi Zheng; Andy Yu (University of Wisconsin-Whitewater); Hsing-Er Lin (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Chia-Ying Li (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Chung-Hsuan Li (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Jau-Rong Chen (Ming Chi University of Technology); Yi-Ching Liaw (Ming Chi University of Technology)
Strategic autonomy, as a firm’s discretion over its goals and strategic choices, enables managers to pursue business opportunities and adapt to changing environments. By granting greater decision-making latitude, autonomy shapes how firms allocate resources and respond to uncertainty. Despite its importance, however, empirical evidence on the performance implications of strategic autonomy remains limited, particularly during severe environmental disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Addressing this gap, we examine how strategic autonomy interacts with internal and external contextual factors —specifically potential slack and environmental dynamism—to influence firm performance. Using a longitudinal design and analyses of 170 publicly listed small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in Taiwan from 2018 to 2023, we apply computer-aided text analysis (CATA) to shareholder letters to construct firm-level measures of autonomy, yielding 991 firm-year observations. Our findings reveal that strategic autonomy is negatively associated with firm performance during the pandemic period. However, this negative effect is mitigated when firms possess greater potential slack, suggesting that internal resource buffers enable autonomous decision making to translate into performance gains. In contrast, heightened environmental dynamism amplifies the performance costs of autonomy. Overall, the results highlight the contingent nature of strategic autonomy and the critical role of internal and external conditions in shaping its performance consequences under extreme uncertainty. Keywords: Autonomy, Resource, Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises, Dynamism, CATA

A Practice-Based Study of Portfolio Governance Work: Senior Managers’ Perspectives

MC
Mariano Garrido-Lopez (Western Carolina University); Véronique Ambrosini (Monash University); Yue Cai Hillon (Western Carolina University)
Project Portfolio Management (PPM) is widely recognized as a central mechanism for strategy implementation in organizations. Senior managers play a critical role in project portfolio governance, yet we still lack rich empirical accounts of how they understand and enact portfolio governance work in practice. Drawing on the Strategy-as-Practice (SAP) perspective, this study adopts an interpretive research approach known as phenomenography to examine how senior managers conceive of portfolio governance work (within PPM) and how these understandings shape its enactment. Analysis of interviews with 32 senior managers across a range of industries revealed four qualitatively different conceptions of project portfolio governance work, each characterized by distinct dimensions through which this work is understood and enacted. The findings show that variation in portfolio governance work is grounded in differences in how the work itself is understood, rather than in differences in individual attributes. From an SAP perspective, these conceptions shape how senior managers mobilize practices in episodes of portfolio governance praxis, giving rise to both recursive and adaptive enactments of portfolio governance work. This study contributes to the PPM literature by reconceptualizing portfolio governance as a practice-based accomplishment emerging from the interaction of practitioners, practices, and praxis. It also contributes to SAP research by demonstrating the value of phenomenography for capturing variation in how strategizing work is understood and enacted in organizational settings.

Shaping Project Culture Through Vendor Engagement Readiness: Evidence from Government IT Implementa

MC
Wendy Mitchell Wilson (Georgia State University)
Insufficient preparation of vendor resources contributes to misaligned expectations, fragmented collaboration, and diminished project learning capacity— conditions that frequently undermine large-scale, complex information technology (IT) projects in the government sector. This study examines how the engagement readiness of vendor functional and technical resources shapes project culture and influences implementation outcomes. It suggests that early knowledge transfer and readiness activities build relational and cultural foundations in addition to technical capability. Drawing on technological frames of reference theory (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994) and social identity theory (Brown, 1999), the study explains how shared understandings and emergent team identities develop during project initiation and become foundational elements of project culture. Project culture is viewed as a multilayered construct shaped across individual, organizational, and interorganizational levels, with engagement readiness fostering cognitive alignment, relational confidence, and cultural integration between vendors and clients. While prior research identifies numerous drivers of IT project failure (including ambiguous requirements, weak governance, insufficient organizational readiness, and ineffective change management) vendor readiness remains underexamined as an antecedent to project culture formation. This study addresses that gap by analyzing how vendor preparation for client engagement influences early project interactions and subsequent implementation trajectories. By linking engagement readiness to project culture and measurable project outcomes, the study contributes to information systems and project management scholarship and offers practical guidance for designing vendor onboarding and readiness interventions that mitigate both technical and engagement risks in complex government IT initiatives (Maruping et al., 2019; Attah et al., 2024).

From Revenue to Strategy: Land Value Capture and the Competitiveness of Inner Cities

MC
Rojestiane Diogenes (University of Fortaleza); Afonso Carneiro Lima (University of Fortaleza)
This paper examines how land taxation and land value capture instruments influence the competitiveness of inner cities. Drawing on a systematic review of international literature on land-based taxation, infrastructure financing, and urban regeneration, the study links these instruments to key dimensions of urban competitiveness, including land use intensity, business density, public space quality, walkability, and regulatory efficiency. The research adopts an exploratory design based on the construction and expert validation of cognitive maps, combining bibliographic evidence with the assessment of specialists in urban planning and fiscal policy for a specific context. The analysis identifies both direct and indirect effects of land value capture mechanisms on urban competitiveness, particularly through incentive zoning, regulatory simplification, improvements in public space maintenance, and reductions in development and licensing costs. The findings suggest that land taxation affects not only fiscal capacity but also the broader business environment of central urban areas. We conclude that land value capture should be understood not merely as a revenue-generating mechanism, but as a strategic land use policy instrument capable of supporting economic revitalization and enhancing the competitiveness in our case, when aligned with coherent, place-based, and long-term urban policies.
