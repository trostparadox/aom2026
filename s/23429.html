Leading on the Tightrope: Inclusion in Police Middle Management

PNP
Maleen Halter; Stephanos Anastasiadis
This study investigates how mid-level police managers interpret structural and cultural conditions that shape inclusion for employees with disabilities. Drawing on seven focus groups with 63 managers participating in a leadership development program, the analysis applies psychological safety and the ethics of care to explore organizational tensions and leadership practices. Findings show that inclusion is hindered by structural rigidity, procedural ambiguity and cultural norms that suppress disclosure. Managers engage in relational practices that reflect care and responsibility, yet these efforts are constrained by organizational inertia. Two emergent dynamics, learned helplessness and the competence-training paradox, further explain how exclusion persists despite formal commitments. The study contributes to theory by demonstrating that psychological safety is structurally contingent and by conceptualizing care as moral labor embedded in bureaucratic systems. Practical implications include the need for clear procedures, flexible accommodations and relationally oriented leadership development.

Ecology of Rule Noncompliance Among Police Officers: Evidence from Traffic Stop Data Reporting

PNP
Shahidul Hassan (The Ohio State University); Bethany Boettner (The Ohio State University); Christopher R Browning (The Ohio State University); Catherine Calder (The University of Texas at Austin)
Extensive research shows that frontline public sector workers often deviate from rules to manage competing demands. Few studies, however, have examined frontline workers’ compliance with rules intended to promote accountability. This study examines police officers’ compliance with an administrative rule requiring them to report traffic-stop data using a form. Although such data are widely used to assess racial disparities in traffic stops, we know little about the extent to which officers consistently report them. Integrating street-level bureaucracy theory with the social ecology model of policing, we assess how neighborhood context shapes officers’ administrative rule compliance. Using geocoded computer-aided dispatch records matched to officer-submitted forms for Columbus, Ohio (2014–2016), we construct a behavioral measure of reporting compliance. We link stop-level records to neighborhood-level data to examine how compliance varies by neighborhood characteristics and structural conditions. Our analysis shows a low compliance rate (40%) over the three-year period. Importantly, compliance varies spatially across neighborhoods, with lower compliance in neighborhoods with a higher proportion of Black residents and higher compliance where collective efficacy is stronger. These findings underscore the role of neighborhood context in shaping police rule-compliance behavior and raise concerns about relying on officer-reported stop data to assess racial disparities.

Social Justice Philanthropy from Political Opportunity and Institutional Perspectives

PNP
Viviana Chiu-Sik Wu; Heather MacIndoe (University of Massachusetts Boston)
This study examines how protest activity shapes philanthropic foundations’ social justice grantmaking. While prior research focuses on how foundations shape movements, we investigate how movements shape foundation behavior. Drawing on the framework of political opportunity structure and institutional perspectives, we analyze 14,987 grants from 680 foundations across seven U.S. cities in 2019. We identify social justice grants through the development of a transparent codebook, paired with manual coding and supervised machine learning to scale classification. We then analyze the grants data with a two-part hurdle model to account for both the decision to engage in SJP and the dollar amount allocated once engaged. Protest activity emerges as the strongest predictor of social justice philanthropy (SJP), increasing both the likelihood of engagement and the level of funding. The geographic embeddedness of foundations alongside grantees increases both the likelihood and the amount of social justice funding. Larger foundations with diversified portfolios are more likely to engage in SJP, while private and family foundations differ in the scale of their commitments once involved. These findings contextualize social justice philanthropy as an elite institutional response that is both responsive to and shaped by collective action.

Towards A Gestalt Model Of Mentoring For The Marginalized

PNP
Shehnila Athar (Indian Institute of Technology, Delhi); Vivek G. Nair (Indian Institute of Technology, Delhi)
Mentoring can serve as more than just a tool for human resource development. It can help address the grand challenge of social inequality. Through a longitudinal, qualitative study involving participatory action research, ethnography, and netnography, we demonstrate how non-profit mentoring organizations (MOs) are working to help marginalized mentees with their career entry and advancement. The MOs differ in their processes and functions, yet they deliver an efficient model of mentoring, which we call the Gestalt mentoring model. We show how MOs provide exclusive access to their programs to the marginalized, solve scalability and efficiency issues alongside, thoughtfully curate the programs to enable the mentees with not just advice but practical skills, and ensure increased pathways for mentoring effectiveness. We also highlight how issues that may be minor for the privileged are actually important for the marginalized. Our study contributes to the literature on mentoring and the theory of capital by highlighting how there is value on the margins - margins within society and margins drawn by dominant discourses and geographies.

The Limits of Representative Bureaucracy: Gender, Race, and Intersectional Inequality in Leadership

PNP
Marcela Garcia Carvalho (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute); Juliana Costa de Carvalho (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute); Gustavo Tavares (Insper); Henrique Yu Jiunn Wang (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute); Sandro Cabral (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute)
Research in public administration has established that demographic representation can enhance legitimacy and responsiveness, yet evidence on the effects of representative leadership remains equivocal and limited, despite leadership’s central role in concentrating authority and discretion. We address this gap by examining access to leadership, rather than leadership behavior, as a critical but understudied dimension of representative bureaucracy. Drawing on representative bureaucracy and Implicit Leadership Theory (ILT), we conceptualize leadership attainment as a cumulative and hierarchical career process shaped by evaluative judgments that become increasingly consequential as authority becomes more discretionary. Using longitudinal administrative data on more than 400,000 career civil servants in the Brazilian federal public service (2000–2023), we estimate event-history models of advancement into multiple leadership levels. The results show persistent and cumulative gender inequality in leadership attainment, racial disadvantage that emerges most clearly at senior leadership levels, and the most severe intersectional disadvantage for Black women. Across gender, race, and their intersection, demographic disparities intensify as authority increases. These findings advance representative bureaucracy and intersectionality by shifting attention from leadership effects to the hierarchical processes that structure access to leadership authority, helping to explain why representative leadership effects are often muted.
