Does Artificial Intelligence Application Influence Firms’ Relative Exploratory Innovation?

TIM
Xiang Li (South China University of Technology); Zijun Zhu (South China University of Technology); Hailin Lan (South China University of Technology); Ping Zeng (South China University of Technology)
Based on the attention-based view and the stimulus-organism-response framework, this study empirically investigates the impact of artificial intelligence application on firms’ relative exploratory innovation, as well as the mediating roles of executives’ future focus and external focus preferences. Using panel data from Chinese A-share listed companies between 2011 and 2023, the findings reveal that AI application significantly promotes a shift in firms' innovation activities toward exploratory rather than exploitative innovation. Furthermore, executives' future-oriented and external-oriented cognitions serve as key mediating mechanisms in this relationship. This research enhances the understanding of the exploration-exploitation trade-off by identifying AI as a driver for exploratory innovation. It also provides a process-based perspective on how AI influences strategic choices through micro-cognitive pathways, thereby contributing to the literature on managerial attention and decision-making in the AI era.

Managing Technological Exploration and Exploitation Through Sequenced Strategic Entry

TIM
KwangWook Gang (Salisbury University); Boreum Choi (Yonsei University); Gayoung Kim (Gyeongsang National University)
This study examines how firms manage technological exploration and exploitation through sequenced strategic entry in modular product architectures. Using longitudinal data from the U.S. magnetic tape drive industry (1984–2002), we conceptualize entry into new product architecture domains as exploration and subsequent component-level expansion within domains as exploitation. Cox proportional hazards models and Andersen–Gill recurrent event models show that technological distance constrains exploratory entry but not exploitative expansion once architectural familiarity is established. Competitive pressure exhibits a U-shaped relationship with both exploration and exploitation and amplifies the negative effect of technological distance during exploration only. These findings demonstrate that exploration and exploitation are temporally ordered strategic actions, shaped by architectural context and competitive conditions rather than simultaneous organizational trade-offs.

When Being Unique Helps or Hurts:Technology Differentiation, AI Adoption and Ambidextrous Innovation

TIM
Ruichen Zhang (Huazhong University of Science and Technology)
Technology differentiation strategy generates competitive advantages by enabling firms to create unique technology portfolios, but it also triggers legitimacy risks due to deviation from mainstream technology trajectories. However, existing research has not clearly revealed how this dual nature of technology differentiation asymmetrically influences different innovation orientations—namely exploratory versus exploitative innovation—within the context of organizational ambidexterity and digital transformation. Drawing on optimal distinctiveness theory, we investigate how technology differentiation heterogeneously impacts exploratory and exploitative innovation performance, and how AI adoption moderates these relationships. Analyzing patent data of Chinese listed companies from 2003 to 2023, we find an inverted U-shaped relationship between technology differentiation and exploratory innovation, and a negative linear relationship between technology differentiation and exploitative innovation. AI adoption steepens the inverted U-shaped relationship while flattening the negative linear relationship. Mechanism test reveals that the two innovation types exhibit heterogeneous sensitivity to technology differentiation's competitive advantages and legitimacy risks. AI adoption can reconcile this tension by amplifying competitive advantages through enhanced internal technology portfolio change and mitigating risks by partially substituting for traditional co-creation channels. Our study offers insights for firms establishing unique technology positioning to optimize innovation performance in the AI era.

When Is Innovation Not Advisable? A Strategic Refrainment Perspective

TIM
Hao Ma (Peking University); Mengyue Su (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
Innovation is often touted as essential for firm success and competitive advantage. However, unwarranted engagement in innovation can lead to catastrophic failures, resource depletions, and reputational damages. This article argues that strategic restraint in innovation is not necessarily a sign of organizational sluggishness but a deliberate decision rooted in a systematic assessment of firm, market, and contextual factors. It advances a systematic yet actionable framework to identify scenarios where refraining from innovation is the suitable strategic choice. By analyzing customer demand, compatibility with organizational core, complementarity within ecosystems, counterpart dynamics, cost and benefit tradeoffs, and contextual fit, this 6C framework provides helpful insights and practical guidance to balance innovation ambition with strategic prudence so as to preserve core values, optimize resource allocation, and mitigate corresponding risks. Ultimately, it calls for a more nuanced understanding of innovation strategy, emphasizing that the ability to "not innovate" when circumstances demand is as critical as the ability to innovate.
