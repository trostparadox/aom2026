Thriving Apart: When Cognitive Dissimilarity Turns from Liability to Asset in Remote Work

OB
Lu Yu (Missouri State University); Xiaoxia Zhu (Salisbury University); Hong Ren; Guangsheng Liang (Lingnan University)
Purpose: Assess whether perceived cognitive dissimilarity undermines thriving in remote work and whether two boundary management behaviors— spanning (acquisition) and buffering (conservation)—offset this risk. We also examine whether off-job personal ties with coworkers moderate these relationships. Design: We conducted a four-wave survey of remote employees (N?=?680; two-month lags), measuring perceived cognitive dissimilarity and off-job personal ties at T2, boundary spanning and buffering at T3, and thriving at T4; we estimated moderated-mediation models with off-job personal ties moderating the links from perceived cognitive dissimilarity to boundary behaviors. Findings: Perceived cognitive dissimilarity lowered thriving yet increased boundary spanning and buffering. Both behaviors transmitted positive indirect associations with thriving. Off-job personal ties attenuated only the dissimilarity–spanning link, yielding a conditional indirect effect via spanning at low personal tie strength; buffering was not moderated. Originality: We position perceived cognitive dissimilarity as a contextual misfit appraisal within the Socially Embedded Model of Thriving and specify parallel acquisition and conservation based boundary management pathways. We show that off-job ties operate as cross-domain resources that reallocate agentic effort, clarifying when misfit becomes generative in remote work.

Human–AI Collaboration Enables Employee Thriving at Work: A Dual-Pathway Mechanism Based on SDT

OB
Xin Zhao (Tianjin University); Meng Xi (Nanjing University); Yuqi Yin (Tianjin University); Yingyue Zhou (Tianjin University); Xu Wang (Tianjin University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in employees’ daily work, yet we know less about how collaborating with AI shapes employees’ thriving, particularly in the Chinese context. Drawing on self-determination theory (SDT), we propose that human–AI collaboration supports thriving by enabling employees to (a) cognitively reframe AI-enabled work demands and (b) engage in informal learning through experimentation and reflection. We further argue that these benefits depend on perceived AI quality. Using three-wave survey data from 432 office employees and testing hypotheses, we find that human–AI collaboration is positively associated with thriving. This relationship is jointly mediated by cognitive reframing and informal learning. Moreover, perceived AI quality strengthens the effects of human–AI collaboration on both mediators and amplifies the corresponding indirect effects on thriving. Our findings clarify why and when human–AI collaboration contributes to employee thriving and highlight the importance of building and implementing high-quality AI systems to support employee development and well-being.

Curiosity Meets Generative AI: How AI-Elicited Inspiration Fuels Thriving and Creativity

OB
Gang He (Nanjing University of Industry Technology); Shengjun Zhang (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Kun Peng (Nanjing University); Jinsong Li (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics)
As generative AI tools become embedded in everyday work practices, it is increasingly important to clarify their motivational and psychological implications for employees. Integrating epistemic curiosity and inspiration theory, we developed and tested a moderated mediation model in which individuals’ I-type curiosity predicts thriving at work and employee creativity through inspiration arising from interactions with generative AI (e.g., ChatGPT, DeepSeek). We argue that employees higher in I-type curiosity are more likely to feel inspired when engaging with AI tools, and that this inspiration functions as a key motivational conduit that fosters both thriving and creativity. We further propose that these indirect effects are amplified for employees with higher educational attainment. We examined the model using a three-wave, multisource field study involving 363 employees and 128 supervisors from five Chinese firms, and all hypotheses were supported. Our results advance curiosity research by identifying inspiration as a motivational mechanism linking I-type curiosity to thriving and creativity and by highlighting educational level as an important boundary condition. In addition, we extend inspiration theory by conceptualizing generative AI as a novel source of workplace inspiration, and we contribute to the AI-at-work literature by specifying when and for whom AI interactions enhance employee thriving and creativity.

When Cronyism is Indirect! An Experimental Insight into the Relational Mechanisms and Quiet Quitting

OB
Tehreem Fatima (University of Sargodha); Ahmad Raza Bilal (University of Sargodha); Muhammad Farrukh Shahzad (Beijing University of Technology); Tahira Iram (University of Strasbourg)
Indirect cronyism, a practice of granting non-merit-based favours to a subordinate based on third-party relations is present in the Pakistani organizational context, yet not examined systemically. The favoured subordinate group is referred to as a “triadic in-group” and the unfavoured group as a “triadic out-group”. Thus, a model is tested to find out the downstream impacts of indirect cronyism on the relational energy and quiet quitting of the triadic out-group subordinates, where relational energy differentiation is used as a conditional factor. The current examination through a multi-study approach (scenario experiment n=98, time-lagged field study: n=543) tested the hypothesized associations using t-tests, ANOVA, and PROCESS model 7. The findings were confirmed and the implications for theory and practice were given for minimizing indirect cronyism and quiet quitting via enhancing the relational energy.
