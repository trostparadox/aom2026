Crossing the Line: Status, Rivalry, and Response to Variable Penalties

OMT
Pramendra Singh Tank (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Penalties, disincentives, and taxes are commonly used by regulators and organizations to induce desired behavioural changes. The impact of penalties on behavioural change is closely tied to their design. When fixed penalties fail to deter undesirable behaviour, introducing an additional variable penalty may be more effective. This study examines how organizations respond to such variable penalties in competitive settings, and whether these responses differ based on organizational status and the intensity of inter-organizational rivalry. Using a difference-in-differences and a regression discontinuity design on men’s international cricket data, we find that additional variable penalty acts as a deterrent and consequently reduces the frequency of rule violations. More importantly, high status induces rigidity, weakening the deterrent effect, whereas competitive rivalry fosters adaptability, enhancing the penalty’s impact. Our findings contribute to the literature on deterrence theory and the behavioural theory of the firm by showing how the perceived trade-off between costs and benefits associated with a variable penalty can induce behavioural change. These insights inform regulators on how to design more sophisticated penalization mechanisms that account for organizational status and competitive dynamics to achieve differentiated behavioural responses.

To Know Your Place: How Local Status Asymmetries Affect Spillovers from Global Status Shifts

OMT
Taeyang Kim (Yonsei University); Michael Jensen (University of Michigan)
Status shifts generate consequences that extend beyond the actors experiencing the shifts. We develop a relational theory of status spillovers that distinguishes between global status shifts and their performance consequences within local status structures. We argue that the direction of spillover effects depends on the focal actor’s position relative to the actor experiencing the status shift within the local status hierarchy. We test our arguments using longitudinal data from professional sumo wrestling in Japan. We find that status shifts originating from actors positioned above the focal actor undermine the focal actor’s performance, whereas status shifts experienced by actors positioned below the focal actor improve the focal actor’s performance. We further show that these spillover effects vary systematically with the focal actor’s rank within the local status structure. Thus, our study shows that status spillovers are fundamentally relational and clarifies how status change reshapes performance outcomes beyond the actor experiencing the shift.

Making Asymmetrical Tie Intelligible: Status Asymmetry and Narrative Agency in Alliance Announcement

OMT
Andy Back (The University of Hong Kong); Kwangjun An (Waseda University)
Strategic alliance announcements are often treated as neutral disclosures that communicate the existence of interorganizational ties. We challenge this view by examining how alliances are narrated in public communication when partners differ in status. Drawing on status theory, we argue that status asymmetry complicates shared expectations about roles and involvement, creating interpretive ambiguity that increases firms’ reliance on narrative agency—the extent to which announcements emphasize purposeful action. At the same time, status asymmetry constrains how extensively action can be articulated, producing a nonlinear relationship between status asymmetry and narrative agency. We further theorize that these dynamics are amplified in bilateral announcements, where joint authorship heightens comparison and status salience, and muted in unilateral announcements, which provide a clearer narrative anchor. Finally, we argue that in unilateral announcements, higher-status firms are more likely to use narrative agency when partnering with lower-status firms, because association alone provides limited guidance about their role and initiative. Using data on 8,128 dyadic alliances among 5,874 biopharmaceutical firms from 1996 to 2022, our analyses support these hypotheses. Together, the findings show that status asymmetry shapes not only alliance outcomes, but also how extensively collaborative action is publicly articulated.

Rules Are One Thing, Decisions Another: Status, Reputation, and Decision Contexts in Classification

OMT
Taeyang Kim (Yonsei University)
Classification is often treated as a mechanical mapping of product features to formal criteria, yet in many situations classification outcomes emerge from interactions between firms and classifiers who advance competing claims. I theorize classification as a negotiated process in which outcomes depend on how these competing claims are evaluated and settled. Focusing on firm status and reputation, I argue that both reduce the likelihood that classifiers reject firms’ classification claims, but that their effects vary systematically across decision contexts. Under high decision load, classifiers rely more heavily on status as a heuristic cue, whereas under high decision visibility, they place greater weight on reputation as a more accountable basis for evaluation. I test these arguments using data from the age-based game classification process in South Korea. Analyzing 11,261 game classifications between 2007 and 2022, I demonstrate how decision contexts condition the use of status and reputation in classification negotiations.

Legitimating the Contested: Multi-Level Status and the Adoption of Contested Innovation

OMT
Wookyoung Kim (Sookmyung Women's University)
How do gatekeeping organizations navigate the adoption of contested innovations that pose significant reputational risks to their institutional standing? We address this question by introducing a multi-level status framework that distinguishes between organizational status—representing an institution's consecration capacity—and product-level status, which reflects its positional commitment. Analyzing a panel dataset of global art museums and their adoption of Non-Fungible Token (NFT) art from 2021 to 2024, we find that status operates through distinct, independent mechanisms. While organizational status serves as a linear enabler by providing symbolic capital to buffer uncertainty, product-level status exhibits an inverted-U relationship with adoption. We demonstrate that museums with middle-range portfolios lead adoption by balancing legitimacy insurance against positional constraints. Furthermore, artist portfolio breadth acts as a moderator; this repertoire flexibility attenuates the negative effects of high-status portfolios by providing the curatorial latitude to experiment without threatening established hierarchical positioning. Notably, organizational and product-level status exert distinct influences, suggesting that institutional prestige does not fully override the categorical traps of product-level commitments. By reconciling the distinctive roles of status within a multi-level framework, we advance status theory and the institutional understanding of gatekeeping in creative industries.
