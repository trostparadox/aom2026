Nobody’s Power: A Study on the Path from Followers’ Gossip to Leadership Emergence Through Idiosync

OB
Xiaofeng Xu (Jiangxi University of Finance and Economics); yujie Lin (Jiangxi University of Finance and Economics)
Drawing on idiosyncrasy credit theory, we examined the mediating mechanism and boundary conditions of workplace gossip on leadership emergence from a follower-centered perspective. Through scenario experimental and multi-wave survey studies, we found that positive workplace gossip was significantly and positively related to leadership emergence. Idiosyncrasy credit mediated the relationships between workplace gossip (both positive and negative) and leadership emergence. Furthermore, self-promotion positively moderated the mediating effect of idiosyncrasy credit on the relationship between positive workplace gossip and leadership emergence. These findings enrich the research perspective on the impact of workplace gossip on leadership emergence and provide implications for theory and practice.

When Gossip Backfires: Attributional and Relational Dynamics of Gossip

OB
junaid khalid (University of Science and Technology of China); WENG Qingxiong (University of Science and Technology of China); Anastasiia Popelnukha (University of Science and Technology of China); Zahid Hameed (Ajman University); Rana Muhammad Naeem (The Islamia University of Bahawalpur); Maaria Batool (The Islamia University of Bahawalpur)
Workplace gossip is a powerful social force that shapes employee behavior, yet little is known about how receivers’ attributions and interpersonal relational ties shape their responses. Drawing on attribution theory, we examined how gossip receivers interpret derogation-based gossip, with particular attention to attributions concerning the sender’s perceived self-interest. These attributions serve as a key psychological mechanism explaining why receivers choose to socially exclude gossip senders. Moreover, we investigated the moderating role of relationship closeness between sender and receiver as a boundary condition in determining gossip receivers' response. We gathered data from 288 service sector employees in Pakistan and found that gossip receiver attribute derogation-based gossip to the sender’s self-serving motives, which in turn leads to social exclusion toward the sender. However, receivers who have close relationships with gossip senders are more likely to behave in a lenient manner. These findings emphasize the relational and dynamic nature of gossip, highlighting that its consequences depend on both its content and relational context.

How Inconsistent Social Evaluations Undermine Employees’ Self-Concept Clarity and Career Success

OB
Wenxiao Xu (University of Nevada, Reno); Zhonghao Zhang (University of Nevada, Reno); Ziyi Wang (Wuhan Business University)
Evaluations from others often influence an individual’s self-perception. While prior research has examined the independent effects of leader feedback and coworker gossip, limited attention has been paid to how their contradictory combinations shape employees’ self-view. This study investigates how inconsistent social evaluations influence employees’ self-concept clarity and downstream career outcomes. Drawing on social information processing theory, we propose that both positive leader feedback combined with negative coworker gossip and negative leader feedback combined with positive coworker gossip reduce employees’ self-concept clarity and in turn diminish subjective career success. Further, we explore which configuration is more detrimental based on the studies into cognitive bias. A three-wave survey of 364 Chinese employees supports these predictions and reveal that the interaction of negative leader feedback and positive coworker gossip makes a stronger negative effect. Results also demonstrate that self-concept clarity mediates the relationship between contradictory evaluations and subjective career success.

Ambiguous Computer-Mediated Communication, Cyber-Incivility, and Digital Replacement Theory

OB
David Joseph Keating (Seton Hall University); Cynthia Kay Maupin (University of Mississippi); Fatima Koko (University of Mississippi)
Many employees in the modern workplace have virtual or hybrid work arrangements. Such arrangements would not be possible without the aid of computer-mediated communication (CMC). A rich set of theories discusses why CMC degrades the quality of information transfer compared to face-to-face communication, resulting in greater ambiguity in messages exchanged. The ambiguous nature of this communication is a precursor of cyber-incivility, a major form of virtual negative work behavior (VNWB). This paper advances theorizing in this space beyond a focus on CMC’s degradation of communication quality to identify characteristics of modern CMC that can reduce uncertainty and, thus, inhibit instances of VNWB. This is essential given that businesses now operate in a virtual world that relies on CMC. To this end, we introduce Digital Replacement Theory, which proposes that different elements of CMC can improve the clarity of information transferred between sender and receiver. To test our theory, we conduct three online experiments manipulating three elements of CMC (emoticons, punctuation, and formatting) that play a critical role in enhancing message clarity. Our studies reveal that through digital additions, senders of CMC can decrease message ambiguity, which increases clarity of the sender’s true intent, and subsequently alters the response behavior of message receivers, influencing the level of civility in their responses to text-based CMC.
