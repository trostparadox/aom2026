An Ontological Perspective into the Ways of Being Present Within Silicon Valley

OB
William Francis Massy
The purpose of this paper is to provide a human ecology economics framework for understanding the attitudes toward artificial intelligence in Silicon Valley. This work addresses the research gap in examining the Silicon Valley social ecology by using the theoretical framework of ways of being to understand those that develop the technology. Allen’s human ecology economics framework provides the institutional foundation to marshal the Silicon Valley ecosystem into explainable fields, which the Gioia Methodology was used to extract their ways of being. The main findings are a gradient trove of the Ways of Being that interplays the actor’s deep-seated beliefs in the technology with the economic reality that the adoption of the technology is inevitable and the actor’s acceptance that the technology will be in some form in their lives. The Human Ecology Economics framework and ways of being can be used to research other ecologies in question, as both are flexible tools to unveil deep seated beliefs are potential phenomena in question, so that scholars and practitioners can understand how the ecosystems imprint their ways of being onto those who inhabit various ecologies.

Middle Managers as Change Agents: The Contingent Role of Territoriality in AI-Driven Transformation

OB
Jian Liang (Tongji University); Yuqi Yang (Tongji University)
As an increasing number of organizations adopt AI tools for management, growing attention is being paid to middle managers as a critical bottleneck—given their fluctuating stance between endorsement and resistance to the digital implementation. We attribute this tendency to their territorial mindset, defined as a defensive, prevention-focused form of ownership over organizational objects. To examine this proposition, we collected both quantitative and qualitative data from a state-owned energy enterprise in China that has implemented an AI-enabled management system. We first conducted a longitudinal empirical study to assess how the territorial mindset of middle managers results in varied implementation outcomes across teams. We then performed an in-depth qualitative analysis to uncover the behavioral patterns exhibited by middle managers during implementation, thereby providing insight into why digital transformation cannot achieve the designed benefits. By integrating these insights, this study brings middle managers into focus, offering a process-driven understanding while yielding actionable recommendations to enhance the success of AI-driven organizational transformations.

Work in the Age of AI: A Multilevel Review

OB
Ping Zhu (Beijing Institute of Technology); Yunyun Emily Yuan (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Pingqing Liu (Beijing Institute of Technology)
With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), human-AI collaboration is becoming a new norm in organizations. Although AI-related scholarship has expanded rapidly, prior research in organizational behavior remains fragmented and lacks systematic integration. To address this gap, we review AI research through a multi-level framework spanning the individual, organizational, and societal levels. At the individual level, existing studies mainly capture employees’ attitudes at discrete points in time, leaving the dynamic evolution of these attitudes and the role of human-AI emotional interaction underexplored. At the organizational level, while AI can enhance productivity, it may also intensify job polarization and workplace inequality, raising the challenge of balancing efficiency with employee well-being; evidence from nonprofit organizations is particularly limited. At the societal level, AI is reshaping labor markets but has increased employment instability, exposed gaps in social protection, and may widen the global digital divide. By synthesizing findings and limitations across the three levels, this review outlines future research directions and offers theoretical and practical insights for navigating AI-driven transformations.

A Bump or a Boost? AI Recommendations, Criminal Risk, and Job Complexity in Hiring Decisions

OB
Jakari Griffith (Bridgewater State University); Dan Chen (Texas Woman's University); Oyinkansola Sodiya (The University of Texas at Arlington); Nicole C. Young (Franklin & Marshall College)
Artificial intelligence (AI) hiring tools are increasingly used to evaluate candidates with criminal records, yet little is known about when AI recommendations influence hiring decisions for stigmatized applicants. Across two studies (N = 528), we examined interplay between AI recommendations, criminal offense type, and job complexity. Study 1 (N = 144) established occupational attribution bias: fraud convictions were rated as more job-relevant for high-complexity managerial positions (M = 4.83) than low-complexity coordinator positions (M = 4.40), d = 0.64. Study 2 (N = 384) revealed bounded AI influence: AI increased hiring rates for fraud convictions from 6.1% to 31.4% (OR = 7.01, p = .001) for low-complexity positions but had no effect for high-complexity positions. Pre-registered analyses confirmed significant three-way interactions for perceived job fit, candidate potential, and perceived risk (all p < .05). Mediation analyses showed AI shifted hiring through reduced perceived risk—but only for low-complexity roles. We introduce the "permission to hire" framework: AI provides psychological legitimacy for hiring stigmatized candidates, but this permission is granted selectively based on perceived stakes. These findings reveal that technology alone cannot eliminate discrimination, particularly for higher-status positions.

When AI Acts Human: The Hidden Cost of Anthropomorphism on Employee Self-Control and Performance

OB
Zimo Zhang (Renmin University of China); Xin Ai (Tsinghua University); Siqi Wang (Renmin University of China); Jingwen Zhu (Renmin University of China)
With the widespread adoption of generative artificial intelligence in organizations, employee interactions with anthropomorphic AI systems have become increasingly frequent. While existing research highlights the positive effects of AI anthropomorphism, its potential negative influence on employee self-control resources and task performance remains underexplored. Integrating the Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) paradigm with ego depletion theory, this study proposes a theoretical model centered on the mediating roles of AI-directed politeness and AI-facilitated loafing between AI anthropomorphism and task performance as well as the moderating role of self-monitoring. Through an experimental study, we found that: (1) High AI anthropomorphism significantly increases both AI-directed politeness and AI-facilitated loafing; (2) AI-directed politeness and AI-facilitated loafing mediate the negative relationship between AI anthropomorphism and task performance; (3) Self-monitoring moderates the effect of AI anthropomorphism on social responses, with high self-monitors exhibiting stronger politeness and loafing behaviors when interacting with highly anthropomorphic AI; (4) Self-monitoring further strengthens the negative indirect effect of AI anthropomorphism on task performance through AI-facilitated loafing. The findings offer theoretical and practical insights for organizations to balance the benefits and psychological costs of anthropomorphic AI design.
