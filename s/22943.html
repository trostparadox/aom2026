A Quantile Regression Perspective on Trust and Organizational Readiness for Change

ODC
Stephanie Christine Mercedes Benz (Philipps University Marburg); Thomas Armbruester
This study examines the influence of trust on organizational readiness for change. Differentiating between multiple forms of trust, we reason that all of them effect the employees' perception of the organization’s general readiness for change. We enrich the academic discussion about trust by extending the view beyond the conditional mean perspective, analyzing the varying effects of each form of trust along the conditional distribution of readiness for change via quantile regression. The data was collected in Germany between 2018 and 2019. The sample includes 365 companies and a total of 73,166 employees. The model was analyzed via OLS regression before conducting the quantile regression. The results show that all forms of trust, the trust in co-workers, the trust in managers and the trust in the organization, significantly influence the organizational readiness for change. Trust in managers has the strongest relationship, which aligns with current research on leadership and its major influence on organizational change. Moreover, our quantile regression provides insights into the varying effects. It shows that the lower end of the conditional distribution of organizational readiness for change is influenced significantly stronger by the three forms of trust while top quantiles are less effected by trust.

Managing Successful Innovation Changes in MNCs: Impact of Subsidiary Employees’ Psychological Safety

ODC
Indu Ramachandran (Texas State University); Kim Clark (Saint Mary's College of California); Vishag Badrinarayanan (Texas State University)
Multinational Corporations (MNCs) constantly search for ways to enhance their knowledge base given that access to superior knowledge can enable them to maintain a competitive advantage in the global marketplace. They also have a unique advantage given that they can set up subsidiaries within their global network and give them specific mandates to engage in different kinds of innovation activities. In this paper, we develop a conceptual framework to explain how MNCs make decisions to innovate and manage changes within their subsidiary network. We propose that the institutional distance between home and host country influences how an MNC makes strategic choices to manage exploration (competence-creating mandates) and exploitation (competence-exploiting mandates) innovation within their subsidiaries. Furthermore, given that engaging in these mandates may involve interpersonal risks for subsidiary employees engaging in these mandates, we theorize that psychological safety, and its related relational contextual factors will influence the effectiveness of these mandates. Our conceptual framework suggests that MNCs can pursue seemingly incompatible types of innovation across its subsidiary network by judicious decisions regarding where to pursue a particular kind of innovation (considering differences in institutional distance), as well as carefully managing psychological safety issues, leading to successful implementation of the innovation mandates.

Missing Human Touch: Research in Psychological Safety with Evidence from Non-Western Context

ODC
Irina V. Prosviriakova (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Christian Lechner
Psychological safety – a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking – has become a central construct in organizational research, linked to learning, collaboration, and voice. Prevailing theories emphasize leader behavior and contextual design as key enablers, often assuming that psychological safety can be deliberately created through managerial intervention. However, this perspective reflects research grounded primarily in Western, low power distance contexts, where interpersonal openness and trust are more readily assumed. Drawing on qualitative data from five organizations across India, the United Arab Emirates, Serbia, and Russia, we examine how psychological safety is experienced in high power distance, collectivist settings. Our findings challenge the assumption that safety can be engineered through leadership behavior alone. Instead, we show that psychological safety often depends on relational enablers – pre-existing interpersonal trust, emotional resonance, and authentic connection. We identify “human connection” as a critical, previously underexplored dimension of psychological safety, encompassing deep interpersonal bonds, emotional acceptance, and relational trust with leaders and peers. This study reframes psychological safety as a temporally and relationally grounded phenomenon, rather than a universally constructible team climate. We contribute to theory by (1) introducing human connection as an essential enabler, (2) highlighting the relational foundations required for safety to emerge, and (3) advancing a culturally and temporally nuanced understanding of psychological safety development in global work contexts.

From Safety to Courage: A Psychodynamic View of Psychological Safety in Leadership Teams

ODC
Irina V. Prosviriakova (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
This paper explores how psychological safety evolves in leadership teams through the integration of behavioral, developmental, and psychodynamic perspectives. Drawing on Edmondson’s (1999, 2019) team learning framework, Clark’s (2020) four-stage developmental model, and Bion’s (1961) systems-psychodynamic theory, the study conceptualizes psychological safety not as a static climate but as a developmental and emotional capability. Using a mixed-method case study design, data were collected through Edmondson’s 7-item Psychological Safety Scale, Clark’s Four Stages of Safety questionnaire, and semi-structured interviews with members of a senior leadership team in a multinational organization undergoing transformation. Findings reveal that while the team demonstrates structural maturity, it remains emotionally cautious: collaboration is maintained through civility rather than candor. The study proposes that psychological safety develops through the capacity to think and act in the presence of fear, reframing it as a dynamic equilibrium between anxiety and trust. This integration offers new insights for understanding leadership team learning and for designing developmental interventions that build collective courage.

Distrust as a Teacher: The Productive Role of Scrutiny in Cooperative Governance

ODC
Rosalie Luo (Ivey Business School)
How does distrust shape learning in participatory organizations? Prior research largely portrays distrust as corrosive, emphasizing its tendency to escalate conflict through suspicion and attributional bias. This study, in contrast, examines how distrust, scrutiny, and organizational structure interact over time in a sustainable community-supported agricultural (CSA) organization operating under cooperative principles. Drawing on longitudinal ethnographic data including two years of participant observation, interviews, and archival data over the organization’s lifetime, I trace how relational trust in the farm’s founding farmers sustained organizational stability while obscuring emerging vulnerabilities related to both sustainability and organizational performance. When a new farmer was hired and introduced changes, members increasingly interpreted his actions through a lens of competence- and integrity-based distrust. Consistent with attributional accounts of collective distrust, suspicion intensified and interpersonal conflict escalated, ultimately resulting in the farmer’s termination. Importantly, as distrust spread, scrutiny expanded beyond the individual to encompass taken-for-granted organizational practices and cooperative norms. The participatory structure of the organization both amplified attributional distrust and enabled collective scrutiny. By showing how distrust redirects scrutiny and how participatory structures shape its consequences, this study contributes to theories of trust by clarifying how distrust may, at first, destabilize relationships, but then prompt collective reflection and learning in organizations, thereby enabling greater resilience.
