Team Goal Orientation and Creativity: Mediating Effect of External Activities

OB
Soo Bin Park (Seoul National University); Jin Nam Choi (Seoul National University)
This study investigates the impact of team goal orientation on external activities and, in turn, their effects on team creativity. Drawing on the cost-benefit framework, the study examines the distinct influences of team learning goal orientation (LGO) and avoidance goal orientation (AGO) on external activities, which subsequently lead to proactive and responsive forms of team creativity. Furthermore, we examine the moderating role of team knowledge efficacy on the relationship between team LGO and external activities and the relationship between AGO and external activities. In addition, we test the moderating effect of leader entrepreneurship on the relationship between external activities and proactive team creativity and the relationship between external activities and responsive team creativity. Using data from 56 teams, the results validate a positive relationship between LGO and external activities and a negative relationship between AGO and external activities. Moreover, the results show that external activities are positively related to proactive team creativity, while they are not related to responsive team creativity. The findings indicate that team knowledge efficacy weakens the relationship between LGO and external activities. In addition, the results confirm that leader entrepreneurship strengthens the relationship between external activities and proactive team creativity, while it attenuates the relationship between external activities and responsive team creativity. We discuss the theoretical contributions, practical implications, and future research directions.

Synthesizing, Patchworking, Evaluating, and Abstaining: Patterns of Creative Interaction

OB
Frederic-Alexander Starmann (University of Paderborn); Sylvia Hubner-Benz (University of Paderborn); Michael Frese (Asia School of Business); Jomel Wei Xuan Ng
While team creativity is increasingly viewed as a process, we lack a granular understanding of the underlying team member interactions that drive idea development. In this study we ask: How do team members momentarily, as well as over time, engage with one another to shape the trajectory of an emerging product concept? Using qualitative video analysis of 314 entrepreneurial teams working on exploratory innovation, we identify four distinct patterns of creative interaction. Synthesizing transforms ideas through collective reasoning, patchworking aggregates ideas through parallel individual reasoning, evaluating involves judgment without development, and abstaining reflects disengagement from both team and content. We characterize these patterns along two analytical dimensions, engagement intensity (deep versus shallow) and engagement orientation (developmental versus judgmental). Contrary to the assumption that idea elaboration entails a linear path of refinement, our findings illustrate how distinct patterns of interaction can drive conceptual depth and coherence or lead to additive but often unintegrated contributions. Adopting a dialogical perspective, we provide a theoretical model explaining when and why engagement intensity and orientation shift, offering a new perspective on the dynamic and relational processes that shape innovation.

A Multilevel Model of Team Functional Composition, Boundary Spanning, and Team Creativity

OB
Cheng Chen (The University of Newcastle); Yezi Kang (University of Groningen); Yongkang Yang (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University); Jinghao (Terrence) Zhang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Bernard A. Nijstad (University of Groningen)
A composition of team members with diverse functions can drive team creativity because it encourages boundary spanning that brings diverse external knowledge and resources into the team. However, existing research has predominantly conceptualized both boundary spanning and team functional composition at the team level, overlooking the individual behavioral foundations of boundary spanning and the diversity of functional experiences within individual members. As a result, understanding of how team functional composition shapes boundary spanning and, ultimately, team creativity remains incomplete. To address these limitations, we adopt a multilevel perspective by conceptualizing boundary spanning as an individual-level behavior that varies across team members and by distinguishing between two representations of team functional composition: intrapersonal functional diversity (i.e., the variety of functional experience within individual members) and interpersonal functional diversity (i.e., the difference in functional backgrounds across members). We propose that intrapersonal functional diversity positively predicts boundary spanning, whereas interpersonal functional diversity has a curvilinear relationship with boundary spanning, such that boundary spanning is highest at moderate levels of diversity. Boundary spanning, in turn, enhances team creativity. A sample of 101 work teams supported our hypotheses. This study highlights the multilevel pathways from team functional composition to team creativity through boundary spanning.

Convergent or Divergent? A Multilevel Approach to Team Proactivity Emergence

OB
Davide Bonfio (University of Padua); Andrea Furlan
This study explores the assumption that proactive behavior within teams emerges through a convergent process characterized by increasing behavioral similarity. Grounded in multilevel theory, we contend that team proactivity may instead follow a divergent, compilational pattern, unfolding from contextually embedded and interaction-driven dynamics among team members. To support this claim, we analyze the two most used indices that justify team-level data aggregation (rWG and ICC), using Proactive Idea Implementation as a context-specific measure of individual proactive behavior. Drawing on data from 267 employees nested within 45 work teams across 13 manufacturing firms, we find that greater team tenure is associated with increased within-team dispersion in proactivity, suggesting that repeated collaboration does not foster convergence in proactive behavior. To assess whether proactivity is a divergent team property, we observe the correlation between team tenure and a polarization index, finding that individual proactivity not only does not converge over time, but tends to diverge. The results of non-convergence, paired with the divergence of individual proactivity over time makes the case of team proactivity to emerge following a compilational pattern. We discuss the theoretical and managerial implications of a configural approach to team proactivity and advocate for a process-oriented perspective that attends to the temporal, contextual, and multilevel nature of team-level emergent phenomena.

Team Narcissism and Team Learning: The Role of Team Envy and Team Leader Humility

OB
Guang Xu (Harbin Normal University); chunmei Jiao (Harbin Normal University); Xiangjing Guo (Beihang University); Yuhan Jin; yixuan Dou
This study explores the impact of team narcissism on team learning. Specifically, we suggest that team envy mediates the influence of team narcissism on team learning. Motivated by social comparison, we further propose that team leader humility moderates these relationships. Using multi-time-point survey data from 301 employees nested within 70 teams, we tested our team-level model using PROCESS analyses. Because the model was conceptualized entirely at the team level, all individual ratings of team narcissism, team envy, team leader humility, and team learning were aggregated to the team level by calculating team mean scores after verifying adequate within-team agreement (rwg, ICC[1], and ICC[2]). The empirical results show that: (1) team narcissism decreases the team learning by increasing team envy and (2) team leader humility moderates not only the relationship between team narcissism and team envy but also the indirect effect of team narcissism on team learning via team envy. We provide a theoretical basis to explain the influence of team narcissism on team learning and provide practical recommendations for management of team members.
