Hybrid Work at Scale: Combining Deliberate and Emergent Practices

OB
Akshita Joshi (Harvard Business School); Amy C. Edmondson (Harvard Business School); Magnus Mahring (Stockholm School of Economics); Mark Mortensen (INSEAD)
When hybrid work is adopted, organizations must enable coordination and connection across teams that have diverse configurations and needs. We extend prior work that largely studied distributed work at the team level, to explore how organizations manage diverse hybrid configurations simultaneously. Through a nine-month qualitative case study of Tech Co, a global technology firm that implemented a fully flexible hybrid policy, we identify a set of deliberate organizational practices that functioned as flexible scaffolding for hybrid work. Further, we found that these practices were customized by teams through corresponding emergent practices. Together, the deliberate organizational and the emergent team practices formed a dynamic system that enabled coordination and connection. Our study contributes to theories of hybrid work, local adaptation, and organizational learning. We challenge the assumption that hybrid work requires standardization to be effective and instead theorize how organizations can embrace structural and actor-level variation through adaptable systems that shape to their context.

Why Remote Work Feels Threatening for Some Employees: Remote Work-Related Worries

OB
Linda-Elisabeth Reimann (University of Münster); Carmen Binnewies (University of Münster)
Remote work is often treated as either a beneficial or a detrimental work arrangement, yet empirical findings remain inconsistent. We argue that these inconsistencies stem from neglecting individual differences that shape whether employees perceive remote work as a resource gain or threat. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, we present remote work-related worries (RWRW), defined as the tendency to perceive remote work as threatening to central work-related resources such as career prospects, social relationships, and status. Across three studies, we develop and validate a RWRW measure and demonstrate its theoretical relevance beyond related constructs such as neuroticism, professional isolation, and workplace fear of missing out (wFoMO). Notably, using a daily diary design, we show that RWRW shape employees’ day-to-day experiences of remote work. Cross-level analyses reveal that for employees high in RWRW, daily remote work is more strongly associated with heightened wFoMO. By conceptualizing remote work as a potential resource threat rather than an objective work condition, this study advances theory on individual differences in remote work experiences. Our findings highlight why identical remote work arrangements can produce differing daily psychological outcomes and underscore the importance of incorporating stable threat appraisals into models of remote work and employee well-being.

How Often Is Too Often? The Inverted-U Relationship Between Remote Work Frequency and Creativity

OB
Meng Xi (Tianjin University); Fan Yang (Southeast University); Meng Gao (Southeast University)
Organizations increasingly manage remote and hybrid work as a frequency decision (days per week) rather than a binary location choice. Yet it remains unclear what level of remote work best supports employee creativity and why its effects may be nonlinear. Drawing on work design theory, we develop and test a functional-form approach of how remote work frequency relates to employee creativity, why the relationship is nonlinear, how job autonomy transmits the effect, and when the curve changes as a function of self-leadership. Across three studies—a preregistered vignette experiment (N = 294), a three-wave panel (N = 356), and a multi-source field study (N = 271) with supervisor-rated creativity in an IT outsourcing firm—we find an inverted U-shaped relationship between remote work frequency and creativity. This pattern is explained by job autonomy, such that autonomy increases as remote work moves from low to moderate levels but weakens when remote work becomes extensive, thereby reducing creativity. Moreover, self-leadership acts as a self-regulatory boundary condition that reshapes the autonomy curve by flattening its concavity and shifting the implied peak toward higher remote-work frequency. Together, these findings advance functional-form theorizing in hybrid work research and offer actionable guidance for designing remote-work frequency, autonomy-supportive management practices, and self-leadership development.

Inspiring Remote Citizenship by Bridging the Distance: The Role of Leader Identification

OB
Shainaz Firfiray (University of Aberdeen); Margarita Mayo; Jie Cao (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Ronit Kark (Bar-Ilan University)
Individuals who work remotely may find it challenging to develop meaningful social connections with their leaders. Leader identification is a key mechanism through which leaders exert their influence and is associated with a range of positive outcomes including increased discretionary effort or organizational citizenship behaviors (OCB). Given that remote work reduces physical proximity and weakens social ties, it may limit access to leader behaviors that signal care and social support. While videoconferencing (a specific element of remote work) is purported to facilitate better relationships between leaders and followers, it is also reported to constrain nonverbal cues and undermine social connections. Overall, the impact of remote work and videoconferencing on leader identification and OCB remains theoretically ambiguous. To examine these dynamics, we draw on self-determination theory (SDT) to explain how remote work and videoconferencing intensity shape leader identification and how leader identification, in turn impacts OCB. We argue that remote work and videoconferencing intensity will be indirectly related to OCB via leader identification. Further, we also argue that these relationships will be influenced by idealized leader behaviors and leader relational transparency. Results across three studies, including a pre-Covid-19 sample of remote workers in Spain, a sample of remote workers during the Covid-19 pandemic in China and a two-wave survey of UK employees during the Covid-19 pandemic generally support our hypotheses.

Remote Work Arrangements Signal Lower Valuation of Relatedness Needs

OB
Danbee Chon (University of South Florida); Josephine Tan (Stanford University); Frank Flynn (Stanford University)
We propose that managers interpret work arrangements as a signal of their employees’ basic psychological needs and investigate this claim using a multimethod approach (N = 3,337). In Studies 1 and 2, managers report that remote workers value relatedness—the desire to connect with, and care for, other people—less than nonremote workers do. Studies 3A and 3B demonstrate that this view is held more strongly by nonremote workers and that it is unfounded: remote and nonremote workers do not differ as much in their valuation of relatedness needs as others assume. In Studies 4 and 5, we identify a negative consequence: When remote workers are perceived as less interested in connecting with others, they are seen as possessing less leadership potential. We conclude by highlighting choice as a boundary condition—remote work signals a reduced valuation of relatedness needs, particularly when an employee appears to have chosen a remote position.
