Breaking Boundaries: How Job Insecurity Affects Work and Non-Work Outcomes Through Rumination?

OB
Zongbo Li (China University of Mining and Technology (Xuzhou)); Ruolei Wu (China University of Mining and Technology (Xuzhou)); Jiaxin Xue (Renmin University of China); Yingxin Deng (Beijing Institute of Technology); Rui Li (Hefei University of Technology)
Job insecurity is a typical stressor that not only affects employees’ psychological and behavioral responses at work but can also extend its impact into their leisure time. However, little is known about how and when it breaks the work-home boundary and affects different outcomes. Drawing on the cognitive appraisal theory of stress and boundary theory, we suggest that job insecurity impacts both work and non-work outcomes via different forms of work rumination, with individuals’ work-home segmentation preferences moderating these mechanisms. In a three-wave survey of 489 married employees, we found that cognitive job insecurity triggers problem-solving pondering, thereby promoting job crafting. Both cognitive and affective job insecurity lead to employees’ affective rumination during leisure time, which then contributes to marital deviance. Employees’ work-home segmentation preferences mitigate the impact of cognitive job insecurity on problem-solving pondering but amplify its effect on affective rumination. However, these preferences do not moderate the relationship between affective job insecurity and affective rumination. Finally, the theoretical and practical implications are discussed.
