Multiple Dimensions of Demand-Ability Fit and Employee Outcomes

OB
Tae-Yeol Kim (Hope College); Nana Yaa Antwi-Gyamfi (China Europe International Business School); Ziyi (Zoey) HE (University of Maryland College Park); James M. Diefendorff (University of Arkansas)
This study theorized and tested an integrated model that examines the effects of job demand-employee ability (D-A) fit for social, physical, emotional, and cognitive dimensions on employee outcomes. Using a multisource, two-wave survey design and a sample of 477 employees and 84 supervisors, we tested the effects of D-A fit vs. misfit and D-A fit level (low vs. high fit) on effects of self-presentation on job-related self-efficacy and subsequent task performance. We found that D-A fit was better than D-A misfit for job-related self-efficacy and enhancing task performance for all four job dimensions. In addition, fitting at high levels was better for job-related self-efficacy and subsequent task performance than fitting at low levels for all dimensions except the cognitive one. Meanwhile, perceived organizational support enhanced the effect of D-A fit levels (high fit levels vs. low fit levels) on job-related self-efficacy and task performance for the cognitive dimension. Furthermore, employees may be better able to cope with the absence of D-A fit, and thus the effect of D-A fit vs. misfit would be weakened at high levels of POS for all dimensions except the physical one.

Self–Other (In)Congruence in Conscientiousness and Job Performance

OB
In-Sue Oh (Temple University); Saehee Sam Kang (Florida State University); Yongsuhk Jung
Within the multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) framework, discrepancies between raters are typically treated as method-specific variance or measurement error that researchers seek to minimize. However, within the self-other agreement (SOA) framework, discrepancies between raters, particularly disagreements between self- and other-rating, are widely examined as important predictors of individual effectiveness. This study integrates the MTMM and SOA perspectives and applies them to self and other-ratings of conscientiousness, the most valid personality trait for predicting job performance. Using polynomial regression and response surface methodology, this study examines the impact of self-other (in)congruence in conscientiousness on job performance based on a sample of military cadets. The results indicate that, in general, (a) self-other congruence in conscientiousness is more positively related to job performance than self-other incongruence in conscientiousness; (b) self-other congruence at a higher level is more positively related to job performance than self-other congruence at a lower level; and (c) underestimation incongruence (self-ratings < other-ratings of conscientiousness) is less negatively related to job performance than overestimation incongruence (self-ratings > other-ratings). Interestingly, although not hypothesized, (d) self-other congruence at a lower level is more strongly related to job performance than either form of self-other incongruence. These findings suggest that the integrative use of the MTMM and SOA approaches has the potential to enhance the validity of conscientiousness in predicting job performance.

Advice Seeker-Giver (In)Congruence in Prescriptive Solution Orientation and Advisory Outcomes

OB
Ryan Tao Zhou (The University of British Columbia); Jonathan Evans (The University of British Columbia); Michael Daniels (The University of British Columbia)
Advice research has traditionally examined advice seekers and advice givers in isolation, leaving unclear how advisory outcomes hinge on congruence between the two parties. We address this gap by theorizing prescriptive solution orientation (PSO)—the extent to which individuals prefer advice in the form of concrete, directive solutions—as a dyadic compatibility mechanism that shapes whether advice interactions generate responsiveness and use. In a multi-stage study of virtual career-advisory sessions, we capture both seekers’ and givers’ PSO and estimate polynomial regression models to test how the degree of congruence predicts advisory outcomes. Results reveal a robust incongruence penalty: when seekers’ general preference for prescriptive solutions diverges from givers’ general tendency to offer prescriptive solutions, seekers perceive givers as significantly less responsive to their requests. This deficit in perceived advice giver responsiveness is, in turn, associated with diminished advice utilization and weaker intentions to continue the advisory relationship. Supporting our theory, response surface analyses further reveal that: (1) the benefits of congruence are level-independent, persisting whether dyads match at high or low levels of prescriptiveness; and (2) the costs of incongruence are symmetric, occurring in comparable magnitude whether seekers are more prescriptive than givers or vice versa. By showing that advice effectiveness is rooted less in either party’s orientation alone than in the congruence between them, our findings reposition effective advice as compatibility-based and offer a dyadic account of when advisory interactions produce perceived responsiveness, uptake, and relationship continuation.

Negative Gossip as Environmental Signal: Value Incongruence, Inauthenticity, and Social Withdrawal

OB
Jingzhou Pan (Tianjin University); Wenjing Yang (Tianjin University); Jinlong Zhu (Renmin University of China)
Negative gossip, a ubiquitous form of workplace communication, typically occurs in conversations between gossipers and recipients. However, prior research has largely examined recipients’ perceptions and reactions within dyadic interactions involving a single gossip sender. Challenging this dominant perspective, our research draws on the State Authenticity as Fit to the Environment (SAFE) model to illuminate the hidden costs of negative gossip when it functions as a collective environmental signal. Results from a multi-wave, multi-source field study and a 15-day experience sampling study consistently show that repeated exposure to negative gossip from coworkers heightens recipients’ perceived value incongruence and felt inauthenticity, which in turn elicits social withdrawal. These detrimental effects are attenuated when recipients more strongly agree with the evaluative viewpoints conveyed in the gossip. We conclude with a discussion of the theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

The Mediating Role of Organizational Identification and the Moderating Role of Need for Power

OB
Xiaochuan Song (Texas A&M University-Kingsville)
This research investigates the relationships among person-organization (P-O) fit, need-supply (N-S) fit, demand-ability (D-A) fit, organizational identification (OI), and employees’ workplace supportiveness, revealing key insights into how different fit dimensions influence employees’ OI with their organization and subsequently their workplace supportiveness. Grounded in Social Identity Theory and Social Exchange Theory, the findings underscore the critical role of P-O fit and D-A fit in fostering OI, which further leads to employees’ supportive behaviors toward the organization. Furthermore, this study reveals that employees’ need for power (NFP) amplifies the positive effect of OI on employees’ workplace supportiveness. Theoretical and practical implications were discussed.
