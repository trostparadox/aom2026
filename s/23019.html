Managing Family Opposition to Repatriation: The Case of Self-Initiated Senegalese Repatriates

IM
Mamadou SARR; François Grima; Michel Naim (EBS Paris)
This study aims to understand how self-initiated repatriates manage opposition from the extended national family during the decision-making process of returning to their home country. Based on 42 interviews with Senegalese self-initiated repatriates (SIRs), the process begins with a shared initial phase involving a projection strategy—anticipating and becoming aware of potential family resistance. This is followed by a second phase of implementation, divided into two distinct strategies: a concealment strategy for single repatriates and an affirmation strategy for those in a relationship. The former involves proactive behaviors aimed at shedding the image of a foreigner. It culminates in a focused effort to persuade the biparental core family. The latter strategy reflects a focus on the nuclear family, with the spouse’s emotional support, and ends with a reduction of the family system. This research contributes to the literature in three key ways. First, it shows a process of managing the opposition of the national extended family, emphasizing individual agency in the repatriates’ adjustment. Second, it introduces a new typology of family systems by distinguishing between the transnational family, the extended national family, the nuclear family, and the biparental family—an innovation in this field. Third, by focusing on Senegalese SIRs, this study sheds light on the realities of repatriation in an African context, complementing existing research that largely centers on Western countries.

Expatriates as Boundary Spanners: Trust and Knowledge Transfer in Global Organizations

IM
Kyoungmin Lee (University of Oklahoma); Margaret A. Shaffer (University of Oklahoma)
This study examines how expatriates’ boundary-spanning behaviors foster trust and enable knowledge transfer in multinational enterprises based on structural hole theory and social capital theory. Using three-wave data from 256 corporate expatriates, the results show that functional and cultural boundary-spanning enhance trust with headquarters and host country nationals, whereas linguistic boundary-spanning has no significant effects. Trust operates in an actor-specific manner, such that trust with headquarters facilitates knowledge transfer with headquarters and trust with host country nationals facilitates knowledge transfer with host country nationals, thereby mediating the effects of functional and cultural boundary-spanning on knowledge transfer. Moreover, other-orientation strengthens the trust-building effects of functional boundary-spanning with host country nationals.

Born Global Ties: CEO Hometown Qiaoxiang Proximity and OFDI Decision-Making of Chinese Firms

IM
Suqin Shen (Guangdong University of Technology); Wanlin Hu (Vlerick Business School)
Despite extensive upper echelons research on the impact of CEOs’ national backgrounds on firm behavior, we know little about how micro-level geographic imprints embedded in top executives’ hometown environments influence international strategic decisions. Integrating upper echelons theory with an imprinting perspective, we examine how the geographic proximity between a CEO’s hometown and a qiaoxiang—Chinese hometowns historically known for extensive overseas migration and enduring ties to overseas communities—affects firms’ outward foreign direct investment (OFDI) decisions. Using a sample of Chinese listed firms, we find that firms led by CEOs whose hometowns are closer to a qiaoxiang are more likely to undertake higher-commitment OFDI, select host countries with greater economic and geographic distance, and prefer wholly owned subsidiaries over international joint ventures. We further show that these effects are attenuated when CEOs have international work or study experience but strengthened under contemporary deglobalization trends. By highlighting the CEO’s hometown exposure to overseas-oriented communities as a persistent early-life imprint, our study advances upper echelons theory. It contributes to international business research by identifying a previously overlooked micro-level antecedent of OFDI decision-making.

The Next Frontier: Cultural Integration For International Business

IM
Sheri L Mackey (Not Associated)
As organizations increasingly operate across multiple cultures simultaneously, global work is no longer defined by expatriation into a single host context but, instead, by continuous coordination across diverse cultural logics. Yet dominant approaches to cross-cultural management remain anchored in individual-level competence models, offering limited insight into how multicultural teams integrate meaning in practice. This study reconceptualizes cultural integration as a system-level, relational capability through which diverse global teams coordinate understanding and action. Drawing on Epistemic Network Analysis and thematic analysis of data from 20 global executives and 173 team members in two multinational firms, the study examines how leaders and teams perceive the role of cultural integration in everyday work. Results demonstrate that cultural integration is structurally central to leaders’ sensemaking and is most strongly associated with awareness of worldviews, team effectiveness, and business outcomes. Cultural differences are consistently experienced as sources of misalignment and operational friction, yet integration is perceived as a boundary-spanning mechanism that enables learning, coordination, and performance. By making visible how culture, work, and outcomes are relationally organized in global firms, this study advances international business and organizational behavior theory beyond individual adaptation toward a systems-based account of multicultural collaboration. Cultural integration emerges not merely as a developmental concern, but as a foundational capability through which diversity becomes a source of competitive advantage in complex global environments.
