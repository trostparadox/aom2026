Strategic Superstars and Extreme Productivity Thresholds

STR
Omid M Ardakani (Georgia Southern University); Katia De Melo Galdino (Georgia Southern University)
This paper develops an information-theoretic framework for identifying strategic superstar firms by addressing the threshold selection problem in extreme value analysis. We introduce a Kullback–Leibler penalized criterion that formalizes the bias-variance tradeoff in peaks-over-threshold modeling and demonstrate superior finite-sample performance over conventional methods. We identify the optimal superstar threshold at the 98.2nd percentile and model extreme productivity using a conditional generalized Pareto distribution using World Bank Enterprise Survey data on US firms. Results show that strategic capabilities differentially influence extreme performance. Scale advantages increase productivity exceedances, innovation capabilities raise breakout potential, and global engagement enhances both performance levels and extreme outcome probabilities. Institutional constraints limit upper-tail performance, and financial constraints reduce exceedance rates.

When Rareness and Scarcity Interact in Resource-Based Competitive Advantage

STR
Temitope Stephen Oladimeji (University of Massachusetts Amherst)
Resource-based theory has long held that resources must be limited in supply to generate competitive advantage. Two distinct traditions emerged— Barney’s emphasis on rareness (the degree to which a resource is possessed by few competing firms) and Peteraf’s emphasis on scarcity (the degree to which aggregate demand exceeds available supply)—which Peteraf and Barney (2003) reconciled as “very nearly the same.” While this phrasing left conceptual space for differences, subsequent literature has often treated rareness and scarcity as completely interchangeable. We refine this reconciliation by showing that while functionally similar, rareness and scarcity operate through sufficiently distinct mechanisms that their interaction creates dynamics interchangeability cannot predict. When resources exhibit both characteristics, three patterns emerge: complementary (mechanisms amplify advantage beyond additive effects), substitution (context determines dominance), and conflicting (mechanisms work at cross-purposes—differentiation creating unfulfillable demand, scarcity preventing network effects, or resource allocation dilemmas reducing net advantage below either alone). Conflicts indicate mechanistic distinction within functional similarity, as identical concepts cannot conflict. We specify boundary conditions determining patterns and examine how resource origins (exogenous versus endogenous) moderate strategic flexibility. Our framework refines Peteraf and Barney's reconciliation: functional similarity dominates when one mechanism operates; mechanistic distinctions require specification when both operate simultaneously.

Does Resource Redeployment Flexibility Enhance or Mitigate Risks? Evidence from European Soccer

STR
Huanyang Xu (Peking University); Pengxiang Zhang (Peking University); Teresa Antonia Dickler (Philipps University Marburg); Dmitry Sharapov (Imperial College London)
Scholars emphasize that an organization’s ability to revise past resource deployment decisions, i.e., redeployment flexibility, has profound risk implications. Despite its conceptual appeal, the effect of redeployment flexibility remains challenging to examine, as it concerns potential rather than actual redeployment. We address this gap by leveraging a quasi-natural experiment in professional soccer, where a rule change increased substitution allowances. We find that greater redeployment flexibility raises risks of competitive erosion by 26%, partly driven by experimentation with riskier synergies. These effects arise before redeployment occurs and are amplified for organizations with more fungible resources or weaker resource endowment. We enrich resource-based perspectives by disentangling the effect of redeployment flexibility and showing how it can ultimately amplify organizational risk, even after accounting for its buffering potential.

Redeployment Costs, Organizational Structure, and the Value of Resource Redeployability

STR
Harrison John Munro-Clark (HEC Paris)
Should diversified firms centralize or decentralize resource allocation decisions? In this paper we examine how organizational structure affects the value multi-business firms derive from resource redeployability. Prior theory assumes redeployment costs dissipate after adjustment, implying that structures maximizing flexibility should dominate. Drawing on the Behavioral Theory of the Firm, we propose that redeployment can trigger cumulative political costs, side payments, policy commitments, and objective adoptions, that persist and compound over time. Using a computational model integrating real options valuation with behavioral insights, we show that optimal structure depends on the cost regime: centralization dominates under dissipating costs, but divisional structures can dominate under cumulative costs by bounding political burden accumulation. This paper introduces a temporal distinction between redeployment costs that dissipate and those that accumulate, suggests that organizational structure is a focal to managing the political costs of scope, and offers a more complete answer to when flexibility in resource deployment creates value.

Vision and Velocity: A Dynamic Model of Strategic Factor Market Advantage

STR
Daniel Thimons (University of Cincinnati)
In strategic factor markets, heterogeneous expectations explain how one firm can acquire an underpriced resource, yet assuming that an observed lone buyer means no other viable rival exists is problematic when competitors may emerge later or act faster. This study asks how a firm’s entrepreneurial insight magnitude and implementation velocity interact to influence resource pricing and how long any resulting advantage remains unique before rivals catch up. We develop an insight-velocity model by drawing on the distinction between entrepreneurial (opportunity magnitude) and managerial (execution speed) services. Our formal framework posits that a firm’s effective resource valuation equals the insight’s value magnitude discounted by a competitive shadow (the hazard of rival catch-up). We derive propositions predicting that underpricing is most likely when one firm outpaces others in the magnitude of its insight or in implementation speed, whereas even a high-magnitude insight yields little advantage if execution lags. The model explains why apparent uniqueness is fragile and how fast-moving or cross-domain entrants can compress the uniqueness window. This work contributes to resource-based theory by reframing resource uniqueness as a transient equilibrium outcome shaped by firm heterogeneity in insight and speed, and to entrepreneurship research by integrating value and temporal dimensions to clarify when and how firms secure and sustain rent-generating resource positions.
