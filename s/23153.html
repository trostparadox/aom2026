Embedding Digital Aid in Reciprocity Economies: Temporal and Relational Pathways

CTO
George Kuk (Manchester Metropolitan University); Stephanie Giamporcaro (KEDGE Business School)
Digital humanitarian aid is often seen as undermining local institutions by prioritizing immediacy and need over enduring social obligations. This study examines how indigenous savings groups (chamas) in Kenyan informal settlements integrate digital aid without eroding reciprocity-based community economies. Drawing on qualitative fieldwork around a complementary currency (CC) – digital aid initiative, we show that integration is actively accomplished through two mechanisms. Chamas temporally embed digital aid by aligning CC flows with cyclical saving and lending routines and relationally embed it by forwarding benefits through practices that extend exchange across social networks. These mechanisms transform episodic transfers into circulating resources governed by obligation and accountability. The study contributes to research on reciprocity economies and digital aid by theorizing digital infrastructure as socially governed and locally embedded.

Repurposing Digital For-Profit Platforms for the Common Good: A Process Model of Purpose Transition

CTO
Alexander Engelmann (Vienna University of Economics and Business)
Since the early 2000s, digital platform organizations have operated under de facto corporate sovereignty over users and data. However, a growing body of academic and legal evidence suggests that this authority can negatively affect consumer welfare, fair competition, and democratic interests. In response, regulatory and societal pressures are pushing incumbent platforms to reorient their purpose. Using a purpose-oriented lens, we examine how a large European B2B e-commerce platform, operating in the niche of indirect spend, undertook a multi-year process of repurposing—redefining its mission and vision to align with evolving social and regulatory expectations. Based on an in-depth five-year qualitative case study, we develop new process theory that explains how platform managers craft the transition from a growth-centric to a duty-centric purpose. We identify two interdependent sets of actions that enable repurposing: the development and implementation of a non-market strategy targeting governments and industry interest groups, and a legal strategy that seeks entrepreneurial opportunities through compliance and the anticipation of regulatory changes. Our findings further reveal that adherence to values embedded in regulation, such as neutrality and transparency, when activated through external policy discourse (e.g., EU digital regulations), can not only guide platforms toward more responsible practices but also drive differentiation relative to dominant incumbent platforms. We contribute to research at the intersection of organizational purpose and non-market strategy in the context of digital platforms.

How to Configure Software Pricing Models for Firm Performance? Evidence from Swiss Software Firms

CTO
Julie Quick (University of South Florida); Mayra Nina Spizzo (University of Bern); Sunil Mithas (University of South Florida); Jens Dibbern (University of Bern)
Software firms face growing pressure to move beyond traditional project billing toward subscription, usage-based, and value-based pricing. Current literature portrays these models as superior ways to scale digital businesses, yet firms that adopt similar “modern” pricing approaches often experience sharply different outcomes. We reframe monetization not as a standalone pricing decision but as part of digital governance: the organizational and contractual arrangements through which firms coordinate work, allocate risk, and convert knowledge into revenue. Using firm-level data from the 2024 Swiss Software Industry Survey (n = 80), we analyze how combinations of pricing structures and governance characteristics relate to labor productivity, measured as revenue per employee. We apply fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis to identify multiple configurations associated with high productivity and contrast these with configurations linked to high profitability. The results reveal strong equifinality. No single pricing model consistently delivers high productivity. Usage-based billing contributes to productivity only under specific governance conditions, either in small domestic firms with tightly scoped pricing portfolios or in larger exporters that combine usage-based billing with project-based work. Value-based billing does not appear in any high-productivity configuration. Notably, high R&D presence is absent across all productive pathways. The profitability contrast further shows that configurations associated with high revenue per employee do not necessarily yield high margins. Together, the findings caution against one-size-fits-all monetization prescriptions and highlight the importance of aligning pricing structures with governance context.

Tariff Protection and AI-Related Human Capital Investment: Evidence from the US–China Trade War

CTO
Taekyun Kim (Chungnam National University); Sukwoong Choi (University at Albany, State University of New York); Gangmin Park (Kookmin University); Namil Kim (Yonsei University)
This study examines how tariff protection shapes firms’ investments in AI-related human capital. Despite the resurgence of protectionist policies, prior research, particularly in information systems, has paid limited attention to how such interventions influence firms’ decisions to invest in emerging technologies. We argue that the tariffs imposed during the 2018 US–China trade war reduced competitive pressures for protected US firms, creating organizational slack that facilitated greater investment in AI-related human capital. Leveraging the trade war as a quasi-natural experiment, we employ a difference-in-differences approach with coarsened exact matching to compare protected firms with their unprotected counterparts. Our analysis reveals that tariff-protected firms significantly increased their investment in AI-related human capital relative to unprotected firms. We further find that this effect is heterogeneous: it is more pronounced among firms with concentrated global value chains, suggesting that higher exposure to trade shocks intensifies adaptive investment behavior. Additional mediation analyses indicate that these human capital investments serve as a precursor to subsequent digital infrastructure upgrades and are associated with improved firm performance. These findings highlight an unintended channel through which trade protectionism fosters strategic capability upgrading, demonstrating that reduced competition can paradoxically incentivize firms to accumulate the AI-related human capital essential for digital adaptation.

Trade Wars and Digital Transformation: Evidence From China

CTO
xinjun yang (Nanyang Business School); Haoyuan Liu (Nanyang Technological University); John Qi Dong (Nanyang Technological University); Anand Gopal (University of Maryland College Park)
This study investigates whether and how firms leverage digital transformation to build resilience against geopolitical uncertainty. Situating the research in the context of the 2018–2019 U.S.-China trade war, we employ a difference-in-differences (DID) design leveraging historical foreign-trade infrastructure to identify trade-sensitive provinces. We measure digital transformation using a novel BERT-based machine learning approach that captures concrete action statements in firm annual reports. Our findings demonstrate that the trade war significantly accelerates digital transformation among Chinese firms. This strategic move addresses disruptions through three channels: expanding domestic market access, improving demand forecasting accuracy, and fostering autonomous innovation. Furthermore, we find that provincial “technocrats” amplify this response, while internal managerial technical expertise plays a negligible role in the initial decision to transform. Taken together, this research highlights geopolitical uncertainty as a critical antecedent of digital transformation.
