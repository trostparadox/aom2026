Wayfinding as Strategy-as-Practice

SAP
Alina Bas (New York University); Viktor Dorfler (University of Strathclyde)
How do organizations strategize when maps, plans, and routines fail? Drawing on lived accounts from clinicians during the first wave of COVID-19 and knowledge workers in finance, technology, and engineering, we conceptualize wayfinding as a distinct form of strategizing under uncertainty. Unlike navigating, which presupposes reliable maps, or autopiloting, which relies on routinized behavior, wayfinding unfolds through sensing, learning, and improvising in action. Our empirical analysis reveals six intertwined aspects of wayfinding: sensing and sensemaking, learning in action, cross-validating the outcomes of sensing with sequential analysis, dropping what no longer works, improvising, and constructing narratives. We theorize wayfinding as an embodied and narrative-based practice that enables progress when aims are articulated vaguely at most and paths must be discovered along the way. By contrasting wayfinding with navigating and autopiloting, we reframe strategizing as a dynamic repertoire the effectiveness of which depends on strategy– context fit. The paper contributes to Strategy-as-Practice (SAP) by conceptualizing wayfinding as a novel practice of strategizing in uncertainty and extreme contexts. It also extends the literature on emergent strategizing by showing how strategies crystallize through embodied sensing and improvisational adjustment in real time, rather than only retrospectively as patterns.

Our New Normal: How Objects Strategize Responses to Extreme Events

SAP
Laura Albareda (LUT University); Oana Branzei (Ivey Business School)
This inductive study extends SAP by using the social-symbolic lens to elaborate on the distinct roles objects play in strategizing in response to extreme events. Using a multi-modal 3-year ethnography of a first response unit facing uncertain futures, we model the recursive relationship between strategic objects and strategists. We explain how objects perform proximal futures through fast strategizing cycles during extreme events and perform distal futures through slow strategizing cycles between extreme events. Our findings extend SAP to settings where events once considered extreme have now become normalized by showing how strategic objects manage both attention and comprehension in ever shifting onto-epistemological landscapes.

Dancing on the Edge: Turning Adversity into Strategic Renewal via Dramaturgical Reframing

SAP
Renate Kratochvil (Stockholm School of Economics); Jeanine Pieternel Porck (Oklahoma State University)
Unexpected adversity can mean the downfall of a business—unless managers grasp its serendipitous potential and convert it into strategic advantage. Even then, however, new strategies are not always successful. Prior literature offers limited insight into how serendipity is performed such that it becomes a site of successful strategic renewal. Drawing on extensive archival data on Taylor Swift’s trajectory as a singer, songwriter, and business leader, we show how she grasps disruptions, turns them into episodes of serendipity, and leverages them for strategic renewal. Through a four-phase process, we reveal that her success lies not only in introducing new strategies, but in making them appear, in hindsight, as a coherent trajectory. We present practices of retrospective– prospective performative reframing as a dramaturgical process through which Swift, in her role as a strategic actor, performs renewal and establish strategic coherence. Swift reactivates past reputational frames in the present and reframes them through performance, while simultaneously using musical innovation to steer into a new strategy, thus making renewal feel like continuity. We show that her enduring success derives from her ability to stage new strategies after adversity and retrospectively integrate disruption into a strategically coherent strategic trajectory. This study contributes to research on serendipity, dramaturgical performance, and practices for strategic renewal.

Enacting Shifts in Work Rhythms in Extreme Contexts: Introducing Organizational Allostatic Load

SAP
Fannie Couture (HEC Montréal); Paula Jarzabkowski (The University of Queensland)
Organizing in extreme contexts rarely means that people work in extremism all the time; they enact different work rhythms and must learn to shift from one to another. While we have a fairly good idea of the challenges involved in shifting from idle to extreme, we know relatively little about how people embody these shifts in extreme contexts. We examine this puzzle through an in-depth case study of Pacific City’s disaster management center, a city-based initiative responsible for ensuring adequate disaster prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery for its community. Adopting a practice-based view of shifting work rhythm and extremeness, we show how the enactment of multiple transitions from idle to extreme can over time lead to organizational allostatic load, i.e. the cumulative strain that accrues within an organization when its members must repeatedly adjust their emotional, temporal, and bodily states to shifting work rhythms, producing a collective misalignment between idealized patterns of functioning and the rhythms enacted in practice, resulting in embodied struggles. Drawing on these insights, we develop a conceptual process model of how efforts to address the rising complexity of “natural” disasters can generate individual struggles that ultimately translate into collective dynamics which can impede the ability to organize around extreme events.

The Feel of Time: Emotional Regimes of Industrial Decline

SAP
Jarmo Taskinen (University of Jyväskylä); Roosa Oinasmaa (Tampere University of Applied Sciences); Antti Sihvonen
When industries face existential decline, decision-makers struggle to navigate uncertainty using narrative schemas built on past successes. While research has examined cognitive rigidities in strategic change, the role of emotions in constructing temporal relationships between an industry’s past, present, and future remains underexplored. Drawing on 101 inductive interviews with elite members of the Finnish pulp and paper industry, we investigate how emotions shape strategic outlooks during industrial decline. We find that emotions act as a constitutive force in guiding how leaders prioritize signals across temporal horizons. Our study identifies the emergence of broader industry-level emotional-cognitive patterns and introduces the concept of emotional regimes that construct strategic outlooks during periods of decline. These shared affective patterns link past experiences, current challenges, and future expectations, anchoring collective cognition over long-term decline.
