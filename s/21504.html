Session overview

Employers function as hidden social policy actors, shaping workers' economic security and well-being through decisions about wages, benefits, and scheduling. Yet management scholarship rarely examines how workplace practices intersect with social policy and public benefit programs. At one healthcare system, screening of the social determinants of health of 1,932 full-time employees revealed that 11% experienced food insecurity, 12% financial strain, and 10% inability to pay for housing or utilities, with rates higher among administrative support staff. Such workers may rely on public programs such as SNAP or Medicaid; however, they may face administrative burden, defined as the learning costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs citizens experience when interacting with government. Work conditions can amplify or buffer this burden. A compounded burden often results when public policy shifts risk onto citizens through burdensome eligibility processes while employer practices further shift risk onto workers through volatile hours, inadequate benefits, and managerial discretion. These burdens fall unequally on marginalized workers and nontraditional households who navigate eligibility systems designed around married-couple family structures. In this symposium, panelists and attendees will address these challenges by sharing their research, experiences, and organizational best practices such as employee social needs screening and employer-based social supports. With this aim, we look to illuminate the workplace-policy interface to develop research and practice that bridge management scholarship with social work and public policy to mitigate poverty and inequality, as well as enhance employee well-being and their economic safety net.
