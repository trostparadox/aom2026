Hypocritical But Practical: Moral Reasoning in Platform-Based Gig Work

OB
Hanh Thi Duc Doan-Voutilainen (Aalto University)
Unethical behaviors at work are costly for organizations and society, as evidenced in some renowned scandals such as the collapse of Enron. While majority of prior research has been conducted within traditional organizational contexts, there is an increasing need to understand unethical behavior in rapidly growing nontraditional work environments such as gig work. In this paper, I explore moral decision-making processes of workers within platform-based gig work, aiming to understand the kinds of moral dilemmas platform workers encounter and how they navigate these dilemmas. Through a qualitative study of app-based food delivery workers in Finland, I identify two distinct prototypical moral dilemmas: rule-breaking, with well-defined moral boundaries, and labor outsourcing, with more ambiguous moral character. To navigate these dilemmas, workers engage in different reasoning modes, including externalizing blame, motivated reasoning, and compromising. While rule-breaking typically involves externalizing blame, labor outsourcing often involves motivated reasoning and compromising. I also highlight situational factors that amplify these behaviors. This study contributes to research on moral decision-making by uncovering mechanisms that explain platform workers’ engagement in morally questionable behaviors. In addition, this study expands our understanding of moral disengagement, showing that platform workers approach moral dilemmas through motivated reasoning and compromising.

How Monochromatic Work Environment Affects Employees’ Recovery: A Boundary Theory Perspective

OB
Hongli Wang; Xixia Zhang (South China University of Technology); Jialiang Pei
Monochromatic work environment is pervasive yet under-theorized aesthetic strategies in contemporary organizations. Drawing on boundary theory and moving beyond perspectives that treat color as a transient stimulus, we theorize the monochromatic work environment as a boundary cue that embodies organizational identity. We argue that such environments help employees enact work-life boundaries and, in turn, support their recovery after work. We further introduce work-life environmental color inconsistency as a key boundary condition. When this inconsistency is high, the monochromatic work environment enhances recovery through boundary enactment, whereas low inconsistency causes the same environment to impair recovery. Evidence from a three-wave field study (Study 1) and a scenario-based experiment (Study 2) supports this model. Taken together, our research portrays organizational color use as a deliberate visual design strategy with important cross-domain consequences. It shows how the symbolic management of space shapes the work-life interface.

Color Matters! The Influence of Monochromatic Work Environment on Innovation

OB
Hongli Wang; Xixia Zhang (South China University of Technology)
The prevailing view has suggested that long-term exposure to the visually monotonous environments is detrimental to employee innovative behavior. However, based on trait activation theory and the dichotomy of innovative behavior, we explore how monochromatic work environments can stimulate employees’ sense of order to promote exploitative innovative behavior while simultaneously inhibiting exploratory innovative behavior. Additionally, we further examine the moderating role of employees’ personal workspace color personalization in this process. Our perspective is validated through a mixed-method approach consisting of a three-wave field study in a large manufacturing enterprise (Study 1) and a scenario experiment (Study 2). In doing so, we provide more nuanced insights into the relationship between the monochromatic work environment and employee innovative behaviors.

Leadership Effectiveness in Virtual Work: A Dyadic Conceptualization and Test of Leader Distance

OB
FUHE JIN (The College of New Jersey); Biying Yang (South Dakota State University)
Virtual work has become a central organizing feature of contemporary organizations, yet empirical findings on leadership effectiveness in virtual contexts remain mixed. We argue that these inconsistencies stem from treating virtuality as a structural condition rather than as a relational and interpretive experience. Integrating leader distance theory with sensemaking theory, we conceptualize leader distance as a dyadic condition reflected in the degree of alignment between leaders’ and followers’ perceptions of social distance and interaction frequency. We examine how such alignment shapes the effectiveness of leader–member exchange (LMX) in predicting follower task performance in virtual work settings. Using multisource, time-lagged data from 226 leader–follower dyads in remote sales teams, we employ polynomial regression and response surface methodology to model congruence and incongruence between leader- and follower-rated LMX and to test the moderating role of distance agreement. Results show that follower task performance increases as leader and follower perceptions of LMX align at higher levels. Moreover, agreement on interaction frequency and social distance strengthens the positive relationship between LMX and performance relative to disagreement. Importantly, these effects are asymmetric across distance dimensions: agreement on higher interaction frequency amplifies the performance benefits of LMX, whereas agreement on lower social distance is more beneficial than agreement on higher social distance. By reframing distance as a dyadic sensemaking condition, this study advances theories of virtual leadership, highlighting that leadership effectiveness in virtual work depends not only on relational quality but also on shared interpretations of distance.
