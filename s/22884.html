Navigating Fear: How Threat Sources and Cooperative Goals Shape Helping Behavior in Teams

OB
Xiaoling YANG (Ritsumeikan Asia Pacific University); Jie Wang (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Hongxu Lu; Xiangjia Tang (Yunnan Normal University)
Under the backdrop of an economic downturn and artificial intelligence taking over jobs, employees frequently experience a sense of fear. Given the increasingly prevalent emotion of fear, there is no definitive consensus on whether it inclines employees to behave more selfishly or to become more engaged with the collective. This study explores the complex relationship between fear and helping behavior in team settings, drawing on the functional model of emotions and Lebel’s (2016) distinction of fear sources. We categorize fear sources as personal threats, which trigger self-interested motivation, and collective threats, which promote prosocial motivation. Our research posits that team characteristics, particularly the level of cooperative goal, significantly influence how these motivations manifest in helping behavior. We argue that high levels of team cooperative goal can strengthen fear-driven prosocial motivation’s positive effects and weaken its self-interest-motivated negative effects on helping behavior. This study contributes to understanding the nuanced role of fear in workplace interactions and highlights the importance of contextual factors in facilitating helping behavior within teams. By addressing the inconsistencies in previous findings regarding fear and helping, our research provides valuable insights for organizations aiming to develop cooperative teams in challenging environments.

The Nomological Network of Multiple Team Membership: An Individual-Level Meta-Analysis

OB
Damian Claudio Zivic (Indiana University); Michael Jared Emanuel Nugent (Indiana University); Tammy L. Rapp (Ohio University); Jaclyn Ann Margolis; Ozias A. Moore (Lehigh University); Ernest O'Boyle (Indiana University Bloomington)
Multiple team membership (MTM) is an increasingly common work arrangement in modern organizations. Although two decades of research have highlighted the double-edged nature of MTM, inconsistent operationalizations and limited integration across studies have hindered consensus on how and when MTM affects employees. To provide a stronger foundation for future theory and research, we meta-analyzed MTM relationships to a variety of individual-level constructs (k = 140, N = 116,896; 2,974 effects). We found that number of teams one is on negatively relates to job resources, positive well-being, and attitudinal outcomes, positively relates to job demands, negative well-being, and performance, and possesses ambiguous (mixed) relationships to withdrawal outcomes. Finally, we test whether MTM relationships differ as a function of gender, profession, and cultural context. Our comprehensive synthesis of the MTM literature clarifies the complex effects of MTM and offers a sound empirical foundation for future theory and managerial practices concerning MTM arrangements.

The Compromising Effects of Less Inclusive Team Members on Psychological Safety

OB
Michael White (Columbia Business School); Mabel Abraham (Columbia Business School); Sandra Matz
Fostering psychological safety is critical for promoting learning, innovation, and performance in organizations. In the present research, we deepen our understanding of psychological safety by studying how inclusivity among team members affects an individual’s perception of psychological safety. We integrate group composition and error management theory to argue that psychological safety is particularly compromised by those team members who are not reliably inclusive. Examining the relationship between the other-rated inclusivity of an individual’s team members and the subjective experience of an individual’s psychological safety in 414 randomly assigned project teams (n = 1,947), we find support for this proposition. While higher overall team inclusivity is positively related to psychological safety, the presence of even a single less-inclusive team member has an outsized negative effect on how psychologically safe individual team members feel. That is, the presence of a less-inclusive team member not only lowers psychological safety independently of average team inclusivity but also tempers, or even eliminates, the expected positive effects of otherwise inclusive teammates. Our findings also suggest that the impact of less-inclusive team members on psychological safety is particularly detrimental for individuals with token status (i.e., the only member of their racial group).

How Performance-Oriented Attributions Shape the Team Impact of Leader Pro-Group Unethical Behavior

OB
Yue Yuan (The University of Hong Kong); Zhiming Wu (Tsinghua University); Qi Zhang
Unlike employees, when leaders engage in pro-group unethical behaviors, they are more likely to produce team-level effects. However, existing research on leader pro-group unethical behavior has primarily focused on individual-level reactions, leaving its impact on team-level outcomes unclear. Drawing on attribution theory and social identity theory, this study addresses this gap by developing a moderated mediation model to examine how leader pro-group unethical behavior influences team performance. Using two-wave survey data from 407 employees and 101 leaders, we found that leader pro-group unethical behavior negatively affects team performance through reduced leader identification. This negative indirect effect was stronger when employees attributed leaders’ behaviors to self-performance motives, but was attenuated (and even reversed) when employees attributed leaders’ behaviors to team-performance motives. This study extends research on leader pro-group unethical behavior to the team level and reveals how performance-oriented motive attributions resolve the dual nature paradox by determining whether employees focus on the unethical or pro-group aspects of such behaviors.
