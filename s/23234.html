A Process Model Of Entrepreneurial Framing, Investor Response And Fundraising Performance

ENT
Yuxuan Dai (Tilburg University); Jiahe Wang (Tilburg University); Qingyun Lu (Shanghai University)
Entrepreneurs frequently employ framing strategies when pitching ventures, yet the optimal approach across different stages of entrepreneur-investor interactions remains unclear. Under conditions of incomplete information, unknowable uncertainty, and time pressure, investors typically move from forming initial impressions to probing concerns during Q&A sessions. This study investigates how entrepreneurs can strategically utilize framing to guide investors’ decision-making and improve pitching performance, arguing that prioritizing abstract framing in the presentation stage is critical. Analyzing 177 televised pitches from “We Are the Hero,” a Chinese youth entrepreneurial pitching reality show involving 1584 entrepreneur–investor dyads, we find that greater abstract framing during the initial presentation is associated with increased feasibility-focused investor queries in the subsequent Q&A, which, in turn, is linked to higher investment intentions. Moreover, the translation of feasibility queries into investment intentions depends on entrepreneurs’ response framing: more informative and concrete responses strengthen this relationship. Our study contributes to entrepreneurial finance and framing research by demonstrating how entrepreneurs can strategically manage heterogeneous investor evaluations through stage-structured interactions, highlighting the process through which initial framing shapes investor information search and ultimately influences investment intentions.

Dueling Missions?: A Contextual Model of Investor Support for Hybrid Ventures

ENT
Vyas Sreenivas (Santa Clara University); Todd Schifeling (Temple University)
Hybrid ventures face challenges in attracting investment due to their dual missions. Investors may penalize hybrids because these firms prioritize other stakeholders. Using panel data on 26,420 ventures (2010-2018), we examine how the type of nonfinancial mission (social vs. environmental), founder background, community context, and investor proximity shape fundraising outcomes. Each of these factors shapes whether investors perceive alignment or conflict between the venture’s financial and nonfinancial. The findings contribute to research on hybrid organizing, social and environmental entrepreneurship, and the geography of resource acquisition.

Seeing Through the Story: Investor Empathy and Expectancy Violations in Entrepreneurial Narratives

ENT
Katrina Brownell (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Indunil Prabodha Dharmasiri (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Hype has become a defining force in entrepreneurship, especially in nascent and high-growth markets where entrepreneurs are expected to project confidence, passion, and the transformative potential of a new venture. At the same time, underdog framing – wherein entrepreneurs emphasize adversity, constraints, or relative disadvantages – has emerged as potentially effective, leaving open the question of when and for whom each may help or harm. Drawing on expectancy violation theory, we propose that hype and underdog narratives represent strategic expectancy violations that differentially influence investment intentions – effects that are channeled by trust and shaped by investor empathy. Across three randomized experiments (N = 166, 326, 598), we generally find support for our predictions. Taken together, our work reframes hype and underdog narrative strategies as audience-dependent expectancy violations, extends entrepreneurship applications of EVT, and highlights investor empathy as a crucial socio-cognitive lens through which narrative strategies translate into resource allocation.

Sparking the Deal: The Role of Value Proposition in Driving Investor Decision-Making

ENT
Scott Shane (Case Western Reserve University); Kwanrat Suanpong (Chulalongkorn University); Santhaya Kitikowit (Chulalongkorn University); Sebastiano Massaro (University of Surrey)
We conducted a randomized controlled trial to investigate the effect of including value proposition information in pitch decks on investor interest in seed-stage startups. We manipulated the presence of a single additional slide containing value proposition information (i.e., the treatment) in otherwise identical pitch decks of real startups. Our findings indicate that including one “value proposition” slide has economically substantive and statistically significant positive effects, which are consistent across various companies. Given the randomized controlled trial design, we conclude that providing value proposition information increases investor interest in new ventures.

Words Worth Millions? A Quasi-Replication of Gendered Language Impact on Entrepreneurial Pitching

ENT
Elisabeth S.C. Berger (Johannes Kepler University Linz); Yuhang Cui (Johannes Kepler University Linz); Theresa Übleis (Johannes Kepler University Linz)
Gendered language is a salient but understudied factor in entrepreneurial fundraising, particularly outside English-speaking contexts. This study investigates how gendered linguistic styles and explicit investor address influence funding outcomes in entrepreneurial pitches delivered in German, a grammatically gendered language. Building on Balachandra et al. (2021), who found that women and men employ masculine and feminine discursive styles at similar rates in English pitches, we replicate and extend their analysis to 269 German-language pitches, including both solo and team presentations. Our findings reveal that gendered language and investor addressing act as strategic cues that shape investor evaluations, with effects distinct from those documented in English-language settings. By leveraging both lexical and semantic analyses, we demonstrate that linguistic strategies are culturally contingent and embedded in local norms. This research advances understanding of the communicative mechanisms underlying gender gaps in entrepreneurial finance and highlights the importance of context-sensitive approaches to studying language, gender, and resource acquisition.
