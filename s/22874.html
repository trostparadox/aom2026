Leadership Through the Lens of Social Comparison: A Multilevel Integrative Review

OB
Sabreen Kaur (Monash University); Nathan Eva (Monash University); Herman Tse (Monash University)
Social comparison theory in leadership has garnered increasing attention due to its influence on leaders’ behaviours, leader–follower dynamics, and follower outcomes, driving team and organisational effectiveness. Social comparisons occur between leaders and followers, among followers of the same leader, and between leaders of teams and organisations. Thus, social comparison theory within leadership is inherently multilevel. Yet, the majority of the existing literature focuses narrowly on the individual level, sidelining the multilevel features of this phenomenon. The overemphasis is partly due to how scholars have integrated complementary theories with the social comparison theory (e.g., social identity) and the methodologies utilised (e.g., single-source surveys). The result has been a fragmented literature with minimal multilevel theoretical development and constrained empirical insights, hindering the broader application of social comparison theory to understand leadership phenomena across other levels. As a catalyst for progress, we develop a multilevel framework, synthesising findings from 118 studies across five levels of analysis: within-person, individual, dyadic, team, and organisational. We also address fundamental challenges that have hindered the development of multilevel theory by analysing critical theoretical and methodological pitfalls in existing research and proposing promising avenues for future studies to deepen our multilevel understanding of leadership and social comparisons.

Speaking Strategically: Managing Impressions Through Voice

OB
Tory D. A. Taylor (University of Nevada-Reno); Kathryn A. Coll; Alexis Hanna
Theory suggests that impression management motives should influence how people frame their voice at work. Because problem-focused (prohibitive) voice evokes less favorable reactions than solution-focused (promotive) voice (Chamberlin et al., 2017), those concerned with their image should favor solution-focused approaches. We tested this relationship across two studies. In Study 1 (N=703 university employees), psychological safety and perceived impact predicted both forms of voice, but impression management did not moderate these effects or differentially predict prohibitive versus promotive voice. Impression management did, however, positively predict overall voice likelihood. In Study 2 (N=218 Prolific workers), we measured voice framing using a continuum ranging from exclusively problem-focused to exclusively solution-focused, allowing participants to indicate within-episode blending of voice strategies. Results revealed that 86% of participants would blend problem-focused and solution-focused framing rather than use one approach exclusively. Specific impression management tactics (self-promotion, exemplification) again predicted voice likelihood, but impression management did not predict where participants fell on the framing continuum. Together, these findings suggest that impression management may shape the likelihood of voice, but not whether messages are framed as problem-focused, solution-focused, or blended. Further, voice framing may be more fluid than is typically captured by categorical measures.

Guardians of Trust: How First Sergeants Sustain Psychological Safety in the U.S. Air Force

OB
Omaima Hajjami (Louisiana State University); Bethany A. Hager (Louisiana State University); Petra Alaine Robinson (Louisiana State University); Jennifer Cook (Louisiana State University)
Psychological safety is critical for learning, trust, and performance, yet remains difficult to cultivate within hierarchical military organizations characterized by high power distance and risk. This qualitative study explores how U.S. Air Force First Sergeants cultivate psychological safety within their units and how they perceive the support systems that enable this work. Drawing on semi-structured interviews with 19 First Sergeants, the study employs an interpretive thematic analysis to examine leadership practices, relational labor, and organizational conditions shaping psychologically safe climates. Findings reveal that psychological safety is enacted through sustained presence, mentoring, transparent communication, shared experiences, and proactive intervention, rather than formal policy alone. Participants also highlighted the importance of multilevel support systems, including peer networks and informal resources, alongside persistent cultural barriers such as stigma and limited leadership preparation. The study extends psychological safety scholarship into high-reliability, hierarchical contexts and underscores the central role of relational leadership in sustaining readiness, resilience, and well-being in military organizations.

Power as Interpretation: Construal, Psychological Distance, Climate, and Instigated Incivility

OB
Aastha Tripathi (Tata Institute of Social Sciences); Sasmita Palo (Tata Institute of Social Sciences)
This paper develops a meaning-based model explaining when and how a sense of power translates into workplace incivility. Moving beyond magnitude-based accounts of power, we theorize that psychological distance operates as a central cognitive mechanism through which perceived influence reshapes interpersonal conduct. Critically, we introduce power construal—whether power is framed as discretionary opportunity or relational responsibility, as a first-stage moderator that conditions whether distancing processes emerge in the first place. When power is construed as opportunity, heightened self-focus and abstraction amplify psychological distance, increasing the likelihood of instigated incivility. When power is construed as responsibility, relational attunement constrains distancing despite elevated influence. By integrating research on power, construal processes, and interpersonal mistreatment, the model clarifies why similar levels of perceived power yield divergent social consequences. The framework advances theorizing on the cognitive organization of influence, specifies boundary conditions on power’s distancing effects, and identifies intervention points for sustaining civility without undermining the functional benefits of power in organizations.
