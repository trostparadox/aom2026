Author: Marilla Hayman Le Moyne College

Session overview

Despite growing scholarly attention to bodily experiences at work (e.g., pregnancy, breastfeeding), menstruation remains largely absent from organizational research—even though it affects roughly half the workforce on a recurring basis. Menstruation is highly stigmatized and typically concealed, limiting employees’ willingness to disclose symptoms or seek accommodations and constraining managers’ ability to provide support. Drawing on theories of concealable stigmatized identities and disruptive self-disclosure, this research examines how supervisors can reduce disclosure barriers by engaging in vulnerable self-disclosure themselves. Across a qualitative interview study and a preregistered experiment, I show that supervisor vulnerability increases perceived supervisor status and affective trust, which in turn promote menstruating employees’ disclosure and accommodation-seeking intentions. The findings reframe supervisor vulnerability as a status-enhancing, relational tool and extend disclosure theory to embodied, cyclical stigmas at work.
