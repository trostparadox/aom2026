Session overview

Management education does not travel seamlessly across borders. It encounters cultural discontinuities, institutional gaps, and pedagogical assumptions that are tested the moment students cross into unfamiliar territory. This session examines how soft skills performatively reshape business realities across UK-China cultural crossings, how students themselves perceive the difference between study abroad and traditional learning, how short-term international programs can organically elicit critical reflection without deliberate scaffolding, how large-scale global virtual team projects like X-Culture prepare students for a genuinely global workplace, and how entrepreneurial education ecosystem thinking is spreading globally from the ground up. A rich, internationally grounded session that takes the global seriously.

The Performative Role of Soft Skills Education: Reshaping Business Realities Through Cross-Cultural

MED
Jie Wu (University of Aberdeen); Juan Ignacio Canales (University of Aberdeen); Wensong Bai (Shenzhen MSU-BIT University)
This study examines how soft skills education performatively shapes business realities across cultural boundaries. While prior research has treated soft skills as static capabilities, we demonstrate their role as performative forces actively reshaping organizational practices. Through in-depth interviews with Chinese graduates of UK universities working in China, our findings reveal an inverted U-shaped relationship between structural performativity and organizational transformation, moderated by collective orientation (weakening effect) and absorptive capacity (strengthening effect) and pinpoint six key domains through which soft skills education acts as a performative mechanism. Our findings suggest that successful organizational transformation through soft skills education requires balancing international connections, cultural values, and knowledge integration capabilities. The study advances performativity theory in management education by demonstrating how educational practices actively construct organizational realities rather than merely describing them and provides insights for business schools on effectively harnessing performativity to create desirable organizational outcomes in cross-cultural contexts.

Student Perceptions of the Differences in Learning from Study Abroad to Traditional Courses (WITHDRAWN)

MED
Kent Walker (University of Windsor); Francine Schlosser (University of Windsor); Marcia Carvalho De Azevedo
Study abroad courses offer unique opportunities for students that both differ and complement traditional courses. We interviewed 25 students that participated in various short-term faculty-led study abroad courses offered at a Canadian university. Participant retrospectives noted differences in study abroad compared to traditional courses across 10 themes: (1) improved engagement; (2) improved retention; (3) access to better resources in the host versus home country; (4) improved quality of work; (5) increased confidence; (6) increased respect, connection and commitment for the academic institution, (7) the role of the professor, (8) the role of other students, (9) significant learning experiences; and (10) employability and career readiness. Our results provide support for the use of study abroad courses to enhance student learning, engagement, confidence and employability, with the potential to provide self-described life-changing experiences.

A Guiding Framework for Instructor Effectiveness in Global Collaborations Using Global Virtual Teams

MED
Antonis Klidas (Deree College, The American College of Greece); Ernesto Tavoletti (University of Macerata); Daria Panina (Texas A&M University); Vas Taras (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
Global virtual team-based projects (GVTs) are increasingly used in international business education to prepare students for today’s workplace, where work in globally dispersed virtual project teams is common. This study explores the teaching practices of educators participating in X-Culture, the most prominent example of large-scale international collaboration employing student GVTs. In the context of our ongoing qualitative study, we report findings from interviews with 35 X-Culture educators from 17 countries. Data analysis based on the Gioia method highlights the importance of increasing the assessment weight of X-Culture in course design and maintaining a stronger focus on process rather than task output. Based on our findings, we provide recommendations for educators participating in intercultural virtual collaborative projects, such as X-Culture, to enhance instructional effectiveness and benefit student learning.

Critical Reflection Emerges Organically During Short-Term Study Abroad

MED
Sara Hendrick (University of Northern Colorado); Kyle E Ingram (University of California Riverside)
This research examines the hypothesis that women’s work identity is high, regardless of parenthood status, whereas men’s work identity is contingent on parenthood. We posit that women prioritize personal fulfillment while men emphasize salary at work, and that these different motives shape work identity. Across two studies, we find consistent support for this pattern. In a nationally representative sample (Study 1), women reported stronger work identity than men, partially explained by women’s greater motivation for personal fulfillment. In a study of partnered employees (Study 2), women’s work identity was strong regardless of parenthood status, while fathers reported stronger work identity than non-fathers.
