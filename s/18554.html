When Adaptation Hurts: Language, Life Satisfaction, and the Inner Dissonance of Discrimination

Saturday, Aug 1 2026 · 10:00AM–11:00AM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 3
DEI

People

Authors: Eun-Ji (Raina) Oh Korean Women's Development Institute; Youngsang Kim Sungkyunkwan University; Carrie Jihyun Song Sungkyunkwan University

Session overview

Drawing on self-discrepancy and social identity theories, this study examines how workplace discrimination affects the life satisfaction of foreign-born employees by generating inner dissonance between adaptation and belonging. While international human resource management (IHRM) research has predominantly focused on expatriates and inpatriates engaged in temporary assignments, far less is known about foreign-born employees who permanently integrate into host-country labor markets. Contrary to the prevailing view that nonwork satisfaction buffers strain in the work domain, we argue that it can paradoxically intensify the detrimental effects of workplace discrimination, deepening psychological drain rather than replenishing it. Using 1,083 foreign-born employees from South Korea, the results reveal a negative association between workplace discrimination and job satisfaction, and further show that nonwork satisfaction exacerbates this relationship, indicating a cross-domain spillover of psychological strain rather than a replenishment effect. Contrary to theoretical expectations, local language proficiency does not exacerbate this effect. This study contributes to IHRM research and highlights the complex psychological costs of adaptation in discriminatory work environments for foreign-born employees.
