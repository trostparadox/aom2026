Meaningful Work Begins With Search: How Job Search Quality Translates Goal Orientation to Meaningful

CAR
Anh Thi Phuong Nguyen (Vlerick Business School); Dirk Buyens; David Leonard Patient (Vlerick Business School)
Understanding how young graduates achieve meaningful work in their first job is crucial for career counselling practices. Whereas most research has investigated organizational influences on meaningful work, this research looks at the individual's role in this process. Utilizing a three-wave dataset from 285 university students in Vietnam, we examine whether learning and performance job search goal orientations influence meaningful work attainment through the job search quality process, and how this relationship is moderated by social support. Our findings reveal that the positive indirect effect of learning job search goal orientation on meaningful work via job search quality is significant only for individuals perceiving medium to high social support. These results underscore the importance of providing social support to enhance job search quality and facilitate the attainment of meaningful work, particularly for those with a learning orientation. This study extends meaningful work literature by integrating goal orientation theory and self-regulatory perspectives.

The Emotional Ecology of Meaningful Work: How Emotional Diversity Sustains and Amplifies Meaning

CAR
Elaf Basri (King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals); Susan E. Murphy (The University of Edinburgh); Yumeng Yue (The University of Edinburgh)
Contemporary careers unfold under persistent emotional complexity, raising a central question: how do people sustain meaningful work as roles, demands, and setbacks evolve? We advance an emotional account of meaningfulness sustainability by conceptualizing global emotional diversity (emodiversity), the variety and evenness of discrete emotions, as an emotional ecology that shapes how work is appraised as significant and worthwhile. Across two complementary designs, we show that emotional diversity predicts meaningful work above and beyond mean positive and negative affect. Study 1 uses a two-wave, long-horizon design drawing on a representative sample of the MIDUS dataset (approximately five years apart) to demonstrate that individuals with higher average emotional diversity report higher meaningful work over time, net of affective valence and personality. Study 2 uses a 15-day experience-sampling design to show that day-level emotional diversity predicts daily meaningful work and systematically shapes how emotional valence translates into meaning in real time. Results converge to reveal a double-edged pattern: emotional diversity is positively associated with meaningful work overall and over time, yet it also functions as a salience amplifier, strengthening the positive association between positive emotions and meaningful work while intensifying the negative association between negative emotions and meaningful work. By distinguishing repertoire-based emotional diversity from conflict-, volatility-, and shift-based forms of emotional complexity, we clarify when emotional diversity sustains versus erodes meaningful work. We discuss implications for career sustainability, showing how emotional ecology can support meaning endurance while increasing sensitivity to affective strain under constrained or chronically negative conditions.

Securing Legitimacy Through Meaningfulness in the Corporate Social Responsibility Professional Field

CAR
Hyemi Shin (Royal Holloway, University of London); Mai Chi Vu (Monash University)
This study examines how professionals in an emerging and unsettled occupational field claim professional legitimacy amid intra-professional competition. Focusing on Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) professionals in South Korea (hereafter Korea), we analyze narratives of meaningful work drawn from interviews with 32 CSR professionals. We show that meaningful work functions as a key discursive resource through which CSR professionals construct professional legitimacy in the face of field expansion and the influx of newcomers. Our analysis identifies two interrelated narrative pathways through which legitimacy is claimed: hardship-based narratives, in which individual endurance is rendered meaningful by alignment with a shared collective past, and value-based narratives, in which personal moral congruence gains legitimacy through affirmation within a morally committed in-group and contrast with perceived opportunistic outsiders. Through these pathways, self-connection and unification pathways of meaningful work mutually constitute and validate one another. This study contributes to research on professional legitimacy by demonstrating how meaningfulness operates as a moral and narrative resource in intra-professional legitimacy struggles within emerging fields. It also advances the meaningful work literature by theorizing a relational interplay between self-connection and unification, an underexplored dynamic in existing research.

Eudaimonia at Work: The Development and Validation of the Eudaimonic Well-Being Behavior Scale

CAR
Susan Kinderen (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Sergey Gorbatov (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Wenjing Cai
Organizational well-being research has often focused on helping employees “feel good” at work, while paying less attention to the role of “doing good”— the eudaimonic aspect of well-being. This study develops and validates a new measure of eudaimonic well-being behaviors (EWBB) at work. Grounded in self-determination theory and the eudaimonic activity model, we conceptualize EWBB as comprising three dimensions: pursuit of purpose, positive relationships, and personal growth behaviors. Across five diverse samples (total N = 1,767), we establish the scale’s factorial structure, internal consistency, and construct and criterion validity. We demonstrate EWBB’s distinctiveness from related constructs and its incremental predictive power for flourishing, work engagement, and performance. A three-wave study of employed adults confirms that EWBB predicts both well-being and performance outcomes, including indirect effects through work engagement. The EWBB scale offers researchers and practitioners a validated tool to study and support behaviors that foster sustainable psychological well-being and effectiveness at work.
