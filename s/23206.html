Design Identity: Linking Self-Concept to Design Practice

ENT
Michelle Pannone (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Design thinking has become a widely adopted approach for innovation in both organizational practice and entrepreneurship research, yet much of the literature has emphasized macro-level outcomes while overlooking the microfoundations that shape design practices at the individual level. This paper introduces design identity as a construct for understanding how individuals see themselves as designers and how this self-concept influences engagement with design processes. Drawing on identity theory and microfoundations research, I propose six elements of design identity, Visionary, Connector, Design Process Manager, Design Leader, Implementor, and Technical Contributor. This conceptual work contributes to design and entrepreneurship literature by advancing a microfoundational perspective that connects identity to design behavior, establishing a basis for future research.

Economic Policy Uncertainty and Entrepreneurial Strategic Change: Pivot or Strategic Addition?

ENT
Ruilu Yang (University of Science and Technology of China); Weiliang Zhang (University of Science and Technology of China); Yiwen Zhong (University of Pittsburgh)
Entrepreneurial strategic change has received growing attention from entrepreneurship scholars. Previous literature on entrepreneurial strategic change has emphasized endogenous uncertainty as the primary driver of such change, whereas environmental uncertainty has been largely treated as a contextual condition and remains underexplored. This study examines how a representative type of environmental uncertainty - economic policy uncertainty - can affect the likelihood of two types of industry-related entrepreneurial strategic change (dominant business pivot and business strategic addition). We propose that economic policy uncertainty increases inducement factors for entrepreneurial strategic change while weakening the capability factors required for implementing such change. Therefore, economic policy uncertainty can discourage dominant business pivots since this change requires stronger capabilities. In contrast, economic policy uncertainty can encourage business strategic additions that are less affected by the capability factor and more by the inducement factor. Using a novel dataset of over thirty million Chinese startups and a novel LLM-based measure of economic policy uncertainty, we corroborate our main hypotheses.

Entrepreneurship Benefits Society? A Language Embedding Approach

ENT
xu xuexin (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Jianyi LIU (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics)
By applying large language models to analyze millions of Chinese publications from 1956 to 2018, we developed an index to measure public sentiment toward entrepreneurship. Extensive internal and external validation demonstrated that this index reliably reflects long-term public perceptions of entrepreneurial attitudes. The sentiment series reveals sustained positive sentiment across China's entrepreneurial landscape, with notable fluctuations occurring in pivotal years (1959,1975,2009,2013). However, most downturns were quickly reversed by overshooting rebounds. Further analysis through local projection and multiple regression analysis revealed that improved entrepreneurial sentiment consistently drove per capita GDP growth within a 10-year window, with partial effects stemming from credit expansion, business environment improvements, and output growth in three major industries. Policy analysis showed that government initiatives do not automatically boost social sentiment, highlighting structural misalignment between policy incentives and public psychology. The study's key contributions include: 1) Proposing and validating a cross-period entrepreneurial social sentiment index as a new theoretical and empirical tool for understanding entrepreneurial social externalities; 2) Uncovering complex interactions among sentiment, policy, and economic growth, providing new directions for future research on the psychosocial effects of entrepreneurship policies.

Formulating Better Enterprise Policy in the Periphery: A Design Science Approach

ENT
Rodrigo Frias (Durham University Business School); Jonathan Kimmitt; Christopher William Storey (Northumbria University); Pablo Munoz (Durham University)
This paper addresses the persistent misalignment between enterprise policy design and the contextual realities of peripheral regions. While entrepreneurship scholarship increasingly recognizes the importance of spatial, social, and institutional context, enterprise policy remains dominated by urban-centric, high-growth models that neglect the specificities of peripheral areas. Drawing on design science, we reconceptualize policy development as a design challenge rather than a knowledge gap. We introduce REFLECT, an assessment policy artifact that enhances reflexivity and dialogue in enterprise policymaking. The artifact raises awareness and stimulates dialogue within and across policy communities, enabling policy agents to better understand and navigate the structural uncertainties faced by meso-level actors who mediate between national directives and local entrepreneurial realities. Using RE-AIM, we evaluate REFLECT’s utility as both a technological tool and a boundary object that supports adaptive, place-sensitive policy design. The artifact provides a blueprint for action in enterprise policy design, supporting policymakers who seek to improve the formulation and delivery of enterprise support in often-overlooked peripheral contexts. It also improves our theoretical understanding of enterprise policymaking and advances the use of design science as a method in entrepreneurship and innovation research.

Governing Derived Entrepreneurship Through Belief Alignment

ENT
Artur Marion Ceolin (Instituto Mises Brasil)
This article advances a judgment-based theory of the firm by explaining how organizations make delegated entrepreneurship governable under genuine uncertainty. Building on the judgment-based approach and the Beliefs–Actions–Results (BAR) sequence, I argue that the central organizational problem is not whether judgment will be exercised, but how its delegation can remain coherent. Existing accounts emphasize asset ownership, authority, incentives, and monitoring, yet they under-specify the belief formation that initiates action and disciplines decentralized discretion. I close this gap by introducing a Misesian, thymology-informed micro foundation of belief. Entrepreneurial and employee beliefs are interpretive doctrines that connect imagined future ends to present means and structure how actors classify situations, evaluate trade-offs, and justify action. On this basis, I theorize ideology, understood in Mises’s sense as a practical doctrine guiding conduct, as a core governance mechanism inside the firm. Firms mold ideology through selective membership, socialization, shared language and narratives, routinized decision procedures, and authority practices that sanction bounded discretion. Internal ideology reduces interpretive variance, lowers coordination costs, constrains destructive entrepreneurship, and accelerates organizational learning by standardizing how feedback is interpreted. The paper re-specifies the firm as an institution that institutionalizes entrepreneurship. The firm scales original judgment by aligning derived judgment within a shared perspective while preserving adaptive experimentation. I derive implications for entrepreneurship theory, organizational economics, and organization design, emphasizing why performance under uncertainty hinges on belief governance, not merely on the allocation of decision rights.
