A Multi-Stakeholder Perspective on Disadvantaged Jobseeker Recruitment: Refugee Integration

HR
Betina Agata Szkudlarek (The University of Sydney); Eun Su Lee (The University of Melbourne); Sophia Johnson; Chris Brewster (University of Reading); Yijia Du (University of Sydney Business School)
A call for a multi-stakeholder approach to advance understanding of human resource management (HRM) processes, practices and outcomes is ever more relevant in the current dynamic and interconnected business environment, where collaborations are key to organisational success. This paper uses refugee employment as its empirical case to investigate how multiple stakeholders beyond organisational boundaries influence the recruitment of disadvantaged jobseekers. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative inquiry conducted in Australia, which included 59 interviewees representing four primary stakeholder groups (employers, service providers, government agencies, and refugees), this study challenges the internally focused perspective on recruitment practices. This paper proposes a multi-stakeholder perspective that highlights the pivotal role of external actors in (co-)shaping recruitment processes. Built around six primary functions (sensitising, agenda-setting, gatekeeping, educating, bridging, and role-modelling), this paper demonstrates how a diverse set of stakeholders can enable, facilitate and/or hinder refugee recruitment and how their roles and levels of engagement shift over time, adding a dynamic dimension to the recruitment literature. This research expands the existing conceptualisations using a multi-stakeholder analysis in the refugee context, informing and enriching theory and practice in the recruitment of disadvantaged jobseekers.

Heterogeneity in the Effects of Political Fit on Applicant Attraction: Racial and Gender Differences

HR
Youjeong Song (Rutgers University-New Brunswick); Aaron Sojourner (University of Minnesota)
This paper examines how political fit between job seekers and potential employers influences job seekers’ attraction to employers, how this influence varies by gender and race, and how such effects are mediated by perceived membership. We present evidence from three laboratory experiments using samples recruited from the U.S. The studies show that political fit strongly predicts job seekers’ attraction to employers, but contrary to our prediction, White people were more willing than racial minorities to match with employers with greater political fit. Gender interaction showed inconsistent results: Studies 1 and 2 showed that women responded to political fit more strongly, but Study 3 showed that men responded to political fit more strongly through higher perceived membership. This research highlights the importance of political ideology in job selection, emphasizing its varied valuation of political fit across key demographic groups.

Signaling AI Skill Demand in Traditional Industries: Evidence from Online Job Advertisements

HR
Bangxi Fu (University of Leeds); Chunyu Xiu; bo wen (Swansea University)
AI-related skill demand is spreading into traditional industries, yet we know little about how employers signal and structure these requirements in recruitment. Using a dynamic HR capabilities lens, we treat job advertisements as signals of sensing (emerging AI needs), seizing (recruitment communication and screening), and reconfiguring (hybrid role design). We analyse 2,308 LinkedIn job ads posted in June 2025 by 24 large UK employers across nine traditional SIC sectors, mapping where AI requirements appear, how explicitly they are labelled, and which non-AI skills co-occur. AI signalling varies sharply by industry even after accounting for posting length and overall skill-signalling intensity. Within AI-related ads, people-oriented industries are far more likely than object-oriented industries to use portable “artificial intelligence” and “machine learning” labels, indicating that job ads capture signalling strategy as well as capability demand. Finally, AI-related recruitment is predominantly hybrid: AI signals are embedded in industry-specific bundles that combine technical foundations with coordination and judgement capabilities. These findings extend vacancy-based evidence beyond technology-intensive settings and show how industry context shapes the visibility, explicitness, and bundling of AI skill signals as firms adapt HR systems to AI-driven work change.

Agnostic Bricolage: From Ideal to Pluralistic Notions of Validity in Recruitment

HR
Frans Bévort (Copenhagen Business School); Katrine Møller Solgaard
Despite universal agreement on the organizational importance of recruitment, the dominant psychometric research neither inform practice sufficiently, nor do it explain the actual recruitment practices. This study wants to unpack the limited scientific understanding of recruitment and demonstrate how a widened epistemological view on how validity is constructed and used, lies behind the apparent practitioner – research gap within recruitment. The paper sets out to describe how the extant research has simplified the recruitment process by focusing primarily on psychometrics. Four different schools within recruitment research are identified, starting with the hitherto dominant psychometric school, followed by the less prominent strategic–capability, social-exchange– process, and the critical–institutional school. A central argument is that the hegemony of the psychometric school, has made research into recruitment lacking scientifically and of reduced relevance for practitioners. Furthermore, this has made it difficult to understand and explain why recruitment practices unfold as they do. The paper contributes to such an understanding by analyzing three cases of recruitment and demonstrates three examples of how validity is “displaced” during the recruitment process between three kinds of validity: predictive, strategic and learning validity. The paper draws on a longitudinal ethnographic study to analyze three distinct recruitment processes. The concluding recommendation for research as well as practice, is to embrace the complexity of recruitment practice and assume a posture of “agnostic bricolage”.

Post-Interview Ghosting Casts Dark Shadows on Applicant Reactions

HR
Philip L. Roth (Clemson University); Phil Bobko (Gettysburg College); Hyunji Suh (Clemson University); Emily Ferrise (Clemson University); Harvell Jackson Walker (Auburn University); Ronald S. Landis (Illinois Institute of Technology); Jason Thatcher (University of Colorado-Boulder)
It is commonplace for organizations to “ghost” job seekers after interviews; i.e., for organizations to suddenly cease communication with no justification. Given that job searches can be high stakes processes for job seekers, ghosting may lead to strong reactions by those job seekers. Our integrative literature review suggests that post-interview ghosting is associated with lower levels of perceived justice and negative emotions. To study the implications of post-interview ghosting, we highlight the role of these explanatory variables (including ressentiment), as well as “dark” (and maladaptive) dependent variables such as retaliation and schadenfreude. To control common method bias, we employ a psychological separation task between the measurement of our mediators (justice and ressentiment) and our dependent variables. Results suggest that post-interview ghosting leads to i) lower levels of applicant informational and interpersonal justice and ii) higher levels of negative emotions (i.e., ressentiment). In turn, ghosting is related to our dependent variables such that ghosting leads to iii) lower levels of organizational image and intent to pursue employment, and iv) higher levels of retaliation and schadenfreude. Our results suggest ghosting costs organizations in terms of image, intent to pursue, retaliation, and schadenfreude. More generally, we encourage the addition of negatively focused variables (such as ressentiment, retaliation, and schadenfreude) in human resource selection outcome spaces when researchers study inherently negative phenomenon.
