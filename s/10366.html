Session overview

Increasing political polarization and declining trust in institutions have become defining features of contemporary life (Gauchat, 2012; Iyengar et al., 2019; Valgarðsson et al., 2025). These trends necessitate a better understanding of sociopolitical divides, conceptualized here as differences in how individuals and groups construe social reality, define collective identity, and interpret the legitimacy of institutional actors and elite authority in politically charged contexts. These divides operate both between individuals (e.g., partisan differences in identity, beliefs, and information processing) and between institutions and the publics they serve (e.g., gaps in trust, credibility, and perceived intent). Understanding mechanisms underlying sociopolitical divides is critical not only for explaining polarization, but for clarifying how organizations and leaders might more effectively communicate, coordinate, and rebuild legitimacy in polarized contexts. To this end, the proposed symposium brings together five complementary perspectives to examine sociopolitical divides across levels of analysis, integrating evidence from political messaging, information environments, markets and stakeholder relationships, inequality, and organizational identity to illuminate how these divides are constructed, sustained, and navigated. -- In Who is the “We”? Defining the Ingroup in Political Messaging, Waldfogel and Ponce de Leon examine how political leaders and everyday partisans define collective identity through language. Using large-scale text analysis and experimental evidence, they distinguish inclusive (who “we” are) versus exclusive (who “we” are not) constructions of the political ingroup and demonstrate that although exclusive identity framing is prevalent in elite messaging, voters across parties prefer inclusive frames grounded in shared values. This project foregrounds identity construction as a mechanism through which sociopolitical divides are mobilized and sustained by political elites. -- In Forecast Skepticism and Economic Behavior: Do Political Views Buffer the Economic Costs of ‘False Alarms’? Sels and Nagaraj shift attention to information processing and trust in expert institutions. Leveraging high-resolution data on weather forecasts, realized weather, consumer spending, and political ideology, they propose that political context shapes behavioral responsiveness to expert forecasts, with downstream consequences for consumer spending. This work highlights how sociopolitical divides influence coordination and decision-making even in ostensibly apolitical contexts by examining how political identity shapes responses to shared information environments. -- In Partisan Markets: Employee-Customer Political Alignment and Firm Outcomes, Kagan examines how sociopolitical divides manifest in market relationships between firms and their core stakeholders. Using novel large-scale data linking employees’ political partisanship to the partisan composition of firms’ customer bases, this work explores stakeholder political alignment—the correspondence between the political makeup of a firm’s workforce and its consumers—and documents substantial heterogeneity in alignment across firms and locations. By showing how polarization manifests between firms and customers, this work extends the symposium’s focus from individual cognition and information processing to the organizational consequences of sociopolitical divides. -- In A Political Psychology of Inequality: How Elites Exploit Public Psychology to Increase Inequality, Connolly and colleagues offer a broad theoretical framework explaining how sociopolitical divides around inequality persist. They argue that evolved features of human cognition—local focus, group identification, and individualizing explanations—render the public susceptible to elite strategies that divert attention away from structural inequality. This work situates sociopolitical divides within larger systems of power and demonstrates how psychological tendencies are strategically leveraged to sustain unequal organizations and economic arrangements. -- Lastly, in Do Organizational Identity Statements Matter? Evidence from Law Enforcement Agencies and Civilian Interactions, Bailey examines whether organizational efforts to signal identity and moral purpose can meaningfully shape behavior in contexts characterized by deep sociopolitical divides and low institutional trust. Analyzing thousands of law enforcement mission statements and linking them to administrative performance data, this paper tests whether moralized identity claims—often deployed to build legitimacy and repair trust—translate into improved behavior. The findings suggest that identity statements frequently reflect existing organizational culture rather than causally shaping conduct, and that moralized, ingroup-focused language can be associated with worse organizational outcomes. By evaluating the limits of symbolic identity signaling, this work brings the symposium’s themes directly into the organizational domain, highlighting when institutional attempts to bridge sociopolitical divides fail or backfire. Together, these presentations show how sociopolitical divides are constructed through identity and meaning, expressed in differential trust and information use, embedded in stakeholder relationships and market dynamics, exploited and sustained at scale, and how institutions can bridge or exacerbate such divides. Collectively, the symposium makes three integrative contributions. First, rather than simply documenting polarization as an outcome, these presentations identify underlying mechanisms —identity construction, trust and skepticism, stakeholder political alignment, cognitive framing, and moral signaling—that produce and reinforce sociopolitical divides. Second, the symposium provides cross-level integration, connecting micro-level psychological processes (identity, trust, attention) to macro-level outcomes, including stakeholder relationships and market behavior, societal inequality, and organizational performance. Finally, by clarifying how sociopolitical divides are constructed and sustained in an era of polarization and declining institutional trust, this symposium provides a foundation for thinking about how sociopolitical divides might be navigated and addressed.
