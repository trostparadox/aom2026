Hope by Facilitation and Relation: A Study of Futures Thinking in Management Education

CMS
Cat Spellman (Durham University Business School); Nora Alomar (Durham University Business School); Raysa Geaquinto Rocha (University of Essex); Dawn Whitton (Durham University Business School)
Management educators face growing pedagogical challenges as students confront heightened anxiety about uncertain organisational, societal, and planetary futures. While critical approaches to management education have powerfully problematised instrumental and technocratic paradigms, they have been less successful in theorising how learners can be supported to engage constructively and ethically with uncertainty. This study examines how management education can cultivate hope as a moral, relational, and pedagogical resource for futures-oriented learning. Drawing on a qualitative, longitudinal study of an innovative futures-thinking module at a UK business school, the paper explores how educators and students co-create learning spaces in which hope emerges through experiential, dialogic, and reflective practices. Integrating virtue ethics, Freirean critical pedagogy, and a narrative-pedagogical lens informed by Coelho’s The Alchemist, we theorise hope not as an individual affective state but as a socially cultivated virtue enacted through facilitation, relational engagement, and meaning-making under conditions of uncertainty. The findings generate a Hope in Management Learning Model comprising four interrelated dimensions: Personally Transformative Hope, Collaborative and Dialogic Hope, Ethically Grounded and Introspective Hope, and Communal and Solidary Hope. Together, these dimensions illuminate the pedagogical conditions and relational processes through which students transform anxiety about the future into moral imagination, agency, and collective responsibility. The study contributes to management learning theory by reconceptualising hope as a meta-virtue that bridges critique and praxis, integrating emotional, ethical, and relational dimensions of learning. Practically, it offers guidance for designing futures-oriented pedagogies that move beyond instrumental skill development toward transformative education, positioning business schools as sites where learners are equipped to engage critically, responsibly, and hopefully with uncertain futures.

Alternative Organizing as Borderland Praxis

CMS
Nimruji Jammulamadaka
This paper argues that from a post-colony perspective counter-hegemonic organizing is not limited to indigenous perspectives. It has to also account for inequalities of access and structural dependencies to overcome colonial difference. Through a case study of a nonprofit eye care hospital in India the paper demonstrates two features of alternative organizing from the post-colony perspective that overcome colonial difference. Using Diane Vaughan’s analogical theorizing, the study finds that alternate organizing in the post-colony is a borderland praxis. This praxis does not engage in a hegemonic versus alternative stance. Instead, such borderland organizing simultaneously incorporates some features from the Western, hegemonic capitalocentric as also alternative organizing, while also categorically rejecting certain other features from both. This organizing values autonomy, but this is not solidary, instead it is supported by discretion. The organizing nurtures individuals revitalizing their human agency who in turn enable the organization to overcome colonial difference. Such organizing defies a linear predictable core-support function logic instead existing in complex causal relationships and interdependencies between subunits. Following the philosopher William Wimsatt’s work on Complex systems I identify this organizing as Wimsattian. The two features identified through this study are the importance of the situated embodied individual with discretion; and a complex Wimsattian design of organizing sub-unit relations that defies core-support logic. These features exist symbiotically and the organization grows and adapts as a living organism.

Cycles of Organizational Democracy: A Systems Theory Rereading of Degeneration

CMS
Mengyao (Angel) Yang (University of Massachusetts Boston); Alessia Contu (University of Massachusetts Boston)
This paper revisits a central debate in organizational democracy: how democratic enterprises can remain economically viable while preserving democratic member control. A review of the organizational degeneration literature reveals limited theorization that captures the processual and intertwined nature of practices shaping the viability of organizational democracy. Drawing on Koss Hartmann’s (2014) notion of subversive functionalism, I propose a systems analysis that mobilizes the functionalist assumptions of Katz and Kahn’s (1966) open systems theory to examine the viability and degeneration of democratic member control. I illustrate this systems analysis with retention and degeneration examples, which reveal reinforcing mechanisms linking democratic participation and economic viability. This analytical approach reframes organizational democracy as a viable alternative that is continuously sustained through the ongoing enactment of democratic member control in day-to-day practices.
