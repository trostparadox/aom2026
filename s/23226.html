Home Sweet Home? Resource Mobilization and the Effect of Living at Home on Entrepreneurship

ENT
Mohamed Genedy (Stockholm School of Economics); Mattias Nordqvist (Stockholm School of Economics)
Although living with parents during college might be viewed as counterproductive for young adults, it may have hidden benefits for entrepreneurship. Drawing on resource mobilization theory, we argue that living at the parental home during college facilitates the mobilization of tangible and intangible resources needed to start a business––what we conceptualize as the ‘infrastructure of the home’ (household-based resources). The analyses of longitudinal data from the population-wide Swedish registries between 1993 and 2019, covering around 376,000 individuals, show that those who lived at the parental home during college are more likely to start a business after graduation. Mechanism analyses show that the effect attenuates following parental death and persists among affluent families, consistent with the infrastructure-of-the-home concept. We rule out alternative explanations related to location, family background, and individual effects. Boundary-condition tests indicate that the effect is stronger for women and increases with the number of siblings, aligning with resource constraints and mobilization capacity. A post-hoc firm-level analysis further shows that new ventures with at least one founder who stayed at the parental home during college are associated with higher new venture performance. The findings are robust to alternative specifications and identification strategies. The study advances our understanding of how a key life course transition—living at or leaving the parental home—relates to entrepreneurship, and it contributes to the resource mobilization literature in entrepreneurship and management by introducing ‘the infrastructure of the home’ as a household-based source of resource advantages governed through informal, non-market family arrangements.

How Mindfulness Activates Entrepreneurial Alertness and Fuels New Venture Ideation

ENT
Alex Kier (Washington State University); Benjamin Warnick (Washington State University)
New venture ideation is central to entrepreneurship, yet the cognitive factors supporting it are often viewed as innate and difficult to develop. Mindfulness —receptive attention and awareness of the present—has been linked to creativity, suggesting its potential to enhance entrepreneurial ideation. We theorize that trait mindfulness, state mindfulness, and mindfulness practice play distinct and complementary roles in idea generation. Across three studies, we find that trait mindfulness predicts idea generation, and that state mindfulness induced through meditation increases idea quantity and, in turn, idea quality, particularly for individuals with greater mindfulness practice. We further show that state mindfulness increases entrepreneurial alertness, which functions as the proximal cognitive mechanism linking mindfulness to idea generation. Together, these findings offer a more nuanced account of how mindfulness shapes entrepreneurial creativity.

Once Bitten: Financial Insecurity Erodes Joiners' Attraction to New Venture Employment

ENT
Gavin Joseph Williamson (Rowan University); Timothy P. Munyon (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville)
New venture employees (i.e., joiners) labor under conditions of low wages and job insecurity, in exchange for promises of entrepreneurial work and chances at career growth and wealth creation that often never materialize. While joiners are initially willing to make this tradeoff, the financial strain of working for a new venture may undermine incumbent joiners’ willingness to continue making this tradeoff. Across two studies (a phenomenological survey and a repeated-measures experiment), I find that financial insecurity undermines incumbent joiners’ attraction to new venture employment, and financial loss events (e.g., ventures laying off joiners or missing payroll) precipitate changes in attraction via increases in financial insecurity.

Significant Events and Their Impact on New Venture Teams (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Frederic Delmar (emlyon business school)
Significant events represent essential shifts in the new venture's development. These events may increase, decrease, or have no effect on team members' cognitions, decisions, and behaviors. Although this is a distinct possibility, research has yet to fully elucidate the specific impacts on new venture team members following significant events. To address this gap, we adapt Event System Theory to the new venture context to develop a theory of significant events and their effects on team members' cognition and behavior. The theory describes how significant events first capture members’ attention and then undergo a distinctive psychological interpretation, which affects cognitive and behavioral outcomes, such as leaving the team. By describing the processes that precede and follow significant events, our theory provides a needed explanation of how events may change team members’ engagement with their teams or lead to their exit. We derive specific propositions.
