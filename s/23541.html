A Systematic Review of Typology of AI Configurations and Organizational Change Mechanisms

TIM
Efrat Fibi Kremer (Bar-Ilan University); Ella Glikson (Bar-Ilan University)
Organizations increasingly deploy artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, yet empirical research reports considerable variation in the organizational implications of implementation across studies and contexts. Much of this work has examined AI adoption at the level of tasks or individuals, offering limited leverage for explaining when implementation remains confined to local practices and when it becomes implicated in broader organizational change. We develop a change-oriented synthesis of organizational AI research that links AI configurations to core change mechanisms. Drawing on a systematic review of 90 empirical studies, we introduce a parsimonious typology based on two dimensions, Decision Authority and Functional Orientation. Across the reviewed studies, reported dynamics vary across configurations, with some implementations remaining localized and others becoming associated with more extensive shifts in roles, coordination, governance, and meaning. Building on this synthesis, we develop an integrative framework that distinguishes authority-centered and meaning-centered change trajectories, and we derive propositions that specify boundary conditions under which different change patterns are more likely to emerge. The paper contributes to organizational change and AI research by providing a configuration-sensitive account that explains heterogeneity in prior findings and specifies mechanisms and boundary conditions through which AI implementation becomes organizationally consequential.

AI-Driven Organizational Innovation Restructuring: Innovation Power Allocation and Process Logic

TIM
Jin Chen (Tsinghua University); haochao fang (Tsinghua University)
Artificial intelligence(AI) is increasingly embedded in organizational innovation activities, progressively challenging the traditional assumption that "humans are the sole agents of innovation." While extensive evidence—from AI-driven scientific discoveries to algorithm-mediated platform innovations— demonstrates that AI is profoundly reshaping the landscape of innovation, existing research still lacks a comprehensive theoretical framework that systematically explains how innovation power is reconfigured and how the logic of innovation processes evolves accordingly. To address this gap, this paper adopts a theory-building-oriented multi-case comparative approach to construct a two-dimensional analytical framework encompassing "power allocation among innovation agents" and "innovation process logic." This framework identifies four AI-driven innovation paradigms: assisted, delegated, co-creative, and ecological. The study reveals that, through mechanisms of "algorithmic delegation," AI prompts a redistribution of innovation decision-making power from humans to algorithms to varying degrees. In the ecological innovation context, AI acts as an "environmental constructor," continuously influencing innovation through data-model co-evolution and adaptive learning among creators, algorithms, niches, and macro-traffic regulation. By adopting relational and processual perspectives, this paper advances the theoretical understanding of AI's innovative agency, proposes a unified analytical framework for explaining human-machine collaborative innovation systems, and provides theoretical insights for organizations to design innovation governance, adjust organizational structures, and manage innovation ecosystems in the AI era.

Coordination Without Judgment: Theorizing Role Enactment by Agentic AI in Scrum Planning

TIM
Hrishitva Patel (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Soumee Roy (The University of Texas at Dallas); Durgesh Babu P (Visvesvaraya Technological University); Kyrie Zhou (The University of Texas at San Antonio)
Scrum relies on sustained human interaction through discussion, negotiation, and judgment to coordinate work under uncertainty. This study theorizes how Scrum coordination routines change when non human agents enact formal organizational roles. Using a role based multi agent simulation, we model a complete Scrum sprint cycle across nineteen applied problem scenarios in which large language model agents perform the roles of Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developer. The simulations generate standard Scrum artifacts, including product goals, backlogs, sprint goals, sprint plans, and retrospectives, enabling systematic analysis of commitment formation and coordination mechanisms. Results reveal consistent decision logics centered on backlog readiness, Sprint Goal alignment, capacity constraints, and dependency ordering, producing non arbitrary sprint commitments across scenarios. Interpreted through Engeström’s Activity Theory, these patterns reflect an interconnected activity system in which roles, rules, and mediating artifacts structure coordination and trade offs in the absence of human judgment. At the same time, the findings identify clear limits of computational agency, as contextual interpretation, value alignment, and accountability remain outside agent capabilities.

How Do Organizations “Seat” AI into the Right “Chair”?

TIM
Yulong Du (Zhejiang University); Tngbang Yang (Zhejiang University)
Generative AI (GenAI) is diffusing rapidly across innovation work, yet most initiatives stall at pilots and fail to translate adoption into measurable value. We argue that this “adoption-impact gap” is driven less by model sophistication than by a pervasive paradigm-task misfit. To address this, we theorize AI augmentation and AI automation as distinct pathway logics. Augmentation expands and sustains alternative execution paths by supporting human sensemaking and exploration, whereas automation converges execution by pruning variation, stabilizing action sequences, and scaling selected paths. Building on task-technology fit, we theorize AI fit through a triadic decomposition of task structure: products (well- vs. ill-defined), acts (well- vs. ill-defined), and information cues (tacit vs. non-tacit). This lens yields five propositions that specify when augmentation, automation, sequential hybrids, or substitution patterns yield superior innovation performance. Furthermore, we develop a temporally layered model showing how AI augmentation progressively reshapes task structures during execution by articulating ill-defined products, stabilizing action patterns, and partially externalizing tacit information cues, thereby enabling the staged and bounded introduction of AI automation. Together, our theory positions AI not as a static input to fixed tasks, but as an active force in structuring innovation work over time. By integrating task theory, pathway-based complexity, and AI paradigms, we reconcile conflicting findings on AI and innovation and offer a dynamic account of how organizations can continuously “seat” AI into the right role as innovation tasks evolve. For managers, we offer a practical diagnostic and orchestration playbook for “seating” GenAI into the right organizational role, improving both innovation outcomes and scaling prospects.

Testing a Hybrid Sensemaking Framework for Ethical Decision-Making in AI Leadership

TIM
Maura Rotolo Bowen (West Virginia University); Thomas A. Zeni (West Virginia University); Jeff D. Houghton (West Virginia University)
Ethical decision-making is rarely straightforward, especially for leaders steering the development of AI systems through ambiguous terrain. This study demonstrates how a hybrid sensemaking framework can facilitate that work. Drawing on Weick’s organizational sensemaking theory and Mumford and colleagues’ cognitive-ethical decision-making model, the authors designed a training approach to help leaders navigate uncertainty while maintaining ethical clarity. To evaluate its impact, they conducted a randomized controlled trial using a difference-in-differences design, with striking results: participants trained in the framework demonstrated significantly stronger ethical reasoning, greater confidence in the face of ambiguity, and a more consistent use of structured decision processes than the control group. These findings suggest that ethics education for AI leadership can be systematic, scalable, evidence-based, cross-functional, and particularly valuable when the path forward is unclear.
