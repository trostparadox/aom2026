Provisional Commitments: How Innovation Teams Navigate Uncertainty (WITHDRAWN)

MOC
Derrick P. Bransby (Harvard Business School)
Innovation teams face a familiar tension: they must advance their work toward some end yet remain open to new possibilities as problems and possible solutions coevolve. This study identifies “provisional commitments”—open-ended, mutually affirmed decisions that enable action despite uncertainty—as the mechanism through which teams manage this tension. Such commitments provide enough stability to execute and flexibility to learn, linking the two in practice. Based on a year-long qualitative field study in the concert touring and live event production industry, I show how project teams build shared problem representations that make clients’ aspirations collectively intelligible through two practices: (1) distilling their essence into evaluative criteria and (2) tethering them to shared references to generate working assumptions. Provisional commitments establish a minimal basis for coordination that is stable enough to support collective action yet flexible enough to be revised. This basis, in turn, makes problems actionable, converting representations into task structures (i.e., scaffolds) that enable execution and learning. These findings show how provisional commitments enable action without hardening teams’ trajectory, providing enough stability to execute while preserving the flexibility to adapt and learn.

Too Busy for Board Activism: Concurrent Activist Board Seats and Its Impact on Firm Strategic Change

MOC
Albert Minkyu Ahn (Hong Kong Baptist University); Eugene See (Coastal Carolina University)
This study examines how activist directors holding concurrent activist board seats influence firm strategic change, highlighting the behavioral dynamics between activist and incumbent directors. Building on alignment and legitimacy theories, we argue that concurrent board appointments may weaken activist directors' effectiveness by overextending their commitments, thereby reducing strategic change. Using a panel of 503 U.S. activist-targeted firms from 2010 to 2020, we find that firms with activist directors holding concurrent board seats experience significantly less strategic change than firms without such concurrent appointments. Our findings show that current activist employment mitigates the negative impact of concurrent board seats, and that financial orientation further conditions how incumbents perceive activist directors. In particular, non-financially oriented shareholders enable concurrent board seats to promote strategic change, whereas financial orientation weakens this effect. This paper advances behavioral perspectives on board activism by illustrating how director workload and shareholder motives influence board resistance to activist-driven change.

Performance at a Social Cost? An Experimental Study of Group Decision-Making Structures

MOC
Lena Rudat (University of Mannheim); Nils Schweikard (University of Mannheim); Himani Singh (University of Mannheim)
Decentralized decision-making is widely promoted as a best practice for enhancing group performance, yet little is known about its social and relational consequences for group members. We study how decision-making structure impacts both performance and members’ collaborative experience in virtual groups. Drawing on the collective effort model, we compare decentralized decision-making, where authority is shared among members, with centralized decision-making, where authority is concentrated in a single leader. We argue that decentralization is a double-edged sword. Shared decision-making authority increases group performance, driven by higher perceived instrumentality and social compensation. Yet, it also increases perceived social loafing and undermines task satisfaction, due to increased monitoring of members’ contributions and reduced tolerance for contribution imbalances. We test our predictions in an experiment with 169 virtual groups completing a complex, interdependent escape game, designed to capture key features of modern groupwork, under different decision-making structures (centralized, decentralized, control). We find that decentralized groups outperform centralized groups but report significantly lower task satisfaction. Mediation analyses show that this satisfaction loss is driven by heightened collective perceptions of social loafing. Together, these results show that the same structural design features of decentralization that help groups perform better can simultaneously undermine satisfaction.

Talk in the Fog of Deep Uncertainty: Analogy and Sensemaking in a Covid-19 Expert Panel

MOC
Xiaojian Lin (Tsinghua University); Xiaoli Lu; Lili Zhu (China Agricultural University)
Research in international politics and cognitive psychology has acknowledged the role of analogies in crisis decision-making under deep uncertainty. Yet much of this scholarship has focused on analogies in public discourse, leaving their function in real-time expert deliberation underexplored. This study bridges that gap by examining how experts use analogical reasoning to make sense of the uncertain future, drawing on unique video conference data from expert panel convened by a prominent Chinese think tank to advise on COVID-19 policy adjustments in early 2022. This study finds that (a) experts primarily employ analogies drawn from COVID-19 experiences or historical public health events in China; (b) analogical inference patterns can be categorized based on three dimensions: the alignment of causal relationships, the quantity of shared causal elements, and the position of expert viewpoints in analogical claims; and (c) analogies serve three roles including defining the nature of the situation, providing prescriptions, and warning about dangers.

Why Do Good Apples Compensate for Bad Ones? Coworker Time Theft and Observers’ Proactive Responses

MOC
Ruijing Wu (Renmin University of China); chunmeng yang (Renmin University of China); Jinyi Zhou (Renmin University of China)
In team-based work settings, employees often remain attentive to team effectiveness even when some coworkers appropriate work time. Drawing on the social compensation theory, we examine whether observers respond to coworker time theft by engaging in self-directed and other-directed compensatory behaviors to maintain collective outcomes. We propose that team performance maintenance concerns mediate this process, and that employee collectivism moderates the strength of these indirect effects. Using an experiment and a multi-wave field study, we find that coworker time theft heightens observers’ team performance maintenance concerns, which in turn predict greater responsibility behavior and motivating behavior. These indirect effects are significantly stronger among individuals high in collectivism. Together, the findings illuminate a performance-maintenance response pathway through which coworkers may help sustain team effectiveness and offer implications for peer-based accountability systems.
