Airborne CEOs and Green Innovation

STR
Tian Feng (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
How airborne CEOs, who have transitioned from being a government position to become airborne and parachute in to become a CEO, respond to institutional pressure for environment-themed innovation was investigated in this paper using data from China. A panel dataset covering 3,125 publicly listed firms from 2009 through 2021 was analyzed. The results show that firms led by a former government official—who act as hybrid agents bridging state and market logics—responded actively to government pressure to invest in green innovation. That was especially the case when the CEO had more power and/or a longer career horizon. The strongest effect tended to be when the CEO had moderately-long tenure, neither too long nor too short. These findings contribute to institutional theory by explaining how hybrid agents facilitate corporate green innovation.

When More Is Not Better: Inventor CEO Engagement and Innovation Quality

STR
CHUN BAI (City University of Hong Kong)
This study extends recent research on inventor CEOs by examining how the degree of their hands-on engagement in patenting shapes innovation quality in large, established firms. We theorize that inventor CEO engagement exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with innovation quality, such that moderate involvement enhances innovation quality, whereas excessive involvement yields diminishing returns. We further argue that this curvilinear effect is contingent on CEO origin: insider CEOs amplify both the benefits and the costs of engagement, while outsider CEOs experience attenuated effects. Using a panel of 3,319 inventor CEO–firm-year observations from Chinese A-share listed firms over a 15-year period and measuring engagement intensity through a cumulative annual ratio of patent authorship, we find strong support for our predictions.

Balancing Accountability and Leakage: How Internal and External Transparency Shape R&D Outcomes

STR
Parthiban David (American University); Palash Deb (North Carolina Agricultural and Technical State University); Augustine Duru (American University); Akshay Narayanan (University of Wales Trinity Saint David); Vipin Sreekumar (Masters' Union School of Business)
Transparency in a firm’s information environment can improve governance of R&D, yet poses a “two audience problem” that obscures its overall effect. Information available to monitors for assessing R&D strategies for accountability is simultaneously observable by competitors, risking imitation of the firm’s innovation efforts. Because the benefits of accountability can be offset by the costs of information leakage, the governance implications of transparency for R&D remain ambiguous. To unpack the relative accountability benefits from the leakage drawbacks of transparency, we distinguish between its sources: internal (generated internally from managerial disclosures) and external (generated externally from analyst forecasts and stock trading). We theorize that external transparency enhances accountability more than internal transparency by aggregating dispersed intelligence and providing independent evaluations that are less susceptible to managerial bias. Furthermore, internal transparency can exacerbate leakage more than external transparency; although external transparency provides general evaluative signals through aggregated market intelligence, managerial disclosures can reveal proprietary strategic roadmaps. Empirical evidence from a study of 1,809 firms during 1999 – 2017 shows that compared to internal transparency, external transparency is more positively associated with R&D investments, patents, and citations. This relationship is strengthened under two key contingencies that amplify both the benefits and drawbacks of transparency, the extent of R&D spillovers, and the intensity of competitive rivalry. This study clarifies the governance role of transparency on R&D in the presence of a two audience problem by differentiating between the two sources of transparency.

Strategic Interfaces Beyond the Upper Echelons: How CEO-Inventor Coinvention Shapes Search Behavior

STR
Shikhar Bhardwaj (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Mayank Varshney; Anish Sugathan (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
While strategic interfaces between CEOs and boards or top management teams are well examined, we know little about how CEOs directly interface with employees to shape organizational outcomes. We theorize that when inventor CEOs coinvent with employees, this creates a strategic interface that restructures inventors' network positions within the firm. Using a matched-sample difference-in-differences analysis of 525 inventor pairs from S&P 1500 high-tech firms with hands-on inventor CEOs, we find that coinventors exhibit significantly greater increases in both the breadth and depth of local search compared to their own pre-coinvention patterns and matched non-coinventing peers from the same firm. These behaviors are amplified when the CEO is also the firm's founder and weakened under high technological complexity of the firm. We test the underlying theoretical mechanism and confirm that coinventors become more central in the intra-firm network. Our findings extend strategic interface literature to downward interfaces by identifying CEO-inventor coinvention as a novel interface through which executives influence inventor-level behavior, the temporal effect of these interfaces on inventor-level behavior and indicate how inventor-level heterogeneity in innovative search behavior emerges from collaboration with executives.
