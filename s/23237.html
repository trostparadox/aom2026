Family Firms as Implicit Insurers: Evidence from Employee Health Shocks

STR
Jeroen H. Verbouw (IE Business School); Luc Renneboog (Tilburg University)
We examine how family ownership shapes firm responses to employee health shocks using Dutch matched employer–employee administrative data. Following a health shock, family firm employees experience 2% higher annual earnings losses at the overall and intensive margin. This effect is driven by reduced working hours and wage adjustments and is more pronounced among female, younger, geographically distant workers, and low-earning workers. At the extensive margin, family firm employees are less likely to switch employers, but conditional on switching, do so later and disproportionately switch to other family firms. Eight complementary tests, including on monthly employment records, relocation patterns, and hand-collected survey evidence, suggest that these effects reflect employee-driven flexibility rather than employer exploitation. Results persist when exploiting within-firm ownership changes and restricting to exogenous health conditions. Overall, our results suggest that family firms provide implicit insurance through accommodation, though employees absorb income losses.

Who Do Impact Investors Associate With?Partnerships with Academia, NGOs, Government, and Corporation

STR
Theodor Cojoianu (The University of Edinburgh); Maretno Agus Harjoto (Pepperdine University); Andreas Hoepner; Yanan Lin (Central University of Finance and Economics); Anh Vu (Dublin City University)
While environmental, social, and governance (ESG) investment has become mainstream, impact investment has yet to be institutionalised and legitimised. Using institutional logics for hybrid organisations, this study hypothesises that impact investment with social and financial objectives are distinctly different from philanthropic or corporate social responsibility (CSR) investment and conventional investment. Integrating cooperative strategic alliances into institutional logics, this study argues that impact investing is positively associated with academic and research institutions (academia), non-governmental organisations (NGOs), government, and corporate partners. Combining a cross-sectional data of 9,146 private investment firms across 115 countries with official statements made on their websites, we find that impact investing is negatively associated with philanthropic or CSR investment firms and conventional investment firms. We also find that impact investment firms are more likely to partner with academia, NGOs, government, and corporations than their ESG peers. These network alliances serve as resource-based of legitimacy to achieve social and commercial logics. Corporations give legitimacy to the commercial angle while academia, NGOs, and government provide scientific measurements, validations, and resources and information sharing to achieve social logic. This study is first cross-country comparative quantitative study that investigates the spectrum of hybrid organisations based on institutional logics with strategic alliances.

Relentless Redeployment Under Constraint: Human Capital Orchestration in a Social Enterprise

STR
Han Ming Daniel Chng (China Europe International Business School); Sae Young Lee
How can organizations sustain competitiveness by increasing resource redeployment when their core resources cannot be selectively replaced? We address this question through a longitudinal, inductive case study of Canyou, a Chinese employment-based social enterprise whose mission-locked commitment to lifelong employment for people with physical disabilities severely constrains conventional adjustment mechanisms. Drawing on more than two decades of qualitative data, we develop a process model of relentless resource redeployment under constraint. We show that adaptation in such settings depends on two tightly coupled activities: continuously cycling through market opportunities to identify successive “best” uses for existing human capital and actively enhancing the fungibility and redeployability of that human capital over time. Rather than treating redeployment as a discrete reallocative decision, Canyou enacted it as an ongoing organizational process combining repeated experimentation across business domains with deliberate efforts to fungibilize and deploy its mission-locked human capital despite volatility and erosion in core markets. By theorizing the dynamic interplay between opportunity cycling and resource fungibilization, this study extends research on resource redeployment and orchestration beyond contexts characterized by discretion and exit, and offers a generalizable account of strategic adaptation when flexibility must be achieved without shedding core resources.

Organizational Discretion Under Public Support: Evidence from Credit Cooperatives

STR
Nobuiuki Costa Ito (Ibmec São Paulo)
Private organizations increasingly operate under public support to address social issues, yet we know little about how organizational form shapes discretion in these collaborations. I study Brazil’s PRONAF rural credit program, a government-subsidized instrument implemented by heterogeneous financial institutions. Credit cooperatives are member-owned, aligning governance with user needs and potentially strengthening inclusion of family farmers, an underserved group facing collateral constraints, high exposure to productive risk, and limited access to non-subsidized private credit. Using the staggered entry of cooperatives across municipalities from 2017 to 2024, I estimate equilibrium effects on municipal PRONAF uptake (contracts and volumes aggregated across all lenders) with a staggered-adoption difference-in-differences design. Contrary to the view that cooperatives automatically expand public programs, cooperative entry is associated, on average, with lower PRONAF usage, particularly for working-capital operations. This average masks a contextual heterogeneity: in highly socially vulnerable municipalities, cooperative entry increases the number of PRONAF contracts, whereas in less vulnerable contexts program usage declines. Evidence is also suggestive that cooperatives whose organizational origins are closely tied to family farming are less likely to coincide with retrenchment in vulnerable areas. Overall, the findings indicate that private organizations do not simply implement public programs; they shape how public instruments are used locally. Their public-interest contribution operates mainly through expanding access (capillarity) rather than increasing program scale, and it is strongly conditioned by local vulnerability.

Alignment by Design: How Contractual Choices Shape Value Creation in Public–Private Partnerships

STR
Gustavo Cordeiro; Paulo Arvate (FGV EAESP)
A central challenge in strategic management is how hybrid organizations design boundaries and allocate decision rights to create value. Hybrid arrangements are promoted to address Grand Societal Challenges, yet their capacity to generate public value remains ambiguous. Why do some public– private partnerships (PPPs) create public value while others fail? We treat PPPs as an empirical setting to examine how contractual design, managerial alignment, and context influence value creation. We analyze a PPP in a middle-income country where a private partner managed non-clinical services while the public sector retained clinical delivery, a model of incomplete delegation. Using administrative data and a staggered difference-in-differences estimator, we identify causal effects on workforce, production, productivity, and health outcomes. Treated units increased service scale (consultations and registrations) driven by expanded professionals and hours worked. Despite increased capacity, we find no evidence of changes in productivity or hospitalizations for primary care-sensitive conditions. This suggests that while hybrid partnerships can expand operational capacity, incomplete delegation creates bottlenecks that prevent translating gains into public value. These effects are moderated by context, manifesting in locations with greater accessibility or higher social vulnerability. The findings advance theories of hybrid governance by showing that institutional design, rather than private participation, determines whether hybrid arrangements translate private capabilities into sustained public value, with implications for the strategic management of partnerships across sectors.
