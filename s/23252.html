Regenerative Project Management as Hopeful Practice: Navigating Radical Uncertainty

ONE
Lisa M. Hammond (University of South Florida)
Projects tasked with contributing to sustainability transitions, such as decarbonization, circular economy initiatives, and regenerative development, operate under conditions of radical uncertainty that challenge conventional, risk-based project management approaches. This paper introduces Regenerative Project Management (RPM) as a conceptual governance framework that reorients how projects structure organization–environment interactions under such conditions. Drawing on biomimicry, regenerative design, and scholarship on meaning and identity, RPM reconceptualizes project governance across three interrelated dimensions: (1) ecological sensing, operationalized through Life’s Principles to connect project decisions to long-term ecosystem dynamics; (2) regenerative governance structures, including Phase 0 (Regenerative Opportunity Assessment) and four Regenerative Decision Gates that institutionalize ecological voice across the project lifecycle; and (3) practitioner identity transformation, shifting project actors from control-oriented roles toward stewardship-oriented practice. The framework is developed through integrative theorizing informed by survey evidence, qualitative interviews, and an illustrative case drawn from a large-scale public-sector COVID-19 response program in the United States, demonstrating how hopeful project practice can emerge under extreme uncertainty. The paper contributes to organizations and the natural environment scholarship by operationalizing hope as a governance mechanism, rather than an individual psychological state, and by positioning projects as vanguard spaces where regenerative futures can be prototyped and institutionalized.

Ecological Consciousness: Reanimating Perception, Ontology, and Attention in Organizational Theory

ONE
David Morand (The Pennsylvania State University)
Despite decades of sustainability, environmental strategy, and climate-governance research, organizations still struggle to respond meaningfully to the ecological crisis. We argue the barrier is not only institutional, cognitive, or informational, but perceptual: prevailing modes of attention disclose nature as external, inert, and legible mainly through abstractions. We introduce ecological consciousness as a construct that shifts analysis from values and decision processes to the attentional and ontological conditions under which ecological reality becomes available to organizational sensemaking. Drawing on phenomenology, relational ontology, process metaphysics, and the neuropsychology of attention, we outline four pathways by which the world can be experienced as relational, processual, and alive. We then reconceptualize sensemaking as attentional ecology, showing how routines, technologies, and rationalities shape what can appear as a cue. We conclude with implications for organizational design, leadership, and cultivating perceptual capacities for ecological responsiveness.

Anticipatory Ecological Sensemaking: How Organisations Interpret Biodiversity and Nature Loss

ONE
Patrick Oko Quaye (University of Exeter)
How do organisations make sense of biodiversity and nature loss as strategically consequential when ecological change is slow, diffuse, and non-eventful —conditions that contrast with the sudden, disruptive contexts in which sensemaking has predominantly been studied? We address this question through an inductive study of 44 in-depth interviews with sustainability professionals and 30 corporate reports across multiple sectors. Our findings identify recurring patterns through which anticipatory ecological cues become organisationally meaningful. First, triggering cues draw attention to emerging dependencies between business continuity and ecosystem stability. Second, interpretive processes translate heterogeneous ecological and institutional cues into organisationally legible meanings via familiar anthropocentric business frames, enabling the gradual infusion of ecological reasoning. Third, translational processes embed these interpretations into governance arrangements, tools, and operational routines through boundary infrastructures such as disclosure standards and materiality assessments. Across these patterns, firms develop what we call an anthro-biocentric orientation: a hybrid interpretive stance through which biophysical realities progressively shape organisational decision-making. By documenting these patterns, our study advances organisational sensemaking by showing how meaning forms in anticipatory, rather than crisis-driven, ecological contexts, and how it becomes operationalised in practice. We further contribute to research on strategic framing and sustainability governance by revealing the interpretive and infrastructural work that makes slow-moving ecological change actionable.

Ecological Leadership as Decisional Praxis in Overcoming Organizational Sustainability Paradoxes

ONE
Luciano Munck (State University of Londrina)
Sustainability has become a central concern in organization studies and leadership research as escalating ecological crises expose the limits of growth-oriented and short-term decision logics. Despite extensive scholarly and managerial attention, organizations continue to struggle to translate planetary boundaries into binding decision criteria, resulting in persistent decoupling between sustainability discourse and effective organizational action. This paradox reveals a critical gap in existing theories of leadership and sustainability, which largely rely on adaptive, behavioral, or open-systems explanations and insufficiently address how organizational decision premises are structurally transformed. The objective of this study is to develop a theoretical framework that explains how sustainability can become operationally binding within organizations conceived as decision-making systems. Specifically, the article asks how organizations can overcome organizational inertia and greenwashing by reconfiguring their decision architectures under conditions of planetary limits. Methodologically, the study adopts a theory adaptation strategy grounded in rigorous conceptual synthesis. It advances through three interrelated analytical steps: (i) a critical engagement with classical and contemporary systems thinking in sustainability and leadership research; (ii) the application of Niklas Luhmann’s theory of organizations as autopoietic systems of decision; and (iii) the integration of contributions by David Seidl and collaborators that operationalize Luhmannian concepts into observable organizational rules, routines, and structures. The study’s primary result is the development of the concept of Ecological Leadership (EcoLeadership), defined as a metacommunicative, second-order decision praxis through which organizations observe how they decide, revise decision premises, and stabilize trajectories compatible with biophysical limits. This framework explains how past ecological decisions can become binding premises for future decisions via mechanisms such as second-order observation and re-entry. The article contributes to leadership theory, organization studies, and sustainability research by offering a formal explanation of how systemic ecological constraints become decision-relevant under conditions of operational closure. Practically, it provides a pathway for organizations to move beyond symbolic sustainability toward structurally grounded ecological decision-making, with implications for future research, leadership practice, and sustainability governance.

Clarifying Regeneration: Epistemic, Analogical and Structural Tensions

ONE
Boris Guffond (ESCP Business School); Valentina Carbone (ESCP Business School); Aurelien Acquier
Regeneration has gained rapid momentum in sustainability management, accompanied by growing calls for empirical inquiry, yet it remains unclear whether the concept possesses the clarity and structure required for explanatory use. This article examines the analytical viability of regeneration through a cross-domain conceptual analysis spanning biology, agriculture, the built environment, and management. The analysis shows that regeneration’s normative appeal has expanded faster than its conceptual specification as it travels across domains. Building on this comparison, we develop a diagnostic analysis that identifies three tensions shaping contemporary uses of regeneration in sustainability management: (1) an epistemic tension between discursive expansion and biophysical anchoring, (2) an analogical tension between heuristic appeal and causal transferability, and (3) a structural tension between conceptual breadth and boundary coherence. These tensions clarify the conditions under which regeneration can support empirical and explanatory inquiry, while remaining compatible with its programmatic and mobilizing uses. The article delineates conditional repair orientations that specify when regeneration can function analytically in sustainability management research.
