Performance Implications of Attention Strategies

STR
Jing Cai (University of Aberdeen)
This research builds on situated attention studies, using a dynamic approach to examine temporal shifts between broad and narrow attention strategies. It theorizes two attention management mechanisms —incremental and discontinuous transitions— that differ in the degree of attention change. Results show an inverted U-shaped relationship between incremental transitions and NPD efficiency and a U-shaped relationship between discontinuous transitions and NPD innovativeness. Communication modes moderate these effects: bottom-up communication strengthens the link between incremental transitions and NPD efficiency, while top-down communication enhances the relationship between discontinuous transitions and NPD innovativeness. Hypotheses are tested using panel data from 4,368 quarterly reports across 437 NPD projects led by executives at a leading ICT company. This study contributes to research on situated attention, the attention-based view, and firm innovation.

Managing Attention for Performance: The Interplay of Focus, Vigilance, and Executive Switching

STR
Jing Cai (University of Aberdeen)
While prior research has linked attention dimensions to firm performance, most studies have treated these dimensions separately. This study examines how attentional focus, vigilance, and executive attention switching interact. We propose a theoretical model explaining how firms balance attention management across these dimensions. Using a longitudinal dataset of 2,489 firm-year observations and 10,221 quarterly earnings calls from the top 300 S&P Index companies (2010–2020), we identify four distinct attention management patterns. These patterns align with different performance priorities and are shaped by bundles of five organizational factors. Our findings advance the situated perspective of attention management by demonstrating how configurations of attention dimensions fit with performance priorities within specific organizational contexts.

Rethinking Threat Rigidity: Threat Complexity, Attention Capacity, and Contingent Scale Effects

STR
David M. Gomulya (Singapore Management University); Minjae Kim (University of Alberta); Kibum Noh (York College of Pennsylvania); Leixing Jiang (University of Alberta)
We examine when product quality threats trigger delayed versus rapid organizational responses. Building on threat rigidity theory and attention-based views, we conceptualize threat complexity as the scope of quality problems across a firm's product portfolio, operationalized as the number of distinct vehicle models implicated by complaint clusters. We argue that multi-model scope heightens diagnostic, coordination, and attentional demands that slow recalls, but that organizational attention capacity can attenuate this delay by providing the infrastructure for distributed search and cross-unit integration. Using 72,338 model-component-year observations for publicly traded automotive manufacturers and Cox proportional hazard models, we find that: (1) threat complexity significantly reduces recall hazard rates (slower responses); (2) firm attention capacity slows responses in simple, localized threat contexts; but (3) yields faster responses to complex threats. We extend the literature by examining threat complexity and the moderating role of organizational attention capacity. Our results demonstrate how organizational size can change from friction to facilitation under increasing threat complexity, offering implications for the design of attention infrastructures in crisis management.

When Strengths Constrain Choice: Capability-Driven Attention and Strategic Commitment

STR
Alexandru Roman (University of Maine)
Organizational capabilities often underpin superior performance, yet the extension of a well-developed capability into an ill-suited context can also precipitate strategic failure. This duality raises a persistent question: how can the same capability-based logic generate both advantage and loss? We address this puzzle by developing a theory of capability-driven attentional distortion. We argue that salient capabilities shape how managers perceive strategic options by foregrounding action possibilities that appear compelling regardless of contextual fit. As a result, managerial attention shifts from deliberating whether a capability should be applied to determining how it can be applied—a reorientation we describe as a commitment-focused attention shift. This attentional pattern directs cognitive resources toward execution and renders deployment increasingly taken for granted rather than discretionary. We theorize that this dynamic is reinforced by capability-linked authority holders—such as technical specialists, successful executives, and symbolic leaders—whose legitimacy rests on a particular capability and who translate attentional focus into organizational momentum. We develop a process model that explains why this mechanism produces divergent outcomes: it supports value creation when perceived action possibilities correspond with environmental opportunities, but drives value erosion when they do not. We further identify boundary conditions related to capability attributes, environmental volatility, organizational architecture, and the distribution of capability-based authority. By articulating how capabilities shape strategic attention and commitment, this framework advances behavioral strategy research on the microfoundations of capability deployment and adaptation.
