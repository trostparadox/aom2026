Microfoundations of Team Strategic Foresight: Individual Skill and Volubility

STR
Benedikt Alexander Schuler (University of St. Gallen); Rahul Kapoor (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania); Felipe Csaszar (University of Michigan)
Strategic foresight enables management teams to make good strategic decisions, yet we know little about what drives team strategic foresight. We address this gap by examining two types of potential drivers: team composition (the mix of member attributes) and team process (how members interact with each other). We analyze 359 real-world predictions by 90 professional MBA teams in a forecasting tournament at two leading U.S. business schools. In terms of compositional drivers, we find that an individual member’s strategic foresight is a key driver of team strategic foresight, whereas demographic diversity was unrelated to team strategic foresight within our sample and research design. In terms of processual drivers, we find that volubility—the amount of time spent talking—of the individual with superior strategic foresight is a key driver of team strategic foresight. These findings suggest that social influence rather than diverse information or conflict drives the aggregation of predictions under uncertainty, where an individual’s foresight and volubility become more critical for team strategic foresight than the team’s diversity. We also find that, on average, teams exhibit greater strategic foresight than their average member. This suggests that the team process adds value beyond simple statistical aggregation by assigning greater weight to more accurate predictions. Taken together, this study provides a novel account of how team strategic foresight might operate in organizations, and highlights the important role that individuals with superior strategic foresight might play in strategic decision-making, especially when they are given the space to speak.

Inference in Strategic Decision-Making: Shared Conceptual Meaning and Social Reasoning

STR
Dylan Boynton
The Carnegie School established that organizational structure shapes individual decision-making, yet behavioral strategy lacks adequate theory explaining how individual mental representations combine to enable shared interpretation and coordinated action. We address this gap by developing a theory of social reasoning grounded in inferentialism, a perspective from philosophy of mind arguing that concepts gain meaning through socially established inferences rather than representation. We theorize that shared understanding emerges through scorekeeping, the process by which organizational members make claims, observe concept usage, and challenge or justify how strategic concepts are used in the process of reasoning. To extend inferentialism to organizational settings, we introduce inferential authority, identifying three sources—hierarchical position, specialized expertise, and reputation—that shape whose claims become accepted in reasoning practices. Our integrated model connects individual cognition with social reasoning processes, providing a mechanism for shared interpretation while extending behavioral strategy's understanding of how organizational structure shapes cognition and decision-making.

The Strategic Foresight of LLMs

STR
Felipe Csaszar (University of Michigan); Aticus Peterson (NYU Stern School of Business); Daniel Wilde (Indiana University Bloomington)
Can artificial intelligence outperform humans at strategic foresight—the capacity to form accurate judgments about uncertain, high-stakes outcomes before they unfold? We address this question through a fully prospective prediction tournament using live Kickstarter crowd- funding projects. Thirty U.S.-based technology ventures, launched after the training cutoffs of all models studied, were evaluated while fundraising remained in progress and outcomes were unknowable. A diverse suite of frontier and open-weight large language models (LLMs) completed 870 pairwise comparisons, producing complete rankings of predicted fundraising success. We benchmarked these forecasts against 346 experienced managers recruited via Prolific and three MBA-trained investors working under monitored conditions. The results are striking: human evaluators achieved rank correlations with actual outcomes between 0.04 and 0.45, while several frontier LLMs exceeded 0.60, with the best (Gemini 2.5 Pro) reach- ing 0.74—correctly ordering nearly four of every five venture pairs. These differences persist across multiple performance metrics and robustness checks. Neither wisdom-of-the-crowd ensembles nor human-AI hybrid teams outperformed the best standalone model.

When Strategic Ownership Constrains Strategic Change

STR
Jaemin Kim (Oakland University); Michael Greiner (Oakland University)
This study examines the role of strategic owners in shaping firms’ strategic change and explore how sensing capability, a key component of dynamic capabilities, conditions the effect of strategic ownership on such change. Focusing on the distinctive characteristics of strategic owners, including long-term orientation, heightened risk aversion, and strategic motivation and interests, we find that greater strategic ownership is associated with lower levels of a firm’s strategic change. However, this negative effect is attenuated when firms possess strong sensing capabilities that enable them to effectively scan for and recognize environmental changes. To enhance the robustness of our findings, we employ machine-learning based text analysis methods, including LDA topic modeling and BERT embedding, to measure firms’ sensing capabilities. By doing so, this study extends the behavioral theory of the firm by highlighting strategic owners’ emphasis on routinization as a critical mechanism linking BTOF with the dynamic capability perspective.
