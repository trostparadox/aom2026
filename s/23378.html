The Impact of Policy Uncertainty on Market Reactions to International Joint Ventures

STR
Shao-Chi Chang (National Cheng Kung University); Lien (Sam) Nguyen (National Cheng Kung University)
This study investigates how economic policy uncertainty in partner countries affects the value creation in international joint ventures (IJVs). Based on the real option theory, hypotheses are tested against a dataset comprising 879 IJVs between U.S. firms and their partners from 25 other countries from 2000 to 2022. The results show that partners’ policy uncertainty is significantly and positively related to the focal firms’ abnormal stock returns surrounding IJV announcements. We also find that the abnormal stock returns are greater when the IJV investments are more irreversible. The findings are robust to alternative asset pricing models, event windows, investment irreversibility measures, and the sample selection bias.

Urgent Strategic Investment Amid Persistent Uncertainty: The Role of Temporal Arbriage

STR
Valen Boyd (Ivey Business School); Pratima Bansal
The robust management literature on resource allocation decisions under conditions of uncertainty rests on a critical assumption: that organizations can delay major investment decisions until uncertainty subsides. In line with this assumption, existing theory emphasizes temporal resource allocation strategies such as staging, deferring, or revisiting resource allocation decisions over time. Yet, such approaches break down in contexts where uncertainty is persistent, urgency to act is high, and where delay or deferral carry risk. In this paper, we exploit one such context to answer the question of how organizations navigate contexts in which there is an urgent need for large, irreversible resource commitments and ongoing uncertainty about outcomes. Using data from a 2-year engaged scholarship project with a home insurance company developing a strategic response to climate change, we inductively develop a theory of temporal arbitrage. Temporal arbitrage enables organizations to respond to the competing temporal pressures created by uncertainty and urgency by enabling the reallocation of resources and risks across time periods, allowing an organization to fund long-term investments without visibly changing present-day cost structures. This work contributes to and extends theory on organizational decision making under conditions of uncertainty by illuminating a novel mechanism that enables organizational action when incremental or flexible strategies to managing uncertainty are not appropriate or available.

Capability Activation Under Environmental Uncertainty: External, Autonomous, and Complementary Modes (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Olivia Scheibel (University of Calgary)
Organizations create value for different stakeholders through resource orchestration, yet the resource management process remains underspecified regarding how capabilities transition from latent potential to active deployment. We extend the resource orchestration framework by identifying three capability activation modes, external, autonomous, and complementary, that operate contingently on stakeholder visibility. Analyzing 1,151 German startups during GDPR implementation (2017-2019), we demonstrate that external activation creates customer value through innovation output in high-visibility domains, while autonomous and complementary ac-tivation create owner value through reduced venture capital dependence in low-visibility do-mains.

How SMEs Balance Exploration and Exploitation Under Geopolitical Crisis (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Amir Mehralian (University of Exeter Business School); Arash Sadeghi (University of Leicester); Md Imtiaz Mostafiz (University of Leicester); Ralf Wilden (Macquarie University); Mehdi Samimi (City University of New York)
We investigate how CEO cognitive flexibility influences product ambidexterity in SMEs under geopolitical crisis. Drawing on upper echelons theory, we theorise organisational improvisation as the behavioural mechanism that converts flexible executive cognition into product ambidexterity. Using the relational view, we further develop internal and external boundary conditions by distinguishing top management team relational attachment and political embeddedness as contingencies that enhance this mechanism. We test the framework with multi-wave, multi-respondent survey data from Iranian technology-intensive SMEs exposed to sanctions and market volatility and find robust support for the model. This study extends upper echelons theory by embedding managerial cognition within a crisis context and specifying organisational improvisation as the behavioural pathway converting CEO cognitive flexibility into product ambidexterity. Embedding this mechanism within a relational view, we delineate the boundary conditions governing its effectiveness, distinguishing internal ties within the top management team from external political embeddedness.

Electoral Uncertainty Over the Investment Lifecycle

STR
Sukrit Singh Puri (London Business School)
How do elections affect firms' growth strategies? Using administrative data on large capital expenditure projects in India (1995-2024) and exploiting 171 staggered state elections, I apply a real options framework to show that pre-electoral uncertainty produces opposing effects on investment behavior. In a stacked differences-in-differences design, I find firms delay new project announcements by 7\% in the three months before elections while simultaneously accelerating existing project completions by a similar magnitude. This divergent response---exercising options to delay versus accelerate---challenges the conventional view that political uncertainty uniformly depresses economic activity. After elections, firms appear to strategically reallocate capital to curry favor with new leadership: with the incoming chief minister's home district receiving a disproportionate increase in new investment projects. These findings demonstrate that electoral cycles create distinct strategic logics for capital deployment, with firms using both timing and geography as tools for navigating political uncertainty and building access to power.

Tension Between Stability and Flexibility: How Does CSI Affect Organizational Resilience

STR
Xin Tan; Stacey Fitzsimmons (University of Victoria)
Periodic major crises, such as the 2008 global financial crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, severely disrupt business environments and jeopardize firms’ survival and sustainability. In this study, we examine how stakeholder pressure, observed by corporate social irresponsibility (CSI), interacts with firms’ corporate social responsibility (CSR), to produce organizational resilience for firms facing a crisis. We explain that CSI influences resilience through two opposing forces, producing a curvilinear (inverse U-shaped) relationship. At lower levels of CSI, it can increase discretion and flexible resources available for adaptive capability, increasing resilience, while at higher levels of CSI, it decreases stability due to heightened public scrutiny, decreasing resilience. Using a global sample of 5,316 firms from 83 countries during the COVID-19 pandemic, we find support for our arguments. Moreover, we find that firms’ pre-crisis CSR weakens the relationship between CSI and organizational resilience. Our explanation is that CSR strengthens firms’ moral capital, weakening stakeholders’ sanctions when CSI incidents are revealed. Together, our findings highlight the combined curvilinear effects of CSI, and moderating effect of CSR, in shaping organizational resilience.
