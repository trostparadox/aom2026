Framing Novelty: How Context Shapes the Evaluation of Entrepreneurial Ideas

TIM
Andres Madariaga Espinoza (KU Leuven); Stijn Kelchtermans (KU Leuven)
Selecting high-potential novel ideas is central yet challenging for accelerator programs and other startup support initiatives. We examine how ventures’ framing strategies, emphasizing feasibility and/or innovativeness, affect their chances of advancing in the European Innovation Council Accelerator, and how this depends on two contextual factors: an idea’s contextual novelty (its atypicality relative to competitors) and evaluator congestion (above-expected submission volume). Using 77,717 applications (2014–2020) and text analysis of proposal narratives, we find that ventures benefit from framing that highlights feasibility or innovativeness, while the absence of either emphasis increases rejection risk. Contrary to expectations, contextual novelty alone lowers rejection probability, and its benefit persists under high congestion. However, feasibility framing becomes especially effective for contextually novel ideas when evaluators are highly congested, suggesting that concrete cues help evaluators process atypical ideas under cognitive strain. These findings extend entrepreneurial framing theory by moving beyond the abstract–concrete dichotomy, showing that multiple framing emphases can coexist and that their effectiveness hinges on the evaluation context. We also contribute to research on novelty evaluation, revealing that congestion can enhance rather than diminish support for atypical ideas when organizational objectives prioritize breakthrough potential.

Implications of Task-Agnostic Gen-AI for Awareness, Expectations, and Value

TIM
Amanda Balasooriya (Southern Cross University); Darshana D Sedera (Southern Cross University)
The paper introduces the notion of Gen-AI agnosticism as a deep philosophical argument as to how Gen-AI is fundamentally different to the traditional information systems. In an empirical validation using 327 individual responses of survey data, the study identifies that Gen-AI agnosticism leads to a high degree of expectation volatility, which in turn shapes users’ value perception. Our findings are drawn to better understand the future user patterns of Gen-AI driven systems, where task and user specificity is minimal or non-existent, disrupting the foundational management principles regarding work specialization, routinization, value creation, and performance measurement.

No Second Chance for a First Impression:How Intuition Can Drive Selection of Highly Novel Ideas

TIM
Olga Gonzalez-Salmeron; Dirk Deichmann (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University)
In the process of developing innovations, evaluators need to judge novel ideas. Previous literature has focused on the importance of making rational decisions. However, we propose that intuition could also be a useful evaluation method under specific circumstances, such as when there is time pressure. Using the data of an innovation championship of a multinational company, we show that evaluators seem to have a negative bias against highly novel ideas, independent of the evaluation approach they use (i.e., rational vs. intuitive). Yet, idea evaluators can decrease this negative bias when they use intuition to evaluate highly novel ideas that are also highly mature. Our paper contributes to the literature on idea evaluation by clarifying when and how intuitive evaluation matters for the selection of novel ideas, relative to rational analysis. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings.

The Mean-Variance Innovation Tradeoff in AI-Augmented Evaluations

TIM
Cyrille Grumbach; Jacqueline Lane (Harvard Business School); Georg von Krogh (ETH Zürich)
Evaluating and selecting among numerous alternative solutions shapes the trajectory and rate of innovation. Central to this process is a fundamental tension between novelty and feasibility that evaluators, operating under bounded rationality, cannot consider simultaneously and therefore rely on heuristics to guide their evaluations. A common heuristic is criteria-sequencing, in which evaluators prioritize alternative criteria at different evaluation stages. Yet, the idiosyncratic ways evaluators sequence these criteria often introduce inconsistencies, creating significant path dependencies in the process. In this paper, we propose that artificial intelligence (AI) offers a potential lever to structure evaluators’ criteria-sequencing heuristics. Leveraging a field experiment with 353 evaluators, we investigate how the sequencing of AI recommendations focusing on novelty and feasibility shapes the mean and variance of innovation among selected solutions. Our results reveal a mean–variance innovation tradeoff: a feasibility-then-novelty sequence leads to selections with higher mean innovation, whereas a novelty-then-feasibility sequence yields selections with greater innovation variance. Furthermore, a post hoc analysis uncovers that the format accompanying AI recommendations also matters. A dynamic format (i.e., interactive chatbot) increases the innovation variance among selected solutions but reduces their mean innovation relative to a static format (i.e., fixed explanatory content). Because these effects operate independently, our findings show that in AI-augmented evaluations, both the sequence of criteria and the format accompanying AI recommendations shape the mean–variance innovation tradeoff. These differences have important implications for the composition of innovation portfolios. Our paper contributes to innovation evaluation research and to emerging literature on human–AI collaboration in innovation-related contexts.

Think Higher, Go Further? The Double-Edged Sword of Abstract Thinking in Profiting from Exaptation

TIM
Lianyong Xu (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Zelong Wei (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
How firms can identify novel applications for existing resources while successfully converting such discoveries into economic value remains a central challenge in exaptation-based innovation. Drawing on cognition theory and profit-from-innovation perspective, this study investigates the role of abstract thinking as a key cognitive antecedent of exaptation and examines its differential effects across the opportunity recognition and value appropriation stages. We tested our hypotheses using data from 334 manufacturing firms (two managers in each firm) in China, the economy with the largest scope in manufacturing sector globally. The results show that exaptation positively affects firm performance, while abstract thinking negatively moderates the relationship between exaptation and firm performance. Moreover, product modularity strengthens this negative moderating effect. This study contributes to the exaptation literature by revealing the double-edged role of abstract thinking, extending exaptation research from opportunity identification to value appropriation, and highlighting the influence of cognition process and technological architecture on exaptation outcomes.
