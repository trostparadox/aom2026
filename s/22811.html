Career Recovery: How People Rebuild Wellbeing After Career Setbacks

CAR
Sachini Edirisinghe (University of Southern Mississippi); Amin Alizadeh (Texas A&M University)
Career setbacks including layoffs, demotions, or stalled progression create serious threats to individuals’ psychological well-being, career confidence, and sense of purpose, especially in unstable labor markets. Understanding how people “recover” from these disruptions is increasingly critical for individuals, organizations, and career practitioners. Previous research highlights career adaptability (Savickas, 2013) and Psychological Capital (hope, efficacy, resilience, optimism) as resources for coping and sustaining careers. For example, it was found that Psychological Capital together with adaptability, played a pivotal role in maintaining careers during the COVID-19 pandemic (Zyberaj et al., 2022). Nonetheless, much of this literature manages adaptation and transitions in isolation and neglects the integrated process of psychological recovery following setbacks. Although adaptability and Psychological Capital clearly offer protective value, key questions remain: How do recovery paths/routes extend over time? What are individual (e.g., past experiences, personality) and organizational (e.g., social support) factors in moderate recovery? And how do emotional, behavioral, and cognitive mechanisms interact during recovery? These gaps limit our understanding of post-setback restoration. We conceptualize career recovery as a distinct, multidimensional process through which individuals rebuild confidence, purpose, and well-being after career setbacks, and develop a process model specifying its stages, mechanisms, and boundary conditions. We derive testable propositions and outline implications for longitudinal and intervention research, offering a recovery-oriented lens for sustainable careers in volatile labor markets. Keywords: Career recovery, wellbeing, career setbacks

The Curse of 35: Involuntary Job Loss, Identity Disruption, and Existential Insecurity

CAR
Rongrong luo (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Chao Fang (University of Liverpool); Alastair Comery (University of Bath); Weize Huang (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
The labour market precarity for mid-career workers, often described as the ‘curse of 35’ exposes individuals aged 30–45 face involuntary job loss, age-related exclusion, and barriers to re-employment. This article explores how an abrupt loss of employment during one’s prime working years leads to an existential crisis, disrupting self-continuity and pushing individuals to reconstruct their identities. Drawing on biographical storytelling interviews with middle-aged workers who experienced involuntary job loss, this study provides an in-depth understanding of how individuals engage in the complex process of re-orienting their ‘being-in-the-world’ during a career rupture. The analysis demonstrates that existential insecurity arises at the intersection of structural displacement, life-course timing, and the subjective value of work. This study contributes to career literature by foregrounding the temporal and biographical dimensions of career disruption. It further demonstrates that career precarity is not confined to marginal employment but can emerge within previously privileged career paths. It highlights the need to consider existential concerns when examining employment disruption and the strategies individuals adopt to reconstruct identity coherence.

Coping with Employment Uncertainty: The Role of Emotion Mindset in Job Search and Mental Health

CAR
Eva Hsin-Lian Lin (New York University Shanghai)
Employment uncertainty is a major life stressor that damages workers’ job search attitudes and mental health. During this difficult time, a psychological paradox emerges: hope, while essential for navigating career challenges, is incredibly difficult to maintain. What differentiates people who maintain hope even in this most difficult career moment from those who do not? I propose a new theoretical framework grounded in the study of mindsets about malleability. I predict that to the extent that individuals facing employment uncertainty believe that emotions can change (i.e., hold a growth emotion mindset), they experience greater hope in their job situation, which in turn shapes better job search (i.e., job search attitudes and success) and better mental health. Study 1 examines adults facing layoffs during the COVID-19 pandemic and shows that those with a growth (vs. fixed) emotion mindset reported greater hope in their job situation, which predicted better job search attitudes and mental health. Study 2 provides causal evidence that experimentally inducing a growth emotion mindset enhances hope, and thus better job search attitudes and mental health among a field sample of furloughed workers in the UK. Finally, Study 3 employs a three-wave longitudinal design with MBA job-seekers facing employment uncertainty during a global economic disruption, showing that mindsets about emotion, through hope, are associated with a real-world marker of job search success: salary. I discuss theoretical and managerial implications for organizational behavior scholarship on hope, mindsets, and employment uncertainty.

Career Identity and Embodied Disruption: Narrating Careers in the Context of Chronic Illness

CAR
Mila Jones (University of Bath School of Management); Stefanie Gustafsson (University of Bath); Simona Giorgi (University of Bath); Katharina Chudzikowski (University of Bath)
Chronic illness is long-term, fluctuating, and unpredictable, with the potential to disrupt individuals’ sense of self and working lives. While prior research in management and organisation studies has examined chronic illness at work primarily through accommodation, disclosure, and stigma, less attention so far has been paid to how it may reshape career identity. Drawing on 40 life-story interviews with professionals living with chronic illness, this paper adopts a narrative identity approach to examine how people make sense of their careers amid ongoing embodied disruption. We identify three self-narratives: performing stoicism, in which individuals conceal illness and sustain performance; adaptive reauthoring, in which careers are redefined to accommodate bodily limits; and seeking recognition, where narrative coherence depends on social validation of illness-related difference. These narratives reveal distinct, situated forms of narrative agency through which professionals preserve, revise, or contest their career identities. The paper contributes to chronic illness research by foregrounding career identity as a central site of illness meaning-making, and to career identity research by theorising chronic illness as embodied disruption that reconfigures narrative agency at work.
