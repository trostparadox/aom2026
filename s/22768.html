Coworker Support, Frustration, & Affective Commitment: The Role of Affective Trust in the Supervisor

OB
Michael Edward Babick (West Virginia University); David Dawley (West Virginia University)
This study examines how affective trust in one’s supervisor influences affective commitment through frustration, and how coworker support moderates this relationship. Drawing on social exchange theory, leader-member exchange theory, and affective events theory, this study conceptualizes affective trust as an emotional mechanism that shapes employee attitudes by reducing workplace frustration. Using a two-wave survey design, data were collected from 150 full-time working executive MBA students and analyzed using partial least squares equation modeling (PLS-SEM). The findings indicate that affective trust in one’s supervisor is negatively related to employee frustration, and that frustration is negatively related to affective commitment.

The Surprising Power of Sadness in Workplace Conflict: When Vulnerability Outperforms Dominance

OB
Peter James Aungle (Harvard University); Yushi Zhang (Independent Researcher); Viknesh Nagarathinam; Daniel Chen
Negative emotions are common at work, yet employees often fear expressing them. This research investigates the interpersonal perceptions of expressing anger, sadness, or no emotion during workplace conflict. We conducted two studies to test the distinct effects on instrumental outcomes (agency, effectiveness, behavior change, status) and relational outcomes (likability, appropriateness). Study 1 reveals that sadness is far more effective than traditionally assumed. It achieves nearly 90% of anger’s perceived effectiveness while receiving more favorable relational evaluations, whereas withholding emotion is rated least effective. Study 2 tests the persistence of gender backlash in expressing anger or sadness. Contrary to long-standing stereotypes that penalize “angry women” or “sad men,” we found no significant gender differences in instrumental outcomes. On relational outcomes, women expressing sadness were rated slightly higher than men, though both were rated positively. When emotion expressions are contextually appropriate (e.g., calm anger or tempered sadness), observers evaluate men and women similarly. These findings suggest that contextual fit of an emotion display, rather than dominance or gender, shape social evaluation in modern workplaces.

Concealed Cynicism in Mission-Driven Organizations

OB
Cathy DiGennaro (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
Organizational scholars and laypeople alike often assume that mission-driven and prosocial organizations are less vulnerable to the cultural and bureaucratic dysfunctions that afflict more instrumentally oriented firms. Yet recent organizational crises, such as Amnesty International’s 2018 breakdown, suggest that these settings may in fact display these dynamics in sharper relief, offering a clear window into what drives them. This work develops a theory of concealed cynicism, a condition in which employees privately doubt their organization’s integrity and effectiveness while continuing to publicly perform optimism, loyalty, and moral alignment. Concealed cynicism emerges from the interaction of three forces that may exist everywhere but are especially pronounced in prosocial work. First, the moralization of organizational mission increases the social, reputational, and identity costs of criticism, casting dissent as disloyalty or moral failure. Second, cynicism arises when a gap perceived by employees between their expectations and organizational realities threatens both the meaning of their work and employees’ sense of self. Third, loyalty to the mission makes exit less attractive. Concealment is then an adaptive response. It is sustained through emotional labor and indirect expression, which provides psychological relief while preserving outward conformity. Although individually protective, concealed cynicism silences potentially useful feedback and allows dysfunctional practices to persist. The paper offers five propositions which specify how mission moralization, expectation–reality gaps, emotional labor, loyalty dynamics, and hidden networks of cynical employees produce and reinforce concealed cynicism. It shows how the same features that make prosocial organizations attractive to employees can also render them fragile.

Unintended Audiences: The Emotional and Behavioral Impact of Leader Compliments on Observers

OB
Shuqi Li (Santa Clara University)
Compliments are a powerful tool leaders can use to enhance employees’ development, work engagement, and job performance. Although the positive effects of compliments on recipients’ well-being and performance are well-demonstrated, it is important to consider how leader compliments influence the broader audience (i.e., employees who witness compliments but are not the recipients). I draw on social comparison theory to suggest that leader compliments can both positively and negatively influence observers through the emotional experiences of inspiration and envy, respectively. Importantly, I identify perceived similarity as a boundary condition that determines the degree to which inspiration and envy occur. Furthermore, while inspiration leads to a moving-up tendency to increase one’s own in-role and extra-role behaviors, envy leads to a pulling-down tendency aimed at harming the complimented coworker. By examining the observer effects of leader compliments, I expand the social functions of compliments beyond the dyadic level and provide a more holistic understanding of compliments in the workplace.

High Performers at Work: Threatened or Admired? Unlocking the Role of Work Climate

OB
Erum Ishaq; Ramsha Zakariya; Jabran Khan (University of the West of England)
This study investigates the hidden dynamics of work climates that shape how performance influences social support at work. Drawing on the data from public and private sector organizations in Pakistan, the findings suggest the double-edged nature of work climates. More specifically the results suggest that in environment with high work avoidance, high performers face envy, eroding peer support, while leaders’ dependency on high performers in similar environment paradoxically strengthens leaders’ support. Conversely, in climates that embrace work approach, peer admiration enhances peer support, but high performers may at the same time trigger perception of threatened hierarchy, reducing leaders’ support. Furthermore, leaders support was found to have a stronger relationship with job performance than satisfaction, whereas peer support was found to have a stronger relationship with job satisfaction than performance. These striking results reveal how work climates can either amplify or diminish the ripple effects of individual performance, offering a fresh perspective for organizations seeking to cultivate collaboration and trust in a competitive landscape.
