Who Serves First? The Role of Experience and Networks When Volunteering as Trustee of a New Charity

PNP
Nara Yoon (James Madison University); Diarmuid McDonnell; David Clifford
In this study, we ask: who serves on the founding boards of newly established charitable organisations? We develop a dual-side perspective that conceptualizes trustee involvement as the outcome of an individual-organisational alignment process. On the individual side, prospective trustees volunteer based on their personal motivations and perceived fit with the organization; while nonprofits seek individuals whose skills and resources meet organisational needs. We contend that certain forms of board capital are pivotal in shaping trustee involvement. To test this claim, we construct a large-scale longitudinal sample of trustee appointments to the boards of all charities in England and Wales between 2011 and 2023. Findings demonstrate that individuals with extensive experiential capital—cumulative prior service as charity trustees—and relational capital—concurrent board affiliations—are more likely to join founding boards. These results highlight the significance of board capital and suggest promising avenues for future research on nonprofit governance.

The Guarding-the-Guardians Dilemma in Cooperatives: Ownership, Control, and Short-Termism

PNP
Fabiana Cherubim Bortoleto (FIA Business School); Fernando Deodato Domingos (FGV EAESP)
Cooperatives are member-owned organizations created to meet collective needs, yet when members also act as managers, the absence of separation between ownership and control raises governance concerns, particularly around short-termism. This study examines how these dynamics shape the financial resilience of Brazilian credit cooperatives, a sector central to financial inclusion under public policy incentives. Using panel data from 2017–2022, complemented by semi-structured interviews, we assess whether member-managers employ discretionary mechanisms that prioritize immediate surplus distribution over reserve accumulation. Results show a consistent link between managerial discretion and short-term decisions, with implications for long-term stability. While short-term allocations may respond to member needs, persistent reliance on them weakens organizational resilience. By connecting these findings to agency problems and horizon dilemmas, the paper advances scholarship on cooperative governance, introduces earnings management as a proxy for managerial discretion, and provides insights for policymakers seeking to balance member participation with institutional sustainability.

Accounting for Social Value Without Accountability: The Social Reporting in Food Banks

PNP
Massimiliano Tufo (University of Sannio of Benevento); Paolo Esposito (Università degli Studi di Napoli "Parthenope")
Food banks play a critical role in addressing food insecurity, yet they often generate social value without formally accounting for it. Drawing on institutional theory, this study investigates the “silence of accountability” by examining social reporting practices among Italian regional food banks and the consequences of noncompliance for legitimacy and stakeholder relationships. Using a qualitative multiple-case study of 5 regional food banks, based on semi-structured interviews and documentary analysis, the findings reveal heterogeneous accountability practices despite a shared institutional framework. Noncompliance with social reporting is primarily driven by resource constraints, procedural complexity, and the absence of legal obligations. While the lack of formal social reporting does not undermine the capacity to meet social needs, it limits the formalization and external recognition of social value. This silence constrains engagement with institutional stakeholders, funders, and strategic partners requiring standardized accountability mechanisms.

The Social Life of Money: Fiscal Bureaucrats in Public Sector Organizations

PNP
Sandra Okonofua (Yale University)
Public organizations live and die by their budgets, yet between federal funding mandates and classroom services lies an opaque social process: fiscal bureaucrats translating abstract appropriations into organizational capacity. This paper examines fiscal bureaucrats—public sector employees whose primary responsibility involves managing finances within mission-driven organizations—focusing on CFOs, budget directors, and school business managers in K-12 education. Drawing on 28 months of participant observation and 19 interviews across resource contexts, I theorize everyday fiscal statecraft as the daily labor of making policy material through fiscal mediation. I demonstrate that fiscal bureaucrats do more than administer funds: they engineer possibility. Through everyday fiscal statecraft, they translate local needs upward into policy-compliant requests while converting abstract regulations downward into operational procedures. Through meso-level discretion, they exercise authority that shapes what services street-level workers can deliver. Through fiscal meaning-making, they construct symbolic boundaries around "categorical" money as instruments of organizational power. These findings reveal fiscal bureaucracy as hidden infrastructure of inequality—an organizational layer where resource disparities become institutionally embedded through differential capacity for policy implementation. By illuminating how fiscal bureaucrats mediate between state power and organizational mission, this research reconceptualizes budgeting as relational work where technical compliance masks discretionary authority that fundamentally shapes public service delivery.

Antecedents, Mechanisms, and Outcomes of Participation in Regulatory Governance

PNP
Greici Sarturi (Federal University of Santa Maria); Bianca Bigolin Liszbinski; Carlos Henrique Rodrigues; Carla Bonato Marcolin (Federal University of Uberlândia)
Public participation has become a central feature of contemporary regulatory governance, yet participatory reforms often produce uneven and sometimes symbolic outcomes. Existing research tends to explain participation either as a function of individual citizen attributes or as the result of institutional and procedural design, offering limited insight into how participatory inputs are translated into regulatory decisions. This article addresses this gap by developing a multilevel, mechanism-based framework that conceptualizes participation as a relational governance capacity emerging from the interaction of individual, organizational, and institutional conditions. Drawing on a systematic literature review, the study organizes evidence using an antecedents– mechanisms–outcomes logic. At the individual level, cognitive and emotional capacities shape engagement but affect outcomes only through mechanisms of interpretation, learning, and sense of ownership. At the organizational level, structures, resources, culture, and leadership condition whether participatory inputs are processed through mechanisms of responsiveness, administrative translation, and organizational learning. At the institutional level, political culture, legal frameworks, and state capacity shape participatory authority through mechanisms of authority attribution, incentive alignment, and legitimation. The analysis further identifies a set of processual mechanisms that operate across levels and foster (or undermine) alignment over time. The article contributes to public administration theory by moving beyond input-oriented accounts of participation and specifying how participatory arrangements become substantively influential in regulatory contexts.
