Cognitive and Emotional Consequences of Algorithmically Mediated Video Interviews

CTO
Akshat Lakhiwal (University of Georgia); Hillol Bala (Indiana University Bloomington); Wei Chi (Tsinghua University); Jinyi Zhou (Renmin University of China); jiayi yin (University of Science and Technology Beijing)
Algorithmic evaluation systems are increasingly used in hiring, yet their psychological impact on candidates remains weakly understood. While such systems promise greater consistency and objectivity, emerging research suggests they may also generate stress and discomfort during evaluative interactions. We propose a dual-pathway framework distinguishing candidates’ cognitive appraisals, i.e., perceived uniqueness, from their affective responses, i.e., anxiety, and argue that these pathways are shaped by distinct features of algorithmic evaluation design. Using a 2×2 experiment, we manipulate evaluation structure, i.e., single-agent vs. human–AI dyad, and evaluative agency, i.e., human-primary vs. AI-primary, in a standardized AI-enabled interview context. Results show that evaluation structure primarily influences cognitive appraisals: candidates viewed human–AI dyads as more standardized than single-agent systems, particularly AI-only evaluation. In contrast, evaluative agency shaped affective responses: AI-primary conditions heightened anxiety and discomfort relative to human-primary conditions, even when the interview process remained constant. These findings reveal a core tension in algorithmic hiring design: features that enhance cognitive legitimacy may not mitigate, and may even exacerbate, affective anxiety. Our study contributes to research on algorithmic decision-making by introducing a structural–agential framework for evaluating algorithmic systems and focusing on the trade-offs that hiring organizations must navigate when integrating AI into evaluative roles. We discuss implications for human–AI collaboration, fairness in technologically mediated judgment, and the psychological design of hiring systems.

Identity Alignment and Generative Artificial Intelligence Governance in Swiss Universities

CTO
Ilaria Falvo (Università della Svizzera Italiana); Benedetto Lepori (Università della Svizzera Italiana)
This study examines how Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) govern Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) under conditions of distributed, bottom-up adoption, in which “non-adoption” is not a practicable strategic option due to user-driven diffusion. Building on organizational identity (OI) literature (Gioia et al., 2000; Livengood & Reger, 2010; Tripsas, 2009) and Diffusion of Innovation theory (Rogers et al., 2014), we analyze how universities interpret GenAI in relation to salient identity claims and how adopter category – early adopters and latercomers – shapes the stabilization of governance responses. We conduct a comparative multiple-case study of three Swiss universities exposed to the same technological innovation following the public release of ChatGPT (November 2022), in a context of high institutional autonomy and limited uniform regulation. Across cases, GenAI rapidly entered core academic routines through students’ and faculty’s use, shifting the organizational challenge from adoption choice to governance. Findings show that identity alignment and enactment capacity jointly explain heterogeneous governance architectures. Where GenAI was framed as identity-aligned, universities moved toward early stabilization by creating institutional devices such as living guidelines, training infrastructures, endorsed tools, and disclosure standards. However, early adoption was not synonymous with open integration: depending on which identity facets dominated (innovation/service or integrity/authority), early governance took either an enabling or a controlling form. A comparative model maps governance configurations as the joint outcome of identity alignment and enactment capacity/field-position pressure.

Self- vs Team-Identification With GenAI: Perceptual (In-)Congruence and Technostress Effects

CTO
Mario Schaarschmidt (University of Duisburg-Essen); Dugi Xhigoli (University of Duisburg-Essen); Ajurthan Rameskumar (Universität Duisburg-Essen)
Personal generative AI assistants such as ChatGPT are increasingly embedded in everyday work practices, evolving into personalized digital companions. As employees engage more deeply with these systems, interactions with GenAI become intertwined with self-concepts and team dynamics, raising questions about employee well-being and sustainable adoption. While prior research has focused on individual-level identification, little is known about how identification with personal GenAI assistants jointly interacts with perceived team-level identification with GenAI assistants and how alignment and misalignment shape technostress and its downstream consequences. Consequently, this study examines how self-identification and perceived team-identification with personal GenAI assistants influence technostress and, in turn, social isolation and adoption difficulties. Survey data from 349 professional GenAI users were analyzed using polynomial regression and response surface analysis with bias-corrected bootstrapping. Results reveal that technostress dimensions respond differently to identification dynamics. Techno-complexity is shaped by (in-)congruence patterns, with misalignment between self- and team-identification leading to increased techno-complexity. Techno-insecurity is primarily driven by individual self-identification rather than relational alignment. Both dimensions significantly increase social isolation and adoption difficulties, with techno-complexity emerging as the stronger mediating pathway. This study demonstrates that identification with personal GenAI assistants is socially embedded. Organizations should manage GenAI adoption as a socio-technical change process, aligning team-level identification to reduce techno-complexity while supporting individual competence to mitigate techno-insecurity.

Owning Work in Human-AI Collaboration: Psychological Ownership and Emotional Consequences

CTO
Sona Gachayeva (University of Stirling); Daria Morozova (Leiden University)
Drawing on psychological ownership theory, this study examines how AI integration into professional work reshapes job-based psychological ownership and the affective consequences of this disruption. We argue that job-based psychological ownership, grounded in control, job-relevant knowledge, and self-investment, becomes disrupted when AI intervenes in core work activities. We employ a multi-study research design, combining two qualitative case studies and an experimental study, with additional studies currently underway. The qualitative studies examine legal professionals and marketing professionals in Azerbaijan, revealing how AI reshapes ownership by redistributing control, expertise (knowing what to do), and effort between humans and technologies. Across both professional contexts, ownership was preserved when individuals retained control over task direction and decision-making, applied their expertise, and invested personal effort, but diminished when AI assumed responsibility for defining task substance, executing work with minimal human involvement, or providing substantive advice on what to do. Reduced ownership was consistently associated with diminished feelings of accomplishment and pride. Building on these insights, an experimental study using a professional task simulation provides initial causal evidence that reduced control over AI-supported outputs increases negative self-conscious emotions, particularly shame. The findings highlight ownership as a critical mechanism linking AI integration to professionals’ emotional experiences of work, with implications for human-AI collaboration and the future of work.

AI as a Teammate? Exploring Gen Z’s AI Self-Efficacy, Remote Work Attitude and Social Connectedness

CTO
Priya Chaudhary (International Management Institute); upasana Dhanda (International Management Institute); Simple Arora (University of Delhi); Rajni Hira (Humber College); Dian Ekowati (Airlangga University)
This study interrogates how Gen Z defines remote work through their unique engagement with AI. Positioning AI as a quasi-teammate, the research examines alternative truths about technology-mediated work: that subjective AI self-efficacy, rather than technical fluency alone, drives positive attitude toward remote work and fosters social connectedness. Through a survey of 625 Gen Z professionals in India, Indonesia, Australia, and France, findings reveal that anthropomorphic interaction and perceived AI assistance significantly predict positive attitude toward remote work, which enhances social connectedness. These results challenge assumptions of universal remote work dynamics, highlighting the need for culturally attuned AI design. The study highlights that organizations may rethink digital workplace designs and embrace generational management truths.
