Teleworking in the Metaverse: Exploring a Polymorphic Workplace Through Immersive Netnography

CTO
David Jiwon Hong (Paris 1 Panthéon-Sorbonne University); Alain Lacroux
While numerous studies on telework have emphasized its limitations, such as isolation and reduced opportunities for informal interaction, immersive technologies now offer new possibilities for collaboration. In this context, metaverse-based telework can be understood as part of the broader concept of New Ways of Working (NWW), contributing to a shift from a stable, physical workplace toward a polymorphic environment that blends physical and digital dimensions. This study adopts a phenomenological approach and employs an immersive netnography methodology, combining observations inside a virtual world and semi-structured interviews, to explore how employees experience this way of working and how it differs from traditional remote work. Findings show that immersive remote work can foster engagement, enjoyment, a sense of belonging, and avatarial embodiment, while also recreating certain dimensions of presence that enhance conventional teleworking. Nonetheless, technical and physiological challenges persist, highlighting the need to support user adaptation and well-being. The paper concludes with practical implications for organizations considering the implementation of immersive forms of teleworking.

Smart Cities as Wonderland: Digital Legitimacy and the Digital White Rabbit

CTO
Filippo Marchesani (Gabriele d'Annunzio University); Francesca Masciarelli; Federica Ceci; Andrea Prencipe (Luiss university)
Contemporary cities are increasingly shaped by accelerated processes of digital implementation, often pursued under conditions of urgency, competition, and institutional pressure. While smart cities are commonly framed as initiatives aimed at enhancing efficiency, inclusion, and sustainability, critical urban scholarship has highlighted their ambivalent and contested effects. This paper conceptualizes smart cities through the allegorical lens of Wonderland, capturing their spatial and institutional complexity, and introduces the metaphor of the Digital White Rabbit to account for the accelerating tempo of digital technologies implementation. Building on critical debates on smart cities and urban governance, we argue that the advancement of digital technologies produces non-linear effects on urban digital legitimacy, thereby challenging linear and cumulative understandings of smart city development. We test this argument using a longitudinal dataset of 30 Italian cities observed over a 14-year period (2010–2023), revealing systematic inverted U-shaped relationships between digital technologies and three dimensions of digital legitimacy: adoption, responsiveness, and diffusion. These findings contribute to current debates by reframing smart city development as a site of tension between technological acceleration and digital legitimacy, highlighting the importance of aligning its implementation with citizens’ everyday practices. The study offers both theoretical and empirical implications by encouraging a critical reflection on when, how, and for whom digital technologies are implemented in cities.

Digital Twins in Marketing: Conceptualization, Value Mechanisms, and a Research Agenda

CTO
Alexandra Dominique Danielle Theben; Alberto Lempira Guevara Chacon (UPF Barcelona School of Management); Anna Akhmedova (International University of Catalonia)
Digital twins are widely used in engineering and operations, yet their relevance for marketing remains conceptually unclear and empirically underexplored. This paper develops a marketing-grounded conceptualisation of digital twins that extends beyond data-driven targeting to emphasise live state representation, simulation, and policy-bounded intervention across the product and customer lifecycles. We distinguish product, consumer, and market or system digital twins and organise their use around four recurrent stages of marketing work - developing, testing, communicating, and engaging. Building on this framework, we articulate a consolidated research agenda that specifies the mechanisms and boundary conditions through which digital twins shape insight generation, decision quality, customer experience, and interfirm coordination. By clarifying when and how digital twins create value in marketing, this paper provides a foundation for cumulative empirical research on a rapidly emerging class of technologies.

How Virtual Reality Affects Cultural Souvenirs Purchase: A Narrative Perspective

CTO
lifan gao (University of Science and Technology of China); Hefu Liu; Huiyu Liu (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Jiahong Xu (Hainan University)
As tourism institutions increasingly pivots toward immersive technologies, AI-integrated Virtual Reality (VR) offers transformative potential for deepening visitor identity and fostering engagement. However, the integration of AI-driven personalization with digital environments poses challenges to both the visual and auditory aspects of storytelling. Rooted in a narrative perspective, this research interrogates two pivotal design dimensions central to AI-driven VR storytelling: narrative authenticity (objective vs. reconstructed) and narrative voice (internal vs. external). Through two experiments, we examine the mechanisms by which these structural features influence visitors’ cultural identity and subsequent souvenir consumption. Our findings reveal that reconstructed authenticity, when synergized with an internal narrative perspective, significantly amplifies cultural identity. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the effectiveness of these narrative combinations in promoting visitor purchases is moderated by the symbolic value of cultural souvenirs. This study advances the theoretical boundaries of immersive marketing and provides strategic prescriptions for cultural institutions seeking to harness AI’s potential. Our research provides practical guidance for virtual reality design and tourism marketing, and these findings can also be applied to other industries focused on cultural value.
