Session overview

This session investigates how decisions are shaped by technological systems, social hierarchies, and the design of workplace environments. The papers provide a multi-level perspective on how AI, social context, and physical workspaces jointly influence judgment, fairness, and performance.

Differences of Fairness Perception in Human and AI Decision-Making: Evidence from ERP

NEUB
Bin Ling (Hohai University); Qingying Lu (Hohai University); Long Chen (Hohai University); QIONG JIA (Hohai University); Yihan Wang (Hohai University)
This paper investigates how decision-maker agent and allocation fairness jointly influence fairness perception. Using event-related potential (ERP) techniques, the study observes dynamic brain changes during the experiment. The findings reveal that under conditions of low anthropomorphic robot, unfair stimuli elicit more negative N100 amplitude and more positive P300 amplitude. These results demonstrate that the degree of anthropomorphism of the AI allocator not only modulates fair decision-making behavior but also cognitive processing of fairness, providing neurophysiological evidence to support the differences in fairness perception towards various allocators (AI vs. human).

Some Like It Loud - Effects of Occupational Noise on Perception, Physiological and Work Performance

NEUB
Sajjad Shooshtari (Politecnico di Milano); Marco Mandolfo; Charles Corbett (University of California Los Angeles); Dominic Loske (Technical University of Darmstadt); Matthias Klumpp (Politecnico di Milano)
Ambient sound is a ubiquitous but understudied operational variable in human-centric work systems. While occupational noise is typically treated as a nuisance or regulatory concern, its effects on individual performance remain poorly theorized, especially in settings requiring sustained manual precision. To address such aspects, this study investigates how varying ambient sound levels influence human performance in a manual assembly context. We report findings from a controlled within-subject experiment in a realistic manufacturing setting, where participants performed an industrial assembly task under four occupational noise levels. Accounting for individual differences in noise sensitivity and neurophysiological responses related to cardiac and cortical activity, gathered via electrocardiography and electroencephalography during task execution, our analyses reveal that objective sound loudness does not directly affect performance outcomes. Instead, performance outcomes effects arise through individual subjective appraisals of sound condition. We find that perceived loudness interacts with individual noise sensitivity to shape productivity in manufacturing environments. Counterintuitively, noise-sensitive individuals performed better under higher-level ambient sound exposure, while low noise-sensitive individuals’ performance dropped under low-level sound exposure. By integrating behavioral performance data with neurophysiological evidence, these findings challenge the assumption of uniform responses to occupational sound and reframe ambient noise as a variable to be strategically calibrated in organizational blue-collar work design.

Cognitive Biases Affect Financial-Term Disclosure on Investor Evaluations (WITHDRAWN)

NEUB
Sebastiano Massaro (University of Surrey); Frédéric Ooms (HEC Liège, Management School of the University of Liège, Liège, Belgium); Scott Shane (Case Western Reserve University)
Entrepreneurs receive conflicting advice about whether to include deal terms—such as the desired valuation, the amount of capital to be raised, and equity to be sold—in startup pitch decks. Some investors argue that transparency regarding desired terms increases credibility, while others caution that it dampens interest. Whether such disclosure helps or harms founders is a theoretically-relevant empirical question, which we address through two randomized controlled trials. Study 1 examines the causal effect of adding a “deal-terms” slide to otherwise identical startup pitch decks. Across 1,008 real investor–pitch evaluations, including deal terms significantly decreases investor favorability across four measures of investment intent, irrespective of whether the terms are favorable or unfavorable. Having established this behavioral effect, in Study 2, we investigate the underlying mechanism by testing seven candidate explanations drawn from prior behavioral decision research: ambiguity aversion, psychological reactance, signaling, cognitive load, decision avoidance, anchoring and adjustment, and loss aversion. In a multilevel experiment manipulating high versus low values for valuation, capital, and equity, we find that only perceived ambiguity acts as a significant mediator: quantitative specificity reduces informational ambiguity, which in turn shapes investor judgments. Collectively, these findings provide evidence on both whether and why financial term disclosures influence investor judgment, demonstrating that deal-term transparency alters perceived informational clarity rather than the substantive attractiveness of the opportunity.

When Beliefs Translate into Performance: Metacognitive Regulation under Feedback Conflict

NEUB
Yaqi Yang; Sohvi Heaton (Baylor University); Yi Huang (Lingnan University); Oi Ling Siu (Lingnan University)
Managers often face feedback that is incomplete, delayed, or internally inconsistent, raising the question of when learning-oriented beliefs translate into effective performance. Building on self-regulation theory, we propose that metacognitive strategy use (MSU)—planning, monitoring, and revising one’s approach—functions as a regulatory amplifier that conditions whether growth mindset becomes behaviorally consequential under feedback conflict. Across two complementary studies, we test this account. A quasi-experimental field study shows that increases in growth mindset predict improvements in job performance only when MSU is high; when MSU is low, belief change has negligible effects. A complementary event-related potential study distinguishes conflict detection from motivational engagement during feedback processing, showing that growth mindset primarily amplifies sustained attentional engagement with desirable feedback, particularly among individuals high in MSU. Together, these findings demonstrate that learning-oriented beliefs are conditionally effective and advance a process-level account of belief-driven adaptation under uncertainty.

Is it Just Coping?: A Gene-Environment Interaction Perspective on LGBTQ+ and Prosocial Career

NEUB
Yimo Zhu (National University of Singapore); Zhaoli Song (National University of Singapore)
Management scholarship on LGBTQ+ career development has predominantly focused on responses to discrimination, with less attention to individual agency and the interplay of inherent traits with environmental factors. This study advances career theory by integrating Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) with Gene-Environment interaction (GxE) principles, proposing a more comprehensive model of prosocial career choices among LGBTQ+ individuals. We theorize how genetic predispositions linked to same-sex behavior (SSB-PGS) influence prosocial occupational choices via prosocial personality. Crucially, we find that this mediated relationship is positively moderated by self-reported sexual orientation—conceptualized as reflecting a unique set of social and developmental experiences. Specifically, identifying as a sexual minority acts as a contextual "activator," amplifying the translation of genetic predispositions into prosocial personality traits. Using data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health) combined with O*NET occupational information, we find robust support for this first-stage moderated mediation model. By shifting focus from primarily reactive behaviors to generative gene-environment interactions, our research illuminates the agency and distinct strengths LGBTQ+ individuals bring to their careers, particularly in prosocial domains.
