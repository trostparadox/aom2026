Political Stewards in SOE Halls: How Officials-Turned-Executives Shape Risky Geopolitical Investment

STR
Xu Jiang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Tao Xu (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Jun Xia (The University of Texas at Dallas); Manxin Wang
This study proposes a political stewardship perspective to elucidate how executives in centralized political systems shape corporate outward investment strategy within state-owned enterprises (SOEs). We investigate the role of officials-turned-executives (OTEs)—former government officials appointed as SOE executives—within the framework of international geopolitical investment. We contend that traditional governance theories, such as classical stewardship theory and agency theory, inadequately explain executive behavior in state-dominated firms. Our political stewardship perspective reframes executive alignment as a function of political loyalty, bureaucratic trust, and responsiveness to regime priorities. Analyzing a comprehensive panel dataset of 1,384 listed Chinese SOEs from 2014 to 2022, we find that OTE-led firms are more inclined to invest in state-led geopolitical initiatives. This tendency is particularly pronounced during abrupt successions, which heighten political entrustment to OTEs, and when OTEs are appointed by central authorities, signaling deep political embeddedness. These findings enhance a context-sensitive understanding of executive governance in hybrid political economies, providing novel insights into the state-corporate nexus in global strategy.

The Impact of Board Dynamics on Hybrid Organization Performance: An Institutional Logics View

STR
Shisong Jiang (Wuhan University); Shuo ZHANG (Wuhan University); Shaoqun Gao (Wuhan University)
Hybrid organizations are viewed as the outcome of blending institutional logics. While the coexistence of multiple institutional logics enables firms to expand their practices and access critical resources, it also engenders tensions and conflicts. Prior research has largely assumed that institutional logics are already established in hybrid organizations and has focused on resolving these tensions, thus overlooking how new institutional logics are introduced and how the interplay between incumbent and emergent logics shapes organizational performance. Drawing on a board dynamics perspective, this study conceptualizes directors of boards as institutional logics carriers and examines the context of reverse mixed ownership reform in China. Specifically, we investigate how directors appointed from SOEs introduce a state socialism logic into private firms and how the relationship between this logic and the prevailing market capitalism logic affects firm performance. We find that, upon introduction of directors appointed from SOEs, the state socialism logic both complements and conflicts with the market capitalism logic, resulting in an inverted U-shaped relationship between directors appointed from SOEs and hybrid firm performance. Moreover, the political connections of remained chairman exacerbates this inverted U-shaped effect, whereas the proportion of foreign directors in hybrid firm mitigates it. Using a sample of 275 merger and acquisition events involving 232 publicly listed private firms undergoing reverse mixed ownership reform in China between 2007 and 2023, our empirical results support these hypotheses. Theoretically, this study contributes by explaining how new institutional logics are introduced into hybrid organizations and how the interplay between emergent and incumbent logics influences performance from a board dynamics perspective. Additionally, it uncovers directors of boards’ deeper motivations under varying institutional logics contexts, revealing a more dynamic process of director interactions.

Canadian Universities: Underfunding or Inability to Adapt? A Resource Dependence Perspective

STR
Sara Murphy (Ivey Business School); Seemantini Madhukar Pathak (Ivey School of Business); Dusya Vera (Western University)
Public universities across the Western world report unprecedented financial losses following government funding restrictions, yet substantial performance variation within jurisdictions experiencing identical policy shocks presents a strategic puzzle. Resource dependence theory (RDT) suggests organizations can mitigate environmental constraints through strategic action, particularly executive succession, but recent scholarship identifies power imbalance as a potential boundary condition limiting this theory's applicability to resource-disadvantaged public institutions. We test whether executive succession strategy moderates the impact of government funding cuts on Canadian public university financial performance, examining if RDT's prescriptive power holds for public organizations facing extreme resource constraints. Using difference-in-differences analysis, we compare 20 Ontario universities to 18 control institutions across 10 years (2014-2024), leveraging Ontario's 2019 tuition cut and freeze as a natural experiment. Findings reveal the policy shock produced modest negative effects on operating surplus (~$21 million decline) but did not independently predict deficits. Ontario universities with inexperienced or internally hired presidents after 2019 showed 8 times higher probabilities of deficit. In contrast, longer provost tenure generated revenue gains while frequent provost turnover reduced financial performance. Results demonstrate that strategic leadership choices critically determine organizational outcomes under resource scarcity, while revealing nuanced boundary conditions for RDT in public institutional contexts.

Interpreting Governance Roles: Board Members’ Role Mindsets in Entrepreneurial Ventures

STR
Krystal Zhao-Jansson (Stockholm School of Economics); Mattias Nordqvist (Stockholm School of Economics); Roxana Turturea (Stockholm School of Economics)
How do board members in new ventures interpret and enact their governance roles under conditions of uncertainty? Prior research on venture boards has largely conceptualized board roles through predefined functional categories, such as monitoring, resource provision, and strategic advising, while giving limited attention to how individual board members themselves make sense of what it means to govern entrepreneurial firms. Drawing on a qualitative study of 40 interviews with board members and CEOs across six deep-tech life science ventures, we develop an interpretive, cognitively grounded account of how board members make sense of their governance roles under uncertainty. We introduce the concept of role mindsets—recurring cognitive frameworks through which board members interpret their responsibilities and determine appropriate forms of engagement. Our analysis identifies two role mindsets, dynamic and anchored, shaped by configurations of thinking disposition, motivation, role perception, and engagement logic. We further show how board members navigate interpretive tensions—intra-individual, peer-evaluative, and context-specific—that prompt ongoing recalibration of role understanding. By conceptualizing venture governance as an interpretive accomplishment rather than a set of fixed role prescriptions, this study advances research on the cognitive micro-foundations of board governance in entrepreneurial settings.

Board Size, Governance Effectiveness, and Higher-Education Closure

STR
Dali Ma (Drexel University); Seungju (Suri) Lee (Drexel University)
This study examines how board size relates to the survival of higher education institutions. Based on a longitudinal analysis of U.S. private non-profit universities/colleges, we find a U-shaped relationship between board size and closure risk, suggesting that while moderate board expansion may support governance, further expansion can be associated with elevated risk. This pattern is substantially more pronounced among smaller universities with limited buffering capacity. Together, these findings are consistent with the argument that an increasing emphasis on university board’s secondary functions, such as fundraising, representation, and external engagement, may crowd out effective oversight and timely strategic intervention. Our study offers important insight into how the board’s primary governance function can weaken as boards appear to gain resources and external reach.
