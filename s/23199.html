Entrepreneurial Pathways and Students’ Well-Being: The Mediating Role of Human Capital

ENT
Ilaria Cascavilla (University of Bergamo); Sílvia Costa (University of Groningen)
Entrepreneurial activity is increasingly promoted within universities and among students, and student entrepreneurship has gained special attention as a distinct form of entrepreneurship and as a field of research. However, it remains unclear what implications engaging in an entrepreneurial pathway during one’s studies has for students’ competency development and well-being in this critical period of their personal development. We examine how students’ entrepreneurial pathways – starting, continuing, disengaging, or not engaging in entrepreneurship – affect their subjective well-being. We further investigate how perceived shifts in entrepreneurship-related human capital, gained through university educational offerings during the same period, mediate this relationship. Drawing on Human Capital Theory, we test a mediation model using longitudinal data from 1,830 students across 33 countries (GUESSS, 2021–2023). Students who started or continued entrepreneurial activities reported greater increases in entrepreneurship-related human capital, which in turn was associated with higher subjective well-being. No significant mediation was found for students who disengaged. By clarifying the mechanisms linking entrepreneurial engagement and well-being, these findings offer critical insights for universities striving to effectively support students in their entrepreneurial endeavors in an healthy manner.

Is Faster Entrepreneurial Process Always Better? The Role of Prior Experience

ENT
Masatoshi Kato (Kwansei Gakuin University)
This study investigates whether the speed of the entrepreneurial process is inherently advantageous by examining the duration of nascent entrepreneurial activity. Using a rich dataset of new firms in Japan, we analyze how the duration of preparation relates to start-up size. Drawing on the resource-based view in combination with the concept of time compression diseconomies, we argue that the nascent phase is a critical period for the steady accumulation of strategic resource stocks. The results reveal a significant heterogeneity based on human capital: longer preparation increases start-up size for entrepreneurs without prior experience, whereas its marginal returns diminish—or even become negative—among those with such experience. These findings suggest that “faster is better” only when entrepreneurs possess sufficient prior experience. Consequently, instead of promoting a one-size-fits-all acceleration, policies should be tailored to the specific initial resource stocks and human capital of the individual entrepreneur.

Orchestrating Human Capital Investment and Technological Capabilities for ENT Firms’ Performance

ENT
Yueqi Wang (Dalian University of Technology); Yuchen Shao (Dalian University of Technology)
Considering the lack of favorability in labor market compared to incumbent rivals, entrepreneurial firms have to obtain and develop human resources via human capital investments (HCI) in creating competitive advantages. Yet, present literature offers contradictory views towards the nexus between HCI and firm performance, thus raising difficulties in predicting the performance effects of HCI among entrepreneurial firms. Anchoring upon the resource orchestration perspective (ROP), we illustrate the dual effects of HCI on firm financial performance: HCI not only increase performance through the resource acquisition mechanism, but also entails inefficiency in resource allocation mechanism and leads to inferior performance. Integrating these two opposite mechanisms, we expect an inverted U-shaped relationship between HCI and financial performance among entrepreneurial firms. Furthermore, considering the notion of fit between resources and capabilities in optimizing firm performance as ROP suggests, we contend that the curvilinear relationship is moderated by firms’ technological capabilities in terms of digital and R&D ones. While the former attenuates the relationship, the latter amplifies it. With archival data of 235 Chinese listed entrepreneurial firms in manufacturing sectors from 2010 to 2020, our predictions are supported. Overall, by elucidating the contingent performance implications of HCI among Chinese entrepreneurial firms, we contribute to the strategic entrepreneurship literature in the context of emerging economy.

Path-Dependent Effects of the Initial Human Capital on Further Development of the Founding Team

ENT
Tatiana Zabara (University of Antwerp)
New managerial hires are critical milestones in new venture development, enabling professionalization and adaptation but requiring resources that many young firms lack. Prior research identified a professionalization paradox rooted in founding team human capital: ventures with limited initial human capital tend to face the strongest need to professionalize, yet access to the external investment often required to enable managerial hiring is itself conditioned by the quality of the founding team. As a result, it remains unclear whether external investment allows ventures to overcome early human capital constraints or instead reinforces initial differences. Building on human capital theory and research on organizational path dependence, we examine how initial human capital and external investment jointly shape new managerial hiring over time. Using longitudinal event-history data on 130 technology-based new ventures in Flanders, we show that initial human capital robustly predicts both access to external investment and subsequent managerial hiring. External investment further increases the likelihood of managerial hiring without offsetting the direct effect of initial human capital. Together, these findings reveal a Matthew effect in entrepreneurial team evolution: early advantages in new ventures’ initial human capital are reinforced rather than compensated for, leading to cumulative and path-dependent professionalization trajectories in new ventures.

Same, Same, But Different: Effects of Multilayered Founder Team Diversity and Startup Performance

ENT
Jae Cho; Massimo Gaetano Colombo; David Heller
Entrepreneurial teams often consist of individuals with diverse backgrounds and capabilities that shape new venture performance. This paper provides an integrated perspective on diversity as a multilayered and dynamic concept that determines the trajectories of nascent ventures. We theorize that diversity entails both benefits and costs, which depend on specific diversity layers. Their relative importance hinges on the advantages of complementary capabilities and the disadvantages of heightened communication and coordination costs, both of which vary with startups’ market environment. To test these predictions, we combine detailed founder characteristics from Revelio Labs with startup funding data from Crunchbase, covering 12,625 founders and 4,933 startups. Using an inverse Jaccard index to measure founder team diversity, we find that startup performance is optimally supported by moderate levels of internal diversity (i.e., demographic features assigned at birth) and high levels of external diversity (i.e., social skills and human capital acquired over founders’ lives). These relationships are dynamic: the relevance of diversity for performance outcomes is substantially lower for startups founded during economic downturns than during boom times. Our results highlight the need for an integrated perspective to better understand the role of diversity in entrepreneurial outcomes by examining different diversity layers over time.
