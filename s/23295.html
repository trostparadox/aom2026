Political Polarization and Venture Capital Investment

ENT
Shiqiang Chen (University of Macau); Douglas Cumming (Stevens Institute of Technology); Antai Li (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); Yonggen Luo (Guangzhou University)
Political homogeneity preferences can significantly impact venture capital (VC) and startup ecosystems, creating challenges and opportunities associated with exacerbated uncertainty and behavioral biases. On the one hand, political homogeneity preferences could worsen VC markets due to problems with cooperation and information sharing. On the other hand, VCs are very sophisticated financial intermediaries, and the standard negative political polarization outcomes in other contexts might not necessarily apply to VCs. U.S. data from 1990-2021 indicate that political homogeneity preferences shrink the number of syndicated investors, lengthen the time between staged investment rounds, reduce the total number of staged rounds, lower the likelihood of IPO and acquisition exit outcomes, and reduce IPO valuations.

The Humble Narcissist’s Advantage: A Paradox Perspective on Entrepreneurial Political Will

ENT
Woody Go (University of North Texas)
Entrepreneurship is an inherent social endeavor that requires the navigation of complex political landscapes. While existing research examines the capability to influence others (political skill), it largely overlooks the motivational aspect (political will). This paper extends the concept of political will into the entrepreneurial context, examining how the paradoxical interplay of dark and bright personality traits shapes the motivation to engage in self-serving versus benevolent political will. Utilizing paradox theory, I argue that the rivalry facet of narcissism promotes self-serving political will, while the admiration-seeking facet promotes benevolent political will. I further posit that the lower-order facets of humility – accurate self-view, appreciation of others, and teachability – moderate these relationships. These propositions offer a theoretical framework for understanding how more narcissistic entrepreneurs may successfully navigate social influence opportunities through the tempering effects of humility.

The Error(s) in Our Ways: Entrepreneurship in the Age of Political Extremism

ENT
Maria-Irene Angelidis (Kean University); Scott L. Newbert (Baruch College, CUNY)
Political ideology provides a lens through which prospective entrepreneurs understand and act within their social and economic realities. Drawing on significance quest theory, we argue that because political ideologies provide simple explanations of how the world works, extreme adherents to them tend to overestimate their knowledge of their environments, such that they are less likely to seek out new and/or disconfirmatory information about entrepreneurial opportunities. By failing to engage in this information-gathering process, these extremists should be more likely to commit errors of omission and commission, rendering them less likely than centrists to be entrepreneurs, subject to important boundary conditions. Using data from the World Values Survey, we test our hypotheses on more than 17,000 respondents across 39 countries and find that political extremism is negatively related to entrepreneurship and that this relationship is magnified for individuals experiencing precarity and attenuated for those living in weak institutional environments. We validate our findings through a series of robustness checks and a self-replication. Our study contributes important new insights regarding the value of information-gathering for entrepreneurs in an increasingly polarized world.

Walk the Talk? Entrepreneurs’ Political Ideology and New Venture Performance

ENT
Lea Raemy (University of Bern)
How does the entrepreneur’s political ideology influence new venture performance? This paper addresses this novel and intriguing research question by building on political ideology literature and upper echelon theory. The paper’s theorizing suggests that the entrepreneur’s political ideology – and specifically, their economic and social liberalism – is linked with their new venture’s social and environmental as well as financial performance in distinct ways. Additionally, a moderating effect of the entrepreneur’s narcissism on these main relationships is expected. The empirical analysis of 10,628 entrepreneurs from 55 countries partially confirms the theorizing and provides additional nuanced insights. By introducing the entrepreneur’s political ideology as a key explanatory factor of relevant new venture outcomes, this study provides several important contributions to the entrepreneurship literature.

Where Do the “Grand Challengers” Emerge? The Role of Spatial Proximity and Political Ideology

ENT
Mariza Gabriela L. Mesquita (Louisiana State University); Joseph J. Cabral (Louisiana State University)
Drawing upon the construal level theory and institutional theory, this study examines under what conditions entrepreneurs initiate ventures in response to different opportunities. By dissecting grand challenges into root and symptomatic opportunities, we explore the impact of spatial proximity and institutional context as predictors of where new ventures emerge. Findings indicate that regions exposed to root opportunities, as well as those with liberal political ideologies, are more likely to see the emergence of new ventures in the short term. In contrast, regions facing the consequences of those grand challenges (symptomatic opportunities) are associated with the emergence of ventures over the medium term. These insights contribute to understanding the contextual factors that shape venture creation, complementing known individual-level drivers of engagement in entrepreneurial action.
