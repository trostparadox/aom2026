When Fairness Fails to Earn Gratitude: Explaining Negative Megaphoning

OB
Maysam NOUR EDDIN (Toulouse I Capitole University); Marc Ohana (KEDGE Business School); Togan KILIC (KEDGE Business School)
Gratitude in organizations is often assumed to arise naturally from fair or favorable treatment. Drawing on moral affect theory, we challenge this assumption by arguing that gratitude is a moral emotion that emerges only when such treatment as benevolent rather than instrumentally motivated or contingent on expectations of repayment. Focusing on supervisors as central organizational representatives, we propose that overall supervisory justice perceptions (OSJP) elicit employee gratitude, which in turn discourages employees from engaging in negative megaphoning—informal communication that broadcasts unfavorable views about the organization to internal and external audiences. We further argue that this process depends on supervisors’ bottom-line mentality (BLM), which shapes employees’ attributions regarding the motives underlying fair treatment. Using three-wave data from 419 employees in the United States and United Kingdom, structural equation modeling supports a first-stage moderated mediation model. OSJP positively predicts gratitude, gratitude negatively predicts internal and external negative megaphoning, and gratitude mediates this relationship. However, high supervisor BLM weakens the OSJP–gratitude link and eliminates the indirect effect. These findings clarify when OSJP elicits gratitude and identify BLM as a key boundary condition shaping gratitude’s moral force.

Timing Matters: How Pre-Help Gratitude Expression Influences Help Autonomy and Willingness to Help

OB
LIMEI CAO (Sun Yat-Sen University); Yu Zhou (National University of Singapore); Zijie Song (Sun Yat-Sen University); Yuewei Yao (Sun Yat-Sen University); Tian Jin (Zhejiang University); Xin Qin
Although a substantial body of research has examined the benefits of expressing gratitude after help is provided, far less is known about the consequences of expressing gratitude before help occurs. Drawing on psychological reactance theory, we investigate pre-help gratitude expression—a common yet understudied practice in which individuals express gratitude at the time of making a help request. We propose that, despite appearing well intentioned, pre-help gratitude expression can backfire by implicitly threatening the receiver’s perceived freedom to refuse, thereby reducing their help autonomy and willingness to help. Across five studies employing a multi-method design (a critical incident study, an archival analysis, and three scenario-based experiments), we find that expressers systematically overestimate the positivity and effectiveness of pre-help gratitude expression, whereas receivers experience it as autonomy-threatening. Consistent with this account, pre-help gratitude expression reduces receivers’ willingness to help through diminished help autonomy. We further find that embedding options within pre-help gratitude expression can mitigate this negative effect. Together, our findings reveal a counterintuitive cost of pre-help gratitude expression, clarify its underlying psychological mechanism, and offer a theoretically grounded intervention for expressing gratitude more effectively in help-request contexts.

Grateful but Not Better: When Employee Gratitude Fails to Improve Performance

OB
Sophia Chia-Min Chou (National Taiwan Normal University)
Research suggests that gratitude can motivate employees to reciprocate benevolent treatment with stronger performance. Yet in practice, employees may feel grateful without performing better. Prior work rarely examines when this gratitude–performance link decouples and has largely emphasized motivational explanations. Addressing this gap, this study examines when and why employee gratitude toward leader benevolence fails to translate into performance improvement. Adopting a qualitative, dual-perspective design, this research drew on semi-structured interviews with 30 supervisors and open-ended survey narratives from 129 employees. Thematic analysis reveals three sets of contingencies that can block gratitude-based performance gains. Motivational constraints operating at individual, relational, and organizational levels undermine sustained effort investment. Capability constraints at these same levels limit what employees can deliver even when willing. Contextual constraints spanning work resources, organizational structures, and the external environment restrict the conversion of effort into results regardless of motivation and capability. By extending a predominantly motivation-centric view of gratitude at work, this study offers a contingency framework that clarifies why benevolent leader–employee exchanges do not reliably yield performance improvement.

Not All Subordinate Gratitude Expression is Created Equal: A Leader Identity Perspective

OB
Yuting Chen (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Wen Feng (Renmin University of China); Xiao-Yun Xie (Zhejiang University); Zijun Dong (Zhejiang University)
Gratitude expressions have attracted increasing scholarly attention, with prior social psychology research highlighting their positive effects on expressers, recipients, and expresser-recipient relationship. Yet, in hierarchical organizations, studies have largely focused on subordinates but leaving the mechanism of how gratitude expression shapes leadership unstudied. By shifting the focus from leaders as expressers to recipients, this article investigates whether, how, and when subordinate gratitude expression shapes leaders’ perceptions and behaviors. Drawing on leader identity theory, we explore the mechanisms through which subordinate gratitude contributes to leadership development. Using an experience-sampling design with 84 supervisors over 10 days, we find that subordinate gratitude expression enhances transformational leadership behavior via leader identity. Moreover, not all subordinate gratitude expression is created equal, the effect depends on the strength of leader agreeableness. These findings advance theory and practice by illuminating the role of gratitude in leadership processes and identifying boundary conditions that shape its effectiveness.

Getting What They Wish For? Public vs. Private Leader Gratitude and Its Consequences

OB
Can Zhang (Beijing Jiaotong University); Linran Wang
Leaders’ expressions of gratitude can motivate employees’ positive attitudes and behaviors. However, in which setting (public vs. private) and to whom leaders should express gratitude remains unclear. We argue that this question is crucial because not all recipients welcome leaders’ public acknowledgments. Drawing on expectancy violations theory, we propose that relative to one-to-one expressions, leaders’ expressions of gratitude in front of an audience can evoke a sense of contempt in the recipient, thereby reducing identification with the leader, particularly when leader–member relationship quality is high. By contrast, private expressions are more likely to foster recipients’ sense of belongingness and strengthen their identification with the leader, especially under weaker leader–member relationship quality. Evidence from a survey study and an experiment employing a real-world vignette design supports our theorizing. Our study offers theoretical guidance on the appropriate contexts and targets for leaders’ expressions of gratitude and enriches the workplace gratitude literature.
