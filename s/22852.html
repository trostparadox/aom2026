Leader Accountability: A Framework of Emotional vs. Task Accountability on Team Member Well-Being

OB
Jacob Levitt (Kelley School of Business, Indiana University)
The benefits of leader accountability for task outcomes are well established. Despite this, it is unclear whether leader accountability is beneficial, harmful, or even related at all to team member well-being. I propose that whether leader accountability is beneficial or harmful depends on what part of their role leaders feel accountable for—their task or emotional impact. I suggest that the established construct of leader accountability is better considered as leader task accountability—leaders feeling responsible for how their reasoning and decisions impact the team’s task—and propose a novel construct of leader emotional accountability—leaders feeling responsible for how their affective expressions impact team members. I argue that leader task accountability is harmful for team member well-being whereas leader emotional accountability is beneficial. To explain this, I draw on theory of compassion and contend that leader compassion mediates these relationships such that greater leader task accountability reduces leader compassion and greater leader emotional accountability increases it. Lastly, I argue that leader task interdependence moderates these effects. I test my model across several studies including a scale validation pilot, two field studies, and two supplementary experiments finding general support. This paper contributes to research on accountability and leader emotions.

You Wanna Know My Boss? How Supervisors React to Subordinates’ Skip-Level Leader-Member Exchange

OB
Feng Qiu (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Wei Wang (University of Manitoba, Asper School of Business); Xueqi Wen (Tongji University); Lingtao Yu (The University of British Columbia); Michelle K. Duffy (University of Minnesota); Sijia Zhao (Tongji University)
While research has begun to examine Skip-level Leader Member Exchange (SMX), defined as the exchange relationship between an employee and their leader’s leader (i.e., skip-level leader), we know little about how the employee’s direct supervisor, who sits outside of the SMX dyad yet could still be affected by it, would respond to SMX, and how these responses would alter interpersonal dynamics. Integrating power dependence theory with advancements in jealousy literature, we propose a contingent dual-pathway model that explains how supervisors emotionally and behaviorally react to a subordinate’s high SMX, contingent upon the power-dependence dynamics within the triadic relationships. Specifically, we argue that depending on the supervisor’s dependence on the skip-level leader and the subordinate, high SMX could evoke supervisor jealousy toward the subordinate but also— surprisingly—toward the skip-level leader. These two forms of jealousy exert opposing effects on the supervisor’s support for the subordinate, with the former diminishing support and the latter enhancing it. We tested our model through two studies: a field study in the hotel industry and a second field study in the chemical industry. Across both studies, the results consistently supported our hypotheses. We conclude by discussing the theoretical contributions, practical implications, and future research directions.

Benefitting or Suffering From A Curious Leader? A Conservation of Resources Perspective

OB
Fengya Chen; Zhining Wang; Yuanmei (Elly) Qu (Rowan University)
Curiosity is increasingly recognized as a key leadership trait that drives innovation, enhances team performance, and fosters employee engagement. However, the dark-side of leader curiosity remains underexplored. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, our study investigates the complex relationships between leader curiosity and employee outcomes, highlighting both the bright and dark sides of leader curiosity. By conducting an experiment (Study 1) and a field study (Study 2) , we examine how leader curiosity influences employee creativity, task performance, and workplace withdrawal behavior. Our findings reveal that while leader curiosity can trigger employees’ inspiration, it can also lead to emotional exhaustion. Such inspiration and emotional exhaustion can further exert impacts on employee outcomes, including creativity, task performance, and workplace withdrawal behavior. Moreover, cognitive flexibility emerges as a critical resource that moderates these effects, amplifying the positive and buffering the negative outcomes of leader curiosity. Practical implications and future research directions are discussed.
