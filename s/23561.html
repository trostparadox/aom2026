Behavioralizing Absorptive Capacity: Organizational Slack as a Precondition in R&D (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Yanni Chen (Huaqiao University); James C. Hayton
Despite extensive research on absorptive capacity (ACAP), prior studies have primarily conceptualized it as a cognitive capability, with limited focus on the behavioral processes through which it develops. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm, we view ACAP as a multi-component construct, involving knowledge acquisition, assimilation and/or transformation, and exploitation, all unfolding through organizational search processes triggered by problems or slack. In the R&D context, we treat technological innovation as a behavioral manifestation of ACAP. We argue that assimilation-based exploitation is linked to incremental innovation, while transformation-based exploitation enables the application of knowledge, leading to radical innovation. Using a panel dataset of general equipment manufacturing firms from 2017 to 2022, we empirically test a behavioral precondition model and find that both financial slack and technical human resource slack positively impact technological innovation, with stronger effects on radical innovation. These findings clarify the behavioral foundations of ACAP and highlight organizational slack as a critical enabler in the R&D context.

Orthogonality and Synergy in Innovation Portfolios

TIM
Dieter Penninckx (KU Leuven); Robert A Burgelman (Stanford University); Bart Van Looy (KU Leuven)
Building on real options theory, this paper models value dynamics in innovation portfolios composed of induced (exploitation-oriented) and autonomous (exploration-oriented) initiatives. Rather than valuing initiatives in isolation, our analytical model focuses on how portfolio value emerges from interactions among multiple real options, which are traditionally treated as residuals. Within the model, both orthogonality and synergy are conceived as governance design choices: separation preserves variety and hence value when uncertainty is high, while selective integration enables learning, scaling, and value capture as uncertainty resolves. In line with Ashby’s notion of requisite variety, the model allows firms to align innovation portfolios with unfolding diffusion patterns. As such, the analytical framework provides a unified and tractable language for understanding how top management can continuously shape, realign, and scale corporate innovation portfolios over time to secure strategic renewal.

How Sequence Positions Shape Technological Evolution: Novelty, Impact, and Branching Across Technology Topics

TIM
Amit Jain (National University of Singapore); Changhong Yi (National University of Singapore)
Technological evolution is often viewed as a process from emergence to stabilization, with maturity implying exhaustion. This paper challenges this endpoint view by developing a sequence-based account of technology topics, defined as emergent categories of inventions organized around prototypical problem-solution patterns. Because prior inventions reshape later search conditions, sequence position should shape an invention’s novelty, impact, and branching. Within topics, inventions may first lose novelty and impact as search becomes locally constrained, but later regain both as inventors differentiate from established topical patterns. This logic also extends across topics: inventions may become knowledge inputs to new technological directions, a process we conceptualize as branching. Branching is expected to be strongest at early and late positions. Early inventions provide raw but foundational building blocks, whereas late-stage differentiated outputs can be redeployed in new application contexts. Using 44,178 biotechnology patents filed between 1970- 2015, we recover technology topics through hierarchical Latent Dirichlet Allocation and construct topic-specific invention sequences. Sequence analysis and high-dimensional fixed-effects models show that novelty and impact decline and later rebound, while branching is especially pronounced at early and late positions. Technological maturity, therefore, is not exhaustion; it can generate differentiated inventions that regain influence and seed new directions.

Unblocking the Path to Original Innovation: The Synergistic Mechanism of Innovation System

TIM
Zixian Wan (Tsinghua University); Xinzhi Chang (Beijing University of Civil Engineering and Architecture); ZOU JI (Tsinghua University)
This study addresses the dual challenge of generating and transforming original innovation within enterprises by examining the synergistic mechanisms of endogenous drivers and exogenous incentives integration. Integrating Technological Innovation System (TIS) and Sectoral Innovation System (SIS) perspectives, the paper constructs a Set-theoretic mediation model to explore the causal pathways driving innovation system effectiveness. Utilizing a multi-method approach—fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), regression analysis, and case studies—on data from 73 Chinese listed companies hosting national key laboratories (2018–2022), the research identifies the structural configurations that catalyze original innovation generation capacity and original innovation conversion efficiency (TFP).The findings reveal that: (1) The deep synergy between TIS (endogenous drivers) and SIS (exogenous incentives) constitutes the fundamental mechanism for bridging the "generation–conversion" gap. (2) Three distinct high-performance models emerge: the Government-guided innovation ecosystem development model, the Market-Policy synergy-driven hub-linkage Model, and The structural dominance model driven by strategic leadership and market demand. These configurations highlight the diverse roles of enterprise leadership in optimizing innovation resource allocation. (3) While the innovation system promotes TFP through a chain-mediated path, outcomes are highly heterogeneous. Optimal configurations achieve a synergistic leap in innovation system effectiveness by balancing "capacity" (generation) and "efficiency" (conversion); however, resource misallocation can trigger a crowding-out effect, leading to an "efficiency paradox" where high generation capacity fails to translate into productivity gains. Finally, the study proposes a three-dimensional integrated framework to optimize the national innovation system's collaborative capacity and strategic responsiveness.

What Firms Know Before They Integrate: The Role of Architectural vs. Component Knowledge

TIM
Woo-Yong Park (University of Nevada-Las Vegas); Faisal Khurshid (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); Inkyu Kim (San Diego State University)
Technological change often increases product complexity by introducing tighter interdependencies among components, prompting firms to reconsider market versus hierarchical governance. While Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) predicts that rising complexity favors vertical integration, firms that integrate at ostensibly similar points frequently experience markedly different performance outcomes. We address this puzzle by examining how firms’ pre-existing technological knowledge shapes both the timing of vertical integration and its performance consequences under conditions of rising complexity. Integrating TCE with the attention-based view and the architectural innovation & modularity literature, we theorize that architectural knowledge and component knowledge generate distinct cognitive representations that influence how managers interpret coordination challenges and assess governance alternatives. Architectural knowledge broadens managerial attention to system-level interdependencies, delaying vertical integration, whereas component knowledge narrows attention to localized frictions, accelerating integration decisions. These timing differences have important performance implications: firms that integrate later, when complexity becomes high, outperform early integrators. Moreover, even among late-switching firms, pre-existing architectural knowledge supports superior coordination and performance relative to component knowledge. We test our hypotheses using data from the U.S. bicycle gear-shifting market from 1985 to 2000, a setting characterized by sequential innovations that progressively increased product complexity. Our findings foreground bounded rationality as a central mechanism shaping both when firms vertically integrate and why the performance consequences of integration vary systematically across firms.
