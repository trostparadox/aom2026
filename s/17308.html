Preparing Career-Ready Business Graduates: AI-Enhanced Workplace Education

Authors: Chung-Kai Huang National Taipei University of Business; CHUNYU LIN National Taipei University

Session overview

The rapid diffusion of generative artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed the way learning, communication, and professional development are conceptualized in higher education (Jin et al., 2025). As a variety of language models and other tools become increasingly accessible, business and management educators face a shift in pedagogical challenges and a paradigm change in teaching and learning (Idris et al., 2024). This new AI phenomenon raises concerns about integrating AI into existing curriculum and instruction in ways that enhance cognitive engagement and higher-order thinking (George, 2023). Given such a transformation, the new changes do not intend to undermine learner agency, reflective judgment, or professional identity development (Mouta et al., 2025). This challenge is particularly prominent in vocational-track universities, where business and management education is closely tied to applied competence, employability, and immediate workplace readiness (Liu, 2025). While prior research has investigated models regarding AI adoption and technology acceptance in higher education, more attention needs to be paid to the learning processes through which AI use shapes students’ motivation, engagement, and higher-order thinking in workplace-related training courses and domains (Saad et al., 20255). Responding to this gap, the present study investigates the pedagogical design and learning outcomes of an AI-enhanced workplace communication and training course implemented in a vocational-track university in Taiwan. Adopting a Management Education and Development (MED) perspective, the study focuses on how students’ intrinsic and extrinsic learning motivations influence higher-order thinking through cognitive engagement and how AI application functions as a contextual condition that moderates these learning relationships. Rather than treating AI as a stand-alone instructional intervention, this study conceptualizes AI use as a learning partner embedded pedagogical element that helps scaffold students’ professional inquiry (Wungow et al., 2025; Msambwa et al., 2025). The course was delivered to senior and junior undergraduate students majoring in International Business, most of whom were approaching graduation and preparing for entry into the workforce. Given such orientation in vocational-track higher education, this workplace course was designed around the concept that AI serves as a scaffolding tool, integrated with individual reflection, iterative feedback and revision, and collaborative problem solving. The overarching goal was to cultivate professional communication competence while participating in different instructional course modules that help promote the relevant workplace knowledge, skills, abilities, and necessary characteristics. For the individual assignment, students were required to participate in learning tasks designed to connect the inside-out reflection with professional practices. First, students were introduced to the Japanese concept of ikigai framework to engage in a reflection exercise. This reflection invites them to consider what brings happiness and meaning to their lives and articulate the intersection of their personal values, passions, strengths, and anticipated contributions based on their career aspirations. This activity aimed to improve intrinsic awareness and understanding of one’s aptitude and preferences. Afterwards, students developed a career map linking professional expertise and expectation trajectories to short- and long-term planning. Upon completing this map, students translated their reflections into resume writing work tailored to the job description they found on the manpower job bank. Their crafting of a resume should reflect their occupational values and industrial or societal needs by emphasizing alignment with workplace expectations. Overall, these tasks were geared towards integrating motivation, identity, and communication practice in developmental manners. At the group level, design thinking was employed to help students focus on diagnosing and improving workplace environments and issues in real-world contexts. Ground in the design thinking model, students were involved in five non-linear, iterative stages: empathize (understand respondents), define (clarify the core problem), ideate (brainstorm solutions), prototype (build tangible models), and test (get respondent feedback) to solve complex problems in a human-centered way. Working in small teams, students identified workplace-related challenges by interviewing and talking to job incumbents from the profit or non-profit organizations they were interested in exploring. For example, developing healthy workplace habits for prolonged sitting in the office, looking for strategies to achieve work-life balance in Asian business culture, and preventing and responding to workplace bullying. Through iterative stages of empathy building, problem definition, ideation, and solution prototyping, students collaboratively developed proposals aimed at enhancing workplace quality and employee well-being. This project emphasized experiential learning, collaborative team efforts, and the application of communication concepts to authentic workplace problems. Incorporating multi-stage feedback and revision is a main characteristic of the course, through which we anticipate cultivating learner agency and reflective learning. Initially, students completed drafts of their individual and group assignments. In the second round, students engaged in structured peer feedback activities guided by explicit criteria and guidelines. This stage fostered noticing the mistakes, constructive dialogue, and positioning students as potential contributors to one another’s learning. Only in the third revision stage were students guided to use generative AI by using prompts to refine their writing output. In addition to language refinement, students used AI for idea clarification and alternative ways of expressing ideas clearly, rather than for content generation only. The AI tool was intentionally adopted in a pedagogically purposeful manner by following the principle that AI technology does not make decisions for learners, but it helps support the thinking and learning processes. As a result, students were reminded to take responsibility for evaluating AI-generated suggestions, including their lexical appropriateness and ethical considerations. This act aimed to prevent overreliance on AI while promoting flexible usage. To examine students’ learning outcomes underlying this research design, the study focused on five variables: extrinsic motivation, intrinsic motivation, cognitive engagement, AI application, and higher-order thinking. We collected data by convenience sampling through validated survey instruments administered at the end of the course. Three classes of business undergraduate students registered in the Workplace Communication and Training course at a vocational-track university in Taiwan were invited to participate in this research and we ended up with 100 valid samples. We used AMOS and SPSS software to conduct confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation modeling, mediation analysis, and moderated mediation analysis to test the hypothesized relationships in the proposed learning mechanism. The results revealed several important findings and implications. First, the measurement survey showed satisfactory reliability and construct validity across the selected variables. Both intrinsic learning motivation and extrinsic learning motivation were positively associated with higher-order thinking. That is, students who reported stronger internal interest and enjoyment in learning, as well as those motivated by external goals, demonstrated higher levels of analytical, integrative, and critical thinking. These findings suggest that intrinsic and extrinsic motivations can coexist as complementary drivers of advanced vocational-track students’ learning outcomes in this given setting. Also, cognitive engagement emerged as a critical learning mechanism that partially mediated the relationship between both intrinsic and extrinsic learning motivation and higher-order thinking. This indicated that intrinsically motivated students were more likely to invest mental effort and deep processing, which in turn sustained higher-order thinking. Moreover, externally motivated students can achieve cognitive outcomes as well when instructional design facilitates their meaningful involvement in learning tasks. The mediation effects were partial rather than full, suggesting that motivation continued to exert direct influences on higher-order thinking beyond the influence of engagement alone. Last, the AI application demonstrated a positive moderating effect on the relationship between cognitive engagement and higher-order thinking. When students reported higher levels of AI application, the positive association between cognitive engagement and higher-order thinking became stronger. This finding indicates that using AI as a supportive tool can help students reorganize ideas and explore alternative perspectives and refined expressions, which consequently amplify the cognitive benefits of engagement. Nonetheless, the analyses did not support a moderated mediation effect. Although AI application moderated the direct relationship between cognitive engagement and higher-order thinking, it did not moderate the indirect effects of intrinsic or extrinsic learning motivation on higher-order thinking through cognitive engagement. Namely, AI influenced what happened after engagement was established, but it did not reshape the motivational pathways fundamentally that led students to become cognitively engaged. These findings, from an MED perspective, are theoretically and pedagogically insightful, as they show that AI functions effectively as a learning partner rather than a motivational driver. Students’ intrinsic and extrinsic motivations remained grounded in instructional task meaning and social learning processes while AI strengthened the merits of learning once engagement was already present. This distinction reinforces the concept of grounding AI integration in solid design in curriculum and instruction rather than expecting technology alone to motivate learning. In summary, this study makes several contributions to the literature of business and management education by shifting the focus from AI adoption to learning mechanisms. It highlights how motivation, engagement, and the use of AI collectively lead to higher-order thinking in vocational training and development. While promoting learner agency and professional identity development, generative AI can be integrated into workplace communication courses to advance cognitive outcomes pedagogically. For business and management educators, the study underscores the importance of designing an AI-integrated curriculum that prioritizes reflection, peer interaction, and meaningful engagement. These are the needed competencies for preparing students to grow sustainably in AI-supported work environments in the future.
