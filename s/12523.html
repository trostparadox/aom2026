Resolving the Sustainability-Profitability Paradox Towards a Regenerative Ethical Paradigm

Author: Nisha Maria Ninan University of Deusto

Session overview

Organisational responses to sustainability challenges continue to be shaped by mechanistic leadership paradigms that prioritise control, efficiency, and short-term financial performance. From a Critical Management Studies perspective, we interrogate the sustainability–profitability paradox as a manifestation of deeper power relations and normative assumptions that render ecological and social concerns subordinate to economic rationality. Despite the proliferation of sustainability frameworks, existing approaches rarely integrate the cognitive, relational, and mindset-based capacities required to navigate this paradox effectively. This paper addresses the research question: How do leaders navigate the paradoxes and tensions embedded in sustainability initiatives, and what capacities enable or constrain transformative change? The study develops an integrative framework combining paradox theory, stakeholder pluralism, and the sustainability mindset to conceptualise sustainability leadership as both inner and relational work. Empirically, it draws on qualitative action research conducted within a collaborative sustainability network, involving reflexive engagement with leaders navigating competing financial, social, ecological, and moral logics. Data from interviews, observations, and collective sensemaking were analysed iteratively to surface patterns of tension, regression, and reorientation. The findings demonstrate how instrumental and compliance-driven sustainability practices are frequently reproduced under organisational pressure, while reflexive and values-based practices create openings for alternative, regenerative logics. The paper contributes to Critical Management Studies by challenging technocratic approaches to sustainability and reframing leadership as a contested, ethical, and political practice. It offers critical insight into how paradox navigation can support more relationally accountable and socially just forms of organising within complex socio-ecological systems.
