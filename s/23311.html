Shifting Stances: Consumers' Reactions to Corporate Sociopolitical Activism Inconsistency

STR
Ishva Minefee (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Lori Qingyuan Yue (Columbia University in the City of New York); Der-Ting Huang (Copenhagen Business School); Taeksang Jang
Conventional wisdom holds that firms will maintain a consistent stance during sociopolitical debates. Yet, firms increasingly shift their stances, which has prompted consumer boycotts and buycotts. We address this relatively unexplored but important phenomenon by developing a theory of consumers’ reactions to corporate sociopolitical activism (SPA) inconsistency. Drawing on expectancy violations theory (EVT) and attribution theory, our framework posits that consumers who support progressive causes react negatively to SPA inconsistency that entails retrenchment—shifts in a firm’s stance away from consumers’ desire for corporate support of a progressive cause. Conversely, these consumers react positively to SPA inconsistency that entails realignment —shifts in a firm’s stance towards consumers’ desire for corporate support of a progressive cause. Furthermore, we argue that these reactions are contingent upon the strength of moralized expectations, or beliefs about what a firm ought to do, at the individual and societal levels. Specifically, consumers’ moral conviction regarding the sociopolitical issue and shifts in the sociopolitical climate surrounding the issue independently and jointly shape expectations of consistency and the attributions consumers make when firms shift their stances. Taken altogether, our framework reorients corporate SPA research away from treating inconsistency as a uniformly negative phenomenon.

No News is Good News? How Firm Nonresponse Shapes Social Activist Strategies

STR
Junhyung Byun (IESE Business School); Desiree F. Pacheco; Massimo Maoret (IESE Business School)
Firms increasingly face demands from activist organizations, yet we know little about how activists interpret corporate nonresponse, defined as the absence of any visible or substantive reaction to an activist’s claim. We argue that nonresponse is a common but ambiguous signal whose meaning depends on both dyadic and field-level relational contexts. Drawing on 1,291 interactions across 157 activist–firm dyads from 2015 to 2024, we find that nonresponse, on average, escalates activists’ confrontational tactics. However, this escalatory effect weakens when firms have more frequent interactions, both with the focal activist and with other activists in the field. We also find that a stronger historical pattern of nonresponse to other activists reduces the likelihood of confrontation following a focal nonresponse episode, suggesting that activists use broader relational patterns as interpretive cues. Our findings advance theory by identifying nonresponse as a consequential, but previously overlooked driver of activist escalation. They also deepen our understanding of how the contentiousness of activist–firm interactions depend on the historical relational context.

Is Where You Sit, Where You Stand? Exploring Social Positioning and Strategic Differentiation

STR
Young Hou; Jared D. Harris (University of Virginia); Michael Lenox (University of Virginia)
There is broad and increasing interest in the sociopolitical positions of business organizations. Leveraging the geographical distribution of large U.S. publicly traded companies, we study the conditions under which firms adopt social positions, highlighting a relationship between the political leanings of a firm’s stakeholders, as measured by their voting records, and the social positions the firms adopt. This reveals the importance of stakeholder responsiveness in driving the social positioning of specific firms: While some firms benefit from taking progressive stances, political polarization also creates opportunities for them to take stances that align with stakeholders who lean more conservative. This study demonstrates that a firm's strategic stakeholder position is responsive to the social preferences of some of its primary stakeholders: the customers and employees proximate to its geographically dispersed locations.

Corporate Accessibility as a Hedging Strategy Against the Internationalization of Social Activism

STR
Shubham Singh (Rutgers University)
Transnational activism has become increasingly visible, yet we lack a systematic understanding of when and why local grievances escalate into cross-border campaigns. We address this gap by theorizing and testing how the interplay of corporate and political opportunity structures influences the internationalization of activism against MNEs. We argue that transnational escalation is most likely when firms have closed corporate structures, especially those that restrict stakeholder engagement, and when activists face closed political environments that constrain domestic avenues of contestation. Under these dual constraints, activists lack both organizational allies and institutional outlets, prompting them to internationalize local grievances by mobilizing transnational networks, global media, and external institutions to amplify their claims. Leveraging a novel global dataset of over 150,000 activism events (2010–2022), we examine how corporate internal accessibility and external accessibility affect the likelihood that local contention becomes internationalized. Our results show that greater corporate accessibility significantly reduces the likelihood of transnational escalation, and this mitigating effect is especially pronounced in countries with more open political opportunity structures.

Stripping the Veil: Market Reaction to Word-Deed Alignment (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Samuel Kim (Washington University in St. Louis)
Organizations often make claims on their employee racial diversity that overstate the actual workforce composition because talk is cheap but action is costly. Yet we know little about how the market reacts to these racial diversity washing behaviors be- cause it is difficult to observe the gap between diversity rhetoric and actual workforce composition. Exploiting the mandatory disclosure of EEO-1 reports by 19,000 U.S. federal contractors that exposed their true employee diversity levels as an exogenous shock, I examine how the market responds to the revelation of actual racial diversity relative to firms’ prior diversity narratives. Using cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) as the main dependent variable, I find that firms with higher actual employee racial diversity experience significantly positive abnormal returns upon disclosure. More strik- ingly, firms exhibiting high racial diversity washing (i.e., those whose prior diversity talk on employee racial composition substantially exceeded their actual workforce racial di- versity) experience significantly lower abnormal returns relative to their non-washing peers. These findings reveal that mandatory transparency converts diversity washing from a low-risk strategic practice into a costly liability, with important implications for the credibility of corporate social claims and the role of disclosure in disciplining organizational decoupling.
