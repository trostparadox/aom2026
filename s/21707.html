CoCoh: Hybrid Collaboration Coordination, Team Empowerment, and Psychological Safety

Authors: Lisa-Maria Marquardt Technische Universität Chemnitz; Carsten C. Schermuly

Session overview

Hybrid work has become a defining feature of contemporary organizing. Yet the field is increasingly confronted with a paradox: widely debated levers such as increasing office presence or implementing return-to-office mandates do not reliably improve core employee or organizational outcomes. At the same time, collaboration in hybrid settings remains fundamentally team-based and interdependent, suggesting that the question is less where people work and more how teams coordinate collaboration across time, place, collaboration modalities and preferences. Recent work has developed a behavioral construct capturing such coordination: Coordination of Collaboration in Hybrid Teams (CoCoh). Building on this measurement foundation, we shift attention to an unresolved explanatory gap: why and when does hybrid coordination translate into higher-order team outcomes, particularly team innovation? Drawing on team self-determination theory, which emphasizes how need-supportive team interactions shape collective motivational dynamics and performance-relevant team processes, we theorize CoCoh as a motivationally consequential coordination process that fosters team innovation via team empowerment. Team empowerment reflects a shared experience of meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact, and has been linked to enhanced team functioning and performance. We further propose psychological safety as a climate-based boundary condition. We test this moderated mediation model using multiwave survey data from hybrid team members. Preliminary evidence from a two-wave dataset indicates a strong positive association between CoCoh and team empowerment (r = .62), supporting the central motivational premise. A second field study will test the whole model, results will be presented. By integrating a validated hybrid coordination construct with team SDT and a safety-based boundary condition, this paper advances a process-focused account of when hybrid work becomes innovation-enabling—and offers actionable implications for designing coordination practices and climates that unlock teams’ innovative potential.
