Humanizing AI Without Accountability: How Anthropomorphic Design Shapes Blame Attribution

Saturday, Aug 1 2026 · 4:00PM–5:00PM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 6
SIM

People

Author: Sannah Asif Rutgers Business School

Session overview

Organizations increasingly deploy artificial intelligence (AI) systems in ethically consequential domains such as hiring, evaluation, and decision support. To promote trust, engagement, and adoption, firms often anthropomorphize AI through human-like names, voices, or conversational styles. While prior research suggests that anthropomorphism increases perceptions of agency, its implications for moral evaluation and accountability in organizational contexts remain unclear. In particular, it is unknown whether human-like AI design increases responsibility attribution or instead alters moral judgment in more subtle ways. This study examines how anthropomorphic AI design shapes blame attribution and perceived moral agency following an unethical algorithmic decision. Using a between-subjects experimental vignette, participants evaluated an organization that used an AI system to support hiring decisions, resulting in a biased outcome. The AI was presented either in an anthropomorphized form, using human-like design cues, or in a non-anthropomorphized, technical form. Participants then assessed blame attributed to the AI system and the organization, as well as the AI’s perceived moral agency. Results reveal a counterintuitive pattern. Anthropomorphic design significantly reduced blame attributed to the AI system, yet did not significantly increase perceptions of the AI’s moral agency. Moreover, anthropomorphism did not meaningfully shift blame away from the deploying organization, which remained a primary target of responsibility across conditions. These findings suggest that anthropomorphic AI design may soften moral evaluation without increasing perceived accountability.This research contributes to strategic and innovation management by highlighting an unintended ethical consequence of anthropomorphic AI design. While humanizing AI may enhance acceptance, it may also reshape moral judgment in ways that reduce scrutiny without clarifying responsibility, raising important implications for responsible innovation and AI governance.
