The Hidden Cost of Adaptability: When Support Fuels Exhaustion

OB
Leila Afshari (RMIT University); Razan Al Aghbar (RMIT University)
Adaptability is widely celebrated as essential for effective performance in contemporary organizations. Accordingly, organizations invest heavily in support systems intended to help employees adapt while protecting their well-being. Drawing on the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) framework, we challenge this prevailing assumption by theorizing that adaptive performance occupies an ambiguous position in the JD-R model. We argue that adaptive performance simultaneously operates as a capability that enhances effectiveness and as an effortful behavior that depletes psychological resources. We develop a multi-level model in which workplace support enables adaptive performance through cascading resource processes, while also activating health-impairment dynamics. Specifically, organizational-level family-supportive supervision shapes peer support climates that facilitate adaptation. While these support systems are expected to safeguard employee well-being, we show that they can unintentionally intensify emotional exhaustion by enabling sustained adaptive effort. By positioning adaptive performance as a boundary-crossing construct within JD-R theory, this study introduces a new perspective on why support initiatives often deliver strong performance benefits but weaker-than-expected occupational health outcomes. Our framework contributes to ongoing conversations at the intersection of adaptability, burnout, and workplace support, and highlights the need for more sustainable approaches to managing adaptation in high-change work environments.
