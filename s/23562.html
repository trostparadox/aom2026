Access to the Frontier: Open Source AI and Downstream Innovation

TIM
Dominik Asam (Max Planck Institute for Innovation and Competition)
How does open sourcing a frontier AI model affect downstream innovation? I provide empirical evidence on the effect of shifting from a closed to an open frontier on subsequent AI development by studying the leak of Meta’s Llama model in early 2023. I find that Llama lowered entry barriers and led to a massive surge in new models without compromising average perceived utility. The increase in model creation is almost entirely driven by new entrants, who show similar experience characteristics to incumbents. This suggests that the previous dominance of closed models imposed technological entry barriers that left a large pool of skilled contributors untapped. An analysis of incumbents’ reactions to Llama shows that ex ante exposed contributors pivot away from Llama-affected domains towards unaffected fields, indicating a strategic reallocation of innovative effort. Overall, the results emphasize the importance of open source AI as a platform for experimentation and innovation.

Artificial Intelligence and Innovation Before and After ChatGPT

TIM
Lovrenc Svegl (Ljubljana School of Business); Miha Skerlavaj (University of Ljubljana)
This study maps how research on AI for innovation evolved across two periods: the pre-generative-AI era (2010–2022) and the post-2023 diffusion of large language models. Using the Web of Science Core Collection, we analyze a curated business, management, and economics corpus with three science-mapping techniques—co-citation, keyword co-occurrence, and bibliographic coupling—implemented in VOSviewer. The comparison identifies five research streams: (1) strategy and economics of AI as a general-purpose technology; (2) digital transformation and dynamic capabilities; (3) behavioral, services, and market perspectives on adoption and customer experience; (4) organization/HRM and innovation management, including human–AI teaming and trust; and (5) value creation and performance via capability bundles (data, technology, skills, and governance). Post-2023 maps show denser cross-links and a shift from whether AI creates value to how firms scale deployment, with greater emphasis on governance, risk, transparency, and workforce design. The study consolidates the field’s knowledge structure and offers a research agenda prioritizing cross-level designs, comparative industry evidence, measures of augmentation versus automation, and in-situ evaluations of generative-AI use cases. These insights help scholars and decision-makers link AI capabilities to innovation outcomes in the generative-AI era.

How Generative AI Enables Solo Entrepreneurship While Preserving Team Advantages

TIM
Hyunso Kim (Seoul National University); Hyo Kang (Seoul National University); Jaeyong Song (Seoul National University)
This paper studies how generative artificial intelligence (AI) reshapes entrepreneurial organization. Using more than 330,000 new product launches on Product Hunt and leveraging the November 2022 release of ChatGPT, we examine how entrepreneurial entry, organizational form, and early market evaluation change. We find a sharp increase in venture creation, driven disproportionately by solo founders. This shift is most pronounced among AI product-oriented ventures and in categories that historically relied on team-based ventures. Yet, this expansion does not translate into comparable representation at the top of the platform’s rankings. Ventures founded by teams, particularly team-based AI ventures, continue to dominate the highest-ranked outcomes. These findings suggest that generative AI lowers barriers to solo entrepreneurship while preserving the team-based advantages at the upper end of the quality distribution, highlighting a nuanced relationship between entrepreneurial team size and success in the age of AI.

Specialization Tradeoffs in General-Purpose Technology The Case of Open Foundation Model’s Ecosystem

TIM
Mostafa Khoshbash (University of California Santa Barbara)
This paper investigates how provider-driven specialization reshapes innovation in open foundation model ecosystems that are often framed as pathways for generative AI to function as a general-purpose technology (GPT). I theorize a central tension: specialization can expand adoption by improving fit and lowering use costs, yet it can also internalize expertise in the core and limit complementors’ room to differentiate. As a result, specialization reallocates complementor effort across two distinct innovation activities: interface-based application development that uses models “as-is,” and deeper, core-modifying innovation that adapts models through fine-tuning. Empirically, I compile a longitudinal panel from Hugging Face covering 12 major model families, 1,529 foundation models, and 2,768 developers from March 2022 to December 2025. I study two specialization moves by ecosystem orchestrators: releasing sponsor fine-tuned variants and adopting Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, which embed granular specialization within the core model. Using model-level regressions and developer-level staggered difference-in-differences around quasi-experiment introductions of MoEs, I find asymmetric effects: sponsor fine-tuned releases are associated with more application creation, while MoE introductions reduce downstream fine-tuning and have negligible effects on applications. These effects are heterogeneous across ecosystems and more consistently negative for individual developers. Importantly, larger MoE models attenuate the discouraging effect, implying that scale can preserve remaining opportunity space for downstream innovation.

The Dynamic Returns of Generative AI to Knowledge Breadth vs. Depth: Evidence from GitHub

TIM
Alexey Rusakov (University of Zurich); Jonathan Jensen (Jonathan Jensen)
This paper studies how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) affects developer productivity differently, depending on whether they are a generalist or a specialist, and how these effects evolve over time. We hypothesize that generalists benefit more initially from GenAI due to faster but more constrained adaptability, whereas specialists realize slower but superior long-term gains through returns to deep domain learning. Using GitHub contributor data from pre and post GitHub Copilot’s general availability in June 2022, we find, contrary to our hypotheses, that average performance declines for both generalists and specialists. Specialization exhibits some favorable level effects initially, yet negative time trends dominate. Further analysis indicates short-run productivity gains for generalists, with specialists overtaking over time. Our study contributes to ongoing discussions about the effects of GenAI on knowledge-intensive work by emphasizing the role of dynamics that are contingent on prior individual knowledge.
