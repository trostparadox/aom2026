Wisdom and Assertiveness in Line Managers' Impact on Employee Role Overload

MOC
Pranas Šmaižys (ISM University of Management and Economics); Ilona Buciuniene (ISM University of Management and Economics)
Fluctuations in workplace demands may cause excessive employee workloads, leading to role overload. Line managers’ practical wisdom, referred to as the capacity to notice changes in the workplace, to respond to them appropriately, and to comprehend the connections among individuals, may help to mitigate employee role overload. Drawing on dual concern theory and the job demands-resources theoretical framework, we examine the effects of five dimensions of line managers’ practical wisdom – namely, perspective-taking, situational adaptation, intellectual humility, search for compromise/resolution, and ability to take an outsider’s viewpoint – on employee role overload, and the mediating effect of the line managers’ assertiveness tactic in these linkages. We assess these relationships using a three-wave field survey data from 218 employees in white-collar non-management jobs from several private companies in a Northern European country. Our findings revealed that three dimensions of line managers’ practical wisdom – perspective-taking, intellectual humility, and search for compromise/resolution – significantly reduced employee role overload. The remaining two dimensions of line managers’ practical wisdom – situational adaptation and ability to take an outsider’s viewpoint – were not associated with employee role overload. We also found that each dimension of line managers’ practical wisdom decreased line manager assertiveness, which in turn increased employee role overload.

When Bonds Break: Restoring Meaningfulness in the Aftermath of Relationship Fractures

MOC
Elizabeth Nguyen Trinh; Krystal Laryea; Madeleine Rauch (University of Cambridge)
Prior research highlights the benefits of meaningful work and the role of relationships in cultivating it, yet we know little about how relationship fractures and relational injuries arising from violated expectations within interpersonal connections can threaten work meaningfulness and how people restore meaningfulness in the face of fractures. We conducted a longitudinal, inductive study of 43 U.S. nonprofit executive directors, drawing on 64 semi-structured interviews (2020-2023) and archival interviews with these leaders. Our analysis shows that meaningfulness becomes precarious when external disapproval triggers internal self-disapproval, i.e., the perception that one has violated core aspects of one’s self-concept as a leader. We identify two divergent paths amid such self-disapproval. On a fortification path, individuals actively reform their relational practices, reaffirming and even strengthening the meaningfulness of leadership. On a destabilization path, individuals either rigidify current practices, which leads to the eventual erosion of work meaningfulness, or exit from leadership roles. By illuminating how relationship fractures spark self-disapproval and how adaptive practice change can re-anchor meaningfulness, we extend the literature on meaningful work and workplace relationships, revealing the relational fragility and resilience of meaningfulness for leaders.

Why Benevolent Leadership Does Not Always Elicit Employee Gratitude?

MOC
Sophia Chia-Min Chou (National Taiwan Normal University)
Benevolent leadership is widely associated with positive employee and organizational outcomes, often mediated by employee gratitude. Yet, employees do not always feel grateful in response to leaders’ benevolent acts. To clarify why benevolence sometimes fails to elicit gratitude, this study adopts a qualitative, dual-perspective design that integrates accounts from both employees and supervisors. The analysis reveals a multilevel set of barriers operating across individual, dyadic, and organizational levels. At the individual level, employee gratitude is muted when employees experience psychological resource depletion, appraise benevolence as insufficiently gratitude-worthy, or hold gratitude-inhibiting dispositions. In parallel, gratitude is inhibited when supervisors’ benevolence is enacted alongside behavioral inconsistency or perceived untrustworthiness. At the dyadic level, gratitude is dampened by benevolence misalignment and benevolence insufficiency that impede trust formation. At the organizational level, organizational injustice and cultural deficits shape interpretations of benevolence and neutralize gratitude. The dual-perspective design further highlights supervisor–employee asymmetries in the visibility of barriers. Together, these findings extend benevolent leadership and employee gratitude research by offering a multilevel account of benevolence-to-gratitude failure and identifying barriers that may be missed in single-source designs.

When Do CEOs Joke in Public? Humor as a Situational Risk Decision

MOC
Jora Li (Kellogg School of Management, Northwestern University)
Using humor in public communication promises high potential returns but also carries a substantial risk of misinterpretation. When, then, do CEOs actually use humor in such settings? I theorize managerial humor as a situationally contingent risk decision, in which executives weigh uncertain returns against potential costs given concrete interactional contexts. Drawing on humor identified from more than 18,000 earnings call transcripts from S&P 500 firms, I quantitatively examine when CEOs employ humor in public interactions. The results show that CEO humor is neither random nor purely dispositional. Recent negative earnings surprises and larger numbers of participating analysts suppress humor, whereas analyst-initiated humor and greater CEO structural power facilitate it. This study contributes to research on organizational humor and strategic public communication and invites dialogue with humor studies in accounting.

The Stewardship Dilemma: Elites and the Hidden Politics of Field Identity

MOC
Ina Toegel; Shubha Patvardhan (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Existing accounts portray field identity formation as a democratic, organic process of collective meaning-making, often overlooking how power asymmetries shape which meanings prevail. We conducted an inductive study of the early psychoanalytic field (1890–1920), drawing on rare archival materials, including confidential correspondence among elite actors. Our emergent process model reveals a dual-level dynamic: overt processes of open deliberation and consensus were, beneath the surface, strategically orchestrated by a small group of powerful elites. We develop the concept of the stewardship dilemma to explain this janus-faced process. In democratically governed fields, elites face a legitimacy–identity dilemma when shaping the field’s core matters, especially its identity: asserting control too overtly undermines both, their legitimacy as impartial stewards and the field’s legitimacy; whereas stepping back risks loss of the control and influence that sustain their identity and authority as founders. To navigate this double bind, elites deploy covert interventions, quietly consolidating control while preserving the appearance of democratic consensus. By revealing the political foundations of identity formation, our process model advances institutional theory. Further, by showing how fear, envy, and resentment fuel field partitioning, we uncover the affective foundations of institutionalization. More broadly, this study exposes the hidden dynamics through which elites shape collective meaning and illuminates the dark side of institutional entrepreneurship.
