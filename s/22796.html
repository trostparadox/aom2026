Imprinting in Place: How Early Career Locations Shape the Implementation of an Anti-Poverty Program

SIM
Himanshu Bhatt; Rocio Bonet (IE University); Srividya Jandhyala (ESSEC Business School)
Governments worldwide have adopted welfare programs to combat extreme poverty, but outcomes vary considerably across regions. Drawing from imprinting literature, we investigate whether the locational characteristics of public officials' initial career assignments influence their subsequent effectiveness in implementing such programs. Specifically, we propose that officials posted to difficult environments early in their careers acquire lasting skills in navigating complexity and managing competing demands. Leveraging the quasi-random allocation of Indian Administrative Service officers to their early-career postings, we analyze implementation outcomes of a welfare program across 918 district-year observations between 2009 and 2016. Our results indicate that officers who began their careers in more challenging districts deliver stronger program outcomes. Furthermore, this relationship is more pronounced in states governed by left-leaning parties, where rewards for effective welfare delivery create stronger incentives to deploy these acquired capabilities. Our findings demonstrate that the context of earlier career assignments also matters for performance outcomes, with implications for how organizations allocate managers to initial postings.

Responsible Participation of Family SMEs: Addressing Societal Grand Challenges and the SDGs

SIM
Folajimi Yesir Ashiru (Coventry University); Franklin Nakpodia (Durham University); Maria Alejandra Gonzalez-Perez (EAFIT University); Emmanuel Adegbite (University of Nottingham)
Existing international business (IB) theories offer limited insight into how family-owned small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the Global South engage with Societal Grand Challenges (SGCs) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). This study addresses this gap by theorizing responsible participation, a novel concept that explains how family SMEs in the Global South navigate, internalize, and act on sustainability imperatives amid institutional voids and socioemotional motivations. Drawing on multi-level qualitative data from 101 international family SMEs and six policymakers across Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria, and Turkey (the MINT economies), we inductively develop a principle-and-process model of responsible participation as a developmental hierarchy encompassing motivational, relational, institutional, and governance dimensions. The findings reveal how responsible participation connects micro-level behavioral drivers with macro-level sustainability governance, creating a mid-range theory that links family firm agency to global governance structures. By advancing SDG 17's vision of inclusive partnerships, this study reframes family SMEs from peripheral actors to key co-governors of sustainable development in economies in the Global South.

A Cut Above the Rest? Social Exchange Relationships and Quality of Working Life in Small Hair Salon

SIM
Lisa Chamberlain (University of Glasgow); Emma Hughes (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester); Rory Donnelly (University of Liverpool); Caroline Jane Gatrell (University of Liverpool)
Despite the economic significance and relational intensity of the hairdressing industry, hairdressers’ working lives receive limited attention within management studies. Our paper addresses this omission by examining how hairdressers’ relationships with clients, peers, and salon owners shape their employment experiences in small salons. Drawing on 36 hours of field observations and 37 semi-structured interviews in hairdressing salons in the UK, we show how hairdressers’ working lives emerge from an interdependent network of relational exchanges, sustained over time and embedded in wider market and regulatory contexts. Our focus includes multi-level relational dynamics, non-standard employment arrangements and tensions between social and legal obligations. Our findings advance theoretical understanding of work in small businesses (here, hairdressing) and foreground the importance of everyday relational exchanges in shaping workers’ experiences. Through our analysis of these aspects of working lives that have been previously under-researched we integrate and extend the lenses of social exchange theory and the concept of quality of working life. Such theoretical developments have broader relevance for organizational studies, offering insights into how relational and contextual dynamics shape working lives beyond small business settings, but also potentially in more structured corporate settings.

Investigating the Impact of Firm-Bank Geographic Distance on Corporate Sustainable Performance

SIM
Weilong Zhang (Qingdao University); Wei Liu (Harbin Institute of Technology); Yameng Li (The University of Newcastle); Junjie Liu (University of Macau)
This study, grounded in signaling theory and resource dependence theory, constructs an analytical framework that integrates geographic space, institutional environment, and information intermediaries. It aims to investigate how the geographic distance between firms and surrounding banks affects corporate sustainable performance, and how local government environmental concern and analyst coverage moderate this relationship. Utilizing panel data from Chinese A-share listed companies spanning 2009 to 2023, we empirically test the hypotheses by employing fixed-effects models and the double/debiased machine learning method. The findings reveal that increased geographic distance to banks has a significantly positive impact on corporate sustainable performance. However, this positive effect is attenuated by strong environmental concern from local governments and high levels of analyst coverage. The theoretical contributions of this study lie in its systematic integration of the geographic dimension into ESG research through the lens of signaling theory, deepening the understanding of the non-subjective spatial influence mechanisms of financial institutions, and fostering cross-fertilization among multiple theories. The conclusions also offer practical implications for firm strategy, financial policy formulation, and banking practices.

Living Under a Name:Eponymous Naming and ESG Engagement in Family Firms

SIM
Yize Qin (Tongji University); Yuanyuan Hu (Zhengzhou University); Shili Chen (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
Building upon recent theories and studies about the socioemotional wealth (SEW) theory and organizational identity, this study examines how eponymous naming shapes family firms’ engagement in environmental, social, and governance (ESG) activities. We argue that embedding the family name in the firm name strengthens family firms’ organizational identity by constituting an enhanced identity commitment, which increases their intrinsic motivation to engage in ESG activities. We further propose that the relationship between eponymous naming and ESG engagement is contingent on family governance contexts. In particular, we suggest that family involvement and generational succession strengthen this association by intensifying the salience and enactment of family identity in organizational decision-making. Using a sample of 13,876 firm-year observations from 2,283 Chinese listed family firms, we find empirical support for our arguments.
