Political Leverage at Home, Strategic Moves Abroad: Domestic Lobbying and Cross-Border Acquisitions (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Dimitrija Kalanoski (The University of Manchester); Kamyar Goudarzi (College of Charleston); Peter J. Buckley (The University of Manchester)
Cross-border acquisitions help firms enter new markets and secure strategic assets, yet they are vulnerable to political and regulatory hazards that can delay or derail deals. Using publicly listed U.S. firms (2000–2019), we examine how home-country lobbying relates to cross-border acquisition behavior and target choice. Lobbying firms are more likely to announce cross-border acquisitions and less likely to cancel announced deals. They also concentrate acquisitions in politically aligned countries and in destinations with greater formal institutional distance, while systematically avoiding culturally distant markets. These findings position lobbying as an important, underappreciated correlate of acquisition initiation, completion, and geographic scope.

Foreign Political Connections, Government Procurement, and Firm Value

IM
Douglas Cumming (Stevens Institute of Technology); David Jay (Florida Atlantic University); Masim Suleymanov (University of Houston)
We theorize that political ties help foreign firms overcome the liability of foreignness, and as a result, connected foreign firms secure significantly larger government contracts and generate positive firm value. In contrast, local firms, typically more embedded in domestic political and institutional networks, should experience weaker or even negative value effects from political connections, since these ties may entrench rent-seeking or agency problems. Investigating the channel of value creation, we conjecture that politically connected government contracting improves investment sensitivity to growth opportunities for foreign firms but reduces investment efficiency for local firms. Institutional and economic development moderate these effects: higher home-country economic and financial development weakens the positive impact of political connections on government contracting for both foreign and local firms. Furthermore, the value created through politically connected contracting is amplified for foreign firms from more developed home countries but diminishes or disappears for local firms in similar contexts. The data examined from 27 countries are consistent with these predictions on how political connections influence government contracting outcomes. The findings underscore the context-dependent role of political ties in global government procurement and their heterogeneous effects on firm value.

Political Institutions and Ownership: Role of Ideology and Regime in Cross-Border Acquisitions

IM
Sowmya Kannan (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
International business literature has paid little consideration to the influence of host country political institutions on foreign firms’ ownership strategies in cross-border acquisitions. Integrating insights from partisanship theory and the real options view, we specifically theorize how political ideology and regime jointly affect foreign acquirers’ risk perceptions and equity commitments. We argue that acquirers perceive lower uncertainty in host countries with right-leaning governments, resulting in higher ownership stakes. Extending this logic, we develop a typology that captures the interactive effects of political ideology and regime, generating distinct political configurations. Our framework reveals that democratic institutions have contrasting consequences for ownership: while democracy reduces ownership stakes under right-leaning governments, it increases them in left-leaning governments. We attribute these heterogeneous effects to the limited discretionary powers of the ruling government in democratic regimes. Using a large sample of 30,755 cross-border deals from 1995 to 2020, we provide empirical support to our theoretical predictions. Our work highlights the central role of political institutions in foreign ownership decisions, advancing a thicker view of institutions in international business.

Sovereign Wealth Funds: The Investment Effects of Organizational Purpose and Political Regime

IM
Richard Carney (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen); Michelle Xue Zheng (China Europe International Business School)
State-owned organizations, such as sovereign wealth funds (SWFs), are increasingly important global investors. Despite widespread acknowledgement that SWFs are heterogeneous in their investment profiles and strategies, existing research has treated them as a homogeneous investor class. This study seeks to advance the understanding of SWFs by distinguishing among them based on their organizational purpose and the moderating effect of the home country political regime. For example, the organizational purpose of savings SWFs is to share the wealth of a country across generations whereas the organizational purpose of foreign exchange reserve funds is to invest surplus reserves overseas to reduce the negative carry costs of holding reserves. Consequently, savings funds can make larger, longer-term investments than foreign exchange reserve funds, which in turn enables savings funds to have larger valuation effects on target firms. Autocracies are posited to amplify SWF valuation effects in relation to democracies due to weak or absent constraints on the government intervening in an SWF’s investment strategy, thereby magnifying the potential for SWFs to pursue political objectives at the expense of financial performance. Findings based on a sample of 10,488 SWF investments support these expectations.

From Resources to Statecraft: The Strategic Foundations of Sovereign Wealth Fund Formation

IM
Faisal Altalhi (Not Associated); Asli Musaoglu Arikan (Kent State University); Ilgaz Tahir Arikan (Kent State University); Alvaro Cuervo-Cazurra (Northeastern University)
Sovereign Wealth Funds (SWFs) have emerged as a distinct type of firm over the last two decades, with a growing number of new SWFs and assets under management. Despite their growing prominence, the theoretical foundation explaining why some countries establish SWFs while others do not remains unclear. This study addresses this gap by conducting a comprehensive global panel analysis of SWF formation, which examines 90 national-level funds and a range of macroeconomic and country-specific variables. Drawing on the resource-based view (RBV), we argue that SWF creation depends on both resource endowment, such as national resource rent and trade surplus, and governance capabilities, such as state capacity and political regime characteristics. Using a fixed-effects regression model and a rare-events logistic regression as a robustness check, our findings reveal that fuel exports and trade surpluses significantly increase the likelihood of SWF establishment, whereas foreign reserves alone are insufficient unless prior resources are present. Interestingly, democratic regimes are increasingly associated with SWF formation, which contradicts prior research and the common belief that SWFs are mostly dominated by authoritarian states. These results advance strategic management and international business research by extending RBV to the state level as a new level of analysis and highlighting SWFs as instruments of long-term statecraft, as they reflect the capacity and the governance of the state.
