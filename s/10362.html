Session overview

Organizations run on talk. From strategic decisions and project coordination to mentoring and small talk, it is through language that people share information, align on goals, negotiate trade-offs, and signal who belongs. Recent work has leveraged text data—emails, reports, transcripts—to study how individuals and organizations present themselves to the world. This monologue-based research has yielded important insights into culture, identity, and strategy. Yet many of the most consequential outcomes are not determined by monologues but by dialogue: back-and-forth exchanges where others respond, challenge, and reinterpret what has been said. This symposium focuses on the linguistic analysis of interaction, with a particular emphasis on dialogue across divides— racioethnic, functional, gender, and network-community. Our premise is that treating conversational exchanges, rather than isolated utterances, as the unit of analysis provides leverage on foundational questions in organization and management theory: How do people coordinate without shared visibility? When and why does diversity unlock or stall the gains from specialization and trade? How do network interventions reshape belonging? Why do men and women walk away from the same conversation with different assessments of its depth and relational meaning? We bring together five contributions that span laboratory experiments, agent-based modeling, field experiments, and computational linguistics: Levine et al. identify communicative friction in diverse experimental economies and show that a brief linguistic intervention can unlock the economic gains of diversity by restoring cross-racioethnic trade. Koçak and Puranam model how shared communication codes emerge through interaction, clarifying when convergence is easier or harder—and how power asymmetries shape who adjusts to whom. Sun and Shih document robust gender differences in perceived conversational depth and subsequent intentions to connect and seek opportunities, revealing a perceptual mechanism that shapes social capital accumulation. Bevis, Shi, and Yeomans introduce the politeness package, a state-of-the-art toolkit for representing sentence structure and speech acts in conversational text, offering an interpretable, theory-driven measurement framework for organizational dialogue. Yeaton, Green, Cormier, Yang, and Srivastava implement a network-based field experiment to rewire workplace connections via convergent versus divergent exposure to unfamiliar colleagues, using Slack communication and language-based models to study how network change affects social belonging. Together, these papers scaffold an integrated research agenda on interactive language in organizations: from the emergence of shared codes and the construction of measurable conversational features, through the governance of economic exchange and network structure, to the subjective experience of everyday talk.
