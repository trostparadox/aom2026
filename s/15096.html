Psychological Dynamics and Roles of Female Directors in Japan: Internal Versus External Appointments

Authors: Tae Funakoshi Osaka University of Economics; Chikae Naito Waseda University; Masaru Wakiya Kyoto University

Session overview

This study explores the perceptions and behaviors of female directors on Japanese corporate boards, identifying differences between internally promoted and externally appointed individuals and their influence on decision-making. Despite a government target of 30% female representation by 2030, women accounted for only 19.3% of directors in Prime Market-listed firms as of July 2025, with internally promoted women representing just 2.1%. Whereas prior research emphasizes macro-level factors such as cultural gender equality, this study examines micro-level dynamics, specifically role recognition and behaviors. In Job Embeddedness Theory terms, externally appointed female directors tend to exhibit fewer organizational links, weaker cultural fit, and limited resources, reducing perceived costs of exit and enabling greater willingness to voice minority dissent. Internally promoted female directors exhibit stronger ties and cultural alignment but possess fewer resources than men, making them vulnerable to isolation and tokenism. We investigated these dynamics through semi-structured interviews with two internally promoted and three externally appointed female directors, analyzing their responses thematically. Both groups acknowledge the perception of fulfilling a quota, while also regarding their selection as legitimate. However, their interpretations of roles diverge: externally appointed directors emphasize providing outside expertise but feel constrained by limited familiarity with internal operations, whereas internally promoted directors, leveraging profit-center experience, join pre-decision processes—signaling its value for women—yet often adopt masculine perspectives. These results suggest that appointment type fundamentally shapes role expectations and influence, raising concerns about quota-based policies that overlook this distinction.
