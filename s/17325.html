Session overview

Strategic management seeks to help leaders make consequential choices under conditions of uncertainty, complexity, and high degrees of interdependence. In practice, managers experience these conditions as persistent dilemmas: How long do advantages actually last? When does diversification create value versus destroy it? How much performance variance is actually under managerial control? Why do mergers and acquisitions so often disappoint? These are not one-off problems but recurring, high-stakes issues for which experience, intuition, and prevailing frameworks rarely provide a single clear answer. For managers, strategy is therefore less about optimization and more about navigating problems that resist resolution despite sustained attention. Under such conditions, understanding the relevant strategic facts matters. A growing body of management research demonstrates that decisions grounded in well-supported empirical regularities and causal relationships outperform those based on untested assumptions or widely held but inaccurate beliefs. Evidence-based guidance disciplines judgment, calibrates expectations, and reduces the costly influence of overconfidence, bias, and narrative-driven reasoning. When managers operate on flawed or misremembered “facts,” the result is often predictable failure: strategies that underdeliver, synergies that never materialize, misallocated resources, and delayed adaptation. In these cases, the problem is not execution alone but misunderstanding the underlying realities that bound strategic outcomes. This symposium examines the gap between what is empirically known in strategic management and what managers believe to be true. Drawing inspiration from Hans Rosling’s work on global misconceptions, we distinguish between strategic management facts, robust empirical regularities that constrain what is likely to occur, and issues where facts exist but are routinely ignored or overridden. Using new survey evidence, the symposium surfaces where miscalibrated beliefs persist, why they endure, and how strategy scholarship can better inform managerial judgment without oversimplifying complexity.
