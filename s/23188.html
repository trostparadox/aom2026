Green Silence: How Dividend Underpayment Shapes Corporate Environmental Disclosure Strategies (WITHDRAWN) (WITHDRAWN)

ONE
Xiao Sun (Sun Yat-Sen University); Puwen Shang (Sun Yat-Sen University)
Although an increasing number of studies have examined antecedents of exaggerated environmental disclosure (i.e., greenwashing), relatively little is known about why some firms walk more but talk less about their environmental actions or achievements (i.e., brownwashing). Drawing on the literature on legitimacy and stakeholder management, we propose a new mechanism to explain the motivation behind brownwashing. By combining experimental and observational data from Chinese listed firms, we find that when a firm fails to meet investors’ dividend expectations (i.e., dividend underpayment), it is likely to adopt a brownwashing strategy to mitigate the tension between investors and external environmental stakeholders. Furthermore, when a firm is confronted with higher online environmental activism of retail investors, the effect of dividend underpayment on brownwashing is diminished. In doing so, our study offers a novel insight into the motivation behind brownwashing as a form of strategic silence in the context of environmental disclosure.

Words Beyond Deeds: Media-Experienced Executives and Corporate Greenwashing in Environmental Disclos

ONE
Zhigang Zhang (Henan Normal University); Yiling Chen (Henan Normal University); Junjie Hao (Henan Normal University)
Greenwashing refers to a strategic behavior in which a enterprise’s environmental performance and disclosed information are decoupled. Its essence lies in the selective disclosure by enterprises under environmental regulatory and market pressures. Using panel data on Chinese A-share listed firms from 2011 to 2023, we find that media-experienced executives significantly exacerbate corporate greenwashing behavior. Further analysis indicates that the internal (sustainable development performance) and external (public environmental attention) pressures strengthen the facilitating effect of media-experienced executives on corporate greenwashing. Furthermore, drawing on upper echelons theory and the perspective of behavior integration, this study discusses the internal logic of how media-experienced executives influence corporate greenwashing from the aspects of executive shareholding, environmental awareness, and professional attention. Our study extends the explanatory boundaries of upper echelons theory in the field of environmental information disclosure and provides a theoretical and empirical basis for regulators to identify hidden greenwashing behaviors and to guide investors to rationally interpret corporate environmental information.

Talking Green, Acting Brown: How Selective Disclosure Undermines Corporate Decarbonization

ONE
Xiaoqing Wang (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Quentin Moreau (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Keith Jin Deng Chan (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology)
Examining how corporations design and implement environmental strategies is central to understanding the private sector’s contribution to climate sustainability. Using a global panel of listed firms from 2011 to 2023, this study documents a robust negative association between selective environmental disclosure and subsequent emissions abatement performance, indicating that “green talk” often substitutes for decarbonization. This substitution effect is significantly weaker in institutional environments characterized by stronger long-term cultural orientation, higher information transparency, and civil law legal origins, which increase the expected costs of greenwashing and constrain firms’ reliance on purely symbolic communication. Analysis of investor responses further shows that selective disclosure is associated with higher market valuation and greater institutional ownership, suggesting that capital markets tend to tolerate—rather than punish—such practices, thereby reinforcing managerial incentives to engage in them. Overall, the findings highlight the pivotal role of institutional environments in aligning financial market incentives with substantive corporate decarbonization efforts.

Sour Grapes or Greenwash: Moralities and the Negotiation of Moral Market Boundaries

ONE
A. Wren Montgomery (Smith School of Business, Queen's University); Thomas Peyton Lyon (University of Michigan); Catherine (Cat) Summers (University of Michigan)
Moral markets, created to reduce the social and environmental harms of conventional industry practices, frequently face a tension between purity and growth as the market expands. Although these divergent pathways can have profound consequences for the market, the literature offers limited insights into the moral foundations and moralization at play in these “moral” markets. These forces may become particularly salient when moral markets face external pressures, such as when conventional market actors seek to enter the moral market through new standards. Thus, in this in-depth qualitative study of the North American eco-friendly wine market, we explore why and how moral market actors interpret lax standards and negotiate moral market boundaries. We identify two types of moral market actors—market stewards and moral stalwarts—that differentially prioritize the market’s foundational moralities (autonomy, community, and divinity) in their interpretation and response to emerging sustainability standards and perceived greenwashing. We establish six mechanisms of moral market boundary negotiation that explain divergent approaches to shaping and defining moral markets. Our work contributes new insights to the defining aspects of moral markets and how markets maintain normative superiority and address incursion. Importantly, our model also integrates the concept of greenwashing into the moral markets literature for the first time, thus opening up new avenues for research into the role of firms in fighting rampant greenwashing.

Greed is Not Green : How Executive Risk-Taking Incentives Fuel Environmental Misconduct

ONE
Mowen Wang (East China Normal University); Jianrong Wang (East China Normal University); zhenyuan wang (East China Normal University)
Risk-taking incentives are a double-edged sword. While they can motivate executives to pursue growth and value-enhancing opportunities, overly strong risk incentives may also tilt managerial attention toward short-horizon payoffs and away from long-term responsibilities such as environmental stewardship. Drawing on agency theory and integrating institutional and resource-based perspectives, this study finds a robust positive association between executive risk-taking incentives and corporate environmental misconduct: firms that provide stronger risk incentives are more likely to engage in environmentally irresponsible behavior and to do so more frequently. We unpack two primary mechanisms: a direct crowding-out of environmental investment and an expansion-driven amplification of compliance risk. Further analyses indicate that monitoring and constraint mechanisms weaken this relationship, whereas opportunities and incentive distortions strengthen it. Notably, environmental subsidies with resource support and policy signaling functions distort executives’ risk–return trade-offs, not only reinforcing the inducement of risk incentives toward environmentally irresponsible behavior but also further triggering firms’ strategic greenwashing. This study extends the explanatory scope of agency theory into the domain of environmental governance, systematically elucidating how executive incentives generate environmental externalities through the interaction of institutional and resource forces, and offers actionable implications for regulatory policy design and corporate governance practices.
