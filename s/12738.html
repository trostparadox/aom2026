Session overview

Over the past few decades, research on creativity – defined as the production of novel and useful ideas (Amabile, 1988; George, 2007) – has proliferated within organizational scholarship. Much of this work has appropriately focused on identifying when ideas, products, or processes are creative and what conditions facilitate or impede their development. Yet, even as this literature has generated important insights, longstanding evidence suggests that within organizations, opportunities, access to resources, and influence often hinge less on the objective creativity of outputs and more on who is perceived as creative (Elsbach & Kramer, 2003; Goncalo, Flynn, & Kim, 2010; Perry-Smith & Shalley, 2003). Because creative processes are difficult to observe, managers, peers, and external audiences often infer creativity from indirect signals such as past accomplishments, behavior that is stereotypically associated with creativity, or others’ evaluations of one’s creative potential (e.g., Carnevale, Huang, Vincent, Farmer, & Wang, 2021). Such impressions of creativity form a distinct and growing substream in modern creativity research. While still a relatively nascent stream of research, a burgeoning body of scholarship is beginning to illuminate how perceptions of individuals as creative emerge, how those perceptions are sustained over time, and the consequences they carry for work-related attitudes and behaviors (Benegal, Vincent, & Carnevale, 2026; Carnevale et al., 2021; Carnevale, Huang, Vincent, Yu, & He, 2024; Katz, Mann, Shen, Goncalo, & Ferguson, 2022; Ng & Yam, 2019). Despite this progress, research on impressions of creativity remains in its early stages, and many important questions remain open. For example, although prior work documents cases where impressions of creativity emerge from indirect cues (e.g., Carnevale et al., 2024; Goncalo et al., 2010), there is still limited understanding of the specific conditions under which these cues meaningfully shape impressions and whether their influence aligns or misaligns with actual creative performance. Similarly, although accumulating evidence suggests that others’ perceptions of creativity can motivate behavior (Katz et al., 2022), we know less about how such impressions are socially transmitted or reshaped when individuals receive feedback that affirms, threatens, or intensifies their creative image. Furthermore, research has only begun to explore the psychological and relational consequences of being seen as creative, particularly when that image creates ongoing expectations to perform creatively. Our symposium directly engages these open questions by examining how impressions of creativity form in the absence of visible creative work, how those impressions motivate or burden employees, and how they break down when creative identity is threatened. Across four papers, we move toward a richer understanding of when impressions of creativity emerge, how they influence ongoing interaction and evaluation, and what unique consequences arise for those who become known or positioned as “the creative one” at work.
