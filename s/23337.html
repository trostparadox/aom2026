Converting Symbolic Capital: The Inverse Political Soft Budget Constraint for SOE Innovation

STR
Gang Liu (Changchun University); Ying Ding (University of the West of Scotland); Peiran Su (Audencia Business School)
This study investigates the persistent challenge of institutional duality in State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs), where the institutional logic conflicts with the market logic. We address a critical theoretical void: How can an SOE convert its political legitimacy into a robust, sustainable mechanism for radical innovation? Utilizing a longitudinal case study of China FAW Group Corporation (1953–2023), specifically its Hongqi brand, we develop a process model of dual-logic conversion. Our findings show that FAW’s successful joint venture strategy created an innovation paradox, or competency trap, suppressing indigenous R&D. A political mandate helped the SOE broke this trap by activating Hongqi’s symbolic capital—its national prestige—to create a high-margin commercial revenue stream. This revenue was mandatorily re-allocated to fund core technological transformation. This research offers three core contributions: it extends the resource dependence theory by demonstrating a non-market solution to the competency trap; it instruments political corporate social responsibility by defining symbolic capital as a dynamic, convertible economic asset; and it proposes an inverse political soft budget constraint model for hybrid organizations.

Institutional Adaptation and Technological Shock: The Role of AI Regulation in Startup Financing

STR
Yunshu Ling (Tsinghua University); Ke Rong (Tsinghua University); Di Zhou (Tongji University)
Prior research shows that technological breakthroughs heighten market uncertainty and complicate capital allocation for investors. Further, regulatory tech interventions often constrain innovation and venture investment. Despite growing attention to both streams, we still lack a systematic understanding of how regulation moderates technological shocks to shape investment. In this paper, we study the impact of two shocks on China's entrepreneurial finance system - the introduction of generative AI and AI regulation. We find that, in contrast to other areas of regulation that find a chilling effect on innovation, AI regulation moderated the negative impact on venture capital investment from the generative AI shock.

When Government Funding Stops Signaling Quality: Policy Success in the U.S. Space Industry

STR
KwangWook Gang (Salisbury University)
Government funding is widely regarded as a catalytic resource that certifies firm quality and attracts private investment in emerging industries. However, the strategic meaning of government support may not be stable over time. This study examines when and why government funding ceases to function as a positive signal and instead constrains firms’ ability to attract committed private capital. Drawing on strategic signaling and resource dependence perspectives, we analyze firms in the U.S. commercial space industry surrounding a critical policy success in 2017, when repeated private launch achievements fundamentally reduced technological and market uncertainty. Using panel data and difference-in-differences models, we show that while overall private investment increased substantially after this legitimacy shift, the signaling value of government funding eroded. After policy success, firms with greater reliance on government funding attracted fewer private investors and smaller investment amounts, even as their number of investment deals increased. These patterns indicate a strategic meaning shift: government funding initially operates as a quality-certifying signal under uncertainty but becomes a dependency signal once industry legitimacy is widely established. The findings demonstrate that identical resources can generate opposing market interpretations depending on field conditions. By highlighting how the strategic value of state support changes following policy success, this study contributes to strategy research on dynamic signaling, state–market interactions, and firm heterogeneity in post-legitimacy environments.

Political Turnover and Firm R&D: The Role of Multi-City Operations

STR
Yumei Chen (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Junhan Wang (The University of Hong Kong)
Politician turnover is commonly viewed as a source of policy uncertainty that discourages irreversible investments such as R&D, implicitly treating firms as passive recipients of political shocks. Drawing on bargaining theory, we reconceptualize politician turnover as a renegotiation window that can create strategic opportunities for firms, contingent on their organizational outside options. We argue that firms with multi-city operations possess credible reallocation alternatives that enhance their bargaining leverage vis-à-vis local governments when leadership transitions reopen the terms of exchange. Consequently, politician turnover can increase R&D investment among multi-city firms by strengthening governments’ willingness to provide policy support. We further identify two key moderators: subsidiary geographic dispersion amplifies this positive effect by expanding firms’ outside options, whereas the near-retirement status of newly appointed officials weakens it by reducing officials’ incentives to offer growth-enhancing concessions. Using a panel of publicly listed firms operating at the municipal level in China from 2007 to 2019, we find robust evidence consistent with our theoretical arguments. This study advances research on political turnover and innovation by shifting attention from uncertainty to bargaining, highlighting firms’ strategic agency and the role of organizational geography in shaping innovation responses to political change.

How Institutional Entrepreneurship Driving Administrative Innovation Facilitates Internal Corporate

STR
Kang Mo; Robert A Burgelman (Stanford University)
We report findings of a longitudinal qualitative study of the internal corporate venturing (ICV) process of Hikvision a China-based global surveillance corporation within the institutional context of the Chinese State-Owned Enterprise (SOE). Our field study reveals that success of the ICV process within a Chinese SOE depends on institutional entrepreneurship to secure administrative innovation, over and beyond technological innovation in products and/or in business models, to transform the SOE’s structural context and make room for the strategic context of a new venture to take hold and grow. Our study’s main contributions are threefold. First, we present a detailed analysis of the stages of Hikvision’s development within the Chinese SOE that incorporates the strategic concept of Shi (strategic positioning for gaining momentum), which encompasses timing, geographical advantage, and people conditions. Second, we elucidate the interlocking multi-level institutional entrepreneurial activities that constitute an extended substantive grounded ICV process model in Chinese SOEs. Third, our theoretical extensions provide a lens to help study the interplay between institutional entrepreneurial and administrative innovation microprocesses and macro-institutional outcomes within SOEs in other types of social systems than the Chinese one.
