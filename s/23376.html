Be Cautious or Win in Danger? The Impact of Home Country Economic Policy Uncertainty on Outward IJVs

STR
Lien (Sam) Nguyen (National Cheng Kung University); Shao-Chi Chang (National Cheng Kung University)
Drawing on real options theory, this study examines how home-country economic policy uncertainty affects outward international joint ventures. With a comprehensive dataset of United States public firms over the 1999-2022 period, we find that the tendency of IJVs is significantly negatively related to home-country economic policy uncertainty. This result remains consistent following a series of robustness tests. In addition, firms’ and industry growth opportunities, which enhance the value of flexibility related to decision-making rights in the future, significantly shape firms' investment behavior under uncertain conditions. These findings advance our understanding of how firms maintain strategic flexibility when facing economic policy uncertainty.

Political Stability, Economic Policy Uncertainty, and High-Growth SMEs in Europe

STR
Fadi Hawach (University of Salamanca); Ignacio Requejo (University of Salamanca); Chao Zhang (Northumbria University)
This study investigates how political stability (PS), economic policy uncertainty (EPU), and directors’ political-action values (PAV) jointly influence the likelihood that European small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) become high-growth firms (HGFs). Using logistic regression models that track firms over more than a decade (2012-2022), we find that a stable political environment significantly increases the odds of rapid employment expansion, whereas heightened policy uncertainty reduces them. Managerial orientation plays a pivotal role. Directors and managers who exhibit strong norms of conventional political engagement are more effective in converting stability into higher growth and in mitigating much of the drag created by uncertainty. These interaction effects suggest that politically active leadership teams serve as strategic complements to favorable institutional conditions and as partial buffers when policy signals become volatile. The findings contribute to the academic literature on institutional determinants of firm performance by highlighting the combined importance of country-level governance and firm-level political capital. The results highlight the importance of politically engaged leadership for managers and entrepreneurs, and the value of stability and clarity for policymakers seeking to foster high-growth SMEs.

Performance Feedback and Regionalization of MNEs: The Role of Contextuality in Problemistic Search

STR
DAM YOO (Incheon National University); Joon Mahn Lee (Seoul National University); Jon Jungbien Moon (Korea University)
This study examines how performance shortfalls in specific host countries shape multinational enterprises’ (MNEs) regional foreign direct investment (FDI) strategies. Drawing on performance feedback theory, we argue that underperformance triggers problemistic search, leading firms to reduce intra-regional FDI while increasing inter-regional FDI. We further propose that this effect is moderated by institutional and economic diversity within the host region. In more diverse regions, intra-regional FDI can function as a form of problemistic search rather than status quo reinforcement, reducing firms’ reliance on inter-regional expansion following underperformance. Using a novel dataset of 3,444 firm–country–year observations from 336 U.S. manufacturing MNEs (2014– 2022), we find strong empirical support for these arguments. Our findings contribute to international business research by offering a behavioral explanation for regionalization strategies and extending performance feedback theory by highlighting the context-dependent nature of managerial search.

Political Uncertainty and Strategic Reconfiguration

STR
Wang Pin-Yu (National Chengchi University); Bo-Ling Yen (National Cheng Kung University)
How firms respond strategically to political uncertainty remains a central question in strategy research. While prior studies emphasize how uncertainty affects investment levels, less is known about how firms reconfigure the structure of their investments to balance commitment and flexibility when political environments become unstable. Drawing on insights from dynamic capabilities and real options perspectives, this study examines how political uncertainty shapes firms’ strategic reconfiguration of investment commitments rather than their aggregate investment intensity. Using a large-scale panel dataset covering more than 18,000 firm-year observations, we investigate how firms adjust the composition of capital allocation under heightened political uncertainty. Our analyses show that rising political uncertainty is associated with systematic reductions in irreversible investment commitments and corresponding increases in flexible resource allocations. These patterns are robust across alternative measures of political uncertainty, investment definitions, and model specifications. Importantly, we find that firms’ strategic responses are contingent on organizational conditions, suggesting that reconfiguration under uncertainty is not mechanical but shaped by firm-specific constraints. This study contributes to strategy research in three ways. First, it shifts attention from investment levels to investment structure, offering a strategic account of how firms manage the trade-off between commitment and flexibility under political uncertainty. Second, by conceptualizing political uncertainty as a strategic contingency rather than a purely exogenous shock, the study advances understanding of how firms exercise strategic agency in volatile institutional environments. Third, the findings clarify how strategic reconfiguration functions as a dynamic capability that enables firms to preserve adaptability without fully abandoning long-term commitments. Together, these insights position political uncertainty as a central driver of strategic reconfiguration and highlight the importance of investment structure in sustaining strategic flexibility.

Strategic Adaptation to Future Demand Shifts: Evidence from Nutritional Labeling

STR
Or Avishay-Rizi; Oren Reshef (Washington University in St. Louis)
How well can firms predict and respond to future shifts in consumer demand? We investigate this question by examining the effects of a nationwide labeling policy for unhealthy food products. Our analysis uses a novel dataset of hand-collected information over several years, supplemented with administrative data. We first show that sales of products with warning labels dropped sharply, with significant variation across product categories and consumer segments. To avoid the labels, firms strategically adjusted their product designs so that reformulated products fell just below the labeling threshold. Importantly, firms targeted their reformulation efforts toward products that would have been most adversely affected by the labels—both within the most impacted categories and among products sold to the most responsive consumers. Moreover, larger and more successful firms were better at anticipating these demand shifts, which translated into improved performance.
