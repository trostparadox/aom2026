All Grown Up: How Millennials Reframe the Organization–Employee Exchange

OB
Jörg-Michael Gasda (University of St. Gallen); Urs Beda Fueglistaller (University of St. Gallen)
Millennials are moving into middle-management roles, where they both experience and enact hierarchical organization-employee exchange relationships. Managers and popular accounts often portray Millennials as sharply different from prior cohorts; however, longitudinal evidence suggests that only a limited set of work-related characteristics differ reliably and systematically. Building on this evidence base, and drawing on social exchange theory and the organization–member exchange literature, we examine whether such cohort-linked differences translate into different interpretations of relational- and transactional-exchange cues and whether these evaluations matter for employees` innovation-relevant discretionary effort. We conducted a randomized between-subject vignette experiment in a Swiss mid- to large-sized organization (n = 151 middle managers). Participants evaluated either a relational (social) or transactional (economic) exchange scenario and reported perceived exchange type, perceived exchange equity, and relationship desirability. We estimate the hypothesized paths using PLS-SEM with multi-group analysis and complementary OLS models. Across generations, perceived relational exchange predicts higher desirability. However, unlike prior generations of Baby Boomers and Gen X, Millennials interpret relational exchanges between organizations and employees as more employee-advantageous rather than mutually balanced, obligation-based. And – in contrast to prior generations – Millennials do not discount desirability when transactional cues are salient. Moreover, Millennials’ perceptions of relational and transactional exchange are essentially uncorrelated, consistent with separable exchange dimensions, whereas prior generations perceive them more as a trade-off. A relevance check links desirability to higher intrapreneurial intentions. We discuss implications for designing “ambidextrous” exchange relationships as the kids have grown up.

Promoting Successful Aging at Work: The Role of Digital Technology Training for Older Employees

OB
Shuman Zhao (Beijing Normal University); Weiwei Shi (Beijing Normal University); WeiFeng Wang (Beijing Normal University); Ying Wang (Beijing Normal University)
While the rise of artificial intelligence, big data, and automation has transformed how organizations operate, little is known about how digital technology training assists older employees to adapt and thrive in the digital era. As a key human resource practice for managing technological change, digital technology training is essential for continuous employee development. Yet, older employees often face training discrimination, limiting their ability to benefit from technological advancement. Addressing this issue, this study examines when and how digital technology training promotes older employees’ successful aging at work, drawing on the process model of successful aging at work and objective self-awareness theory. Two complementary studies were conducted. Study 1, using field survey data from 272 older employees in a large Chinese state-owned enterprise, showed that digital technology training is positively related to successful aging at work and that informal learning mediates this relationship. Study 2, based on an online survey of 450 older employees, further validated these results and demonstrated that occupational future time perspective moderates the mediating effect of informal learning. Overall, the findings indicate that digital technology training enhances successful aging at work, offering theoretical and practical insights for developing age-inclusive digital training and reducing bias against older employees.

Silver Fox Effects: Gendered Embodied Charisma and Leader Emergence in Later Life

OB
Kimberly K. Merriman (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Sarah Kostanski (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Oscar Aliaga (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Karoline Evans (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Kerstin Pull
The cultural trope of the “silver fox” casts gray hair as status-enhancing for men, whereas women’s gray hair is linked to diminished social relevance. We translate this concept into a theoretical inquiry examining gray hair as a gendered signal of leadership potential. Distinguishing the biological cue (gray hair) from its sociocultural meaning (silver fox), we argue that this signal carries divergent interpretations through the lens of embodied charisma – a signaling process involving dual pathways of influence and affect. Using a mixed-methods approach, Study 1 employs a large-scale experiment to test the causal effects of gray hair on leader emergence as mediated by charismatic influence and affect. Study 2 deepens interpretation through inductive qualitative analysis of how observers construct these judgments. Rather than confirming a uniform male advantage, our findings identify unexpected ways in which gray hair signals positive leadership qualities for women, offering post hoc insights for expectancy violations theory. Together, this research illuminates the gendered signaling function of gray hair, providing insight into the social construction of authority in later life and amid a demographic prevalence of older individuals in organizational and societal roles.

Better with Age? Exploring Age and Interpersonal Emotion Regulation in the Workplace

OB
Aishwarya Verma; Karen Rochelle Niven (The University of Manchester); Nicola Anne Thomas
Workplaces are inherently emotional spaces, where employees often attempt to influence others’ feelings. While effective interpersonal emotion regulation benefits relationships and performance, older workers’ capabilities in this domain remain poorly understood. Drawing on lifespan development theories, we hypothesize that age positively influences employees’ motivation to regulate others’ emotions, their use of adaptive regulation strategies, and their effectiveness at implementing interpersonal emotion regulation. We further explore the moderating role of two contextual factors: the intensity of workers’ own negative emotions and their perceived closeness to the target of regulation. We tested hypotheses using a pre-registered, five-day experience sampling study, with working participants (n =129) completing twice daily surveys during their working day. Results show that age is positively associated with motivation to regulate others’ emotions, but is unrelated to strategy use. Exploratory analyses indicate an indirect effect of age on perceived effectiveness of interpersonal emotion regulation, via motivation. Neither relational closeness nor negative emotion intensity moderates age effects. These findings extend lifespan theories to the interpersonal domain, suggesting that ageing shapes why, more than how, employees regulate others’ emotions, and pointing to motivation as a critical yet overlooked strength of older workers.
