Organizational Subcultures: A Multilevel Organizing Framework and Research Agenda (WITHDRAWN)

OB
Maitane Amorrortu (University of Deusto); Leire Gartzia; José L. González-Pernía (University of Deusto)
Although organizational subcultures are widely recognized as shaping sensemaking, identities, and patterns of action, research has stalled and remains theoretically underdeveloped. Because organizations comprise multiple groups that generate shared values, norms, and practices across levels, subcultures are central to explaining how organizational coherence, conflict, and coordination unfold in complex settings. Yet progress has been constrained by the lack of an integrative approach: the literature is dispersed across disciplines, definitions and operationalizations vary substantially, and the contexts in which subcultures are examined are often difficult to compare—limiting accumulation and generalization. We address this fragmentation through an integrative review that consolidates prior work into an organizing framework for subculture emergence, dynamics, and effects. Drawing on the IMOI (input–mediator– output–input) logic, we synthesize evidence across three interrelated dimensions: (1) multilevel antecedents of subculture formation, (2) mechanisms through which subcultures shape cognition, emotion, and behavior, and (3) consequences for outcomes at individual, group, and organizational levels. Based on this synthesis, we propose a unifying framework and outline directions for future research on organizational subcultures.

Exploring the Role of Organizational Culture on Shaping Employee Agility in the Context of Startups

OB
Jobonika Bora (Jobonika Bora); Pooja Purang (Indian Institute of Technology Bombay)
Startups operate in highly dynamic and uncertain environments that require employees to adapt quickly, continuously develop skills, and respond proactively to emerging opportunities. Employee agility is therefore critical for startup success. While prior research highlights the role of flexible organizational practices in fostering employee agility, startups often lack formal human resource systems, making organizational culture a key guiding mechanism. However, the relationship between culture and employee agility in startups remains underexplored. Drawing on the job demands–resources perspective, this study examines how a culture supportive of curiosity, innovation, and egalitarianism influences employee agility, with work engagement as a mediating mechanism. Using cross-sectional survey data from 170 employees across seven Indian startups, we tested the hypotheses using mediation analysis. The findings indicate that supportive cultural values enhance employee agility indirectly by strengthening work engagement. These results suggest that organizational culture serves as an important job resource in startups, fostering employee involvement, development, and adaptability in uncertain contexts.

Developing and Validating a Family-Like Company Culture Scale in the Chinese Context

OB
Huihua Jiang (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
This study develops and validates a Family-Like Company Culture (FLCC) scale tailored to the Chinese organizational context. Grounded in traditional cultural values and empirical data from interviews and surveys, FLCC is conceptualized as a five-dimensional construct: People Orientation, Moral Edification and Virtuous Conduct, Customer Respect, World-Benefiting Vision, and Pioneering Spirit. A rigorous development process was employed, including qualitative item generation, expert review, and two independent survey samples: one for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and the other for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and validity testing. The final scale demonstrates strong reliability and validity. Empirical findings indicate that FLCC enhances affective commitment and engagement while reducing turnover intention. By establishing a culturally grounded measurement tool, this study addresses a key gap in the literature and advances indigenous management research. It also offers practical implications for organizations aiming to cultivate trust, loyalty, and sustainable performance through culturally aligned practices.

Knowledge Management Practices in Nepali Organizations: A Reflexive Thematic Analysis

OB
Chandan Acharya (College of Staten Island of the City University of New York); Pratigya Sigdyal (California State University-Bakersfield); Roshee Lamichhane (Kathmandu University School of Management)
While decades of KM research have generated valuable insights, much of this work is grounded in Western context characterized by individualistic and egalitarian norms. This study examines KM in a contrasting context, Nepali organization. We use qualitative approach using thematic analysis to examine how workplace cultures in Nepali organizations shape KM practices and their implications for extending or challenging prevailing KM frameworks. We follow Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase approach to collect, analyze, and report findings. We used a two- phased approach to generate themes relevant to Nepali organizations. Phase one involved conversations with founders with 20-plus years of work experience across various sectors to understand the general business environment of Nepal. Phase two included interviews with 12 members from various sectors in Nepal to generate themes relevant to Nepali workplaces. The analysis of interview transcripts resulted in nine themes categorized broadly under four main categories. The nine themes are: leadership influence on organizational culture, hierarchical power imbalance, adoption of informal communication channels, openness to new ideas, collaboration and shared responsibility, expert-led role-clear decision making, periodic reviews with root cause analysis, overworked productivity, and resistance to new employee integration.

Organizational Structure, Power Dynamics, and Decision Making Behind NBA General Manager Turnover

OB
Xiaoan Huang (University of New Haven); Nicholas Masafumi Watanabe (University of South Carolina); Donald J Schepker (University of South Carolina)
The retention and dismissal of Chief Executive Officers (CEOs) have received significant attention within the general business literature. Although there is limited consideration of executives in sport, scholars argued that general managers (GM) play a critical role within organizations, as they potentially impact the overall performance of teams through their acquisition of human resources (Juravich et al., 2017), which also brings financial gains. While there is some understanding of the impact that GMs have on organizational performance in sport, there is a lack of consideration of the tenure of executives, and the drivers of turnover in these positions. Sport management research has focused mainly on coaches, yet GMs and owners also shape organizational outcomes. GM is often the most influential role because it spans both business and on-field operations (Wong & Deubert, 2011), and GM experience and education enhance team performance (Juravich et al., 2017). Moreover, evidence outside sport shows executive dismissals often reflect factors beyond performance. Thus, there is a critical need to examine GMs, given the limited understanding of executive tenure and dismissal in professional sport franchises. Grounded in power theory, we use 35 years of data to examine the determinants of GM dismissal. Our model moves beyond GM characteristics and team performance to incorporate organizational factors such as ownership structure, power dynamics, and competition. Using logit and Cox proportional hazards models, our study shows that organizational structure and power relations play a central role in shaping GM tenure and offer key managerial implications for senior leadership.
