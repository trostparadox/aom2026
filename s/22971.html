Anti-Cronyism Gone Too Far: Bixian and Preliminary Testing of its Triggers and Mechanisms

OMT
Lisha Liu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Han Ren (Sichuan University); Xiao-Ping Chen (University of Washington)
While cronyism is prevalent in a society characterized by guanxi – an informal, particularistic, and personal connection between two parties, its negative externalities have been recognized and criticized by scholars and practitioners from rank and file. Recent years have witnessed an anti-guanxi movement in Chinese public sectors such as state-owned enterprises, but how this movement influences firm and individual behavior remains unexplored. In this study, we conceptualize Bixian (??) as one deliberate response, and define it as a behavior in which managers prefer a non-guanxihu to a close guanxihu in making important business and personnel decisions. Drawing on organization justice theory and guanxi theory, we hypothesize that managers will consider both merit and guanxi in deciding whether they engage in bixian to demonstrate impartiality and fairness. Moreover, bixian is more likely to occur for managers with a weak particularism orientation. Data from an archival study of supplier selection in a manufacturing industry, a qualitative interview study of human resource managers, and a scenario experiment provide convergent evidence for our key predictions. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our findings in Chinese organizations and beyond.

One Man's Meat is Another Man's Poison: A Sensemaking Perspective of Shareholder Activism

OMT
Yuting Yang (Iowa State University); Elle Hyunjung Yoon (Iowa State University)
We examine how shareholder activists with divergent demands interpret the same information differently through a sensemaking perspective. Using CEO activism, a type of CEO behavior where they publicly speak out on a controversial sociopolitical issue, as the context, we theorize that wealth-oriented shareholder activists interpret CEO activism as a problem that reflects a deviation from fiduciary responsibility to create shareholder value, whereas socially oriented shareholder activists interpret it as an opportunity to voice socially motivated initiatives. On that basis, we hypothesize that both wealth- and socially oriented shareholder activism will increase in response to CEOs’ activism engagement. Drawing on the sensemaking perspective’s scanning-trigger-interpretation-action framework, we further investigate four factors that influence the sociocognitive processes of sensemaking: CEO certification, CEO political involvement, environmental dynamism, and CEO liberalism. We test our theory using data on shareholder proposals submitted to S&P 500 firms from 2011 to 2018. Our study shows that shareholder activists with different investment purposes tend to interpret the same observation differently but the interpretation may need to be supplemented by additional information.

When Context Speaks Louder than Character: Managerial Attitudes in Earnings Calls

OMT
Matias Braun; Francisco Marcet (Universidad de Chile); Benjamin Murua (Kelley School of Business, Indiana University)
Earnings conference calls are a central channel through which firms communicate with capital markets. This paper examines the behavioral dimension of these interactions by developing novel measures of managerial attitude---condescension, dismissiveness, and evasiveness---derived from natural language processing of 25,000 calls by S&P 500 firms in the 2005-2023 period. We show that variation in managerial attitude is shaped far more by situational context than by fixed managerial or analyst traits: the tone of analyst questions, earnings surprises, and call conditions explain most differences in response quality. We also show that adverse treatment of analysts predicts subsequent downward revisions in target prices, recommendations and earnings forecasts. Our findings reveal that disclosure bias extends beyond access to information and manifests in how information is conveyed. The results provide evidence of a behavioral information channel linking managerial communication style to analyst decisions and market outcomes.

Newly Hired CEOs Who Infuse Purpose into Organizations’ Values: CEOs with Strong Political Ideology

OMT
Ying Shi (The Pennsylvania State University); Vilmos F. Misangyi (The Pennsylvania State University); Tessa Recendes (The Pennsylvania State University)
The corporate purpose literature has emphasized the influence that founders can have on organizational purpose, but very little attention has been given to whether, and how, newly appointed CEOs change their organizations to be more purpose-driven. In this study, we theorize that because certain new CEOs— those with strong political ideologies, either conservative or liberal—are strongly convicted to their values and beliefs, any changes that they make to their organizational value statements will inherently reflect their ideological values, and because such values connect to broader societal values and moral obligations, these changes thereby infuse a ‘duty-based’ purpose into their organizations’ values. We further posit that this purpose infusion into the organizations’ espoused values shapes employees’ sense of work meaningfulness, and thus positively influences attitudes toward the organizations’ values. We test our hypotheses using a sample of CEO succession events in S&P 1500 firms between 2011 to 2015, and we find strong support for our theory. We conclude with a discussion of our study’s implications for research on corporate purpose, CEO political ideology, and executive symbolic action.

Past, Present, and Future: The Temporal Dimensions of CEOs’ Ideology in Strategic Decision-Making

OMT
Ali Radfard (Aston University); Luca Pistilli (University College Dublin); Georg Wernicke (HEC Paris)
We examine how alignment between a CEO’s temporal focus and the temporal orientation associated with the CEO’s political ideology shapes firms’ responses to underperformance. We propose that when temporal focus matches ideological orientation—past-oriented for conservatives and future-oriented for liberals—negative performance feedback is more likely to elicit strategic change. When temporal focus and ideology are misaligned, CEOs are more likely to engage in self-enhancement and motivated interpretation of feedback, which dampens their inclination to initiate change despite continued firm underperformance. Analyses of S&P 500 firms from 2005 to 2014 provide evidence consistent with our prediction. Overall, our findings identify temporal-focus alignment as a key boundary condition in the performance feedback–strategic change relationship and refine our understanding of when and how CEO ideology influences strategic adaptation.
