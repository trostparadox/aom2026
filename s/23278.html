Beyond Borders: Rethinking Entrepreneurial Identity in Migration Contexts

ENT
Zhen Zhang (University of La Verne)
This study investigates how immigrants construct their entrepreneurial identities in the context of migration through interviews with 19 Chinese immigrant entrepreneurs. Thematic analysis identified five interrelated strategies: (1) rejection of traditional entrepreneurial identity, (2) entrepreneurial identity grounded in value orientation, (3) entrepreneurial identity as contextual pragmatism, (4) resilience as a core entrepreneurial identity, and (5) competence as a core entrepreneurial identity. Findings show that entrepreneurial identity extends beyond economic achievement to encompass self-expression, legitimacy, and adaptive strategies, demonstrating identity work as a dynamic, multi-dimensional practice. By highlighting the voices and perspectives of immigrant entrepreneurs, this study enriches theory on entrepreneurial identity, offering insights from migration and immigrant entrepreneurship contexts. It also provides a more nuanced understanding of entrepreneurial resilience in disruptive settings.

Critical Junctures and Interactions: A Temporal Framework for Entrepreneurial Identity Formation

ENT
Steve Nolan (Carleton University)
Entrepreneurial identity is central to understanding how entrepreneurs think, act, and relate to others, and prior research has examined who entrepreneurs are, what they do, and why they pursue different kinds of ventures. Yet this work remains largely atemporal: it acknowledges that entrepreneurial identity develops over time but offers limited tools to examine how this development unfolds or to distinguish stages along the journey. This lack of temporal resolution obscures when entrepreneurs are most receptive to support, how resilience develops, and why similar interventions have divergent effects across individuals. This paper advances a temporal-based view of entrepreneurial identity development that foregrounds time and interaction. It theorizes identity formation as a dynamic progression through three stages: pre-identity, peri-identity, and post-identity formation. These are defined by shifts from largely unidirectional to increasingly bi-directional interactions with the entrepreneurial environment. Integrating three pillars of entrepreneurial identity; network (who entrepreneurs interact with), cognition (how they think and decide), and outcomes (what they intend and do) the model specifies how these mechanisms signal developmental progression. The framework contributes by enabling: (1) measurable progression, offering indicators to gauge where individuals are in their identity journey; (2) wide applicability, decoupling identity development from venture type or motive; and (3) forensic capability, providing a lens to analyze failure, pivots, and team conflict. Practically, the model informs the strategic timing and tailoring of educational, organizational, and policy interventions to entrepreneurs’ identity formation stage.

Link Threats with Opportunities: Managing Multiple Identities in Intrapersonal Identity Network

ENT
Zhenjing HAN (emlyon business school); Pia Arenius (emlyon business school)
Existing research has widely investigated identity work responding to identity threats but under-explored identity opportunities perceived in identity-implicating experiences. Also, previous studies treat identity threats and identity opportunities as isolated but ignore their interconnections. This paper adopts an intrapersonal identity network approach to study how identity threats link with identity opportunities and how individuals manage identity threats and opportunities in their intrapersonal identity network. Through the thematic analysis of 69 narrative interviews with 55 female successors and some of their parents in 50 family-owned firms, we find a new base of commitment, altruistic commitment, which is based on willingness and prosocial orientation. Our framework of identity work and interidentity work in three forms of multiple identity relationships (synergistic, conflicting, and compatible) and the six senses that can be categorized as identity opportunities (sense of prosocial orientation, sense of accomplishment, sense of recognition, sense of social responsibility, sense of purpose, and sense of mission) broaden the identity theory literature.

Redemptive Entrepreneurship: Identity Restoration Through Venture Creation

ENT
Muhammad A. Muhammad (Texas Tech University); Hans Hansen (Texas Tech University); Ronald K. Mitchell (Texas Tech University)
Drawing on qualitative data across three research sites—Detroit, Austin, and Cairo—we introduce redemptive entrepreneurship as a complementary form characterized by experienced loss as constitutive condition, restorative (rather than liberatory) motivation, and repair-oriented action. Distinguished from emancipatory entrepreneurship by its backward-facing temporal orientation, redemptive entrepreneurship frames adversity as damage to be repaired rather than constraint to be escaped. Cross-site analysis reveals two variants: geo-social redemptive entrepreneurship, in which territorial identity anchors restoration, and ethno-narrative redemptive entrepreneurship, in which heritage community narratives serve this function. We identify boundary conditions —native status, exposure to place-based decline narratives, and home versus destination orientation—that shape which variant emerges. Our findings extend theory on entrepreneurship under adversity, connect entrepreneurship scholarship to literatures on narrative identity and place identity, and carry implications for policy and practice in regional development.

Same Policy, Different Selves: Identity Work as Micro-Mechanism

ENT
Abdul-Kadir Ameyaw (Indiana University Kokomo); Siri Ann Terjesen (Florida Atlantic University)
Research on external enablers has advanced understanding of how environmental changes create conditions for entrepreneurship, yet it often privileges aggregate outcomes while overlooking the micro-mechanisms that explain why similarly positioned individuals respond differently. This study addresses this gap by examining how government policy catalyzes identity work that shapes divergent occupational behaviors. Using an inductive qualitative design, I analyzed 48 interviews with diasporic returnees to Ghana, alongside policymakers and traditional leaders involved in initiatives such as the Year of Return and Beyond the Return. The findings reveal a staged identity shift process—activation, commitment, anchoring, and confirmation—that translates enabling policy signals into enacted identities. Three identity pathways emerged: leadership identity fostering opportunity-driven entrepreneurship, survivalist identity leading to necessity entrepreneurship, and assimilationist identity channeling individuals toward employment. These insights extend external enabler theory by theorizing identity work as the micro-foundation linking policy shifts to heterogeneous outcomes, broaden identity scholarship by highlighting aspirational identity work in enabling contexts, and enrich diaspora entrepreneurship research by accounting for both entrepreneurial and non-entrepreneurial responses to state-led return initiatives.
