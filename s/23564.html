The Future-Making Design Science Methodology

TIM
Konstantin Remke (EM Normandie); Bugra Kilinc (ESCP Business School)
In today's world, crises such as environmental catastrophes, pandemics, wars, and inflation are becoming the new normal. As we observe a shift in crises away from isolated moments of shock toward unresolved, overlapping, persistent multiple crises, it is certain that unprecedented and highly complex problems will arise in the future. Thus, it gets increasingly important, but also more difficult, to align organizations in a way that allows them to anticipate and prepare for potential future problems. Existing research methodologies come here short. They focus on solving known and existing problems, assuming a defined problem space and offering structured processes for addressing them. This lack of methodological guidance for navigating ambiguous future problem spaces hinders the development of solutions that could anticipate, mitigate, or prevent the increasingly prevalent future problems that may arise from today’s poly-crisis reality while shaping organizational and societal trajectories. In response, we propose a new methodological approach, the ‘Future-Making Design Science Methodology (FM-DSM)’ for the development of future-proof contingency solutions. In doing so, we contribute to the literature on design science (DS) by proposing a forward-looking methodological process that builds on and expands upon established approaches, such as Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM), Action Design Research (ADR), and echeloned Design Science Research (eDSR). We also contribute to the literature on future-making by navigating the conversation beyond prescriptive theorizing and the study of harmful or desirable futures toward an action-oriented methodology that helps to anticipate, co-construct, and prepare for the future. Finally, we contribute to the poly-crisis literature by offering scholars a methodological toolkit to engage with the subject in a meaningful- and practice-oriented way.

Using Explainable AI to Compare Mortgage Lenders: A Novel Loan-Level Approach

TIM
Manish Krishna Bhat (Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute); Brian Clark (Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute)
This project proposes a novel methodology that leverages explainable AI (XAI) to systematically compare the lending behavior of mortgage institutions. We use borrower-level credit decision explanations from SHAP and LIME, generated by machine learning models trained on HMDA data (2004–2023), to predict the originating lender. The prediction outcomes are used to build confusion matrices that serve as behavioral similarity maps across institutions.

Development and Adaptation of a Multidimensional Scale to Measure Innovation Theater

TIM
Yarid Ayala (Tecnologico de Monterrey); Cristian Granados (EGADE Business School); Jaime Andrés Bayona; Milton Paredes (Escuela Superior Politécnica del Litoral)
Using the theoretical framework of symbolic actions, we aim to develop a reliable and valid scale for measuring innovation theater with two studies. First, we generate a list of items for the six dimensions of innovation theater and perform an exploratory factor analysis using a sample of 156 responses, four of the original six dimensions align with the original proposal, while two do not and two additional dimensions emerge. Second, we perform a confirmatory factor analysis of the new set of dimensions using a sample of 218 responses. Results provide evidence that an innovation theater scale (ITS) comprises six dimensions: five that explain how innovation theater occurs (symbolic association, communication, internal structures, innovation spaces, sexy management) and one that explain why it occurs (lack of innovation experience). Our proposed ITS shows adequate convergent and discriminant validity (tested by the climate for innovation scale and the six building blocks for an innovative culture scale). We discuss theoretical and practical contributions of our research.

Measuring Task Complexity from Unstructured Text: Evidence from Crowdsourcing Contests

TIM
Andrew Shin (University of Minnesota Twin Cities); Dayton Steele (University of Minnesota); Rachna Shah (University of Minnesota)
Problem specification—the unstructured text descriptions that define objectives, constraints, and evaluation criteria—is the primary means by which contest organizers communicate task requirements to potential contestants in contests. Prior empirical research has focused on contest attributes such as prize or duration that are observable through structured data but ignored the embedded elements in the product specification that require analysis of unstructured text data. In this study, we develop an empirical framework to measure text-embedded task complexity and examine how it impacts contest submission outcomes. We leverage recent advances in natural-language-processing (NLP) and incorporate state-of-the-art text analysis through sentence-level semantic representations (SBERT) to allow for powerful, scalable quantification of task complexity. Using a proprietary dataset from a major European crowdsourcing platform, we find that even after controlling for contest attributes, higher task complexity is associated with fewer total submissions and fewer screened submissions. This underscores the importance of text-embedded task complexity for both contest submission quantity and quality. Overall, our study makes two key contributions. It provides a novel method to operationalize task complexity to settings where tasks are communicated via written descriptions. It extends contest design research by quantifying how problem specification can predict participation beyond traditional levers.

Better Science as a Byproduct: The Institutional Revolution in the German Empire

TIM
Michael Ernst Rose (Max Planck Institute for Innovation and Competition)
I study how a positive shock to teaching facilities affects the direction and rate of research. I do so in the context of German universities around 1900 based on a novel dataset covering all printed dissertations from 1885--1910. I exploit the idiosyncratic roll-out of physics institutes at German universities in a staggered diff-in-diff framework. The institutes were constructed to meet the demands of a growing body of medical students, followed common design principles and were often subject to political tinkering and bureaucratic delays. While data collection is ongoing, I present results along three dimensions: (i) dissertations were more likely to get published in top journals, (ii) dissertation topics become more narrow, and (iii) new physicists were more likely to stay in Science and more likely to become top scientists. I interpret these results in light of exploitation vs. exploration decisions.
