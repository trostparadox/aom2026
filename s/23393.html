Climate Litigation and Corporate Emissions

ONE
Lizhen Wang; Shipeng Yan (The University of Hong Kong); Ze ZHANG (The University of Hong Kong)
Climate litigation against corporations is on the rise, yet research on firms’ responses yields mixed findings. We argue that different types of litigation remedial requests trigger distinctive institutional logics as interpretive cultural toolkit and thus explain this inconsistency. When firms see their jurisdictional peers facing injunctive relief litigation, they activate a climate logic to interpret the legal action, perceive sustained pressures, and thus reduce their emissions. In contrast, when they see their peers facing monetary relief litigation, firms activate a financial logic: they perceive the legal action as financially motivated and see an opportunity to “buy out” the pressure, ultimately increasing their emissions. When peers face mixed relief litigation, firms experience institutional ambiguity and struggle to activate an interpretive system that combines competing logics, resulting in inaction. Using a 2010–2023 dataset linking U.S. climate litigation to facility emissions, a contiguous border discontinuity design, and complementary interviews with legal experts, we provide causal evidence for these arguments. We address concerns on whether there are genuine emissions responses (e.g., pollution outsourcing or reduction), whether it is driven by logic activation or cancelling out of multiple logics, and whether legal threat intensity or corporate-level factors drives both litigation types and emissions responses.

From Firm-Specific Climate Change Exposure to Financial Constraints: Evidence from Global Capital Markets

ONE
Ammar Hussain (University of Science and Technology of China); Karim Mahran (University of Southampton Business School); Prem Thapamagar (University of Science and Technology of China); Ahmed Elamer
We investigate how firm-specific aspects of climate change exposure shape firms' financial flexibility, interest expenses, and value. By employing a panel dataset of global non-financially listed firms, we document that an increase in climate change exposure is associated with a significant decline in a firm's financial flexibility and value, while simultaneously increasing interest rates. Furthermore, we find that these effects are more pronounced in firms characterized by lower R&D, higher volatility in return on assets, larger size, and less earnings manipulation. These heterogeneous findings underscore the importance of a technology-driven approach, operational efficiency, and transparency in mitigating the financial risks influenced by climate change exposure. Concerning the individual components of climate change exposure, the key mechanisms shaping financial outcomes stem from opportunity and regulatory exposure, whereas the impact of physical exposure is rather muted. Overall, we contribute by uncovering the evidence of how the global capital market internalizes climate change exposure beyond risk perceptions and environmental disclosures.

Climate Change Exposure and Environmental Innovation

ONE
Hanh Thi Hong Nguyen; Linh Thi Tue Tran (University of Bristol Business School); Mohamed Husen Elmagrhi (Prince Mohammad Bin Fahd University)
Grounded in neo-institutional theory, this study examines how firm-level climate change exposure (CCE) influences environmental innovation activities and how institutional and cultural contexts moderate this relationship. Using a global panel of 23,358 firm-year observations for 4,079 firms across 39 countries from 2000–2021, we estimate multivariate regressions complemented by instrumental variable estimation, dynamic panel GMM, propensity score matching, and entropy balancing to address endogeneity and selection bias. Results show that CCE consistently predicts higher environmental innovation, even after controlling for firm heterogeneity and dynamic persistence. Governance quality amplifies this effect, while strong climate-related financial policy frameworks initially dampen it, reflecting short-run compliance costs. Cultural dimensions exert heterogeneous moderating effects: individualism strengthens the link, whereas power distance and long-term orientation weaken it. Robustness checks using alternative CCE measures, Paris Agreement shocks, subsample analyses, and bias mitigation confirm these findings. The study advances neo-institutional theory by showing innovation as a strategic response to legitimacy pressures.

Agrarian Labour in Transition: Climate Change, Livelihood Diversification, and Artisanal Mining

ONE
Daniel Siaw (Birmingham City University); George Ofosu (Brunel University London)
This article explores the dynamics of climate change and artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM), and its manifestations in livelihood diversification in the agriculture sector. Integrating the concept of ‘livelihood diversification’ with discussions on ‘cocoa-climate variations’ in Ghana, we empirically examine how climate effects have created a ‘system’ of space and conditions for labour shifts from the agrarian to the ASM economy. The article offers explanatory power to explore how climate-related shocks can become key push factors of farmers’ diversification strategies, highlighting their consequences for the environment and farm yields, and, in turn, their implications for livelihoods.
