How Professional Misconduct Triggers Social Inequalities: The Case of Elite Physicians in Hospitals

SIM
Farooq Ahmad (Siena University); Rashedur Chowdhury (University of Essex)
Using the sociology of professions as a theoretical lens, we explore how misconduct by elite public-healthcare professionals adversely affected rural communities. We examine a public healthcare system in which senior physicians engaged collectively in unethical practices that pressured economically disadvantaged patients to pay high prices for private healthcare. This issue appears to be inadequately theorised in the literature on professional misconduct. Using rich qualitative data from public hospitals and private clinics in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, we show that the public healthcare system contained a factional elite system consisting of a network of medical professionals and affiliated organizations. Senior doctors, private medical laboratories, and pharmaceutical suppliers collaborated in unethical behaviour that worsened the prevailing social inequalities and constituted professional misconduct.

Common Ownership and Employee Welfare

SIM
Nishant Kathuria (Texas State University); Sumit Majumdar (The University of Texas at Dallas)
Common ownership occurs when one or more institutional investors own shares of several competitors operating in a product market. Expanding common ownership research, this article addresses an important but previously unexplored question: How does the presence of common ownership affect employee welfare spending in firms? We deploy a balanced sample of 1,236 observations, with 103 distinct firms across a 12-year window in India. Our analysis shows that common owners’ presence negatively impacts employee welfare spending. Such discretionary welfare reductions are further aggravated when firms face mandated institutional pressures to spend on externally focused defined corporate social responsibility (CSR) activities. When we partition our sample into traditional businesses (agricultural and food processing and textile manufacturing sectors) and modern businesses (information technology and pharmaceutical sectors), we find that common ownership presence negatively impacts employee welfare spending in the information technology and pharmaceutical sectors. Overall, we caution that common owners’ pressures on firms for cutting employee welfare spending can be strategically dysfunctional for firms and for India’s economy.

Moral Hypocrisy and Labor Violations in Supply Chains: The Detection of Modern Slavery Risk

SIM
Yi-Su Chen (University of Michigan-Dearborn); Helet Botha (University of Michigan-Dearborn); Yinyin Cao (University of Michigan-Dearborn); Young Kyun Ro
This study examines buyers’ and suppliers’ respective contributions towards information transparency in the wake of modern slavery risk. Drawing from research on actor-observer bias, we theorize and test a potential discrepancy (i.e., moral hypocrisy) that exists between the desire for transparency as a buyer and the willingness to be transparent as a supplier. We also take into account the effects of relational norms and buyer dependence on the supplier in predicting the degree of moral hypocrisy. Using an online experimental vignette (N = 215), our findings hint at the existence of moral hypocrisy between buyer and supplier reactions towards modern slavery risk: Buyers desire knowing that the root cause of a labor disruption is a modern slavery risk, but state that as a supplier they would not share this information. Results further show that strong relational norms weaken this hypocrisy, while buyer dependence on the supplier strengthens the hypocrisy. This study sheds light on the gap between how one behaves as a buyer versus how one would expect to behave as a supplier. It is the first to detect the moral hypocrisy that obstructs transparency between buyers and suppliers in combating modern slavery.

The Illusion of Progress in Global Supply Chain: Post–Rana Plaza Reform Through a Neo-Gramscian Lens

SIM
Suman Saha (London School of Economics and Political Science); Guido Palazzo (University of Lausanne)
Despite extensive reforms after the Rana Plaza disaster, global supply chain labour governance in Bangladesh’s garment industry continues to produce sharply conflicting assessments of success and failure. Although transnational initiatives have improved factory safety and compliance, workers’ material conditions and broader wellbeing remain precarious. Drawing on a decade-long ethnographic study (2014–2023), this article examines how narratives of progress in global labour governance are constructed and sustained despite limited substantive change. Integrating framing theory with neo-Gramscian political economy, we conceptualise post–Rana Plaza reforms as a passive revolution in which dominant actors respond to moral–political challenges through bounded, managerial reforms that create the appearance of change while leaving underlying power relations intact. We identify a sequential process of hegemonic re-stabilisation through which opposition is selectively absorbed, and contestation muted. Our findings challenge consensus-oriented evaluations of labour governance and call for more politically grounded assessments of participation, reform, and impact in global supply chains.
