Dirty by Design? Work Challenges, Stigma, and Identity Threat in Crowdwork

CTO
Ward Van Zoonen; Monika E. Von Bonsdorff (University of Jyväskylä); Marjo Elisa Siltaoja (University of Jyväskylä)
This study advances theorizing about gig work by adopting a dirty work and occupational stigma lens to examine how crowdworkers experience and navigate identity threats resulting from structurally challenging working conditions. Drawing on a two-wave panel survey of 278 European crowdworkers and employing latent change modeling, we demonstrate that intra-individual increases in experienced work-related challenges predict heightened perceptions of occupational stigma and subsequent identity threat. Contrary to prevailing views that occupational and organizational prestige serves as a stigma buffer, our results show that perceived occupational prestige functions paradoxically: the more prestigious the occupation/platform is perceived, the more respondents perceive challenges at work and an increasing sense of stigma. Our findings reconceptualize crowdwork as dirty work by highlighting how structurally precarious conditions shape the internalization of stigma, leading to identity threat.

Solver Ability Cue and Mitigating Interventions in Crowdsourcing Contests

CTO
Kai Ye (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Tat Koon Koh (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology)
Firms use crowdsourcing contests to seek ideas for solving their problems. To achieve optimal contest outcomes, seekers should judge ideas objectively based on their actual quality. However, drawing from the accessibility-diagnosticity framework, we argue that seekers’ idea evaluation may instead be influenced by non-idea attributes—specifically, cues about the solver’s ability (e.g., skill level or track record). This can lead to suboptimal evaluations, such as giving low ratings to high-quality ideas or high ratings to low-quality ones. We also examine theory-based interventions to reduce the impact of solver ability cues on seekers’ evaluations. The results from a natural experiment on a crowdsourcing design contest platform (Study A) show that solvers received higher ratings immediately after the platform upgraded their displayed skill level, providing evidence of the solver ability cue effect. We then conducted an online experiment (Study B) to better establish causality and test two interventions to mitigate the effect. The results suggest that the influence of solver ability cues can be weakened by reducing the perceived diagnosticity of these cues, leading to more objective seeker evaluations. This research adds to the crowdsourcing contest literature and offers practical ways to improve contest outcomes.

How Creative Process Engagement Drive Generative AI-Enabled Routine Transformation

CTO
Rahul Chandra Sheel (XLRI Xavier School of Management)
Generative artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly used in complex and creative work, yet employees differ markedly in whether they use these tools to improve efficiency or to fundamentally transform how they work. Existing research on individual technology adaptation largely conceptualizes change as a reactive response to technology disruptions, leaving unanswered why some individuals proactively discover transformative uses of new technologies. We address this gap by examining how creative process engagement enables generative AI–driven routine transformation at the individual level. Integrating creativity research with affordance theory, we propose a sequential mediation model in which creative process engagement fosters the discovery of expanded pathways, novel or alternative ways of executing work routines enabled through interaction with generative AI. These expanded pathways enhance perceived usefulness, which in turn leads to changes in work routines. We further theorize that skill fit and task fit operate as compensatory boundary conditions: when generative AI aligns poorly with task requirements, individual skill fit becomes critical for translating creative engagement into pathway discovery, whereas skill differences matter less when task fit is high. We test the model using survey data from employees in the United States, the United Kingdom, and India who regularly use generative AI at work. Results support the proposed sequential mediation and compensatory interaction effects. The study advances research on technology and work by shifting attention from reactive adaptation to proactive exploration and clarifying when individual capabilities enable generative AI–driven work transformation.

Meso-Level Custodianship in Platform Governance: Evidence from Volunteer Moderators

CTO
Hussein Fadlallah (Carnegie Mellon University)
How can unpaid volunteers govern large online communities when they hold neither formal employment nor shared interpretive routines? We analyze 17,340 backstage chat messages exchanged by eight volunteer moderators of one of Reddit’s largest topical communities between 1 April 2018 and 7 January 2019, synchronized with the public trace of posts, announcements, and rule changes. We show how moderators sustain governance through a recursive custodianship loop that links (1) rule maintenance, (2) boundary stewardship, and (3) multi-level accountability. The study makes three contributions: it theorizes meso-level custodianship as a distinct governance layer between platform owners and users; it extends institutional-maintenance theory to settings where actors lack mandates, budgets, or coercive leverage; and it advances polycentric-governance theory by demonstrating how distributed authority remains workable through recursive coordination work and accountability. The findings have implications for researchers, platform designers, and policymakers seeking to understand how governance systems function under conditions of high velocity and contested authority.

Perceived AI Adoption, Human Agency Threat and Crowdfunding Performance

CTO
Kaiwei Dai (Central South University); Ruijuan Wang (Central South University)
Despite the rapid expansion of artificial intelligence as an invisible co-creator in product and content development, its impact on consumers’ perceptions of innovation remains insufficiently examined. We propose that perceived threats to human agency constitute a critical yet underexplored factor shaping audience responses to AI-assisted creation. Using data from Indiegogo, we apply a Swin Transformer–based model to detect AI-generated visuals in project descriptions, validated through manual annotation. Our results show that perceived AI adoption significantly reduces backers’ willingness to invest. This negative effect primarily emerges in domains that heavily rely on human agency and creativity, whereas it is not evident in more technology-oriented or functionally driven domains that involve less expression of human subjectivity. Moreover, projects that emphasize emotional resonance and sensory richness in their narratives experience even stronger negative effects, as these attributes are typically associated with human uniqueness and subjective expression. Our findings reveal the conditions under which AI-assisted innovation backfires, highlighting the psychological limits of AI acceptance and underscoring the importance of transparent communication in mitigating perceived threats to human agency and sustaining trust in creative outputs.
