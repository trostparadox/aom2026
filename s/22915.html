The Divergent Influence of Partners and Communities: Learning from Peers to Form Distant Ties

OMT
Erez Marantz (Tel Aviv University); Lisha Liu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
While leveraging interfirm distant ties with previously unconnected partners provides firms with access to novel resources and fosters innovation, the inherent uncertainty of such ties poses significant challenges. Drawing on organizational learning and social network theories, we investigate how firms engage in vicarious learning from their existing social networks to navigate the formation of distant ties. Through interviews with 26 venture capitalists (VCs) and a quantitative analysis of 6,975 VC syndications in Israel (2000–2020), we reveal that the impact of vicarious learning on distant tie formation hinges on the social proximity of the learning source. We find that VCs use the successful distant partnership experiences of their direct partners for specific and tactical decisions, which paradoxically reduces their propensity to form new distant ties by encouraging collaboration with pre-vetted entities. Conversely, VCs rely on the collective successful experiences of their compatriots to inform broader syndication strategies, which increases their overall formation of new distant ties. Furthermore, high variance in compatriots' outcomes introduces ambiguity, diminishing distant tie formation. Our findings underscore that firms engage in rational, outcome-based learning from their peers' experiences. This study contributes to organizational learning theory by delineating conditions that activate different levels of vicarious learning and to network dynamics by highlighting the social embeddedness of distant tie formation.

Learning About Roads Not Taken

OMT
Daniel Schliesmann (Not Associated); Matteo Tranchero (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania)
Organizational experience is often scarce, making the ability to enrich it through generalization a central feature of learning. However, the consequences of learning in this way remain poorly understood. How does generalization shape the balance between omission and commission errors, and which organizations rely on it more? We develop a computational model that shows how generalization makes learning more efficient by spreading feedback across related alternatives, but this efficiency comes at the cost of increased omission errors. The model further predicts that the optimal degree of generalization depends on the nature of the firm's prior experience. We test these predictions using data on clinical trial failures. Consistent with the model, pharmaceutical firms reduce investment in biologically related drug targets, with the most significant declines occurring for proximate high-merit targets. The effects are larger for firms with more limited or concentrated experience, suggesting that generalization is an imperfect yet useful substitute for direct knowledge. These findings highlight both the promise and the pitfalls of generalization in settings where experience is limited.

Managing Emotion: A Reinforcement Learning Model of Leadership, Social Network, and Emotion Dynamics

OMT
Yeunkyung Cho (State University of New York at Binghamton); Sarah Escotto (State University of New York at Binghamton); Urvi Chintukumar Chintukumar Sukharamwala (State University of New York at Binghamton); Sadamori Kojaku (State University of New York at Binghamton)
Organizations undergoing strategic change often face intense emotional reactions among employees, making effective emotion management critical for organizational resilience. While prior research highlights the importance of leaders’ emotional capabilities, particularly emotional aperture, it remains unclear how leaders dynamically manage team emotions in the presence of peer-to-peer emotional contagion and evolving social networks. We address this gap by developing an agent-based simulation model in which a manager learns to regulate collective emotion through reinforcement learning (RL) while team members interact through adaptive social networks. Our model integrates three core elements: (i) leaders’ emotional aperture, which governs their sensitivity to team emotions; (ii) peer intimacy networks that adapt with emotional contagion; and (iii) learning-based leadership that adjusts intervention timing and strength based on emotional feedback. We simulate six leadership styles ranging from no intervention to fully continuous intervention across three network structures (random, community, and core–periphery). We track the evolution of collective emotion using measures such as emotional homophily and emotional entropy. Together, these findings conceptualize emotion management as a multilevel, learning-driven process. The study advances theories of emotional leadership and emotional contagion by modeling emotion management as a dynamic, adaptive system rather than a static interpersonal mechanism.

Stewarding Skills in Technological Change: Organising Responsibility as Situated Moral Practice

OMT
Banu Saatci (University of Milan); Chris Ivory (Mälardalen University); Maria-Laura Toraldo (University of Milan); Chris Warhurst (University of Warwick)
This paper develops a practice-based account of how responsibility is organised during technological change. It introduces the concept of skill stewardship to capture the moral and relational work through which responsibility is realised in the organisation of skills within socio-technical arrangements. Grounded in virtue ethics, care ethics and stewardship theory, the paper adopts a contextual-realist perspective that treats moral responsibility as both ethically grounded and practically accomplished. Empirically, the analysis is based on three ethnographic vignettes from European manufacturing and craft-based firms undergoing digital transformation. The cases show how responsibility becomes embedded in everyday organising through practices that enable learning, attend to tacit and embodied expertise and support participative skill development. Rather than appearing as formal governance or managerial control, responsibility emerges as a situated accomplishment shaped by technological infrastructures, organisational constraints and relational dynamics. By conceptualising skill stewardship as an organising practice, the paper reframes responsible leadership as an emergent and distributed form of moral work. It contributes to organisation and management theory by explaining how responsibility is sustained through the coordination of skills and advances socio-technical scholarship by identifying the moral practices through which work systems remain collectively oriented.
