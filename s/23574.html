From Voids to Sandboxes: How Firms Reallocate Innovation Under Pilot Policy Regimes (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Yuxuan Cao (Lanzhou City University); Chong Joeyce Yang (University of Science and Technology of China)
Pilot policies fundamentally reshape firms’ innovation behaviors. We argue that pilots are not institutional voids but state-led experimental patches operating within them—regulatory sandboxes that coexist with thin market-supporting domains. This reframing shifts attention from “voids versus logics” to governed complementarity. Firms may retain void-driven practices such as rent-seeking, yet their innovation outcomes are jointly shaped by local implementers and central logic formulators. Using a multi-level empirical design that leverages the staggered rollout of pilot programs across regions and sectors, we find that pilots increase incremental innovation but reduce radical innovation, while relational exchange persists. Effects intensify in deeper voids and vary with decentralization and state ownership, which influence implementation capacity and alignment with state logic. By separating the contextual condition of voids from the governance instrument of pilots, this study offers a new lens on how government intervention interacts with enterprise strategy and identifies actionable pathways to mitigate constraints posed by institutional voids.

Institutional Complexity and the Pursuit of Innovation Among Ultra-Orthodox Jews

TIM
Tal Berman (Ono Academic College); Sven M. Laudien (University of Bayreuth)
Innovation research has predominantly examined contexts characterized by institutional openness and normative flexibility, often overlooking settings where strong moral prescriptions delimit legitimate action. This study examines how individual innovators navigate competing institutional logics in morally bounded environments to initiate and sustain technological innovation. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative case study of nine Jewish Israeli ultra-Orthodox male technological innovators, the study adopts an institutional logics perspective to explore innovation under conditions of religious, communal, familial, and market constraints. The findings reveal that institutional logics at the individual level may be hierarchically ordered, with religious, community, and family logics functioning as non-negotiable moral anchors that define the boundaries of acceptable innovation-related action. Rather than balancing or compromising among logics, innovators engage in a circular process of logic cooperation. Through sanctification, innovation is framed as morally and religiously meaningful, legitimating engagement with market practices. Through constraint transformation, moral and social restrictions are reinterpreted as sources of differentiation, resilience, and long-term advantage. By foregrounding micro-level sensemaking and agency under strong moral constraint, this study advances institutional theory by challenging linear and equilibrium-based accounts of institutional complexity and contributes to innovation research by demonstrating how deeply embedded moral commitments can structure, rather than inhibit, innovative action.

Path Reinforcement Under Institutional Changes: Venture Capital Investments in the Defense Industry

TIM
Joonho Oh (KDI School of Public Policy and Management)
In recent years, the U.S. defense industry has experienced a rapid influx of venture-backed startups and venture capitalists, challenging a sector long dominated by incumbent contractors and rigid regulatory barriers. We explore how these new actors—especially venture capitalists—gain prominence in this sector and identify the mechanisms driving this transformation. We argue that institutional changes initiated by the Department of Defense in 2013 and 2019 attract venture capitalists to this sector and that venture investment in this sector follows a path-reinforcing pattern. Using PitchBook data on all U.S. venture investment from 2000 to 2023, we employ a two-way fixed effects difference-in-differences design to analyze changes in venture capitalists’ defense-sector investment following these policy changes. The results show that growth in defense-sector investment is driven primarily by investors with prior defense investment experience, indicating the presence of a path-reinforcing investment pattern in this sector. The mechanisms underlying path-reinforcing investment also vary across institutional contexts. Information and network advantages dominate as drivers after the 2013 policy change, whereas legitimacy becomes central after the 2019 change. Across both periods, field-specific expertise remains a persistent driver. These findings highlight how institutional change reshapes the mechanisms of path-reinforcing venture investment and offer new insights into the transformation of entrepreneurship and innovation in traditionally closed sectors.

Regulatory Reform and Market Evolution in the Spectrum Commons

TIM
Haiyang Zhang (Harvard Business School); Shane Greenstein (Harvard University); Roberto Fontana (University of Pavia); Do Yoon Kim (Boston College)
How does a decline in regulatory frictions shape innovation and competition in technology markets—and who benefits most from such reforms? This study examines the Federal Communications Commission’s transformation of wireless device regulation between 1997 and 2001, which reduced certification delays, delegated compliance testing to private bodies, and expanded access to unlicensed spectrum. Using administrative records covering nearly 200,000 equipment authorization applications from 1990 to 2015, we analyze how reductions in procedural costs affected product introduction, firm entry and survival patterns, and competitive dynamics. We document a distinctive pattern of market evolution. Following the reforms, regulatory processing times fell sharply,coinciding with a sustained expansion in product introduction and firm participation, particularly in unlicensed spectrum applications. This expansion was driven disproportionately by new entrants, who accounted for most growth in product variety and experimentation across emerging categories. At the same time, established firms retained survival advantages and remained dominant in technically complex product categories. The coexistence of widespread entrant-led experimentation and incumbent persistence is difficult to reconcile with strong forms of regulatory capture, which would predict disproportionate gains for established firms. Taken together, the findings identify a hybrid competitive regime in which regulatory reform broadens access and experimentation without eliminating capability-based advantages. These results inform contemporary debates on privatized certification, commons governance, and the design of regulatory institutions for emerging technologies.
