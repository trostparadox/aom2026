Applications of Technology as a Strategic Choice: Evidence from Startups

TIM
James Addis (University of Toronto)
An emerging conversation in innovation strategy highlights the importance of different ‘applications’ or ‘uses’ for technology. However, the field is only beginning to explain how and why firms strategically choose to pursue particular applications over others. Complementing recent work on internal drivers and processes, this paper focuses on external factors. We hypothesize that firms are more likely to pursue a specific application after observing salient events – such as large funding rounds or acquisitions – experienced by peers commercializing the same technology. We construct a novel longitudinal dataset mapping the product market applications of emerging technologies pursued by US-based startups, utilizing a large language model to analyze archived firm websites. We find that a focal firm is significantly more likely to diversify or pivot into a particular product market application following relevant positive events for other startups active in the same emerging technology. This association is moderated by founder prior experience and levels of competition. Together, these results point towards a phenomenon of collective experimentation, whereby the strategic choice of technology application is driven not only by private information, but by a social process of mutual observation and validation.

Data Posture, Audience Specificity, and Competitive Advantage in Artificial Intelligence Startups

TIM
Yongseok Jang (California State University San Bernardino); Seongmin Jeon (Gachon University)
Data requirements have emerged as a critical source of competitive advantage and a significant barrier to entry in artificial intelligence (AI) markets. However, existing research has only begun to examine how AI data shape competitive advantage. Addressing this gap, this study investigates how AI data influence strategic positioning in AI ventures by developing an integrative strategic framework of AI service businesses. Drawing on a multiple-case study approach, we conceptualize AI data as a key strategic resource for artificial intelligence startups (AISs) and link data characteristics to competitive outcomes. Our findings show that the type of AI data plays a central role in shaping firms’ strategic positions, resulting in four distinct strategic configurations. While data type affects both value creation and defensibility, its strategic impact is contingent on the specificity of the audience for which the data are employed.

Deep Tech Founder Teams: The Effect of Human Capital Depth and Sharedness on Startup Success

TIM
Jannik Nolden (Technical University Munich); Theresa Treffers
Deep technology (Deep Tech) startups are characterized by long Research & development (R&D) cycles, high capital intensity, and substantial technological uncertainty. Despite growing practical interest, research lags behind on how human capital needs to be distributed across the founder team to increase Deep Tech startup success. Addressing this gap, we examine how the depth and sharedness of founder team managerial, technical, and entrepreneurial human capital influence total funding raised. Human capital sharedness refers to the extent to which knowledge is collectively distributed across the founder team, while human capital depth refers to the level of specialized knowledge. Building on balanced skill theory, we argue that founder teams must be “jacks-of-all-trades” who collectively span the startups key functional domains, combining sufficient breadth to adapt with enough specialization to execute. Founder teams benefit from human capital depth, as long as founder team members share management knowledge that enables coordination. We test our hypotheses using a global dataset of 2,855 Deep Tech startups from Dealroom and Crunchbase, complemented by a LinkedIn-matched subset of 7,341 founders. Results show that human capital depth is the main predictor for total funding raised. Both technical depth and managerial depth are positive, significant predictors of total funding raised, whereas sharedness has little explanatory power. We find that Deep Tech startups tend to be more successful when founder teams possess depth of expertise. Total funding raised increases when teams have strong management depth and, especially, strong technical depth. We also find a clear complementarity effect. When a founder team has high technical depth, it performs even better if that technical expertise is shared across multiple founders. Founder team sharedness is not generally associated with greater deep tech startup success. Having a founder team with similar management, technical, or entrepreneurial human capital does not, by itself, lead to higher total funding raised.

The Impact of Big Data on Process Innovation and The Moderating Role of Entrepreneurial Commitment

TIM
Alan Kurahmad (Georg August University Göttingen); Matthias Schulz (University of Göttingen)
Big data is reshaping how startups recognize and exploit opportunities, challenging traditional resource-based approaches, and enabling resource-constrained ventures to enhance process innovation through faster learning, shorter iteration cycles, and data-driven experimentation. Yet, realizing these benefits requires substantial cognitive and temporal effort from founders, highlighting the role of entrepreneurial commitment. Drawing on contingency theory, we examine the moderating role of founder commitment – full-time versus hybrid – on the relationship between big data and process innovation in German startups, by using data from the ZEW panel. Results show that big data positively impacts process innovation only for full-time founders; a finding robust to instrumental variable regression. This study underscores that the value of big data depends on the type of entrepreneurial commitment.
