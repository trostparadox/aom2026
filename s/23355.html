Making Help Visible: Experimental Evidence from a Recognition Program in the Workplace

STR
Silvia Castro (INSEAD); Maren Mickeler (ESSEC Business School); Hoa Ho (LMU Munich School of Management)
Organizations rely on peer-to-peer assistance among employees, yet incentivizing supportive behavior is difficult for organizations. This study evaluates an intervention in the second largest bank in Uganda aimed at fostering peer support. Using a cluster randomized controlled trial, we introduced a public recognition program in which employees identified as the most supportive received an award. The intervention increased employees’ willingness to share expertise by 21% and to mentor by 12%. These shifts translated into higher productivity for junior staff, who rely more heavily on peer guidance, without reducing the output of award recipients or top performers. The results suggest that the intervention worked by reshaping how helping behaviors are perceived in the organization, altering professional reputation and career prospects.

Learning from Qualitative Feedback: How Divergent User Evaluations Shape Entrepreneurial Search

STR
Jaeho Choi (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Jamie Seoyeon Song (ESMT - European School of Management and Technology - Berlin)
Entrepreneurs increasingly rely on qualitative feedback from user communities when refining and redirecting early product ideas. We theorize how two dimensions of qualitative feedback—sentiment and divergence—shape entrepreneurial search. Using natural language processing and data from an online platform where entrepreneurs launch new products and receive user feedback, we develop novel measures of search distance and feedback divergence. We find that greater feedback divergence is associated with more distant search, particularly when feedback sentiment is positive. Together, these findings advance research on entrepreneurial learning by showing that search is shaped not only by whether feedback is favorable or unfavorable, but also by how its informational structure guides entrepreneurs’ interpretation of the opportunity space.

Portfolio Signals and Termination Timing in New Product Development

STR
J. Mauricio Galli Geleilate (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Denise Dunlap (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Gerardo Bastos Neto (University of Massachusetts Lowell)
This study examines how innovation portfolio characteristics influence the timing of new product development (NPD) termination decisions. While prior research advocates for failing fast, the portfolio-level conditions framing how firms accelerate or delay terminations remain unexplored. We argue that portfolio-level attributes provide the interpretive context through which managers assess the meaning and consequences of project terminations. Drawing on a dataset of 5,158 drug development projects from 155 biopharmaceutical firms, we find that portfolio attrition exhibits a curvilinear effect on termination timing where initial increases in attrition accelerate subsequent terminations, but this effect diminishes as attrition intensifies, shifting toward delayed decisions. We further demonstrate that early-stage portfolio attrition prolongs late-stage termination timing by redirecting attention toward pipeline replenishment. Regarding diversification, we uncover asymmetric effects across development stages. Greater diversification in late-stage projects delays terminations due to coordination complexity and institutional pressures to maintain strategic balance. In contrast, increased early-stage diversification accelerates terminations by expanding the menu of alternative opportunities and reducing escalation incentives. These findings contribute to innovation management theory by revealing the portfolio-contingent nature of termination decisions and reconciling competing perspectives on proactive versus cautious termination responses.

Endogenous Market Shaping and Organizational Learning: SpaceX and the Orbital Launch Industry

STR
Petteri Leppänen (IE University); Constance E. Helfat (Tuck School of Business)
Research on endogenous market shaping has shown how firms transform markets by altering industry payoff structures, yet we know little about the relationship between shaping and processes inside firms. In this study, we examine the relationship between endogenous market shaping and organizational learning. Using a mixed-method approach, we conduct an in-depth historical analysis of the orbital launch industry, focusing on SpaceX as a focal case. We identify four major phases in which SpaceX reshaped the industry by altering the structure of supply and/or demand. Across these phases, we document recurring cycles of shaping and learning: in some instances, market shaping enabled organizational learning; in others, organizational learning enabled subsequent shaping; and in still others, a single strategic move generated both shaping and learning simultaneously. We contribute to research on endogenous market shaping by bringing organizational learning into the analysis of how industry payoff structures evolve over time. We also shed light on how repeated interactions of shaping and learning create conditions that can sustain early-mover advantages and explain heterogenous early-mover outcomes.

Balancing Exploration and Exploitation in Business Units: Does Structural Ambidexterity Pay Off?

STR
Nazlihan UGUR (Nyenrode Business University); René Belderbos (KU Leuven); Stijn Kelchtermans (KU Leuven); Bart Leten (KU Leuven); Jinhyuck (Joseph) Park (Sogang University)
While the need for firms to balance exploration and exploitation in organizational learning has received substantial attention in the literature, how to achieve this balance remains a puzzle. Structural ambidexterity, the pursuit of exploration and exploitation across separate structures, has been suggested as a potential solution. However, systematic evidence on how this separation should be achieved is scarce. In this paper, we posit that the separation of exploration and exploitation for innovation in different business units or subsidiaries may be a performant solution for multi-unit firms. Conceptualizing structural separation-based ambidexterity as engagement in both activities with business units specializing in either one of these activities, we find that such an ambidexterity configuration significantly enhances a firm's market value. However, under conditions of low technological dynamism, this ambidextrous configuration fails to deliver positive effects on firm market value with the more limited benefits not outweighing the costs. Our inferences are based on panel data on more than 4000 business units of 117 leading multi-unit firms, 1999-2014.
