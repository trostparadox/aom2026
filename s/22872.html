Seeing Through Shared Expectations: Organizational Entitativity and Competence Image Consistency

OB
Ying Tang (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Marius Van Dijke (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University)
Organizational image research has largely focused on how positively organizations are evaluated, paying comparatively little attention to whether members of the same stakeholder group form similar images of the same organization. Yet within-group consistency in organizational images is widely assumed and consequential. We address this gap by examining the antecedents of, and mechanisms underlying, organizational competence images consistency within both internal and external stakeholder groups. Drawing on research on organizational entitativity and expectancy violation, we argue that people hold category-level expectations that organizations are coherent social actors. When an organization is perceived as highly entitative, this expectation is confirmed and stakeholders rely on a shared interpretive framework where high entitativity is closely linked to organizational competence, resulting in more consistent competence images. When entitativity is perceived as low, this expectation is violated, triggering more exploratory and noise-sensitive processing and increasing dispersion in competence images. We test these arguments in one field survey and two experiments. Results support our predictions and identify expectancy violation as the underlying mechanism.

Emodiversity and Wise Reasoning: Unpacking the Positive Impact of Social Media Usage on Creativity

OB
Yueting Ji (Central University of Finance and Economics); Haiyang Liu (Nanyang Technological University, Singapore); Shengming Liu (Fudan University); Yating Gao (China University of Geosciences Beijing); Jiexuan Zhang; Hongsong Guo (Central University of Finance and Economics); Xuanhong You (Rutgers University-New Brunswick); CHENGKAI PAN (East China University of Science and Technology)
While organizations often view employee social media usage during work hours as a distraction, this study draws on the functional account of emotions to argue that such usage can enhance creativity through emotional processes. We demonstrate that daily social media engagement at work positively relates to daily creativity via emodiversity and wise reasoning. Additionally, we identify extraversion as a key moderating personality trait in this relationship. Our hypotheses were tested across three studies, including two experience sampling studies and one experimental study. We also examined other emotion-based mechanisms, confirming the unique effect of emodiversity on creativity. All hypotheses were supported, highlighting that social media usage can positively impact employee creativity when mediated by emotional diversity and wise reasoning, particularly for extraverted individuals. This research contributes to a nuanced understanding of how the ubiquitous, technology-driven phenomenon of social media usage influences workplace behaviors and creative outcomes.

The Digital Coping Paradox: How Social Media Multitasking Shapes Stress and Performance

OB
Amlan Haque (Central Queensland University); Gazi Farid Hossain (King's Own Institute)
Digital technologies are now deeply embedded in academic and work settings, shaping how individuals manage attention and cope with stress. Drawing on Stress and Coping Theory, this study conceptualizes social media multitasking (SMM) as an emotion-focused digital coping behavior through which academic stress influences performance and satisfaction. Using two-wave, time-lagged survey data from 1,150 full-time university students enrolled in colleges and universities across Sydney, Australia, and structural equation modeling, the results show that academic stress is associated with greater engagement in social media multitasking, consistent with attempts to regulate emotional strain. However, this coping response proves counterproductive over time. While multitasking may provide momentary relief, increased SMM subsequently undermines academic performance and student satisfaction. Mediation analyses further indicate that SMM partially transmits the effects of academic stress to both outcomes, revealing a digital coping paradox in which behaviors that ease stress in the short term ultimately erode task effectiveness and evaluative well-being. By framing social media multitasking as a stress-contingent coping response rather than a simple distraction, this study advances understanding of how stress reshapes technology use and attention allocation in digitally saturated environments, with implications extending beyond education to digitally intensive forms of knowledge work.

Digital Opinion Leadership: Receiver-Validated Signaling via Parasocial Interaction and Credibility

OB
Pawel Korzynski; Olga Protsiuk (Kozminski University in Warsaw)
Digital opinion leadership is central to contemporary influence processes, yet existing research provides limited insight into how influence forms when audiences cannot directly verify expertise. Drawing on signaling theory, this study conceptualizes opinion leadership as a receiver-validated social influence process, in which competence cues become influential only when they are translated into credibility and relational meaning. The study distinguishes between expertise signaling and achievement signaling and examines whether these cues shape perceived opinion leadership through two individual-level mechanisms: credibility and parasocial interaction. Using a multi-source research design, the study combines influencers’ self-reports of signaling behaviors with independent judges’ evaluations of credibility, parasocial interaction, and opinion leadership, complemented by platform-based metrics. The results indicate that expertise signaling strongly predicts parasocial interaction and only weakly predicts credibility; both mechanisms increase perceived opinion leadership. Opinion leadership, in turn, strongly predicts intentions to consume influencer content and to follow advice, while likes, comments, and audience-quality indicators show weak effects once opinion leadership is included. Achievement signaling does not activate credibility or parasocial interaction and shows no indirect effects on engagement intentions. Overall, the findings suggest that digital influence emerges through audience validation of relational and cognitive cues, rather than from visible success indicators or surface-level metrics.

When Leisure Feels Wrong: Evening Media Engagement and Subsequent Workplace Behaviors

OB
Yiming Huang (The Ohio State University); Kathleen Keeler (The Ohio State University)
Media has become a dominant form of everyday leisure, yet its consequences for workplace behavior remain unclear. Integrating the virtue–vice paradigm with self-conscious emotion theories, we propose that the content of evening media engagement shapes employees’ retrospective evaluations of time use and, in turn, their next-day emotions and workplace behavior. We distinguish vice media (high immediate gratification but long-term costs) from virtue media (low immediate gratification but long-term benefits) and theorize a serial process in which vice (vs. virtue) media increases next morning wasted time perceptions, which elicit shame and subsequently motivate impression management and incivility. Study 1 (N = 394) provides initial evidence that choosing vice media heightens wasted time perceptions and indirectly increases shame. Study 2 (N level1 = 1,340; N level2 = 123) replicates these effects at the within-person level, demonstrating serial indirect effects linking vice (vs. virtue) media engagement to higher exemplification, self-promotion, and incivility through wasted time perceptions and shame. Moreover, rest intolerance strengthens the wasted time perceptions–shame link, such that these indirect effects emerge primarily among individuals high in rest intolerance. Together, these findings show that evening media choices can trigger self-evaluative processes that spill over into workplace.
