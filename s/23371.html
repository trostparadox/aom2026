From Science to Society: Predicting Attention Allocation To Scientific Knowledge Commercialization

STR
Gaurav Dilip Tikas (Indian Institute of Management Visakhapatnam)
This study theorises and tests a parallel-mediation framework explaining how strategic leadership and strategic flexibility shape the scientific knowledge commercialization performance of academic entrepreneurial teams. Grounded in the attention-based view of the firm, the framework specifies R&D knowledge management capabilities and R&D network ambidexterity as simultaneous team-level mediators that channel and allocate managerial attention toward commercialization outcomes. Using a multi-method, multi-sample design, survey data were collected from a pilot sample in Europe (N1 = 128), a main sample in India (N2 = 368), and a replication sample in Japan (N3 = 328). An iterative analytic approach combining exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis, and path analysis was employed to validate the measurement model and estimate the proposed parallel mediation structure. The findings indicate that R&D knowledge management capabilities partially mediate the relationship between strategic leadership and scientific knowledge commercialization performance, while R&D network ambidexterity mediates the relationship between strategic flexibility and commercialization performance. Moreover, these mediators jointly transmit the effects of strategic leadership and strategic flexibility on the commercialization performance of academic entrepreneurial teams, underscoring the importance of aligned knowledge and networking capabilities in R&D settings. The study contributes to the attention-based view by extending it to the team level, embedding it in an academic entrepreneurship context, and validating the proposed framework across European, Indian, and Japanese university-based entrepreneurial ecosystems. For practitioners, the results highlight how deliberately configuring leadership, strategic flexibility, and R&D networking portfolios can help academic teams more effectively direct attention and resources toward superior knowledge commercialization outcomes.

Exploring the Process of Niche Leader in Emerging Economies Scale-Up: The Logic of Nesting Growth

STR
Hanning Shi (University of Cambridge); Yongjiang Shi (University of Cambridge); Sihan Li (INSEAD); Jiaming Yang (Zhejiang University of Finance and Economics)
Niche leaders increasingly play a pivotal role in emerging economies' industrial development through their specialized capabilities and innovation-driven strategies. While existing research emphasizes niche leaders as complementors within established ecosystems, we observe their emerging role as ecosystem architects, particularly in institutional environments where government intervention creates unique market opportunities. Through an in-depth longitudinal case study of a semiconductor foundry in China, we unpack how niche leaders leverage institutional support to orchestrate ecosystem formation while maintaining their specialized advantages. Our findings reveal a "nesting growth" logic, encompassing “Facilitating resource availability via government to centralize itself within the ecosystem – Harmonizing multi-layered demands to nurture a nesting ecosystem – Diffusing derived ecosystem identity to facilitate dual ecosystem development”. Based on this, our study contributes to understanding ecosystem formation in emerging economies and enriches theories in niche strategy literature.

From Extraction to Co-Fourishing: Indigenous Knowledge, Rights, and Cooperation with Entrepreneurs

STR
Ricardo Laverde (Insper)
Indigenous territories contain biodiversity and biocultural knowledge that attract entrepreneurs in biotrade, design, and natural products. Yet many engagements collapse into mistrust and conflict because management and property rights theories treat knowledge as separable and privately appropriable, fragmenting relations among land, culture, and authority that ground Indigenous claims. We develop a strategic framework explaining when encounters stabilize as cooperative stewardship and when they tip into extractive contestation. First, we reconceptualize value via a collective, comprehensive utility function integrating pecuniary benefits with ecological integrity and cultural continuity. Second, we embed this value structure in an assurance game with cooperative and conflictual equilibria. Cooperation becomes self-reinforcing only when credible commitments reduce misappropriation risk and governance recognizes collective rights tied to place and decision authority. We derive governance conditions, including benefit sharing design, monitoring, third party mediation, and norms based enforcement, that shift beliefs toward co appropriation. The framework contributes to research on value appropriation, legitimacy, and organizing across institutional worlds.

Specialist or Generalist? Founder Human Capital, Regional Agglomeration, and Growth of Niche Leaders

STR
Yingya Jia (Shanghai University); Shuping Yue (Shanghai University); lijian zheng (Shanghai University)
Niche Leaders are pivotal in the global economy for their dominance in specialized value chains. However, their growth trajectory is defined by a fundamental “specialization paradox”. To resolve this tension, we examine the role of the founder’s human capital. While prior research debates the superiority of “Specialist vs. Generalist” founders, we argue that for niche leaders, the value of human capital is strictly contingent upon the venture’s evolving growth stages: Legitimacy acquisition (proving value in a niche) and scaling (breaking the niche ceiling). Leveraging a longitudinal dataset of 53,746 Chinese technology-based SMEs, we find that founder specific human capital is the primary driver of legitimacy acquisition, whereas general human capital drives scaling. Furthermore, we reveal a critical micro-meso interaction: Specialized agglomeration acts as a substitute for specialist founders but serves as a complement to generalist founders. In contrast, diversified agglomeration consistently exerts a negative effect: it acts as a substitute for generalists and creates cognitive interference for specialists, hindering both legitimacy and scaling. Our findings not only reconcile the human capital debate but also illuminate how niche leaders can leverage internal and external knowledge endowments to escape the competency trap of over-specialization.

Scientists, Tinkerers and Improvisers: A Contingency Theory of Business Model Development

STR
Peter Kalum Schou (BI Norwegian Business School)
Building on the theory-based view, this study examines how entrepreneurs targeting nascent markets use theory in developing business models. Drawing on 85 archival interviews with entrepreneurs who successfully commercialized ventures in nascent markets, I identify three distinct paths of entrepreneurial business model development: Scientists, Tinkerers, and Improvisers. Scientists rely primarily on learning-by-thinking, developing relatively complete theories—composed of explicit premises and conjectures—prior to market entry. Tinkerers combine learning-by-thinking with learning-by-doing, beginning with a rudimentary theory that is subsequently refined through experimentation. Improvisers rely almost exclusively on learning-by-doing, developing business models through action. Comparing these paths, the study shows how differences in when and how extensively entrepreneurs rely on theory shape the process of business model development. The findings further indicate that entrepreneurs calibrate their learning strategies to the learning economics of the market, adjusting the balance between learning-by-thinking and learning-by-doing based on the relative costs and returns of theorizing versus experimentation. By unpacking the variation in how entrepreneurs use theory when creating business models, the study develops a contingency perspective on entrepreneurial business model development and extends work on the theory-based view, business model development and entrepreneurial learning.
