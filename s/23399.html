Session overview

The question is no longer whether to integrate AI into the classroom. It is how to do it well. This session showcases innovative pedagogical architectures that harness AI as a genuine teaching resource: from active learning scaffolds for prompt literacy and role-biased chatbot provocations, to deconstructed AI-persona cases, narrative design for AI-resilient assessment, and embedded AI grading schemes. Grounded in learning theory and instructional design, these papers offer practical, evidence-based blueprints for educators building the AI-integrated classroom of tomorrow.

Active Learning as a Scaffold for AI and Prompt Literacy in Management Education

MED
Antigoni Papadimitriou (State University of New York, Farmingdale State College); Zilong Pan (Lehigh University)
This study examines how an intentionally designed, active learning–based pedagogical framework can support the development of AI and prompt literacy in management education. Drawing on the Active Learning–Informed Generative AI Integration (ALGAI) framework, the research explores how cognitive, metacognitive, collaborative, and ethical dimensions of learning interact to structure students’ engagement with generative artificial intelligence (GAI) tools. Using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, data were collected from 123 undergraduate students enrolled in an Organizational Behavior course. Quantitative results revealed significant improvements in AI and prompt literacy across all subscales, while qualitative findings showed that students engaged in deeper reasoning, reflection, and ethical evaluation when guided by intentional pedagogical design. The study advances active learning scholarship by demonstrating how GAI can function as a reflective and dialogic learning partner rather than a passive content generator. It contributes to management education by offering an empirically grounded, replicable model for developing AI-interaction literacy, encompassing critical thinking, self-regulation, and responsible collaboration. Implications highlight the importance of process-oriented AI integration that aligns with core principles of active learning and supports deeper conceptual understanding in business curricula.

Deconstructed Cases with AI Personas: Within-Student Evidence from Strategy Courses

MED
Steven James Hyde (Boise State University); Christine Madonna Kirkland (Western Carolina University)
Purpose: Generative AI weakens written case assessments; we test a redesign that leverages AI to strengthen strategic thinking. Design/methodology/approach: In a required strategy course, 137 students completed both a traditional written case and a deconstructed case requiring interviews with three AI executive personas (CFO, CMO, COO) and an integrated plan. We conducted within-student comparisons and used three independent AI raters (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) and human validation to code nine learning dimensions. Findings: Deconstructed cases shifted students from description to causal explanation, from framework lists to structural diagnosis, and from generic advice to sequenced, quantified roadmaps. Students integrated cross-functional evidence, articulated explicit tradeoffs, and adopted an executive voice with cash-allocation rules and decision thresholds. Practical implications: Purposeful persona conflict, quantitative scenario testing, and transcript accountability can upgrade case pedagogy. Originality/value: Reframes AI from threat to scaffold and demonstrates higher-order strategic reasoning relative to traditional cases.

Balancing Technology and Practice Through Narrative Design

MED
Hamizah A Hamid (National University of Malaysia)
The rapid adoption of generative artificial intelligence in higher education has intensified concerns regarding superficial learning, assessment validity, and student overreliance on automated tools. These concerns are particularly salient in entrepreneurship education, where learning outcomes emphasize judgment, opportunity recognition, and sensemaking under uncertainty. This paper presents a learning innovation implemented in a first-year entrepreneurship course in Malaysia that deliberately balanced technology use with experiential practice. Students interviewed local entrepreneurs and transformed these lived experiences into a structured five-page comic narrative linked to entrepreneurship theory. AI tools were permitted as creative and supportive aids but constrained through task design that foregrounded human interpretation and reflection. Drawing on constructivism, and narrative learning perspectives, the study analyses student learning artefacts, presentations, peer feedback, and instructor reflections. Findings suggest that narrative comic-based assessment can support deep conceptual understanding while shaping responsible and creative AI use. The paper contributes to management learning theory by demonstrating how assessment design mediates the relationship between technology, experience, and learning.

From Threat to Ally: Embedding AI in University Essay Assessment

MED
Noel Kretz (University of St. Gallen); Johann Peter Murmann (University of St. Gallen)
Many educators fear that the rise of AI is endangering the essay as a form of examination. In our view, universities need to adapt to technological changes to prepare their students for the future. To illustrate this, we conducted an exploratory study using the results from a business course attended by 35 students at a Swiss business school. We transparently introduced AI into the grading scheme, grounded in the theoretical concepts of self-regulated learning, AI literacy, and epistemic cognition. Our findings suggest that using AI does not necessarily lead to superior essay performance. Students with limited skill in the concepts still exhibit a poor understanding of the course’s content and critical thinking, which AI cannot mask. We therefore argue that the essay still has merit. However, educators must revise their grading processes. To account for managers' real-world environment, management education must shift towards the critical evaluation of AI-generated information and reflective writing.

Role-Biased Chatbots as Pedagogical Provocations for Collaborative Learning

MED
Kumaran Rajaram (Nanyang Technological University); Tanmay Sinha (Nanyang Technological University)
This study examined how undergraduate students engage with biased AI perspectives during management learning tasks. Sixty-two students individually interacted with chatbots intentionally configured to reflect differing corporate roles and associated biases in decision-making and strategic management. They later collaborated with human peers, with reflective checkpoints embedded throughout. We investigated how students integrated insights from their initial human–AI interactions, how these shaped their reasoning during group discussions, and how learning was consolidated following human–human collaboration. Findings indicate that students primarily recognized and interrogated the chatbots’ role-driven biases, using them as a basis for critical comparison during groupwork. Collaborative dialogue further deepened their awareness of decision-making biases and the varied strategic priorities embedded within each role, resulting in more nuanced, evidence-informed perspectives on managerial decision-making and strategy formulation. The design promoted reflection, perspective-taking, and collective sense-making. However, some participants remained reluctant to revise established views, primarily due to the complex, context-dependent trade-offs involved in management. Collectively, the results illustrate the value of structured human–AI engagement in cultivating critical thinking. When students are prompted to interrogate decisions through “why” and “how” questions, reflection becomes a tool for sense-making, thereby elevating the quality of learning in management education.
