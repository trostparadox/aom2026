“Under Pressure”: Scientific Production as Legitimacy Work Across Academic and Commercial Audiences

TIM
Quentin Plantec (Toulouse Business School); Eric Michael Laviolette; Benjamin Cabanes; Erwan Lamy
Academic entrepreneurship is widely promoted as a core channel for science commercialization, yet we know surprisingly little about how academic entrepreneurs (AE) conduct and sustain their research while building ventures. Prior work shows that science commercialization can both enhance and distort scientific performance, and that AEs construct hybrid academic–entrepreneurial identities to manage role tensions. However, these literatures largely treat research outputs as downstream outcomes of commercialization or focus on the psychology of identity work, leaving underexplored the micro-level research practices of AEs and, given the collective nature of research and entrepreneurship, how these practices are shaped by social evaluations. Drawing on an inductive qualitative study of 26 AEs in the French academic system, we mobilize organizational legitimacy theory to analyse how laboratory colleagues, technology-transfer officers, investors, and clients evaluate academic entrepreneurs’ dual role, and how AEs adjust their research practices. We find, first, that enacting a hybrid academic–entrepreneurial identity exposes AEs to multi-audience legitimacy challenges, including external pragmatic demands and intra-lab moral, relational, and cognitive questioning. Second, we identify a portfolio of science-based activities—most notably publications, joint projects, and PhD co-supervision within their home laboratories—that AEs strategically deploy as pragmatic, relational, moral, and cognitive legitimacy signals across audiences. Third, we show that this science-based legitimacy work is enabled by hybrid-identity-related synergies, contingent on local academic cultures. The study advances research on science commercialization, hybrid identity, and legitimacy in science by explaining how AEs convert their dual role into sustained scientific production.

Becoming Lead Users: Emergence of a Collective Technological Frame Through Manifestations

TIM
Yuki Miyazawa (Hitotsubashi University); Masaru Karube (Hitotsubashi University); Avimanyu Datta (Illinois State University)
A substantial body of research on lead users has extensively examined their characteristics, with a predominant focus on identifying characteristics of lead users. However, prior research implicitly assumes that a shared understanding of which needs and knowledge are considered “advanced” pre-exists among market participants. We challenge this assumption by focusing on how specific users contribute to innovation in a fluid market context where such shared understanding—or a collective technological frame—is absent. We qualitatively analyze a historical case of market emergence of vocal synthesis technology, in which producer-user interaction led to the emergence of a collective technological frame. The results demonstrate that manifestation—an act of linguistically, visually, or materially embodying a future potentiality foreseen within a specific technological frame—enables producers and users with initially divergent technological frames to communicate, mutually adjust their perspectives, and eventually establish a collective technological frame. This also leads to the emergence of a market consensus regarding who the lead users are and their characteristics. Our study challenges the lead user theory’s assumption that characteristics inherent in lead users are predetermined and identifiable ex ante. Instead, we demonstrate how users interacting with producers become lead users ex post through the practice of manifestation.

Distinctive Narratives, Uneven Leaps: How Strategic Narratives Affect the Catch-up of Latecomers

TIM
Yanting Guo (Xiamen University); Yimeng Xu (Xiamen University)
While the rise of latecomer firms in high-technology industries has attracted growing attention, we still lack a nuanced understanding of how these firms strategically craft distinctive narratives to narrow performance gaps with industry leaders. Drawing on the optimal distinctiveness theory, we theorize that narrative distinctiveness exerts a dual-edged effect on catch-up: it can enhance competitive advantages but also incur legitimacy costs, particularly when firms occupy the middle ground between conformity and radical deviation. Using a dataset of listed high-tech firms in China, we find a U-shaped relationship between strategic narrative distinctiveness and market catch-up performance. Moreover, narrative distinctiveness translates into superior technological catch-up performance only when it is moderated with high levels of technological distinctiveness. Additional analyses reveal that these effects are more pronounced among state-owned enterprises. By integrating symbolic strategy with substantive innovation, this study advances catch-up theory and offers a novel antecedent of latecomer success: effective catch-up depends not only on what firms invent differently, but also on how they construct and communicate strategic narratives that resonate with external audiences.

How History Drives Innovation: Narrative History and Digital Transformation in U.S. Newspapers

TIM
Jun Ho Lee (University of Kansas); Marcelo Bucheli (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Drawing on an interpretive view of history and a longitudinal, qualitative historical analysis of the U.S. newspaper field from the mid-1990s through the early 2000s, we develop a theory of rhetorical–historical change to explain how innovations diffuse through fields and how industries evolve. Through an in-depth analysis of archival and industry texts, we trace how organizational actors including executives, journalists, trade associations, analysts, and entrepreneurs construct and mobilize narratives of the past to legitimate emerging practices, redefine field boundaries, and coordinate collective action during technological disruption. We show that digital transformation unfolded not simply through superior technologies or market efficiency, but through a staged process of rhetorical evolution. Following the Internet’s introduction as an exogenous innovation, field participants engaged in rhetorical variation, generating competing normalizing and problematizing interpretations under conditions of high ambiguity. As performance pressures intensified, these interpretations entered a phase of rhetorical selection, in which authoritative actors integrated local justifications into a dominant field-level narrative that aligned innovation with journalism’s historical mission and enabled diffusion. Finally, during rhetorical retention, this dominant rhetoric became naturalized and institutionalized, restructuring field relationships, reshaping authority patterns, and producing path-dependent yet bounded transformation. By theorizing history as an active causal force in innovation and industry change, this study advances research on innovation diffusion and industry evolution and demonstrates how organizations strategically reconstruct the past to make innovation possible.

Ontological Disagreement in Generative AI: How Technological Attributes Drive Contestation in Anime

TIM
Hafizh Rafizal Adnan (National University of Singapore); Shi-Ying Lim (National University of Singapore)
Emerging technologies generate contestation when their attributes are interpreted through incongruent frames. This study examines how attributes of Generative AI (GenAI) interact with stakeholders' frames about technology's role in anime production. Analyzing two early GenAI anime projects (Netflix and Corridor Digital) using text clustering and inductive analysis of social media discourse, we identify three GenAI attributes (learning appropriation, automated execution, and stochastic generation) that are interpreted through three distinct frames: craft-centered (valuing human execution), evolution-centered (embracing artistic innovation), and utility-centered (emphasizing functional outcomes). Stakeholders interpret these attributes through three frames, which lead to incompatible definitions of anime production, generating ontological disagreement over whether GenAI-enabled production constitutes anime production. We show that this disagreement resists resolution through transparency, demonstration, or dialogue because it stems from incompatible definitions of anime production, not from missing information. Our findings contribute to research on technological frames and contested innovation in creative fields.
