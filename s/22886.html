Trapped in the Present: How Temporal Architecture Shapes Meeting Effectiveness (WITHDRAWN)

OB
Sarah Touzani; Nadav Klein (INSEAD)
We introduce temporal architecture—the configuration of past, present, and future language in dialogue—as a construct for understanding meeting dynamics. By analyzing 790 organizational meetings, we identify four temporal archetypes that are associated with substantial differences in outcomes. Future-Oriented meetings show 30% higher positivity and 59% more decision markers than Present-focused meetings. Critically, our typology explains 6.1 percentage points more variance than future-tense alone, demonstrating configuration effects: Past-Reflective meetings show higher positivity than Balanced-Present meetings despite lower future-orientation. These results contest simplistic “focus on the future” advice. Strikingly, only 12% of meetings exhibited Future-Oriented patterns, suggesting strategic dialogue is rare in organizational practice. Meanwhile, 36% follow a Present-focused pattern (high present tense, minimal past/future tense, low decision markers). This effect replicated across dataset expansion (n=204?790). Our observational design limits causal inference; temporal patterns may reflect meeting purpose as much as they shape outcomes.

Delay No More: Managing Temporal Diversity in Teams Through Temporal Attribution and Regulation

OB
Kuok Kei (Eddie) Law (NUCB Business School); Jacky Hong (University of Macau)
Task delay is pervasive in interdependent work, yet its meaning and consequences remain theoretically contested. Drawing on qualitative interviews with 24 undergraduate students working in highly interdependent project teams, this study examines how team members interpret task delay over time and how emergent temporal leadership regulates the resulting coordination and relational challenges. We show that delay is not uniformly perceived and experienced as self-regulatory failure; instead, team members actively distinguish strategic, agentic delay from chronic procrastination using cues such as trust, past performance, and communication behavior. These attributional judgments shape whether temporal differences generate tolerance or escalate into anxiety and conflict. We further identify emergent temporal leadership as a critical regulatory mechanism, enacted through a flexible repertoire that treats time as structure, resource, and relational process. By theorizing delay as an ambiguous social signal and temporal regulation as an ongoing interpretive cycle, this study extends research on procrastination, temporal diversity, and temporal leadership, and offers new insight into how teams sustain coordination and relationships under temporal heterogeneity.

Adaptive Attentional Strategies in Time Management: Task Demands and Temporal Perception

OB
Yuanyuan Claire Wang (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); Nina Xie (Lingnan University); yujun Liu (Lingnan University); Xutang TONG (Lingnan University)
Effective time management is essential for individual success and organizational productivity, promoting growth, well-being, and efficiency. This article develops a process-oriented account of time management by examining how task characteristics, time availability, and temporal perceptions jointly shape attentional strategies and scheduling performance. Two studies with master’s students combine eye-tracking and Eye-Movement Hidden Markov Modelling (EMHMM) with survey measures of motivation and time perspective in simulated HR scheduling tasks. Study 1 manipulates task importance, complexity, urgency, pacing, and available days to show that individuals adopt distinct gaze patterns—ranging from focused, systematic scanning to broad, exploratory search—and that these strategies adapt flexibly to contextual demands and time pressure. Study 2 demonstrates that learning goal orientation and a present-focused time perspective predict more complete, constraint-consistent schedules, whereas other motivational traits play weaker roles. Across studies, findings indicate that time scheduling is a dynamic, context-dependent process in which attentional strategies mediate the effects of both situational features and stable temporal dispositions on performance. The research advances theory by linking resource-allocation, self-regulation, and temporal focus perspectives; methodologically, it illustrates the value of integrating EMHMM with self-reports; and practically, it informs the design of training and digital tools to foster adaptive scheduling in resource-constrained work environments.

Working Faster, Engaging Less: How AI Reconfigures Temporal Engagement and Creative Work

OB
Yuangeng Guo (Tsinghua University); Yibing Zhou (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Fengxian Liu (Tsinghua University); jiahua zhang (Sichuan University); Ning Li (Tsinghua University)
Existing research commonly assumes that people prefer to spend less time and to avoid interruptions. Using a temporal engagement lens, we question this prevailing view and argue that AI constitutes a mixed influence on work by altering when and how individuals engage with tasks, with hidden costs for creativity. Across three studies, we demonstrate that AI use is associated with lower temporal engagement intensity and reduced variability in engagement patterns, yielding a suppression pattern in which AI’s positive direct effect on creativity is counterbalanced by negative indirect effects transmitted through temporal engagement. Study 1 (N = 355), conducted over 14 consecutive days, shows that AI use decreases both the amount and the variability of time devoted to assigned tasks, and that these changes jointly mediate a negative indirect effect on creativity. Study 2 (N = 262) reproduces the attenuation of engagement intensity and instability in a real task context. Study 3 (N = 97), based on open-ended responses, sheds light on the underlying processes: AI lowers the cost of initiating tasks while reallocating task agency, thereby compressing temporal engagement. Taken together, these findings point to overlooked risks of AI for creative work and highlight the importance of actively managing temporal engagement.
