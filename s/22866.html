Complaint Contagion in Social Networks: Moderating Roles of Social Status and Customer Dependence

OB
Wenhua Shi; Laien Huang (Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications); Yongwei Hu; Nan Jia
In an era of ubiquitous social connectivity, customer behavior is increasingly shaped by social influence, yet the social contagion of customer complaint behavior remains underexplored. Drawing on Social Learning Theory, this paper investigates how peer complaint exposure in social networks affects individuals’ complaint behavior, and the moderating roles of social status and customer dependence. Using panel data from 4,579 subscribers of a Chinese telecom company over 13 months (N = 57,320), we construct 13 monthly social networks (40,106 nodes, 145,853 edges) via call interactions and apply Cox proportional hazards models to test hypotheses. Results show complaint exposure boosts individuals’ complaints; social status weakens this effect, while customer dependence strengthens it. These findings enrich customer complaint and social contagion literature, offering telecom companies targeted complaint management and customer operation insights.

Should I Stay or Should I Go? Why Entrepreneurs Leave Business Networking Groups

OB
Julian Held (University of Neuchatel); Claudia D. Jonczyk (University of Neuchâtel); Emmanuelle Fauchart
Despite the importance of entrepreneurial networking groups in resource access, business referrals, and venture growth, high member turnover remains prevalent — yet prior research has largely neglected behavioral and subjective drivers of turnover and consequences for remaining group members. Drawing on research on networking styles and social capital, we study entrepreneurs’ agentic networking behaviors and subjective evaluations shaping turnover dynamics and group-level outcomes in business networks. Using a 12-year dataset (19,162 member-year observations) from Swiss entrepreneurs in one of the world’s largest networking organizations, we apply mixed-effects models to examine behavioral predictors of turnover. Results show network deepening strongly reducing turnover odds, whereas network broadening increases them over time, demonstrating divergent consequences of networking styles. Unexpectedly, networking-related trainings emerge as one of the strongest protective behaviors, highlighting networking as a learnable skill. Group changes within the organization elevate turnover risk, while external orientation shows no effect. Turnover also significantly harms group-level outcomes. Qualitatively, a Gioia analysis of subjective narratives identifies three aggregated dimensions: cost-benefit assessment, social capital and social liability, and contextual fit. We contribute to the entrepreneurial networks literature by linking individual networking behaviors to turnover dynamics and group-level implications, while integrating behavioral and subjective drivers often studied in isolation.

Newcomers' Proactive Networking in Digital and Face-to-Face Entrepreneurial Networks

OB
Julian Held (University of Neuchatel); Claudia D. Jonczyk (University of Neuchâtel); Emmanuelle Fauchart
Although technology-mediated entrepreneurial activities are increasingly common, our understanding remains limited regarding how digital settings influence socialization processes, signaling mechanisms, and tie development within entrepreneurial networks. Adopting a behavioral approach from network research and integrating signaling theory with proactive socialization models, this study investigates the moderating effect of digital environments on newcomers’ proactive networking behaviors, emergence of advice ties, and peer reputation gains in formal networking groups. We analyze three-year longitudinal data comprising 1,767 person-month observations from 151 newcomers in Swiss entrepreneurial networking groups, employing multilevel mixed-effects modeling. We find proactive networking to enable newcomers to establish advice relationships and enhance their reputation. However, these proactive efforts prove significantly more successful in fostering advice ties within online groups than in face-to-face ones, suggesting a social compensation effect in virtual networking contexts. We contribute by applying an instrumental, signaling-oriented framework to proactive socialization processes; deliver the first longitudinal study of proximity effects (online versus in-person) in organized networks; and challenge prevailing assumptions by showing that digital environments can, counterintuitively, amplify specific socialization outcomes in non-employment settings. Practically, our insights provide recommendations for entrepreneurial networking groups and their facilitators navigating digital transitions.

How Collaboration Networks Shape the Value of Knowledge Diversity for Collective Intelligence

OB
Xiaoyun (Aarn) Cao (Illinois Institute of Technology); Yu Zhang (Tsinghua University); Jar-Der Luo (Tsinghua University)
Collective intelligence depends on groups’ ability to integrate dispersed knowledge, yet research offers mixed evidence on whether knowledge diversity enhances collective outcomes. We propose that this inconsistency reflects a neglected structural condition: whether collaboration networks form an integrated system capable of mobilizing heterogeneous knowledge. We introduce structural integration, defined as the proportion of members embedded in the largest connected component of a collaboration network, as a system-level property that conditions the value of knowledge diversity. Using a four-year unbalanced panel comprising 329 department-year observations drawn from 22,048 employees, we find that structural integration is positively associated with collective intelligence. Knowledge diversity, measured as cognitive variety and balance, exhibits no unconditional benefit and is negatively associated with collective intelligence when collaboration networks are fragmented. As collaboration networks become more structurally integrated, the negative association between knowledge diversity and collective intelligence is attenuated. These findings suggest that diversity represents latent cognitive potential whose realization depends on whether collaboration systems provide unit-wide reachability. By shifting attention from local network mechanisms to system-level connectivity, this study advances research on collective intelligence, organizational networks, and knowledge diversity, highlighting connectivity as the infrastructure that allows collective capability to emerge.

Staying Stable Without Getting Stale: Network Stability and Diverse Information Input for Creativity

OB
Takehiko Murai (Waseda University); Koji Suzuki; Toshio Murase
This study examines how relational stability shapes the informational environments that precede creativity. While stable networks foster coordination efficiency, they also cause rigidity and lock-in, yielding conflicting prior findings. To address this issue, we foreground individuals’ exposure to heterogeneous and nonredundant information—an informational advantage that network research has implicitly assumed as central—and measure it directly using daily chat interactions. Leveraging rich communication logs, we examine the consequences of relational stability and identify the boundary conditions under which it shapes informational environments. Our analyses show that the effects of relational stability are not uniform on average but vary systematically with knowledge stability, defined as the extent to which the knowledge elements exchanged between individuals and their interaction partners remain stable over time. Specifically, relational stability increases informational heterogeneity when knowledge stability is low, but decreases heterogeneity when the knowledge stability is high, revealing a reversal pattern. Thus, these findings indicate that the consequences of relational stability depend not on the persistence of social ties, but on whether the knowledge connected through those ties is updated, thereby reconciling conflicting prior findings as conditional effects.
