A Cold Body is Hot Stuff : The Legacy of Entrepreneurs at the Collective Level of Society

ENT
Andreas Rauch (Audencia Business School); Willem Hulsink; Matthias Fink
The concept of legacy allows us to explore an entrepreneur’s past and look beyond the time of their death. We develop a specific perspective on legacy by exploring how entrepreneurs are collectively remembered after they die. Building on obituaries published in newspapers and theories on legacy, we suggest a typology of five different archetypes of legacies ascribed to entrepreneurs: Partisan Warriors, Emperors, Trailblazers, Networkers, and Saviors. Our typology indicates that entrepreneurs are seen in particular ways: as bequeathing useful resources and positive values, but embodying destructive elements too. We discuss the implications of this new collective level of entrepreneurial legacy.

Beyond Entrepreneurial Resilience: Sustaining Local Ecosystems

ENT
Anderson de Souza Sant'Anna (FGV EAESP); Vania Maria Jorge Nassif; Anderson Diogo Mendes da Silva (Nove de Julho University); Edmilson Lima (Ninth of July University)
This article examines how entrepreneurs contribute to the continuity and adaptation of local ecosystems under conditions of persistent structural adversity. Addressing gaps in the literatures on entrepreneurial resilience, entrepreneurial ecosystems, and horizontal leadership, the study moves beyond approaches focused on individual firm survival, episodic coordination of collective action, or relatively stable institutional structures. The research adopts a qualitative, interpretive, and process-oriented design, based on a comparative multiple-case study of two structurally contrasting Brazilian entrepreneurial ecosystems: the restaurant sector in the city of Guarulhos (São Paulo), characterized by high economic volatility, institutional fragmentation, and necessity-driven entrepreneurship; and the tourism and creative economy ecosystem of Tiradentes (Minas Gerais), marked by strong symbolic density, economic reconversion, and identity-based disputes. The analysis shows that although practices of individual resilience and local coordination are present in both contexts, their ecosystem-level effects differ qualitatively. In Guarulhos, resilience remains largely confined to business-oriented survival, resulting in fragile ecosystem continuity. In contrast, the Tiradentes case reveals practices that go beyond coordination, operating at the level of relational and symbolic mediation of the territory. Based on these findings, the article advances an analytical distinction between coordination and sustenance (environment holding) and introduces the concept of holdership as an analytical lens to capture entrepreneurial practices oriented toward the sustained production of ecosystem continuity under conditions of chronic instability. The study contributes to theory by extending the concept of entrepreneurial resilience, critically refining relational and distributed leadership approaches, and opening new avenues for research and public policies focused on local development.

Entrepreneurial Resilience and Legacy Among Excluded Daughters in Family Firms.

ENT
Felix Akinwumi Orole (The University of Queensland); Bernard Joseph McKenna (The University of Queensland); Modupe Ogunleye (Australian Catholic University); Noor Ul Hadi (Prince Mohammad Bin Fahd University); Emmanuel Mogaji (Keele University); Hormoz Ahmadi (La Trobe University)
This study introduces the Contextual Entrepreneurial Resilience and Legacy (CERL) Model to explain how gendered exclusion from succession in patriarchal family firms catalyzes innovative entrepreneurial behavior among sidelined daughters. Drawing on qualitative insights from Nigerian female founder-CEOs, the paper reconceptualizes resilience not merely as a coping trait but as a culturally embedded, strategic response to systemic marginalization. These women develop entrepreneurial identities and leverage social capital to create independent ventures that extend, reinterpret, and sometimes surpass traditional family legacies. The concept of dual legacy logic is introduced to capture how excluded daughters simultaneously preserve emotional ties to family heritage while pursuing autonomy and innovation. CERL contributes to entrepreneurship and family business literature by offering a gender-based, non-Western lens on resilience, succession, and legacy. The theory has broader implications for inclusive succession planning and highlights the potential of adaptive strategies in contexts marked by institutional voids.

More Than Just Money: Single Family Offices as Guardians of the Family Legacy (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Isabella Listl (University of the Bundeswehr Munich); Elisabeth F. Mueller (University of the Bundeswehr Munich)
Single Family Offices (SFOs) act as guardians of business family legacies, integrating financial and non-financial assets into investment strategies. Through a comparative multiple-case study of nine German SFOs, we identify three legacy archetypes - Balancers, Pioneers, and Renewers - each employing distinct mechanisms to embody the family legacy within their governance and investment approaches. Our findings also reveal that sender-receiver dynamics between generations systematically moderate how legacy is integrated, interpreted, and translated into investment strategies. By extending the concept of legacy beyond traditional family business contexts and demonstrating how generational negotiations shape strategic decisions, this study contributes to family business literature and emphasizes the strategic significance of legacy in wealth management and investment decision-making within SFOs.

When Giving Becomes Legacy: Philanthropy and the Continuity of Business Families

ENT
Rosane Dal Magro (University of Montreal); Isabel C. Botero (University of Louisville); Luis Felipe Cisneros (HEC Montréal)
A relevant part of achieving continuity for business families is being able to transfer the family legacy across generations. So far, we have a limited understanding of how business families transfer their legacy to the next generation, and how to make this process meaningful. Taking a qualitative view, this study explores how business families utilize philanthropic actions in the transfer of family legacy and how this process is meaningful to them. Building on interviews from five business families, we provide a framework that explains how families imprint and develop a shared understanding of family legacy that helps continuity. In this process, philanthropy enables business families to connect with one another and have a space to develop a shared understanding of their family legacy. This shared legacy is co-constructed between members of different generations, which helps to make it relevant to each generation of the family and helps to promote continuity. We discuss the implications of these findings for future theorizing about family legacy.
