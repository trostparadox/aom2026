Legitimacy in the Mind’s Eye: Implicit Prototypes, Altruistic Motives, and Leader Anger

MOC
Yurong Liu (Hunan University)
While anger is often viewed as a destructive workplace emotion, it can also serve as a potent signal for upholding moral norms. To unravel this paradox, we integrate implicit leadership emotion theory with the dual-strategies framework of social hierarchy to examine how and when leader anger acquires moral legitimacy. We propose that leader anger expression simultaneously activates two competing cognitive pathways—perceived prestige and perceived dominance—which exert opposing effects on observers’ moral legitimacy judgments. We further theorize that attributed altruistic motivation (AAM) determines which pathway predominates, and that observers’ implicit moral leader anger prototypes (IMLAP) moderate this contingency. Across a three-wave field survey (Study 1, N = 532) and a scenario-based experiment (Study 2, N = 400), we find that leader anger positively predicts moral legitimacy via prestige but negatively via dominance. A three-way interaction reveals that the prestige pathway is optimally activated—and the dominance pathway suppressed—only when observers attribute anger to altruistic motives and hold strong implicit prototypes that validate anger as morally appropriate. These findings challenge the prevailing view of anger as inherently illegitimate, demonstrating that moral legitimacy is constructed through observers’ matching of perceived motives to cognitive schemas.

Theorizing Leadership from the Margins: A Multilevel Theory of Cognitive Resonance

MOC
Caleb Phillips (The London School of Economics & Political Science)
Organizations increasingly recognize that their employees think, work, and communicate in fundamentally different ways, yet extant theories of leadership often cannot explain why the same behaviors elevate one follower and undermine another. This leaves open the opportunity for more explanatory and coherent frameworks of leadership. Recognizing natural diversity in human cognition, I develop a theory of cognitive resonance. At its core is cognitive orientation alignment, whereby a leader aligns to their follower’s natural ways of thinking, working, and communicating, amplifying follower cognition when aligned and dampening it when misaligned. At the team level, harmonic resonance explains how leaders and diverse teams can achieve amplification without perfectly matching their cognitive orientations. Applying this mechanism to ten major leadership styles, I demonstrate how cognitive orientation alignment enhances their explanatory power. This multilevel theory, developed from neurodivergent perspectives yet highlighting a broader phenomenon, addresses critiques of theory fragmentation and provides practical applications for inclusive leadership that amplifies naturally diverse human cognition.

How Employees Compensate for Unethical Pro-Family Behavior: A Moral Cleansing Perspective

MOC
Wei Li (East China Normal University); Zhenyuan Wang (East China Normal University)
This research examines a morally compensatory consequence of unethical pro-family behavior, whereby employees engage in organizational citizenship behavior toward organizations to restore their moral self-image. Grounded in moral cleansing theory, we propose a parallel-path mediation model in which unethical pro-family behavior increases moral costs through an affective route (felt guilt) and a cognitive route (perceived loss of moral credits), thereby promoting organizational citizenship behavior toward organizations. Drawing on a social constructionist lens, we further argue that family-supportive supervisor behavior strengthens these moral costs by making normative boundaries more salient. Across a scenario-based experiment (Study 1) and a time-lagged field survey (Study 2), results support the proposed moderated mediation: unethical pro-family behavior indirectly increases organizational citizenship behavior toward organizations via guilt and moral credit loss, and these indirect effects are stronger under high family-supportive supervisor behavior. This study reframes the consequences of unethical pro-family behavior through moral agency and highlights how supervisory support shapes post-transgression self-regulation. The findings also highlight important implications for managing employees’ post-transgression behaviors in organizations.

Status Mismatch: Consequences of Holding Higher or Lower Status Than One Desires

MOC
Ji Sok Choi (Cornell University); Angus Hildreth (Cornell University)
Research on the consequences of social status has primarily focused on two perspectives: an interpersonal perspective examining how differences in a person’s status relative to others influences their behavior, and an intrapersonal perspective examining how a person’s perception of their absolute status or their desire for status impacts their behavior. In the current research, we considered an alternative intrapersonal perspective – namely, the mismatch between actual and desired status – and examined its effect on status-striving behaviors (OCB and CWB). Results reveal a nonlinear pattern: actual–desired status mismatch exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with OCB, such that OCB increases when individuals’ actual status falls short of their desired status but decreases when actual status exceeds desired status. In contrast, the mismatch shows a U-shaped relationship with CWB, such that CWB decreases when actual status is lower than desired but increases when actual status is higher than desired. These findings highlight the importance of considering discrepancies between actual and desired status for understanding status-driven behavior at work.

TMT Paradox Mindset in Practice: Advice-Seeking and Reflexivity as Moderator in Coopetition Strategy

MOC
Sicheng Luo (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Erica Wen Chen (Renmin University of China); Roger Chou; Duoran Liu (Renmin University of China)
Coopetition embodies tensions whose implications hinge on the cognitive frames and team processes of top management. This research theorizes how a top management team’s (TMT) paradox mindset, defined as the shared acceptance of and energy toward tensions, shapes firm coopetition engagement, and under what conditions this relationship is reconfigured. Integrating paradox theory and behavioral strategy research, we argue that paradox mindset increases engagement by reframing its dualities and contradictions as manageable through integrative designs. We further propose asymmetric boundary conditions: TMT external advice-seeking strengthens the positive association by injecting novel schemas, independent evaluation, and legitimacy, whereas internal advice-seeking does not strengthen the association due to politicization and discounting. TMT reflexivity serves as a negative moderator by increasing hazard salience, privileging internal consistency, and elongating pre-commitment deliberation. Results based on data from a three-wave survey of 398 executives across 132 firms support our proposed model. The present research extends the microfoundation of coopetition by tracing heterogeneous engagement to TMT cognitions and processes, refines advice-seeking by demonstrating source effects on paradox enactment, and nuances team reflexivity by identifying a contingent frontier. Practically, fostering paradox mindset, building external advisory channels, and calibrating reflexive routines enable leaders to harness the paradox of competing and cooperating.
