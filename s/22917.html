Recognizable by Design? Style Recognizability in the High Fashion Industry

OMT
Teresa Liberatore (University of Amsterdam); Monika Kackovic (University of Amsterdam); Paul Groth (University of Amsterdam); Nachoem Wijnberg (University of Amsterdam)
A recognizable style is widely assumed to be central to a fashion house or a creative director’s identity and cultural legitimacy. Yet we know surprisingly little about whether “style” is actually recognizable in products, especially over time, and, if it is, whether this recognizability resides primarily in organizations’ enduring house codes, in designers’ personal signatures, or in the interaction between the two. We address this gap by taking a longitudinal approach to measuring style recognizability based solely on the visual features of the products. We develop a novel computer-vision approach and measure style recognizability using machine learning, specifically temporally constrained attribution models. We apply our method to 123,299 runway images from 11,209 collections spanning 1992–2025. Estimating fixed-effects panel regressions, we show that organizational style recognizability is strongest when identity is structurally aligned, as it increases when the creative director is also the founder of the fashion house and decreases under dual creative leadership. Additionally, we find that creative director transitions can be disruptive, but only conditionally so, as declines in style recognizability are amplified in couture, but are largest when couture houses appoint prestigious creative directors. At the individual level, prestigious creative directors’ stylistic signatures are more portable—that is, more consistently identifiable across organizational moves—although this portability is dampened in couture contexts. Together, our findings demonstrate that style recognizability is both empirically measurable and systematically shaped by the interaction between creative leadership, organizational heritage, and both individual and organizational prestige.

Interpreting Disruptive Futures: Organizational Identity and Projective Sensemaking

OMT
Kyeyoung Shin (University of Oxford); Kamila Nigmatulina (University of Oxford)
High-legitimacy incumbent organizations are often seen as risk-averse guardians of the institutional status quo. However, when facing disruptive change, different incumbents maintain, cautiously adapt, or innovate. We address why organizations facing the same threat diverge so sharply through a field-level, inductive study of central banks’ responses to cryptocurrencies. Drawing on 90 interviews, field observations, and extensive archival materials across jurisdictions, we trace how central banks interpreted the same disruption yet enacted markedly different institutional responses. We find that organizational identity filters how organizations make sense of legitimacy threats. Specifically, two dimensions of identity—perceived purpose (unidimensional vs. multidimensional) and stakeholder orientation (internal vs. external)—condition how disruption is framed and which institutional pathways are deemed legitimate. For central banks with narrow, inward-facing identities, cryptocurrencies were interpreted as existential dangers, leading to institutional maintenance through discrediting discourses, outlawing use, and policing boundaries. Those with mixed identities treated cryptocurrencies as experiments, engaging in institutional adaptation through experimenting in controlled environments, collaborating in bounded partnerships, and framing initiatives as provisional and reversible. By contrast, central banks with multidimensional, outward-facing identities treated disruption as an opportunity and drove institutional innovation by expanding mandates and roles, shaping standards and norms through global pilots, and building foundational infrastructures. We link institutional, identity, and sensemaking perspectives, highlighting how legitimacy threats are internalized as identity threats and explaining intra-field divergence among high-legitimacy incumbents.

Beyond Sequential Adaptation: Temporal Identity Management Across Disruptions

OMT
Ariel (Ari) De Fauconberg (North Carolina State University)
Organizations face increasing challenges in maintaining consistent identities during compounding industry disruptions, yet existing theory examines identity management primarily in response to sequential stakeholder demands. How, then, do organizations manage their identity when multiple, qualitatively different exogenous shocks create accumulating and interacting demands on the organization? Through a multi-case comparative analysis of six major energy companies during four distinct industry disruptions between 2014-2024 – i.e., market (2014 oil price crash), regulatory (2015 Paris Agreement), operational (2020 Covid-19 pandemic), and geopolitical (2022 Ukraine invasion) – this study identifies four temporal structuring mechanisms and three distinct patterns of identity management that organizations employ to maintain both internal coherence and external legitimacy. These findings advance organizational identity theory by demonstrating how temporal structuring enables organizations to control the pacing of identity claims relative to substantive internal identity changes, thereby managing compounding stakeholder demands without compromising organizational identity coherence. This study further reveals how organizations' engagement with these temporal structuring patterns contributes to reshaping field-level understandings of legitimate organizational identity within industry categories.

Lost in Translation: Language Reform and Identity Penalty in Vatican II

OMT
Keun Woo Jeong (University of Pennsylvania); Donal Crilly (London Business School)
In a linguistically diverse world, organizations often adopt linguistic pluralism to enhance legitimacy and competitiveness. Prevailing perspectives, the communication view and the inclusion view, predict that accommodating diverse languages should improve localization. We test this conjecture in the context of the Catholic Church’s Second Vatican Council, which permitted vernacular languages to replace Latin in the liturgy. Using survey data, we examine whether the reform achieved its intended effect in countries whose spoken languages were most distant from Latin, precisely those the reform aimed to strengthen. Contrary to expectation, we find that these countries experienced sharper declines in Catholic adherence. To explain this anomaly, we complement our quantitative endeavor with qualitative evidence, which suggests a distinctiveness view: linguistic accommodation conflicted with the Church’s identity, historically defined in contrast to Protestantism, a growing rival at the time. By seeking inclusivity, the Church may have inadvertently weakened a source of distinctiveness. Our findings broaden understanding of how language policy can entail complex trade-offs, with implications for organizations operating in culturally and linguistically diverse environments.

Reworking the Categorical Self: How Scaleups Navigate Identity Misalignment Amidst Category Nesting

OMT
Deepti Mishra (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore); Anirvan Pant (Indian Institute of Management Calcutta)
Scholarly work on young ventures has recently started paying attention to the 'scaleup', a venture that has validated its business model and has entered a developmental phase of high growth. In contrast to a startup, a scaleup is likelier to possess an elaborate conception of its organizational identity, or a thoughtful agreement on 'who are we as an organization.' Typically, a scaleup's organizational identity would be aligned with its category identity, i.e., the top-down identity bestowed on the scaleup by virtue of being classified into a specific market category. However, dynamic processes of categorization and recategorization may thrust the scaleup into a context of category nesting where the legacy category of its affiliation gets absorbed into a rising umbrella category. This leads to an identity misalignment between the scaleup's espoused organizational identity and its newly ascribed category identity. In this paper, we examine how scaleups respond to an emergent misalignment between their organizational identity and their category identity in a context of category nesting. We use qualitative procedures to study this question at a 'unicorn' in the global edtech market category. Our findings reveal that the venture's response to identity misalignment was grounded in four intertwined mechanisms of organizational identity work: Acceptance, Calibration, Compartmentalization, and Re-anchoring. Viewing the conduct of organizational identity work as an entrepreneurial process, we report a rare study of the identity consequences of category nesting for a young, but rapidly growing, global venture.
