Algorithms and Autonomy: Variation in Worker Response to Roboticization

OMT
Joshua Hurwitz (University of Liverpool)
Algorithmic technologies increasingly reorganize work, yet we know little about how workers experience and collectively respond to algorithmic change as a disruption to meaning, identity, and purpose at work. Drawing on an abductive ethnographic study of technicians in a hospital pharmacy undergoing automation, this paper examines how the introduction of algorithmic systems reconfigured organizational objectives, evaluative criteria, and everyday work practices. I show how these changes unsettled established understandings of what constituted valuable work and professional contribution, prompting divergent and socially patterned responses among technicians. While some workers embraced a newly emphasized organizational objective—the elimination of delays—deriving enhanced status and a renewed sense of purpose, others experienced the algorithmic reorganization of work as intensifying cognitive, physical, and interactional demands, and engaged in practices aimed at mitigating its disruptive effects. These responses were not merely individual but became embedded in collective narratives and local practices through which workers negotiated the meaning of algorithmic work. I argue that willingness to reconstruct meaning in an algorithmically mediated workplace depends on workers’ organizational and professional commitments, as well as on how algorithmic objectives are made sense of and enacted in practice. The study contributes to research on algorithmic organizing by centering the lived, emotional, and relational consequences of algorithmic change and by showing how meaning and identity are reworked through collective response.

When Does Gender Trump Flexibility? Workplace Flexibility and Domestic Task Assignment

OMT
Minjin Chae (Harvard University)
Organizations increasingly adopt Flexible Work Arrangements (FWAs) under the assumption that these policies will mitigate work-family conflict, particularly for female employees who traditionally bear a disproportionate domestic burden. However, formal policies often decouple from actual practices due to persistent social norms. This study examines the boundary conditions under which organizational flexibility policies can alter the existing division of labor, specifically testing whether the practical logic of availability can override the gendered logic of suitability. Drawing on an original survey experiment with 1,200 U.S. adults, I assess how workplace flexibility policies shape expectations about who should perform household and childcare tasks within different-gender couples. The results demonstrate a striking gender asymmetry. When both partners have flexibility, respondents continue to assign female-typed tasks to women and male-typed tasks to men. By contrast, when only the male partner has flexibility, respondents expect him to assume substantially greater responsibility for female-typed tasks. However, male-typed tasks remain predominantly assigned to men even when only the female partner has flexibility, suggesting greater rigidity surrounding masculine identities. These findings underscore that social norms regarding gender suitability moderate the effectiveness of organizational interventions. While flexibility can modestly reconfigure expectations by altering perceptions of practical availability, this effect depends on which partner possesses the flexibility, highlighting the limits of gender-neutral policies in the face of rigid gender boundaries.

New Employees in Remote Work: Evidence from a Field Experiment

OMT
Stephanie Chan-Ahuja (Rutgers University)
Despite a growing body of research on remote work, little attention has been paid to how it affects new employees. In this paper, I study how and why working in a remote location impacts productivity and retention at the early stages of organizational entry. To advance this inquiry, I founded a technology company to conduct a field experiment where I randomized 235 new employees to work in-office or remotely. I find that employees working in an office location produced 51% more in work quantity and stated a 10% higher reservation wage, indicative of lower retention, than employees working in a remote location. In exploring the drivers of these effects, I find evidence that productivity and retention operate through separate mechanisms. Office employees exhibited higher work effort, which accounted for their significant productivity gains. Moreover, they displayed lower job satisfaction, which explained their higher reservation wages. Finally, I do not find evidence that the documented mechanisms from socialization theory—task mastery, social integration, and role clarity—explain productivity and retention outcomes. My results imply that organizations face a tradeoff in remote work because new employees exert less effort when working remotely despite being cheaper to retain.

When Does Hybrid Work Work? Physical And Social Distance And Organizational Identity

OMT
Alessandra Migliore (Politecnico di Milano); Guang Zhu (Politecnico di Milano)
Prior research has largely conceptualized hybrid work as an organizational policy, emphasizing its benefits for performance. From an organizational identity perspective, we conceptualize hybrid work as an identity suspender that diminishes shared identity beliefs among organizational members. Since organizational output is contingent upon the activation of such shared identity beliefs, we contend that hybrid work leads to a reduction in organizational output compared to traditional work arrangements. Furthermore, we theorize that increased physical distance within the office diminishes the negative impact of hybrid work on outputs, as organizational members’ shared identity becomes less dependent on physical proximity. Additionally, heightened social distance (i.e., segregation) amplifies the negative effect of hybrid work on outputs, as the already fragile organizational identity cues shaped by demographic segregation are further disrupted by the suspension of identity associated with hybrid work. We test our theory through a unique dataset that combines quantitative and qualitative data on an organization shifting from 5-day office work to hybrid work. Our theory finds empirical support. This study contributes to the literature on hybrid work and performance by reinterpreting hybrid work as an identity-centered organizational transformation and by emphasizing its implications for performance due to two essential boundary conditions: physical and social distance.

The Limits of Organization Under Connective Autonomy: Free Riding, Freedom Riding and Overemployment

OMT
Jaime Windeler; Shaila Miranda (University of Arkansas); Susan Scott (Imperial College London)
Overemployment is the covert practice of holding and performing multiple full-time permanent jobs simultaneously. Drawing on multimethod thematic analysis of large-scale social media data and narrative interviews, we examine how subversion of remote work by overemployed practitioners challenges foundational organizing principles of identification and coordination of work. While prior research on remote work has focused on employer-managed work arrangements, we foreground worker-led practices that operate outside formal organizational boundaries and exhibit patterns of alternative forms of coordination and norm-setting. Applying and extending theories of Collective and Connective Action, we conceptualize overemployment as a site of connective autonomy, a self-directed mode of digitally-enabled engagement that fuses Bennett and Segerberg’s (2012) logic of connective action with new forms of organizational boundary redefinition and labor redistribution. In their enactment of connective autonomy, overemployed practitioners simultaneously appear as free-riders—extracting value without contributing to organizational public goods—and freedom-riders—reclaiming autonomy amid precarity, surveillance, and eroded psychological contracts. We show how through overemployment, workers reconfigure traditional and contemporary organizing principles, not by dismantling them, but by repurposing them toward individual goals. In doing so, overemployment surfaces latent tensions in the future of work—between autonomy and obligation, efficiency and engagement, control and resistance. Our findings contribute to scholarship on organizational studies by theorizing how covert, subversive practices paradoxically both depend on and destabilize conventional organizational forms. We offer connective autonomy as a lens for understanding emerging forms of distributed, worker-driven organizing in an era of digital precarity and shifting labor norms.
