Yeah, It’s Biased, But… Recognition vs. Moral Condemnation of Bias Across Party Lines

MOC
Hannah Benner Waldfogel (Columbia Business School); Brittany Solomon (University of Notre Dame); Matthew Hall (University of Notre Dame)
Common use and defense of derogatory rhetoric amid political polarization raises questions about whether and how people recognize and respond to bias across party lines. Prior research documents gaps in bias recognition and assumes that recognition prompts condemnation. And moral psychology suggests that partisan differences in harm appraisals influence condemnation, implying, but not testing, that recognizing bias prompts moral evaluations. While integrating these accounts, we challenge their assumptions and propose that, even when partisans similarly acknowledge bias (“yeah, it’s biased”), they diverge in moral response (“but I don’t care”), depending on the target’s advantaged or disadvantaged status. In six pre-studies (N = 1,197) and eight preregistered experiments (N = 6,889), we examined this “yeah, but” phenomenon. Despite perceptual equivalence in race- and gender-based biased speech, Democrats condemned bias against disadvantaged targets more, while Republicans condemned bias against advantaged targets more (Study 1). Two justifications—(in)vulnerability to harm and honesty—explained partisan differences and predicted willingness to sanction bias (Study 2). These patterns extended to biases based on socioeconomic status, religion, and partisanship (Studies 3-5), and to behaviors (Study 6). However, a brief status-juxtaposition intervention (judging bias against both advantaged and disadvantaged groups) reduced within-party asymmetries among Democrats (Studies 7-8) and narrowed between-party gaps (Study 8). Disentangling recognition from condemnation, we show that perceptual agreement does not yield moral agreement, complicating efforts to establish—and apply—shared standards of fairness, and contesting the notion that aligning partisan perceptions bridges moral divides.

Does Political Misalignment Cost CEOs Their Jobs? Evidence from S&P 1500 Firms

MOC
Pablo Doucet; Enrique Acebo (University of León)
Does the political polarization of corporate leadership influence directors' decisions about chief executive officer (CEO) dismissal? Drawing on social identity theory and attribution theory, we argue that (1) political misalignment between the CEO and the board triggers attribution biases that systematically shape how directors interpret firm performance and, consequently, the likelihood of CEO dismissal; and (2) political polarization among independent directors reduces the board's ability to translate poor performance into unified corrective action in the form of CEO dismissal. We test these predictions using a panel of 1,677 S\&P 1500 firms from 1996 to 2022, employing a donation-based indicator of political ideology (DIPI) to capture CEO--board and intra-board political polarization. Our results show that politically misaligned CEOs are not dismissed at higher rates than their politically aligned counterparts, and that the likelihood of dismissal decreases as the board becomes more politically polarized. These findings extend the socio-psychological perspective on board decision-making by clarifying how political polarization shapes dismissal decisions at the apex of corporate leadership.

Who Comes to Mind? How Network Proximity Drives Recall Bias in Peer Nominations

MOC
Hui Sun (Frankfurt School of Finance and Management); Lisheng He
Social evaluation is integral to organizational life and being nominated for leadership roles or professional awards can significantly advance one’s career. Yet peer recognition often disadvantages those who would benefit most. To address these disparities, many organizations have introduced diversity guidelines into nomination processes, most commonly by diversifying the pool of nominators. The underlying assumption is that demographic diversity among evaluators will counteract in-group favoritism and yield more diverse nominees. Empirical support for this practice, however, has been mixed. We theorize and demonstrate through survey experiments that evaluation bias accounts for only a small share of nomination bias. A much larger share arises from recall bias: the unequal likelihood that certain candidates come to mind during nomination. Unlike evaluation bias, recall bias is rooted in network proximity and is far less responsive to changes in nomination tasks. These findings challenge prevailing diversity guidelines on nomination procedures. They suggest that effective efforts should focus less on diversifying nominators per se and more on diversifying their networks and implementing interventions that encourage nominators to consider beyond their close networks.

Algorithmic Advice as a Strategic Signal on Competitive Markets

MOC
Tobias R. Rebholz (Duke University); Maxwell Uphoff; Christian H. R. Bernges (University of Tübingen); Florian Scholten (University of Tübingen)
As algorithms increasingly mediate competitive decision-making, their influence extends beyond individual outcomes to shaping strategic market dynamics. In two preregistered experiments, we examined how algorithmic advice affects human behavior in classic economic games with unique, non-collusive, and analytically traceable equilibria. In Experiment 1 (N = 107), participants played a Bertrand price competition with individualized or collective algorithmic recommendations. Initially, collusively upward-biased advice increased prices, particularly when individualized, but prices gradually converged toward equilibrium over the course of the experiment. However, participants avoided setting prices above the algorithm’s recommendation throughout the experiment, suggesting that advice served as a soft upper bound for acceptable prices. In Experiment 2 (N = 129), participants played a Cournot quantity competition with equilibrium-aligned or strategically biased algorithmic recommendations. Here, individualized equilibrium advice supported stable convergence, whereas collusively downward-biased advice led to sustained underproduction and supracompetitive profits—hallmarks of tacit collusion. In both experiments, participants responded more strongly and consistently to individualized advice than collective advice, potentially due to greater perceived ownership of the former. These findings demonstrate that algorithmic advice can function as a strategic signal, shaping coordination even without explicit communication. The results echo real-world concerns about algorithmic collusion and underscore the need for careful design and oversight of algorithmic decision-support systems in competitive environments.

Why Heuristics Work; Financial Analysts and the Strong Structuration of Fast-and-Frugal Rules

MOC
Aileen Kennedy (Maynooth University); Joseph Coughlan (Maynooth University); Orla Feeney (Dublin City University); Irene Neville (Technological University Dublin)
This study examines why financial analysts use fast-and-frugal heuristics to make decisions under uncertainty within high pressure work environments. Adopting a sociological perspective our findings deliver novel behavioural and theoretical insights into why heuristic rules are used by individuals which extend well beyond the commonly acknowledged benefit of cognitive efficiency. Adopting a qualitative approach we interviewed 32 financial analysts (buy-side and sell-side) working within the Irish financial services industry. We identify a dichotomy of heuristic rules, operating at the individual and collective levels, and functioning as screening/selection and procedural/decision making rules. Stones’ strong structuration theory is employed as a conceptual framework to analyse identified heuristic episodes. Drawing on a sociologically anchored explanation findings illustrate that financial analysts derive sociologically meaningful benefits from drawing on heuristics as internalised resources for structuration. Findings reveal that these heuristic rules serve functions of signification (meaning and interpretive schemes), legitimation (normative expectations) and domination (power to act) for analysts while also supporting a sense of ontological security. Findings affirm that exploring the strong structuration of fast-and-frugal rules delivers a deep understanding and explanation of the ‘why’ of heuristic behaviour within organisational life.
