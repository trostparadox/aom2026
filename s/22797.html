Bribery Under Uncertainty: When Strategic Motives Legitimize Unethical Practices

SIM
Chomsorn Tangdenchai (Assumption University of Thailand); Asda Chintakananda (National Institute of Development Administration)
Drawing on moral disengagement theory and strategic orientation, this study examines how two strategic motives: profit maximization and market share expansion influence business leaders’ engagement in bribery in emerging markets. We argue that these motives prompt different forms of rationalization, as different individuals who engage in bribery often rely on moral disengagement mechanisms to convince themselves that their actions are acceptable. We further argue that these effects are contingent on perceived bribery arbitrariness, defined as the perceived unpredictability of bribery outcomes. When bribery outcomes are perceived as arbitrary, the strategic logic underlying market share expansion becomes less defensible, weakening its association with moral disengagement. In contrast, profit maximization, which is more closely tied to immediate economic gains, remains less sensitive to such uncertainty. Using survey data from 169 business leaders across regions in Thailand, we find that both strategic motives increase the use of moral disengagement mechanisms. Consistent with our theorizing, perceived bribery arbitrariness attenuates the relationship between market share expansion and moral disengagement but does not affect the relationship involving profit maximization. This study advances research on corruption and strategy by unpacking the cognitive mechanisms linking strategic motives to unethical behavior and identifying important boundary conditions.

From Values to Incentives: How Social and Environmental Orientations Shape Sustainability Performance

SIM
Djordje Zivkovic (University of Liechtenstein); Alexander Zimmermann (University of St. Gallen); Othar Kordsachia (University of Liechtenstein)
Firms face growing pressure to improve sustainability performance, yet substantial variation persists in their social and environmental outcomes. We examine how firms’ sustainability priorities, expressed through social value orientation (SVO) and environmental value orientation (EVO), influence the adoption of domain-specific sustainability incentives and, in turn, shape corresponding performance. Drawing on research on organizational values and sustainability compensation, we argue that SVO and EVO constitute distinct intrinsic priorities that guide firms’ extrinsic incentive design. Using longitudinal data on S&P 500 firms (2016–2023), we find that firms with stronger SVO or EVO are more likely to adopt corresponding incentives. These incentives mediate the relationship between value orientations and domain-specific sustainability performance, while also generating cross-domain spillover effects. Our study advances understanding of how firms’ value orientations translate into governance structures and realized social and environmental outcomes.

Engineering Resilience in Illicit Organizations: Evidence from Transnational Scam Compounds

SIM
Jacob Henry Hoekstra (Baylor University); Luan Corrêa Maia Valério (Baylor University); Yulia Sullivan (Baylor University); Timothy Kayworth (Baylor University); Hope Koch (Baylor University)
Scam compounds across Southeast Asia have rapidly evolved from small ransom operations into capital-intensive, state-protected “scam parks” that have trafficked tens of thousands of workers and forced them to commit industrialized online fraud (UN OHCHR, 2023). Despite extreme environmental volatility and sustained international pressure, these organizations have displayed exceptional resilience and continue to expand operations. We develop a three-stage process model that traces the evolution of scam compounds and shows how they leverage business model innovation to build resilience despite adversity. We show how early ransom-driven schemes gave way to COVID-era fraud “factories,” and eventually to multidivisional scam parks. We find that resilience is produced through diversification, structurally engineered mechanisms enabled by state protection, modularity, and platform-like architectures. Our findings extend resilience theory by illuminating how organizational adaptability unfolds in environments defined by criminality and institutional complicity. Through shifting the unit of analysis from individual compounds to a politically insulated ecosystem, we highlight why traditional enforcement strategies repeatedly fail and why effective policy intervention must target the financial, digital, and institutional infrastructures that sustain the scam ecosystem.

The Impact of Corrupt Environments on Performance in U.S. Firms

SIM
Steven R Lovett; Levent Kutlu (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); Chiquan Guo (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); Yuna Heo (University of Basel)
We investigate whether corrupt environments benefit or harm U.S. businesses. We use stochastic frontier modeling (SFA), and find corrupt environments benefit performance when it is measured by sales, but damage performance when it is measured by profitability. Importantly, we also find firm age strengthens both relationships and explain that corruption is not a “one-off” transaction but rather becomes an insidious relationship in which corrupt officials boost a firm’s growth but diminish its profits. This leads to a concern that short-sighted managers might engage in bribery or other forms of corruption without regard to the long-term consequences of the relationship.

Auditor Characteristics and Corporate Accounting Scandals: Evidence from ASX 200 Firms

SIM
Mehadi Mamun (University of Wollongong); Abdullah Al-Mamun (University of Wollongong)
Motivated by the recent tax scandal involving one of the Big 4 accounting firms in Australia, this paper examines the relationship between corporate accounting scandals and external auditors' characteristics, including audit tenure, opinion, Big 4 vs. non-Big 4, and audit fees. Using data from the top 200 companies listed on the Australian Securities Exchange (ASX) from 2008 to 2023 and fixed-effect regression analysis, the empirical results find a strong and substantial correlation between external auditors’ longer tenure, audit fees and corporate accounting scandals. This finding suggests that auditors may have a greater understanding of a company via longer client relationships, but this familiarity may lead to a loss of independence, a decline in professional scepticism, and an increase in complacency. In contrast, charging higher fees to clients allows auditors to allocate more resources, leading to additional layers of investigation in audit procedures and, in turn, reducing accounting scandals. The environment created by these adverse aspects increases the likelihood that management may manipulate accounting records and cause scandals.
