Exploration from the Core. A Patent Portfolio Perspective on Invention Commercialization and Failure

TIM
Gabriel Franceschini (University of Bologna); Marco Corsino; Paola Giuri (University of Bologna)
Technological commercialization is often described as the pinnacle of firms’ competitiveness and value creation. However, almost half of patented inventions never reach the market, a pattern consistently observed across empirical studies. Prior research has investigated invention level antecedents of patent uses and non-uses, relying on established indicators and proposing early theoretical explanations. Building on this work, we examine how organizational search strategy relates to invention commercialization and failure. We focus on the invention’s core applications as the main explanatory mechanism, a construct that categorizes patented inventions by how close their business applications are to the focal company’s main activities. We argue that the inventor’s corporate role serves as a contingent factor, as distinct functional orientations shape how this association unfolds within the firm’s explorative search strategy. Our original dataset covers the full patent population of a leading multinational company in the material processing industry, comprising 486 inventions between 2000 and 2021 and enriched with extensive primary and secondary data. The findings reveal that inventions in core applications are negatively associated with commercialization, but positively with failure, and that commercially oriented inventors strengthen both relationships. In doing so, we shed light on the locus of search, suggesting that firms experiment from local domains in which they have previously invested heavily, yet this does not insulate them from the risks and uncertainties inherent in exploration

Risk-Taking Incentives in Compensation and Technology Commercialization

TIM
Aman Asija (Nova School of Business and Economics); Solon Moreira; Clinton Ofoedu (NEOMA Business School)
This study investigates the relationship between technology commercialization strategies and risk-taking incentives in the biopharmaceutical industry. In R&D-intensive sectors, firms face a critical decision: whether to internally develop early-stage technologies or out-license them to external partners. While equity-based compensation is commonly viewed as a mechanism to promote internal innovation through high-risk R&D, our findings suggest a contrasting dynamic: Executives with stronger risk-taking incentives are significantly more likely to out-license early-stage technologies rather than advance them in-house. We analyze a unique panel of 1,061 firm-year observations from publicly traded U.S. biopharmaceutical companies between 1992 and 2014, comprising details of executive compensation and records on licensing deals and clinical pipelines. Our findings are robust to multiple identification strategies, instrumental variables leveraging state wage tax rates, and a difference-in-differences design based on the FAS 123R accounting reform. We also find that financial constraints—not misaligned incentives—drive this pattern as risk-oriented executives, when faced with internal liquidity shortfalls or costly external financing, turn to licensing as a pragmatic means to generate cash flows.

The Post-Incubation Journey:A Process Model of Intraorganizational Absorption of Internal Innovation

TIM
Pragathi Mavinakere Swamygowda (Rotterdam School of Management); Murat Tarakci (IMD - International Institute for Management Development); Vareska Van De Vrande (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
While absorptive capacity theory explains how firms acquire, assimilate and apply external knowledge, it underspecifies how organizations absorb their own, internally generated, ideas after incubation. This gap matters as most corporate incubator ideas fail not at ideation, but at the post-incubation juncture where ideas must cross internal boundaries, secure legitimacy, and become operationally usable. Without theorizing this phase, absorptive capacity remains incomplete as a theory of organizational innovation. We followed 15 incubator-origin idea journeys over spanning between one to seven-months and drew on 51 semi-structured interviews to develop a multilevel process model of how post-incubation absorption happens. Our model identifies six absorptive routines enacted across individual, team, and organizational levels: strategic framing, social embedding, evidence building, governance navigation, integration and momentum management. These routines cycle iteratively as ideas encounter shifting technical, social, and governance constraints. This study contributes to absorptive capacity theory by revealing how internal absorptive routines dynamically convert potential into realized capacity and by clarifying absorption as a continuous, cross level coupling process rather than a one-time adoption outcome. Keywords: absorptive capacity; corporate incubator; post-incubation; internal innovation; process theory; routines; multilevel

When Finance Meets Innovation: Female CFOs and the Productivity of R&D Investment

TIM
Maha Mohamed Tantawy (University of Windsor); Nazha Gali (University of Michigan); ibrahim shaikh (Marist University)
A central challenge in technological innovation is explaining why firms differ in their ability to convert R&D investment into commercially valuable outcomes. While prior research emphasizes technological, human-capital, and governance drivers of R&D productivity, the role of financial leadership and gender in shaping innovation efficiency remains underexplored. Notably, little is known about how a female Chief Financial Officer (CFO)—a functional executive with direct authority over capital allocation—affects the productivity of firms’ R&D investments. We ask: Does having a female CFO improve a firm’s R&D productivity, and under what strategic leadership conditions does this effect vary? Drawing on Upper Echelons Theory and research on gendered leadership behaviors, we argue that female CFOs enhance the revenue productivity of R&D—measured using the Research Quotient (RQ)—by applying more disciplined, risk-calibrated, and stakeholder-oriented investment approaches. We further theorize that this influence depends on the gender composition of the top management team (TMT), as intra-team dynamics shape support for long-horizon innovation decisions. Using panel data on U.S. publicly listed firms, we find that female CFOs are associated with significantly higher R&D productivity, but that this advantage diminishes as TMT gender diversity increases. In contrast, board gender diversity does not moderate the relationship. These findings advance research on innovation management by identifying financial leadership—and its gendered dimensions—as a critical determinant of firms’ ability to translate R&D investments into revenue-generating technological innovation.

Why Is It Still So Difficult? A Multilevel Analysis of Barriers to Commercializing Radical Innovation

TIM
Tim Felix Hahne (Technical University Munich); Georg Windisch
Although widely recognized as being of fundamental importance, practice shows that especially large firms still face significant challenges in developing radical innovations in systematic and reliable ways. Particularly the commercialization phase constitutes substantial difficulties. This contradicts previous research, which has established such commercialization as relatively easy task for large firms due to high levels of resource and network availability. To investigate this phenomenon, we conducted a qualitative case study with our case company being a multinational top-player in the industrial IT sector. In total, we combine data from 14 months of participant observation, 32 semi-structured interviews and a wide variety of archival data. As a result, we propose a model of the three fundamental barriers to commercialization success of radical innovations in large, incumbent companies, and how these barriers interact: First, separation during exploration and incubation is beneficial. Yet, firms struggle with separate commercialization efforts, leaving them with the essential challenge of reintegration into their established business. Second, even if reintegrated, the potential to commercialize radical innovations is limited, if the distribution imbalance of commercialization resources between achieving short-term goals and long-term development of innovations remains unresolved. Finally, too rigid organizational structures prevent the exploration of required new commercialization strategies.
