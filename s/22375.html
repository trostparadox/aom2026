When Identification Becomes Extreme: Task Identification, Resilience, and Vulnerability in Everyday

Monday, Aug 3 2026 · 1:00PM–2:00PM
Sheraton Philadelphia Downtown Hotel — Poster Wall 5
OMT

People

Author: Anika Stephan

Session overview

Organizational behavior research has traditionally treated identification, commitment, and resilience as positive psychological resources that foster engagement, performance, and wellbeing at work. Foundational theories emphasize how identification with organizations and roles provides meaning and motivates sustained effort. Yet a growing body of scholarship challenges this unambiguously affirmative view, showing that strong forms of attachment may also intensify vulnerability, normalize strain, and stabilize problematic organizing arrangements. Building on this critical turn, this paper examines how deep attachment to work can generate forms of extremity within ordinary organizational settings. Rather than locating extremity in rare, episodic, or physically hazardous contexts, we conceptualize extremity as an organizing condition that emerges through the interaction of moral responsibility, affective intensity, and constrained exit options. From this perspective, extremity is not exceptional but continuously produced and reproduced through everyday work practices. To capture this dynamic, we introduce the concept of task identification—a mode of attachment in which individuals come to define who they are primarily through what they do, rather than through organizational membership, formal roles, or occupational categories alone. Drawing on care work and related professional service contexts, we show how task identification binds individuals to morally valorized forms of labor characterized by high responsibility for others’ wellbeing and limited possibilities for disengagement. We argue that resilience and vulnerability are not opposing outcomes of identification but co-constitutive processes. Task identification enables individuals to endure demanding and under-resourced conditions, while this endurance simultaneously functions as an organizational resource that masks systemic problems and delays structural change. By foregrounding identification as an organizing force rather than an individual disposition, this paper reframes extremity as a relational condition embedded in everyday work and advances theory on identification, resilience, and care in organizations.
