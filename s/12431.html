Session overview

Management and organizational science theories routinely utilize multidimensional constructs that operate through both general, overarching mechanisms and dimension-specific pathways. While this recurring dual-nature conceptualization is central to understanding organizational phenomena, dominant measurement approaches, predominantly higher-order factor modeling, perpetuate systematic misalignment between our theoretical propositions and the empirical tests researchers conduct by structurally constricting dimension-specific variance from being appropriately realized. In this symposium, three presentations address this reality and its implications on management and organizational science research from three different perspectives. First, Williams discusses the methodological implications of the field's abundant theories that propose distinct relational pathways for general and dimension-specific variance. Next, Hanna examines the dominant operational practice of modeling multidimensional constructs using higher-order factoring and outlines alternative modeling approaches that offer opportunities to accomplish greater conceptual-operational alignment. Finally, Zhang presents the results of a simulation study that demonstrates how the critical decision between higher-order factor, correlated factor, and bifactor modeling when modeling these constructs materializes in real and often unintended differences in analytical results. Together, the authors provide conceptual and evidence-backed arguments that help researchers understand the important implications of these methodological decisions and how to use this knowledge in their research.
