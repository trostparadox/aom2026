Session overview

Behind every great learning experience is a creative, adaptive educator. This session celebrates and scrutinizes pedagogical innovation in management education: from the relationship between teacher creative agency and entrepreneurial learning environments, to action research as a vehicle for organizational safety culture change, to the ecosystem orchestration that enables whole-student development, to the longitudinal dynamics of resilience in student virtual teams. Anchored by a rigorous evaluation of experiential climate simulation using En-ROADS, these papers offer inspiration and evidence for educators committed to reinventing how management is taught and learned.

Papers (5)

Teacher Creativity and Entrepreneurial Learning Environments in Higher Education

MED
Airi Noppel (Tallinn University of Technology)
This study examines how teachers’ creative agency relates to their support for entrepreneurial learning environments in higher education. Using a quantitative exploratory design with 145 higher education teachers, we assessed creative mindset, creative problem-solving, autonomy support, entrepreneurial learning environment characteristics, and support for entrepreneurial learning. Correlation results indicate that creative agency components are positively associated with entrepreneurial learning environment characteristics and teachers report relatively high levels of creative confidence and value for entrepreneurial learning. These findings suggest that teacher creativity functions as an antecedent mechanism in the enactment of entrepreneurial pedagogy. The study contributes to entrepreneurship education by shifting attention from student-side outcomes to teacher-side enablers and provides implications for teacher development and institutional support structures.

How to Change the Safety Culture: The DEGI Action Research Program

MED
Giovanna Afeltra (San Raffaele Roma University); Andrea Montefusco (Luiss Business School); Marco Zamarian (University of Trento)
This case study explores the implementation of cultural change in a steel plant, focused on enhancing workplace safety across all organizational levels through an action research project. The study is framed within the theoretical constructs of sensemaking and sensegiving, highlighting how employees interpret and internalize safety protocols in a high-risk environment, and how leaders and facilitators shape these interpretations to foster a safety-first culture. Drawing on sensemaking theory, the research examines how workers reconstruct their perceptions of safety, shifting from mere compliance to prioritizing safety as a core value. Simultaneously, sensegiving plays a crucial role in guiding employees through this cultural shift, using narrative-building and direct engagement to embed new safety norms. Using an action research methodology, facilitators engaged workers through workshops and discussions to reframe safety procedures and support change in safety related behaviors. The study illustrates the practical application of sensemaking and sensegiving, demonstrating how these processes drive cultural transformation. Results show a marked improvement in safety behaviors, as evidenced by fewer accidents and increased reporting of safety concerns. This study contributes to the literature by providing empirical insights into sensemaking and sensegiving in industrial settings and offers practical implications for managing cultural change in high-risk industries.

Collective Ecosystem Orchestration for Student Development: A Case Study of a NSERC CREATE program

MED
Taoting Li (University of Calgary); Chad Saunders
This study conceptualizes student development as a collective form of entrepreneurial ecosystem orchestration, in which universities, industry partners, and students jointly shape goals, actions, and interactions. Drawing on the student development literature, we theorize involvement, integration, and engagement as microfoundations that enable collective ecosystem orchestration. Through a case study of an interdisciplinary training program in wearable technology, the research reveals three interdependent orchestration roles operating across levels: program administrators act as institutional orchestrators, designing curricular and governance structures that enable stakeholder involvement; industry partners function as organizational orchestrators, facilitating students’ relational integration into organizational projects and mentoring relationships; students emerge as individual orchestrators, actively managing their behavioral engagement across institutional boundaries. This study contributes to the ecosystem orchestration literature by advancing a multi-level model of collective orchestration and demonstrating how orchestration processes sustain the human-capital pipeline on which both entrepreneurs and ecosystems depend.

Resilience in Virtual Teams as a Dynamic Process

MED
Azusa Ebisuya (Hosei University); Gayan Prasad Hettiarachchi (Tokyo International University); Yijie Lin (Hosei University)
This study examines the dynamic co-evolution of virtual acumen, member affinity, and trust in university student virtual teams over a 10-week collaborative project. Although prior research has typically examined these constructs independently, their joint temporal dynamics remain underexplored. Using weekly longitudinal survey data from 29 virtual teams (122 participants), we identify distinct clusters characterized by different developmental trajectories of these three elements. In particular, resilient teams maintained consistently high levels of virtual acumen, affinity, and trust, recovered quickly from mid-project disruption, and sustained elevated trajectories throughout the project. Other teams exhibited patterns of decline or delayed development. We conceptualize virtual acumen as a critical capability that enables coordination, adaptation, and the routinization of digital collaboration practices, and we link these dynamics to transactive memory system theory to explain how shared coordination structures emerge in virtual contexts. The findings demonstrate that resilience in virtual teams arises from the temporal alignment of technological, relational, and cognitive elements rather than from static antecedents. Methodologically, this study highlights the value of a configurational and process-oriented approach to understanding virtual collaboration dynamics. Practically, the results suggest that early development of virtual collaboration capabilities and relational processes can support sustained engagement in virtual teams.

Experiential Learning for Climate Change Education Using En-ROADS: A Climate Action Simulation

MED
Anjali Chaudhry (Dominican University); Isabel Rodriguez Tejedo; Shankar Ghimire (Florida Southern College)
This study evaluates the effectiveness of an experiential learning intervention for climate change education. Grounded in experiential learning theory, it investigates whether the En-ROADS Climate Action Simulation—an interactive, data-driven software that enables learners to experiment with climate policy tradeoffs—supports cognitive, affective, and behavioral learning outcomes related to climate change. Undergraduate students in the USA and Spain participated in a role-play activity simulating a UN climate negotiation using En-ROADS. Pre- and post-simulation surveys were administered based on Yale Project on Climate Change Communication, measuring several dimensions including: knowledge, communication ability, emotions, personal importance, and willingness to act. Results reveal statistically significant improvements across all dimensions, with the greatest gains in students' self-reported ability to discuss climate change. Cluster analysis to identify student characteristics associated with responsiveness to the simulation revealed a high-response cluster with increased awareness and willingness to act, and a low-response cluster showing limited change. Demographic variables such as age, gender, and race were examined to explain these differences. This study contributes to the growing literature on experiential learning in climate literacy by offering cross-national evidence on the impact of simulation-based pedagogy. It demonstrates the use of the En-ROADS simulation in shaping student attitudes and action toward climate change and highlights the importance of tailoring approaches to diverse learner profiles.
