Cross-Border Acquisitions and Firm Performance: The Overlooked Role of Training Investments

HR
Abubakr Saeed; Kalin D. Kolev (Marquette University); Tanveer Hussain (University of Essex); Shaun Michael Pichler (Shaun Pichler); Omer Farooq Malik (COMSATS University Islamabad)
Cross-border acquisitions involving developed and emerging economies serve as an important source of organizational learning and performance renewal. Yet, how these cross-border learning opportunities are internalized through human capital investment and how such investments influence post-acquisition outcomes remain a theoretically rich but empirically underexplored question. Based on the resource-based view of the firm and, in conjunction, the absorptive capacity literature, we propose that acquisitions between firms from different economic categories (developed vs. emerging economies) have a positive effect on the training investments, which, in turn, positively mediates the effect of cross-border acquisitions on post-acquisition firm performance. We further propose firm-specific contingencies that bound the relationships between cross-border acquisitions, training investments, and firm performance. Specifically, we argue that acquirers with prior experience are less reliant on training investments, and the R&D of the acquired firm plays a complementary role to training investments in generating higher returns from the acquisition. We test these propositions using data on 1759 acquisition deals during the period 2004-2023. Our results provide support for our theoretical arguments. The findings advance the human resource management literature by highlighting the capability-building role of training in leveraging human capital to enhance firm performance in cross-border acquisitions. The study also has practical implications for multinational acquirers aiming to strengthen organizational capabilities and build post-acquisition resilience through targeted training investments.

Who Gets What? Equity Compensation Architectures in S&P 500 Firms

HR
Lennart John Wenzel (Georg August University Göttingen)
This study develops a scalable approach to measuring equity compensation architectures in terms of instrument types and eligibility tiers using SEC filings for S&P 500 firms from 2015 to 2024. The resulting panel enables systematic description of broad-based equity eligibility across large public firms. We document that while some form of broad-based equity eligibility appears for approximately 45 percent of firm-years, direct equity grants to non-executive employees are relatively uncommon (about 12 percent), with most broad-based eligibility occurring through purchase plans. Grant-based broad eligibility is more prevalent among younger, R&D-intensive firms. Exploratory analyses of employee outcomes suggest patterns consistent with firm-level selection rather than within-firm change.

Training and Development Needs Analysis Integrated with SHRM: A Systemic Conceptual Model

HR
Daniela Fernandes Nunes (Universidade do Vale do Itajaí - Univali); Tatiana Ghedine (UNIVALI)
This article proposes a systemic conceptual model for integrating Training and Development Needs Analysis (TDNA), the initial stage of Training and Development (T&D), into Strategic Human Resource Management (SHRM). This integration aims to build Useful Theory in applied disciplines, focusing on the conceptualization and operationalization stages. The proposition is grounded in observations of neglect or misguided application of TDNA in both research and organizational practice, coupled with evidence that its outcomes can extend beyond merely supporting T&D processes, offering significant contributions to the broader SHRM system. Guided by Open Systems Theory, the proposed model frames organizations and SHRM as dynamic systems characterized by inputs, processing, and outputs. It elucidates how TDNA results, encompassing organizational, collective, and individual competencies, can foster congruence and interdependence between these practices and SHRM policies, thereby optimizing processes and ensuring alignment with organizational strategy. The article presents three propositions for future research, advocating for qualitative studies to validate the model's practical applicability and to foster theoretical advancements in the fields of TDNA and SHRM, ultimately impacting the strategic performance of HR professionals.

A Multistage Qualitative Investigation on Misalignment of HRM Practices & Opportunities for Learning

HR
Shivinder Kaur; Amandeep Dhir; Joe Jiang
Investigations on misaligned HRM practices are fetching increased academic attention as they result in unintended organizational and employee work outcomes. Although much is known about ‘what’ causes the misalignment and ‘when’ it occurs, a literary lacuna is observable in ‘how’ misalignment occurs, specifically through adoption of a process perspective. Additionally, the literature does not sufficiently capture how ‘learning’ from misalignment is a probable means to overcome it. This study aims to address these questions by drawing on the lived experiences of 54 HR professionals, captured through a multistage qualitative research design involving four data collection phases, including screening survey conducted between December 2023 and January 2024. Integrating lifecycle theory, and organizational learning perspectives, the study explains that the process of misalignment is triggered through ‘flaws in design, implementation and dynamicity of HRM practice process’ while agency of key actors serves as propagators of misalignment along the process. Further, the study outlines proactive agencies of key actors to extract learnings from such misalignment and change design/implementation through an open systems approach. Based on these findings, a process model that guides HR practitioners in achieving alignment by adopting a lifecycle-informed approach to HR practice design and delivery has been presented.

Work-Hour Restrictions, Pay-for-Performance, and Firm-Level Productivity

HR
Joonyoung Kim (University of Missouri-Columbia); Cyrus Khalvati (University of Missouri-Columbia)
Work-hour restriction policies are increasingly adopted worldwide to improve employee well-being, yet their productivity consequences remain contested. We argue that the firm-level productivity effects of such policies depend critically on how employees adapt when effort duration is externally constrained. Drawing on outcome expectancy theory, we conceptualize work-hour restrictions as a negative productivity shock that mechanically reduces effort duration and makes the preservation of productivity contingent on employees’ willingness to reallocate effort toward intensity—a cognitively, behaviorally, and culturally demanding adjustment. We propose that pay-for-performance (PFP) systems condition firms’ capacity to make this transition by shaping employees’ beliefs about whether intensified effort under time constraints yields meaningful rewards. Using 1,498 firm-year observations and 130,010 employee-year observations between 2015 and 2019, we analyze the impact of South Korea’s 2018 work-hour restriction policy. Consistent with our theory, we find that the policy is associated with a decline in labor productivity. However, this negative effect is attenuated in firms with higher PFP intensity. We further show that this buffering effect is stronger in firms with higher average base pay, which scales the outcome value of performance rewards. These findings contribute to the PFP and compensation literature by distinguishing effort duration and intensity as distinct behavioral pathways and by showing that PFP facilitates costly effort reallocation toward intensity when duration-based responses are externally constrained. More broadly, we demonstrate that under work-hour restrictions, incentive systems do not merely motivate effort but enable organizational adaptation by reshaping how employees deploy effort in response to external constraints.
