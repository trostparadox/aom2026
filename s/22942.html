Mimetic Sustainability and Organizational Field Configuration

OMT
Silvia Rita Sedita; Amir Maghssudipour (University of Padua); Annalisa Caloffi (University of Florence); Silvia Blasi (University of Verona)
We contribute to organizational field theory by empirically examining how mimetic sustainability –that is, sustainability-driven mimetic isomorphism –shapes the configuration of organizational fields. Our study focuses on interlocking directorates as key relational mechanisms within organizational contexts. Utilizing a comprehensive dataset of 2234 firms and 12759 interlocking directorate ties, we apply valued Exponential Random Graph Models (valued ERGM) to investigate how differences in environmental, social, and governance sustainability influence the configuration of such connections. Our findings provide evidence that mimetic sustainability operates primarily through homophily mechanisms, with firms preferentially linking to others that exhibit comparable environmental, social, and governance profiles. We also identify similarities and variations across different sectors and clusters categorized according to the degree of sustainability engagement of their constituent firms, investigating the sector-specific and maturity-dependent processes through which mimetic sustainability shapes important inter-organizational networks as interlocking directorates and the broader organizational field structure.

Negative Attention Spillover: How Peer Awards Increase CSR Accusations Against Non-Winning Rivals’

OMT
Anyan Wei (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University); Rasmus Pichler (Rotterdam School of Management)
CEO awards are prominent public signals of a firm’s success. These recognitions often shape how firms are evaluated by investors, analysts, and the media by increasing organizational visibility. We examine whether such recognition is associated with negative attention spillovers for non-winning rivals by changing the external information environment in which firms are monitored for irresponsible behavior. Specifically, we ask whether CEO awards are followed by increased accusations of corporate social irresponsibility against close, non-winning rivals, and whether this spillover is more pronounced when these rivals are already subject to stronger baseline external attention before the award. We test these ideas in a panel of S&P 1500 firms from 2008 to 2019 using a difference-in-difference approach that accounts for staggered treatment timings of CEO awards . We identify close rivals using text-based product-market proximity and measure corporate social irresponsibility accusations using the RepRisk database. Across specifications, we find that for non-winning rivals, exposure to a peer CEO award is followed by increased accusations of corporate social irresponsibility. This effect is stronger where the information environment is richer, consistent with the idea that obsevers’ attention and monitoring capacity shape whether corporate social irresponsibility events are noticed and reported. Our study contributes to research on CEO awards by shifting focus from understanding awards’ attentional impact on the awardee to the their impact on external scrutiny of the competitive field, and to work on corporate social irresponsibility and misconduct by showing how positive recognition can redistribute negative exposure across same market firms.

Matching Audiences to Organizational Cues: CSR Objectives, Practices, and Social Evaluation

OMT
Shuang Xie (USI Lugano, Switzerland); Emanuele Luca Maria Bettinazzi (Università della Svizzera Italiana); Eric Zhao (University of Oxford)
Firms pursuing corporate sustainability must balance conformity with industry norms and differentiation that yields advantage, yet research often simplifies the evaluation of corporate social responsibility (CSR) distinctiveness—either by treating different dimensions of CSR as equivalent across audiences or by overlooking audience heterogeneity across different CSR dimensions. Drawing on an audience-cue matching perspective and the ends-versus-means lens from social evaluation literature, we disaggregate CSR distinctiveness into objectives (“ends”) and practices (“means”) and theorize that heterogeneous external audiences attend to and assess these facets differently. We posit a pair of evaluative effects of CSR distinctiveness contingent on the audience–cue fit: financial analysts, operating from an instrumental logic, focus on CSR objectives and reward greater objective-based distinctiveness; media actors, guided by a normative logic, attend to CSR practices and show a U-shaped response to practice-based distinctiveness, penaliztranslating corporate socialing moderately distinctive firms but favouring either conformity or bold distinctiveness. Using divergence-based metrics derived from an NLP-processed database of over 12,000 sustainability reports from approximately 4,000 publicly listed firms (2015–2020), we construct objective- and practice-based distinctiveness scores and link them to analyst recommendations (I/B/E/S) and media sentiment (FactSet TruValue). Empirical results from between-and within-firm regression models support our hypotheses. The study advances optimal distinctiveness and social evaluation by showing that distinctiveness is audience–cue contingent: evaluations turn on which ends and means cues audiences attend to and how they interpret them. It additionally advances CSR strategy by framing strategic CSR positioning as a dual-cue configuration and demonstrating how objective- and practice-based differentiation strategies trigger divergent responses across stakeholder groups.

The Diffusion of International Sustainability Standard in Emerging Markets: An Infection Framework

OMT
Yahan Hu (South China University of Technology)
Institutional pressures for international sustainability standard (ISS) adoption are rising as mandatory sustainability reporting intensifies and ISSs mature globally, yet substantial heterogeneity persists in firms’ adoption decisions during ISS diffusion in emerging markets. This paper introduces an infection framework from epidemiology to theorize firms’ adoption decisions. Firms’ ISS adoption is conceptualized as an outcome of organizational “infection” by institutional pressures, which is shaped by two mechanisms: the transmission of institutional pressures and organizational susceptibility to the pressures. The paper tests this framework in the diffusion of the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) standard in China using event history analysis. Results show that firms with greater state ownership and broader global expansion are more likely to adopt ISS due to heightened pressures transmitted through political and market routes respectively, whereas organizational maturity and domestic standard adoption reduce organizational susceptibility and delay adoption. Negative media coverage further amplifies both pressure transmission and organizational susceptibility. By introducing an infection framework to examine firms’ adoption decisions, this study clarifies the processes linking institutional pressures to organizational responses and illuminates the diffusion pattern of ISS in emerging markets.
