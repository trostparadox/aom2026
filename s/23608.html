Inclusive Leadership: Promise, Paradox, and Precision

Session overview

This session develops a more nuanced view of inclusive leadership by examining its mechanisms, contingencies, and potential downsides. The papers address trickle-down inclusion, bullying, work stress, behaviorally specific leadership practices, and harmful supervision in a cultural context. Taken together, they move beyond broad claims about inclusive leadership to show when, how, and for whom leadership behaviors promote meaningful inclusion and well-being.

Cultural Values and Asian Bystanders’ Well-Being Under Abusive Supervision

DEI
Mengjie Xu (Eastern Kentucky University); Xueting Jiang (New York Institute of Technology)
Research on abusive supervision has increasingly recognized its impact on bystanders, yet little is known about how such effects vary across cultural contexts. This study explains why Asian bystanders may experience greater harm from abusive supervision than other groups, proposing that their cultural values create a psychologically painful dilemma. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we propose how observing abusive supervision affects Asian bystanders’ stress, burnout, and well-being. We theorize that two salient cultural values, power distance and collectivism, produce internal conflict for those who vicariously experience mistreatment. For Asian employees with high levels of power distance and collectivism, the tension between group loyalty and hierarchical obedience heightens susceptibility to stress and burnout when witnessing abusive supervision, ultimately undermining their well-being. This study advances understanding of third-party abusive supervision by revealing how cultural values shape bystanders’ internal experiences. It offers a counterintuitive perspective: while cultural norms may suppress outward reactions among Asian employees, they can intensify internal strain through resource depletion, leading to hidden costs to well-being.

Inclusive Leadership of Women Managing Women: Test of a Trickle-Down Model

DEI
Kenta Koyama (Tokyo Keizai University)
This study examines the impact of higher-level managers on the inclusive leadership behaviors displayed by female supervisors toward their female subordinates. Guided by the trickle-down model, we hypothesize that managers’ inclusive leadership positively predicts female supervisors’ inclusive leadership, with uniqueness and belongingness serving as distinct mediators. Addressing a gap in the literature, this study examines “women managing women” from an inclusion perspective. A three-wave quantitative survey was conducted with 287 female supervisors across diverse organizations in Japan. Results indicate that when managers of female supervisors demonstrate inclusive leadership, female supervisors are more likely to enact inclusive leadership toward their female subordinates. Furthermore, uniqueness and belongingness emerged as separate mediating mechanisms in this process. These findings contribute to inclusion scholarship by providing empirical support for an employee-centered approach, extending the trickle-down model to the domain of inclusion, and suggesting the differential roles of belongingness and uniqueness. For practical implications, the study underscores the need for organizations to develop supervisors’ inclusive leadership capabilities and to expand diversity management metrics beyond demographic representation to include employees’ perceived inclusion and leaders’ inclusive behaviors. This approach can help embed inclusion meaningfully into workplace culture and avoid policy–practice decoupling.

Inclusive Leadership and Workplace Bullying: Roles of Resilience and Psychological Safety

DEI
Aneeza Azam (University of Central Punjab)
Workplace bullying is a serious problem in workplace which requires researchers and manager attention. Therefore, the study aims to examine the impact of inclusive leadership on workplace bullying, guided by Social Cognitive Theory and Social Exchange Theory. It further investigates employee resilience as an underlying mechanism between inclusive leadership and workplace bullying and psychological safety as a boundary condition between employee resilience and workplace bullying. Data were collected using three wave time lagged research design completed by 270 full-time employees. Convenience sampling has been used in the research study and data was collected between November 2024 to February 2025. The survey assessed employees' perceptions of workplace bullying, their resilience capacity, and their views on leadership in their organisations. Structural Equation Modeling was employed to test the research hypotheses. The study found that inclusive leadership negatively correlates with workplace bullying. Employees with higher resilience capacity were better equipped to handle workplace bullying. Additionally, psychological safety moderates the relationship between employee resilience and workplace bullying. The study is limited by its research design, which restricts the ability to establish generalizability. Future research could explore longitudinal designs to validate these findings. These insights can guide managers to adopt inclusive leadership style to enhance the resilience capacity among employees and to lower the impact of negative organisational outcome of workplace bullying. The future guidelines of the research study and limitations have also been discussed. This study contributes to the limited literature on inclusive leadership's role in addressing workplace bullying. It emphasis the importance of resilience and psychological safety as critical factors in reducing the adverse effects of workplace bullying, offering valuable guidance for organisational leadership and policy-making.

The Paradoxical Impact of Inclusive Leadership on Employee Work Stress: A Dual Pathway Analysis

DEI
Lisa Janssens (University of Antwerp); Kim De Meulenaere (University of Antwerp)
Over the past two and a half decades, inclusive leadership has been established as one of the cornerstones of effective diversity management, fostering workplace inclusion and enhancing employees’ direct work experiences. However, emerging research suggests that its effects are far more ambiguous than the dominant literature assumes, hinting at potential darker sides of inclusive leadership, yet to be uncovered. To examine the more complex nature of inclusive leadership, the present study explores its double-edged sword effect on employees’ work stress. Integrating inclusion theory with social exchange theory (SET), we propose that inclusive leadership reduces employees’ work stress by enhancing psychological safety, yet simultaneously fosters it by eliciting felt obligation – a social exchange mechanism largely overlooked in prior inclusive leadership research. Furthermore, we investigate team diversity as a critical context variable shaping these paradoxical effects. The results from a three-wave survey among 210 U.S. employees provide empirical support for the proposed double-edged sword effect of inclusive leadership on work stress. Moreover, the study reveals that team diversity moderates the felt obligation pathway, leading employees to feel a stronger psychological obligation to reciprocate – and thus experience higher levels of work stress – in more demographically diverse teams. Together, these findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of inclusive leadership and its effects, offering important theoretical and practical implications for organizational management.

Deconstructing Inclusive Leadership Behaviors and Their Differential Impact for Minority Groups

DEI
Jennifer Kim (Tufts University); Wei Zheng (Stevens Institute of Technology)
Despite growing interest in inclusive leadership, research has treated inclusion as a uniform construct that affects all employees similarly. This assumption overlooks two critical questions: Do specific inclusive leadership behaviors produce different outcomes? And do these relationships vary for women and racial minorities? We address these gaps through two complementary studies that adopt a behaviorally specific, contingency-based approach to inclusive leadership. Study 1 employed grounded theory methodology with 43 STEM professionals (47% women; 28% Asian, 26% Black, 23% Latino, 23% White) to inductively identify leadership practices that create inclusion. Through in-depth focus groups and interviews, we identified nine distinct inclusive leadership behaviors and mapped their perceived effects on employee outcomes. Study 2 quantitatively tested these nine behaviors across eight psychological and behavioral outcomes, explicitly examining moderation by gender and race/ethnicity among a professional sample of N=212 on Prolific (47.6% women; 23.1% Asian, 24.0% Black, 18.3% Latino, 34.6% White). By using a nomological lens to examine multiple behaviors and outcomes simultaneously, we identify differentiated patterns—revealing which specific behaviors matter most for which outcomes and for whom.
