From Disembedding to Embedding: Constructing Meaningfulness in Platforms

OB
Jieyu Hu (Nanjing University); Dejun Cheng (Nanjing University); Shijun Huang (Nanjing University)
Within the context of gig platforms, governance has frequently been conceptualized as a form of digitalized Taylorism, where managerial technologies and social constraints generate depersonalizing effects that undermine workers’ pursuit of meaningful work. While European studies have emphasized these challenges, Chinese scholars and practitioners have advocated for more democratic governance models to expand opportunities for meaning-making in labor. This study conceptualizes perceived platform governance practices in China and introduces a novel measurement approach to assess workers’ perceptions. Drawing on survey data, we empirically examine five pathways—pay satisfaction, competence, autonomy, relatedness, and beneficence— through which platform workers experience meaningful work under varying perceptions of governance. Supplementary analyses further demonstrate the moderating role of individual value orientations in these processes. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of critically examining the contextual foundations of meaningful work and provide practical implications for platforms and managers in China and Europe seeking to improve governance practices to foster more meaningful work experiences.

Exploring the Dark Side of Meaningful Work

OB
Wahab Shahbaz (University of Reading); Tahereh Maghsoudi (Henley Business School); Chris Brewster (University of Reading)
In recent years, a substantial body of research suggests that meaningful work is associated with positive workplace outcomes such as enhanced well-being and work engagement. However, the unexpected and potentially negative sides of meaningful work for organisation have received less scholarly attention. In this paper, using an integrative literature review, we integrate and critique research on the dark side of meaningful work and make suggestions for future research development from a human resource management perspective. We identify three key themes: (1) there is a significant hidden cost of meaningful work including burnout, interpersonal conflict and absenteeism; (2) the adverse outcomes of meaningful work are shaped by social factors, sectoral context, organisational practices and individual differences; and (3) there is a need for a balanced approach to meaningful work that integrates personal fulfilment with broader ethical and societal values. We highlight the positivity bias in the existing literature; we suggest that future research could use a critical management lens to critique the Western-centric ideals and advocate for more inclusive and culturally sensitive approaches; and we identify promising future research directions related to the impact of artificial intelligence on the experience of meaningful work and its implications for employee psychological well-being. Finally, we highlight the dynamic nature of meaningful work by arguing that both stable and fluctuating experiences shape how individuals find purpose and calling for organisational strategies that support well-being without exploitation.

The Underdog Gains More: How Low Performers’ Proactive Behavior Fuels Their Eudaimonic Well-Being

OB
Man Cao (Nanjing University of Science and Technology); Boya Xu (Nanjing University of Science and Technology); Jiahe Zhang; Ru Li
As proactive behavior becomes increasingly important for both individuals and organizations, the literature has begun to pay attention to its consequence. Although previous studies have provided valuable insights, they have predominantly focused on instrumental outcomes and few studies examine eudaimonic well-being. Moreover, the limited research that has explored the association between proactive behavior and well-being has yield mixed findings. To address these issues, we investigate how proactive behavior affects eudaimonic well-being and identify job performance as a key contingency factor. Drawing on self-verification theory, we suggest that proactive behavior and job performance interact to shape employees’ psychological meaningfulness, and subsequent their eudaimonic well-being. Specifically, low rather than high job performers are likely to perceive greater psychological meaningfulness and, in turn, eudaimonic well-being. Using multiwave and multisource data, we found that job performance moderates the relationship between proactive behavior and employees’ psychological meaningfulness and ultimate eudaimonic well-being. Our study advances the proactive behavior literature by highlighting eudaimonic well-being as an important outcome and shedding light on the potential bright side of low performers when proactive behavior is taken into account.

Where We Find Meaning Matters: How Anchors of Work Meaningfulness Relate to Work and Life Well-Being

OB
Shawn Chan (Yale University); Winnie Jiang (INSEAD)
Work is increasingly being recognized as a central arena through which individuals derive meaning and purpose in life?and as a key contributor to one’s subjective well-being in both domains. Yet, most research typically treats work meaningfulness as unidimensional, capturing the overall extent to which work feels meaningful, without considering specific aspects of work that make it so. This conceptual simplification overlooks theoretical perspectives from fragmented research traditions emphasizing different sources of meaning, ranging from internal callings to external job characteristics and social relationships. Accordingly, we address two key questions in this research: (1) What are the different aspects of work to which individuals attribute meaningfulness—that is, what makes their work feel significant, worthwhile, or personally fulfilling? (2) How do these distinct attributions relate to subjective well-being, both at work and in life? Across three studies (N = 1,599), we inductively develop and validate a multidimensional framework?that we term anchors of work meaningfulness?comprising six distinct anchors: Livelihood, Community, Stimulation, Recognition, Impact, and Rejuvenation. We further examine how different anchors relate to subjective well-being and find that individuals who anchor work meaningfulness in Community, Stimulation, and Impact report higher well-being at work. Moreover, those anchoring work meaningfulness in Community and Impact report higher overall life well-being.

Burned Out by Purpose: Identity Overcommitment and Sustainability in Meaningful Work

OB
Megan Bolin (John Carroll University)
Research on meaningful work has highlighted its positive effects on motivation, engagement, and well-being. However, growing evidence suggests that work experienced as deeply meaningful may also carry hidden psychological costs. This conceptual paper advances a new explanation for burnout in meaning-driven work by introducing the concept of identity overcommitment—a condition in which individuals’ sense of self becomes excessively fused with their work roles, limiting psychological recovery and boundary flexibility. Drawing on identity theory, burnout research, and boundary management scholarship, the paper argues that identity overcommitment represents a distinct pathway through which meaningful work can undermine long-term sustainability. The paper develops a process model linking meaningful work to vocational identity, identity overcommitment, and burnout, and identifies organizational and leadership conditions that amplify or mitigate this process. By reframing burnout as an identity-level failure rather than an individual coping deficit, this paper contributes to organizational behavior research on meaningful work, identity, and well-being, and offers new directions for designing work systems that support sustained engagement without sacrificing employee health.
