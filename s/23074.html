Psychological Safety and Knowledge Sharing: Evidence from Temporary and Permanent Staff

HCM
Sina Berger (Zurich University of Applied Sciences); Sarah Schmelzer (ZHAW School of Management and Law); Zeynep Erden (ZHAW School of Management and Law); Florian Liberatore (ZHAW School of Management and Law); Bernadette Cruz
Healthcare organizations increasingly rely on temporary nursing staff to address ongoing workforce shortages. While prior research portrays temporary nurses as a risk to team functioning and care quality, their potential contribution to organizational learning and innovation remains understudied. Drawing on psychological safety theory, this study examines how employment status and tenure shape psychological safety and how it enables knowledge sharing among temporary and permanent nurses. Using two Swiss survey waves (N1 = 309; N2 = 282), we conducted non-parametric group comparisons and regression-based moderation analyses. Results show differences in psychological safety between temporary and permanent nurses across contexts, alongside consistent non-linear (U-shaped) tenure effects: psychological safety first declines with tenure, then rises again. Psychological safety is positively associated with incremental and radical knowledge sharing. Moderation analyses indicate that psychological safety reduces knowledge-sharing disparities between temporary and permanent nurses, weakening structural disadvantages linked to temporary employment when psychological safety is high. By showing psychological safety is dynamic, tenure-sensitive, and context-dependent, this study extends psychological safety theory beyond stable team settings and positions it as a key mechanism enabling temporary nurses to contribute knowledge and innovation in hybrid healthcare teams.

Shared Knowing and the Exercise of Judgment: Narratives in Virtual Communities of Practice

HCM
Zuhur Balayah (Bayes Business School); Amit Nigam (City - University of London); Charitini Stavropoulou
The narrative mode of thinking provides a way to interpret complex knowledge. We consider expanding this view by examining knowledge sharing via narrative in a complex organizational system. We explore the structure of narrative, developed in reflective virtual Communities of Practice spaces to diffuse experiential learning and cultivate judgment. We use an empirical context to illustrate the mutual interdependencies among communities and examine community members’ interpretations of experiential knowledge regarding the implementation of innovations in a complex system. The findings indicate the differentiation of distinct narrative styles that, together, reveal a deeper structure of the narrative mode of thinking. We also suggest two dimensions of shared knowing narrative styles: the extent of judgment exercised in each narrative style and temporal sensitivity.

Embracing Multiple Voices at Work: Evidence from a Person-Centered Analysis

HCM
Heeeun Jang (Cornell University); Ariel Avgar (Cornell University); Adrian John Wilkinson (Griffith University); Michael Barry (Griffith University); Paula Mowbray (Griffith University)
Research on employee voice has grown substantially since Hirschman’s (1970) pathbreaking work, yet the field remains fragmented across disciplines with differing basic assumptions and foci. Organizational Behavior (OB) research highlights discretionary and improvement-oriented voice, while Labor Relations (LR) scholarship views voice as a mechanism for protecting workers’ rights and improving working conditions. A stakeholder-oriented perspective has also developed somewhat independently, emphasizing ethical or professional obligations for external beneficiaries. Despite the important insights offered by each perspective, most empirical studies examine voice through only a single form of voice, overlooking the fact that employees frequently engage in multiple forms of voice simultaneously. This study offers an integrative approach by using target beneficiary as a unifying dimension and examining organizational (improvement oriented), self-interest (worker rights), and patient-care (stakeholder) voice together. Using a person-centered approach, we applied latent profile analysis to identify distinct configurations of voice in the hospital context and found six qualitatively different voice profiles that varied in both intensity and dominant beneficiary focus. These configurations were differentially associated with job satisfaction, turnover intention, and perceived quality of care. The study contributes to a more holistic understanding of voice and highlights the importance of environments that support balanced and inclusive forms of speaking up.

The Game of Rules: How Rules Are Negotiated to Enable Interprofessional Coordination

HCM
Karla Sayegh (University of Cambridge); Xian Zhu (McGill University); Samer Faraj (McGill University)
Organizations often introduce formal rules to clarify task and role responsibilities between interdependent professional groups. Yet coordination research suggests that rules, as designed structures, prove less effective when strictly followed in work situations characterized by uncertainty or complex knowledge dependencies. In practice, professional groups may need to negotiate, bend, or even break rules to meet the situated demands of their own work while sustaining coordination with other groups. Drawing on a 23-month ethnography of a Canadian hospital emergency department, we examine how the enactment of four formal rules, introduced to contain and manage patient-flow disruptions, was negotiated between professional groups and how this process restabilized coordination. We argue that groups bend or adapt rules by interpreting and applying them in ways that are deeply contingent on the anticipated actions of their coordinating counterparts. Professional groups exercise discretion in two distinct patterns: resistive, when the rules are interpreted as encroaching on the autonomy of equally authoritative groups and are bent to deflect responsibility, and accommodative, when they are interpreted as enhancing the autonomy of lower-authority groups, who adapt them and absorb responsibility. Across both patterns, discretion is exercised by mobilizing judgment, anticipating counterparty actions, and strategically (in)visibilizing responsibilities. These moves draw on a practical knowing of the counterparty’s interests, work patterns, and organizational arrangements to anticipate their responses and adjust how they apply the rules accordingly. Our study contributes to theories of coordination and interprofessional work by showing how “rule games” might enable complex knowledge coordination and unpacking the effortful relational dynamics that make rules “work” in practice.

The Autopoiesis of Control: Management Control as Communication in Complex Healthcare Environments

HCM
Pingli Li (University of Southampton); Xuegang Cui; Erik Strauß (ESCP Business School)
Healthcare systems worldwide are undergoing profound transformations driven by increasing fiscal pressure, technological advancement, and societal expectations for efficiency and equity. In this context, management control systems (MCS) have become central to balancing financial, professional, and political demands within public healthcare organizations. This study employs Luhmann’s social systems theory to explain how and why MCS evolve as communicative mechanisms that help organizations manage the growing complexity of their environments. Using the Chinese healthcare system as a longitudinal case, we trace the evolution of MCS across three reform phases: central planning (1949–1979), marketization (1980s–2009), and recentralization emphasizing public welfare (2009–2019). Our findings reveal that MCS act as recursive communication structures that continuously recalibrate the balance between external complexity (environmental uncertainty), internal complexity (organizational differentiation), and collaborative complexity (inter-organizational coordination). During the marketization phase, MCS amplified financial pressures and fragmented communication, while post-2009 reforms transformed them into integrative tools linking medical, financial, and political rationalities. Through this transformation, MCS evolved from instruments of market discipline into mechanisms of reflexive and collaborative governance, mediating the relationship between state objectives and organizational autonomy. The study contributes in MCS as well as public sector and NPM research. First, it extends management control theory by conceptualizing MCS as adaptive communication systems that evolve autopoietically through recursive feedback loops between decisions and environmental interpretation. The insights highlight the role of MCS as enablers of systemic coherence and collaboration in complex public service environments.
