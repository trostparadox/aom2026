Forged in Urgency: Temporal Orientations and Strategizing in Public Security

SAP
Alex Borges da Silva (University of the Valley of Itajaí); Sidnei Vieira Marinho (University of the Valley of Itajaí)
While Strategy-as-Practice (SAP) research has extensively explored how strategy is accomplished socially, less attention has been paid to how urgency is constructed and modulated in high-stakes public environments. Drawing on a site-ontological perspective and the concept of teleoaffective structures, this study investigates the Civil Police of Santa Catarina, Brazil, an anomalous "island of success" in a context of high violence. Through in-depth narrative interviews with police chiefs, we uncovered a recursive system in which urgency is not merely an external pressure but a socially negotiated accomplishment. We argue that strategy is "forged in urgency" in a twofold sense: literally moulded through the heat of operational pressure and figuratively fabricated through the political authorisation of temporal narratives. Our findings reveal that hope functions as a teleoaffective mechanism that stretches the present operational reality towards a meaningful future end, sustaining the necessary tension for urgency. Conversely, hierarchical temporal brokerage serves as a political tool to validate or silence such drives. This study contributes to SAP by demonstrating how temporal legitimacy and narrative identity work are deployed to maintain high performance in the face of prolonged crises.

Redesigning of Organizational Routines: Examining In-Situ Redesign in Healthcare Imaging

SAP
Yoojin Jeong (University of South Carolina); Scott F. Turner (University of South Carolina)
This study examines how organizations intentionally redesign routines within ongoing routine performances. Prior research on routine redesign has largely examined redesign as temporally or spatially separated from ongoing performance, leaving limited understanding of how redesign unfolds when routines cannot be paused. Drawing on a two-year ethnographic study of a quality improvement project in healthcare imaging, we examine how an MRI routine was redesigned during its ongoing performance and patient care delivery. We conceptualize this process as in-situ routine redesign, in which redesign and performance are intertwined rather than sequential. Our findings identify in-situ redesign moments which are brief temporal openings created from ongoing routine performance. Within these moments, organizational actors engaged in embodied redesign work that surfaced and shared their embodied knowing, enabling the envisioning and enactment of routine redesign. We show how actors generate time for redesign by repurposing existing temporal structures and accomplish change to the routine through sensory engagement and bodily affordances. By theorizing routine redesign as an in-situ, temporal, and embodied accomplishment, this study advances routine dynamics research by explaining how intentional and endogenous changes to routines intertwine, how temporal regularity and disruption are jointly managed, and how intentional changes to routines are accomplished through bodies.

Caught in the Middle: Aspiration Performance Gaps and R&D Investment

SAP
Yu-Hsuan Hsiao (National Taiwan University); Cheng-Yu Lee (National Chiayi University)
In contemporary corporate strategy, R&D investment has emerged as a critical focal point. However, prior literature grounded in A Behavioral Theory of the Firm has largely treated social and historical aspiration levels as consistent reference benchmarks, primarily emphasizing the congruence of performance feedback. Existing studies tend to examine how the intensity of performance feedback influences firms’ strategic choices when a single performance indicator exceeds or falls below aspirations. In contrast, relatively little research has explored how firms adjust their R&D investment behavior when performance feedback from social and historical aspirations is inconsistent, or even negatively related. Moreover, the role of performance duration in shaping R&D investment decisions has not received sufficient scholarly attention. This study focuses on differences in firms’ R&D investment behavior under conditions of consistent versus inconsistent performance feedback, and further analyzes the moderating effect of performance duration. Given that R&D investment is influenced by multiple internal and external factors, and that prior studies have seldom examined the impact of financial performance duration on R&D investment from a behavioral theory perspective, this research seeks to fill an important gap in the literature. By doing so, it aims to provide more nuanced and refined insights to guide firms in making strategic R&D investment decisions.

Shield or Sword? D&O Insurance and Corporate Risk-Taking: Evidence in China

SAP
Lingying Zhou (Zhejiang University); Junsheng Dou; Dongliang Sun (Zhejiang University)
Despite the important role of Directors’ and Officers’ liability (D&O) insurance in alleviating agency conflicts, there is a lack of research on how its incentive effectiveness differs across enterprises with different ownership structures. We draw on agency theory to analyze the differential effects of D&O insurance on corporate risk-taking across state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and non-state-owned enterprises (non-SOEs). Due to different configuration of institutional logics, the two types of enterprises exhibit distinct principal-agent dynamics, which lead to D&O insurance being more effective in incentivizing corporate risk-taking in non-SOEs. Given the particularities of China's emerging market, we further explore how mixed ownership structures affect the relationship between D&O insurance and corporate risk-taking in SOEs and non-SOEs. Using data from Chinese-listed firms between 2003 and 2022, this study reveals the varying roles of D&O insurance on corporate risk-taking in enterprises with different ownership structures due to differences in agency relationships. It also provides a boundary condition for agency theory by integrating institutional logics perspective into it.

Buzz Breeds Hype: Unraveling Hype’s Micro-Practices

SAP
Melina Schichta (Leuphana University of Lüneburg); Pauline Charlotte Reinecke (Leuphana University of Lüneburg); Olga Kokshagina (The University of Sydney)
This research examines how entrepreneurial hype operates as an industry-making practice in stigmatized markets by analyzing the case of FemTech pioneer Ida Tin, co-founder of Clue and originator of the term FemTech. Whereas prior research largely treats hype as a firm-level phenomenon, we ask how entrepreneurs generate industry-level hype through micro-practices of storytelling, framing, and future-oriented claiming. Using a qualitative single-case design, we assemble a longitudinal dataset of Tin’s public discursive footprint (talks, podcasts, interviews, LinkedIn posts, and press releases, 2012-2025). We combine grounded coding and discourse analysis with systematic verification of high-impact claims against public sources. Our analysis identifies five interrelated bundles of hyping practices - provoking recognition, embodied data sensemaking, category creation, infrastructure coupling, and entrepreneurial sensemaking - through which Tin links activist appeals, investment logics, and infrastructural work to constitute FemTech as a legitimate and investable industry. We further show how she navigates a balancing act between empirically grounded and generative (partly overstated but future-making) claims that simultaneously secure credibility and stretch expectations. Conceptually, we develop a multi-level model of hype’s micro-practices as configurations spanning individual, organizational, industry, and field levels. We contribute to research on hype by theorizing it as founder-driven, industry-level work, and to emancipatory entrepreneurship by showing how marginalized entrepreneurs use hype as a cultural and symbolic resource to transform personal constraint into collective agency and industry creation. We also believe this paper aligns closely with current calls in Strategy-as-Practice research to address societal challenges and underrepresented contexts (e.g., Jarzabkowski et al., 2025) and we would appreciate feedback on how our research aligns with practice-based scholarship.
