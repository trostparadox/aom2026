The Impact of Mother Stereotypes on Women's Decisions to Apply for High-Status Jobs

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 2:30PM–3:30PM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 2
DEI

People

Authors: McKenzie Rees Brigham Young University; Jaycelin Eyre Brigham Young University

Session overview

Current literature identifies how stereotypes about mothers’ work commitment, competence, and family orientation contribute to external discrimination in hiring, promotion, and evaluation. However, less is known about how internalized mother stereotypes shape women’s career decision-making. This study assesses whether women’s self-identification with mother stereotypes predicts their willingness to apply for high-status roles. This question has become increasingly relevant given shifts in norms surrounding working mothers and greater visibility of intentionally childfree adults. We hypothesized that identification with mother stereotypes would predict lower preference for high-status roles, and that mothers would be less willing to apply for such roles than fathers or childfree adults. We further predicted that perceived work–family conflict would mediate this relationship, and that perceived time and energy costs associated with the job would moderate it. We conducted a preliminary survey with 272 university students. Identification with the mother stereotype was operationalized using female participants’ current motherhood status or reported intention to become a mother. Participants ranked five job descriptions, varied by status level, by their willingness to apply for each role. Then they reported the perceived work-family conflict and time and energy cost associated with their top- and bottom-ranked roles. We will analyze these preliminary data to assess our hypotheses and refine measures before surveying a larger sample of 400 U.S. adults (ages 18–65) on Prolific. By focusing on internalized processes, this study helps explain why current and future mothers may be less likely to pursue high-status positions, offering insights into persistent gender disparities.
