From Choking to Breathing: CEO’s Early-life Air Pollution Exposure and Green Innovation

ONE
Junghoon Kim (Sogang University); Yoonjin Kim (Sogang University)
Purpose: This study investigates whether and how CEOs’ early-life air pollution exposure shapes environmental decision-making, as reflected in corporate green innovation (GI), with board independence and state political orientation as moderating factors. Design/methodology/approach: Using panel data of S&P 500 firms spanning 2010–2022, this study focuses on CEO succession events to explore how newly appointed CEOs drive changes in environmental decision-making, as reflected in corporate GI strategies. A Heckman two-stage regression model is employed to account for sample selection. Data on CEOs’ childhood hometowns were collected from multiple public sources and rigorously validated. Findings: The analysis demonstrates that CEOs’ early-life exposure to higher levels of air pollution is more likely to pursue corporate GI. This effect is further amplified by stronger board independence and by headquarters’ location in Democratic-leaning (“blue”) states. Originality/value: This study integrates feeling-as-information and upper-echelon theory to explain how CEOs’ early-life exposure to air pollution influences their environmental decision-making and fosters corporate GI grounded in pro-environmental values. It further extends prior research by uncovering long-term and micro-level mechanisms beyond the traditional focus on singular and traumatic life events such as famine or natural disasters.

CEO Cultural Backgrounds and Corporate Social and Environmental Performance

ONE
Zhaoyi Yan (University of Science and Technology of China)
In this study, we explore the role of CEOs’ cultural heritage in relation to corporate sustainability performance. Drawing upon upper echelons theory and the cultural transmission argument, we argue that CEOs’ inherited cultures—reflected in their uncertainty avoidance orientation—shape sustainability preferences and influence corporate social and environmental performance. Furthermore, we posit two boundary conditions whereby CEO bonuses strengthen the relationship between CEO uncertainty avoidance and corporate sustainability performance, while newly appointed CEOs weaken this relationship. Utilizing a sample of 4,839 firm-year matched observations from the Fortune 500 spanning 13 years, we find that firms led by CEOs with a higher uncertainty avoidance index are negatively correlated with corporate sustainability performance, particularly in environmental outcomes. Additionally, these firms exhibit poorer environmental and social performance when CEO bonuses represent a larger share of total compensation. Conversely, newly appointed CEOs positively moderate the relationship between uncertainty avoidance and social and environmental performance dimensions—including resource use, emissions, and community relations—leading to improved outcomes in these areas when they have been in office for less than three years. Overall, our results highlight that informal institution, such as the national cultural values ingrained in individuals, are important determinants of corporate sustainability performance.

Green Stewards at the Helm: How CEO Green Experience Drives Corporate Sustainability Performance

ONE
jinjiang he (Zhejiang University of Finance and Economics); bingqian wang
This study empirically examines the impact of CEOs' green experience on corporate sustainability performance (CSP) in China’s A-share listed firms from 2010 to 2020. Grounded in upper echelons theory and the natural resource-based view, we posit that CEOs with prior environmental expertise enhance CSP through improved green strategic proactiveness (GSP). Utilizing a robust panel dataset and addressing endogeneity concerns, we establish compelling evidence that CEO green experience significantly improves CSP. Further analysis indicates that GSP mediates this relationship, while stringent environmental regulations positively moderate the link between CEO green experience and GSP. Our findings contribute to corporate sustainability literature by:(1) identifying CEO-specific human capital as a critical antecedent of CSP,(2) uncovering the mediating role of GSP, and(3) demonstrating how institutional factors amplify this effect.The study offers practical implications for boards seeking to improve sustainability through executive selection and for policymakers designing supportive policies.

Directing Executive Bonuses to Address the Climate Crisis: Is it Effective?

ONE
Leanne Keddie (Carleton University); Awais Mojai (Carleton University); Patrick J. Callery (University of Vermont)
Corporate executive compensation contracts increasingly incentivize nonfinancial performance, such as Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) metrics, including greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. This research examines whether GHG-related incentives in executive compensation plans are effective in reducing GHGs. GHGs are worldwide in their impact, with regulators increasingly restricting extra-territorial activity as evidenced by the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive in the European Union. We use a novel artificial intelligence (AI) application to collect data from proxy statements about GHG and ESG incentive use among S&P 500 firms. We go beyond previous studies by examining both explicit and implicit GHG-related compensation in annual incentive plans (not just sustainable compensation policies) and how they affect firm emissions. We find that use of explicit GHG incentives is associated with reductions in Scopes 1, 2 & 3 GHG emissions. However, ESG incentives without GHG metrics, and implicit GHG incentives, are less effective. Our work questions the effectiveness of some approaches to GHG incentives towards reducing emissions. Standard-setters and compensation committees may leverage this research to design more effective GHG incentives.

Paying for Cleaner Outcomes? Non-Executive Director Compensation and Corporate Emissions Performance

ONE
Linh Thi Tue Tran (University of Bristol Business School); Hanh Thi Hong Nguyen
This paper examines the relationship between non-executive director (NED) compensation and corporate emissions reduction performance. Using 8,852 firm-year observations from U.S. listed companies over 2006 to 2020, we find that higher non-executive director compensation, particularly equity-based pay, is significantly associated with better emission reduction scores. Results are robust to firm fixed effects, two-stage least squares estimation, propensity score matching, entropy balancing, and alternative emissions measures. Research and development investment serves as a key channel, partially mediating the compensation-emissions relationship. However, the positive association is significantly weakened when CEOs hold substantial power or when firms face severe financial constraints. Our findings have direct implications for boards designing compensation structures to support climate commitments, for investors assessing the credibility of corporate environmental governance, and for regulators considering disclosure requirements around director pay and sustainability performance. The results extend the compensation and environmental performance literature to the boardroom and demonstrate that director incentives influence climate outcomes contingently, depending on organizational context.
