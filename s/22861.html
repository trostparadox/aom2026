How Leader Humor Spreads: Trickle-Down and Contagion Pathways to Team Effective

OB
Gang Chen (Nanjing University); Shuming Zhao (Nanjing University); Yixuan Zhao (Nanjing University); Jun Zhang (Woosong University); Linpei Song (Nanjing University)
The effectiveness of leader humor has been endorsed by leaders across multiple hierarchical levels, however, limited research has examined how it travels across hierarchical levels and diffuses within the same level. Drawing on social learning theory (SLT) and social information processing theory (SIPT), we conducted two sequential studies to investigate how humor cascades from department leaders to team leaders and how team leader humor ultimately enhances team effectiveness through within-team contagion. Study 1 used a three-wave matched leader-member sample (274 dyads) and found that department leader humor positively predicted team leader humor, with an indirect effect operating through team leaders’ perceptions of department leaders’ leading-by-example. Study 2 employed a three-wave, multi-source team design (79 teams with 258 team members) and showed that team leader humor fosters team Psychological capital (PsyCap), which in turn promotes team organizational citizenship behavior and team performance. By integrating trickle-down model and within-team contagion perspectives, this research clarifies how leader humor moves from department to team levels and translates into team outcomes, thereby extending theorizing on leader humor diffusion and its team-level mechanisms. The findings also offer practical guidance for organizations seeking to leverage leader humor to build collective psychological resources and improve team effectiveness.

Aggressive Leader Humor and Instigated Incivility: A Conservation of Resources Perspective

OB
Velia Meidilisa (National Taiwan University of Science and Technology)
This study identifies leader aggressive humor as a key antecedent of employee incivility and investigates its indirect effects through mediating mechanisms. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we propose a serial mediation pathway whereby leader aggressive humor depletes employees' psychological resources, leading to psychological contract breach and distrust, which in turn results in instigated incivility. Additionally, we introduce sensitivity toward leader treatment as a moderating factor that amplifies the adverse effects of leader aggressive humor on instigated incivility. Data collected across three waves from healthcare employees in Indonesia confirm that leader aggressive humor indirectly increases instigated incivility through the proposed pathway. Furthermore, sensitivity toward leader treatment amplifies this link, suggesting that employees with high sensitivity toward leader treatment are more likely to experience resource depletion from leader aggressive humor and engage in defensive behaviors, such as instigated incivility. Our findings align with conservation of resource theory and provide a nuanced understanding of how leader aggressive humor can inadvertently provoke instigated incivility through resource depletion dynamics.

Earning Respect or Gaining Control? Daily Leader Self-Defeating Humor and Its Mixed Effects

OB
Fu Yang (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Yilan Liu (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
Despite burgeoning scholarly and practical interest in the effects of leader self-defeating humor on subordinates, less emphasis has been placed on understanding how it influences leaders themselves. From a leader-centric perspective, we integrate self-regulatory theory with the approach-avoidance framework to develop a dual-pathway model, illustrating the impacts of leader self-defeating humor. Specifically, we predict that leaders who exhibit self-defeating humor on a daily basis tend to be concerned about their image, leading them to engage in prestige behavior. Simultaneously, displaying self-defeating humor can cause status threat among leaders, leading them to engage in dominance behavior. We further identify leaders’ individual differences as the contingency factors—namely, leader benign and malicious envy—that explain leaders’ varying reactions to self-defeating humor. Applying experience sampling methodology (ESM) with a sample of 596 daily observations nested within 73 leaders over 10 consecutive workdays, the results provide support for the hypotheses. Overall, our work helps disentangle how and when daily leader self-defeating humor can yield beneficial versus detrimental effects on leaders themselves.

When Humor Bites: The Double-Edged Effects of Leader Aggressive Humor on Leadership Behaviors

OB
Fu Yang (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Fan Zhang (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
Existing studies on leader aggressive humor have primarily documented its detrimental consequences on employees’ work attitudes and behaviors. However, scholars still know relatively little about how such behavior affects leaders themselves. Building on the superiority theory of humor, we take a leader-centric perspective to develop a model explaining how leader aggressive humor may trigger distinct leadership responses, and how the change in these responses depends on leader dominance orientation. Results from an experience-sampling field study support our theoretical model. Our study unveils that leader aggressive humor enhances leader sense of power, which increases task-oriented leadership behavior, and simultaneously decreases relationship-oriented leadership behavior. Moreover, these impacts should be especially pronounced for leaders with high dominance orientation. Overall, our research contributes to leader aggressive humor research by challenging the conventional view that leader aggressive humor is always harmful, and provides practical insights for organizations on how to better manage it.
