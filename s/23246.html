Acquiring Disruption: Deal Structures, Artificial Intelligence Targets, and the Influence of ChatGPT

STR
Leila Soleimani (Bucknell University); Ryan Christopher Stauffer (Bucknell University); Nga Nguyen (Universite de Quebec a Montreal)
This paper investigates how firms respond to disruptive innovations through mergers and acquisitions, with a focus on artificial intelligence (AI) targets. We build on disruptive innovation theory and compare acquisition deal structures between AI and non-AI targets in terms of target age, deal price, and payment method. Using Crunchbase data, we find that AI targets are acquired earlier, and less frequently through all-cash deals than non-AI targets. We also examine the effect of ChatGPT introduction on the acquisition structure for AI targets. Prior to ChatGPT, AI targets were acquired at lower prices than non-AI targets. Our results indicate that prices for AI targets increased significantly one year after ChatGPT’s public launch. Our study contributes to the literature of disruptive innovations by demonstrating how incumbents strategically acquire disruptive innovations and how market signals can shape such strategic responses.

Discipline and Conviction:The Dual Role of AI in Determining Acquisition Premiums

STR
Namgyoo Park (Seoul National University); Taekyong Park (Seoul National University)
M&As are one of the most imminent tasks where Artificial Intelligence can affect overall procedures of strategic decision-making but its empirical impact on M&A pricing mechanisms remains theoretically untested. To investigate how an acquiring firm's AI usage reshapes M&A excessive premiums, unlike previous studies, we assume that AI not only automates cost-cutting to reduce premiums but also emboldens strategic bets to increase excessive premiums. Specifically, we examine its interplay with the acquirer's prior acquisition experience and business relatedness with the target. We empirically analyze a dataset of 259 M&A transactions undertaken by U.S. public firms between 2019 and 2024, a period marking the mainstream strategic adoption of AI. Our findings reveal the dual role of AI in M&A valuation. First, we observe a negative effect of AI on acquisition excessive premiums, suggesting that AI with data-driven valuations may act as a disciplining mechanism that may eventually mitigate the winner’s curse. However, contrary to the argument that AI may solely suppress premiums, we find that AI significantly increases premiums when the acquirer possesses high prior acquisition experience or when the target operates in a related business domain. We argue that the effect of AI on M&As can be explained by the discipline and conviction framework. AI imposes discipline by curbing overpayment in the absence of strategic fit but it fosters conviction by validating high premiums in experienced and related contexts.

AI and the Boundaries of the Firm: Revisiting Governance in the Algorithmic Era

STR
Deepika Chhillar (University of Denver); Joseph T. Mahoney (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping how firms communicate, coordinate, control, and create value, raising new questions about the boundaries of the firm in an era of algorithmic decision-making. Building on transaction cost economics, this paper theorizes AI not only as a production technology but as a governance innovation that alters what can be measured, predicted, and contracted across organizational and interfirm interfaces. We introduce the notion of algorithmic integration and develop an Algorithmic Governance Cube defined by three AI-era transaction attributes: dynamic programmability (stability of algorithmic behavior under learning), algorithmic nonseparability (attribution challenges in human–AI co-production), and data/AI specificity (relationship-specific digital assets such as proprietary corpora, fine-tuned models, and governance stacks). Mapping these dimensions yields eight canonical governance scenarios—from algorithmic spot markets and long-term algorithmic contracts to joint AI ventures, inside algorithmic contracting, and algorithmic hierarchy—and a set of propositions specifying when algorithmic contracting substitutes for, complements, or reinforces vertical financial ownership. The framework recasts classic make-or-buy questions as choices among owning, contracting, or co-governing algorithmic systems. It highlights how uncertainty, exchange frequency, and specificity interact with AI-enabled measurement. We conclude by outlining implications for managerial decisions about AI deployment and by proposing an agenda for empirically operationalizing and testing the Algorithmic Governance Cube.

Beaten by Bots?: AI Prediction, Analyst Prediction, and Post-Acquisition Performance

STR
Zixuan Wang (School of Economics and Management, Tongji University)
Strategic decision-making in M&A relies critically on accurate target valuation. We investigate the comparative efficacy of AI versus human analysts in guiding equity stake decisions. Analyzing 2,906 transactions from 2000 to 2023, we contrast neural network predictions with consensus analyst forecasts. We find that ownership decisions aligned with AI predictions generate superior announcement-period returns. Conversely, alignment with human forecasts is associated with inferior outcomes, suggesting traditional judgment is susceptible to bias. However, a three-way interaction reveals a critical nuance: the synergistic alignment of AI predictions, human evaluations, and high ownership amplifies performance beyond either source alone. These results suggest that while AI outperforms humans in processing multidimensional data, it serves as a powerful complement rather than a substitute, advocating for hybrid decision-making frameworks.

Artificial Intelligence and Executive Decision-Making in Corporate Acquisitions

STR
Troy McGarity; Sergio Grove (The University of Texas at El Paso); Mengge Li (The University of Texas at El Paso)
In mergers and acquisitions (M&A), research asserts that CEOs confront extreme uncertainty, asymmetric information, and cognitive constraints that often lead to value-destroying outcomes. At the same time, recent work on artificial intelligence emphasizes augmentation, a human–AI collaboration (rather than automation). Yet we know little about whether, when, and how AI augmentation alters bounded rationality at the CEO level. We address this gap by examining CEO–AI collaboration in M&A decisions using an algorithmic second-opinion, counterfactual evaluation framework, adapted from medicine and clinical decision science. For each announced acquisition between 2012 and 2022, a large language model generates a counterfactual recommendation to proceed or not proceed with the deal, similar to AI recommendations that are retrospectively compared to physicians’ realized treatment choices to assess decision quality. We then compare these recommendations to CEOs’ realized decisions and assess post-acquisition operating performance. Our results show that AI augmentation systematically improves acquisition outcomes. However, AI recommendations are associated with superior post-acquisition operating performance only in firms that have performed poorly before the acquisition; in already high-performing firms, AI guidance offers limited incremental value. Which suggests that generic algorithmic insights can crowd out valuable firm-specific knowledge. These findings contribute to bounded rationality theory by demonstrating that cognitive limits are dynamic rather than fixed. This shifts decision-making toward bounded optimality and moves beyond blanket claims that “AI improves decisions”.
