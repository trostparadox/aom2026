Born to Be Happy? Revisiting the Entrepreneurship Well-Being Premium

ENT
Boris Nikolaev (Colorado State University); Stephanie Brooke Escudero (Colorado State University); Michael Lerman (Iowa State University)
Over a hundred studies across different times and places have documented that entrepreneurs report higher levels of well-being than employees. This “entrepreneurship well-being premium” is typically interpreted causally—“being your own boss” improves well-being through more autonomy, favorable work design, and basic psychological need fulfillment. Yet, what drives this entrepreneurship well-being premium remains theoretically fragmented and empirically contested. We address this gap in two ways. First, we map five theoretical mechanisms—(1) selection on stable traits (2) job demands-resources, (3) self-determination, (4) procedural utility, and (5) person–environment fit—into distinct stages of the causal process, further deriving discriminating predictions about each mechanism. Second, using three waves of the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) survey, we evaluate these competing predictions with a series of tests. Our results replicate the familiar cross-sectional well-being premium. However, this effect is attenuated or completely disappears once we account for individual and within-family fixed-effects designs. At the same time, we show that individuals who later become entrepreneurs already meaningfully differ from employees on several well-being, personality, and coping dimensions even before entry into entrepreneurship. Critically, we replicate these findings with a pre-registered analysis using the Global Flourishing Study, a two-wave panel of approximately 200,000 participants across 22 countries. Taken together, the evidence in our paper suggests that the “happy entrepreneur” is not made happy by entrepreneurship; rather, the entrepreneurship well-being premium is largely driven by selection. Our results challenge previous studies that interpret the effect as causal and call for re-interpretation of theoretical explanations in most of the entrepreneurship well-being literature.

Instability-Based Processes: A Novel Approach to Advance Micro Theories of Entrepreneurship

ENT
Hamid Vahidnia (Tulane University)
In entrepreneurship and management theories, scholars have offered uncertainty-based processes that explain micro underpinnings of entrepreneurship. These theoretical explanations assume conditions of Knightian uncertainty and elaborate behaviors entrepreneurs employ to effectively grapple with unknown futures. I argue that theoretical accounts are also needed for cases involving entrepreneurs for whom instability is salient, where entrepreneurs face the daunting present. While uncertainty-based processes rely on a logic of norm-governed behavior (by leveraging useful prior beliefs, decision rules, Bayesian learning, etc.), instability-based processes lean on the logic of exception-based action (by throwing out the rule book of existing norms, beliefs, and rules frequently and starting from scratch recurrently). I elaborate and illustrate instability-based processes, explore their links with current theories and empirical research, and offer directions for future research.

Let’s Close the Representation Gap in Entrepreneurship Surveys: Reaching Hidden Populations

ENT
Andreas Rauch (Audencia Business School); Matthias Fink; Daniela Maresch; Hans Landstrom (Lund University); Robert Breitenecker; Johannes Gartner (Delft University of Technology)
Entrepreneurship researchers conducting surveys among hidden populations call for viable approaches to representative sampling because traditional approaches fail for lack of a full register of the target population. We propose and empirically validate a sampling approach that identifies members of hidden populations via social media ads. We discuss the limitations and contingencies and provide a step-by-step implementation checklist for future research. By extending the field’s sampling toolkit, the study helps close the representation gap enabling more robust inference about entrepreneurship in understudied groups including refugees and neurodiverse individuals. More inclusive entrepreneurship research in turn informs evidence-based entrepreneurship policy and practice.

The Topic Landscape of Entrepreneurship

ENT
Joseph E Coombs (Virginia Commonwealth University); Sven Kepes (Virginia Commonwealth University); Imran Kadolkar (Ball State University); Blakley Chase Davis (Virginia Commonwealth University)
Entrepreneurship as a scientific discipline continuously evolves through application of new theories and methodologies. When such growth occurs, it is important to take inventory of the literature, carefully assess what has been done, and identify potential new directions. This is especially true for relatively young fields such as entrepreneurship, where shifting theoretical lenses, new methodological approaches, and continuously growing bodies of knowledge act to blur scholars’ conceptions of the literature’s core topics. To address these concerns, we systematically review the published entrepreneurship literature using natural language processing-based topic modeling. This approach allows us to consider an exceptionally broad conceptualization of the published entrepreneurship literature and yields a 28 topic solution. Based on our topic modeling findings, we provide a comprehensive overview of the entrepreneurship literature’s conceptual structure, including how the literature has evolved over time. Finally, we provide an empirically-derived roadmap for future research.

Using Creativity Rigourosly: Guiding Principles for Creativity in Design Science Research

ENT
Marc Emmanuel Kratz (ESCP Business School); Christoph Seckler (ESCP Business School); Rene Mauer (ESCP Business School); Jan Vom Brocke (University of Liechtenstein); Hanna Buyssens (ESCP-Europe Business School - Berlin)
Design Science (DS) is increasingly viewed as an important research paradigm in entrepreneurship. While advances in other fields have been made in the areas of evaluation methods and analytical methods, the methodological repertoire for leveraging creativity systematically lags behind, although designing a solution for a problem demands a creative leap. The purpose of this paper is to bring creativity to the forefront for DS scholars. Building on Amabile’s componential theory, this paper develops and explicates four theoretically grounded guiding principles that foster creativity in DS. These principles show how creativity can be enacted with state-of-the-art rigor, thereby turning the generation of novelty into a repeatable research capability. The article contributes to both theory and practice by positioning creativity at the forefront of DS, thus enabling a continuous discourse on how to leverage creativity and giving scholars methodological guidance on how to achieve innovative solutions to real-world problems.
