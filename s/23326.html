Why Do Firms Choose To Scale? Understanding Scaling Ambition.

STR
Amanda Calderwood (University of Ulster)
Despite growing interest in business scaling, existing research has primarily focused on how firms scale, often assuming that the ambition to grow is present. As a result, ambition is frequently referenced but rarely theorised in ways that explain how scaling intentions are sustained and organised over time. This paper examines the role of ambition in the scaling process by conceptualising ambition as a dynamic organising process rather than a static individual aspiration. Drawing on a qualitative multiple-case study of six for-profit firms that aspire to scale, are in the process of scaling, or have successfully scaled, the findings show how ambition originates in founder identity and values, is reinforced through growth validation, translated into deliberate strategic choices, embedded within organisational structures and practices, and progressively shared across organisational members. By reconceptualising ambition, this study contributes to scaling research by explaining how ambition is formed and shapes persistence in scaling and coordinated organisational action.

Institutional Correction and Private Firms’R&D Investment:Evidence from Government Arrears Clearance (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Shouyu Yao (Tianjin University); Haidong Tao (Tianjin University); Zhenming Fang (Tianjin University); Chunfeng Wang; Lulu Wen (Tianjin University)
Does correcting implementation deviation revive innovation? We study China’s nationwide arrears clearance campaign as an institutional correction, a one-off, high-priority intervention that restored enforcement without redesigning rules. Using a difference-in-differences design with hand-collected exposure to government-related arrears and a text-based firm-level measure of institutional trust from annual reports, we find a significant increase in private firms’ R&D. Mechanism tests favor a trust channel, namely revised beliefs about credible enforcement, rather than a liquidity channel. Effects are stronger for firms without political connections and for firms more sensitive to institutional conditions, and correction amplifies the leverage of innovation subsidies. The findings show that credible corrective interventions can rapidly repair institutional trust without redesigning existing rules, thereby unlocking forward-looking investment and revealing a distinct source of institutional resilience.

How Does HQ–R&D Distance Shape Employee Spinouts? Evidence from Medical Device Industry

STR
Narae Shin (Seoul National University); Joon Mahn Lee (Seoul National University); Joonhyung Bae (Sungkyunkwan University)
Drawing on the literature on organizational design and employee entrepreneurship, we examine how geographic separation between corporate headquarters (HQ) and R&D units shapes employee spinout formation. We argue that greater HQ–R&D distance increases spinout formation by weakening managerial control and intensifying employees’ ideational frustration. We further suggest that this association is contingent on both internal coordination and external entrepreneurial conditions. We show that the positive association between HQ–R&D distance and spinout formation is attenuated when HQ and R&D units are tightly connected through internal linkages, but amplified when R&D units possess superior technological capabilities relative to HQ. We also show that a stronger local venture capital environment surrounding R&D units strengthens this relationship. Using a firm-year panel of publicly traded U.S. medical device firms, we provide evidence in support of our hypotheses. Overall, our results highlight how organizational design interacts with internal coordination and local ecosystems to shape employee entrepreneurship.

Divisionary Growth and Startup Performance

STR
Constantin Prox (Not Associated)
When is an increase in organizational size beneficial? In response to recent disagreements on the benefits of early startup growth, this paper constructs and tests a theory of how the addition of employees to a growing organization impacts the organization’s performance. It proposes different effects by whether the new employee increases the functional division of labor. Analyzing a large sample of startups selling internet technologies, distinctive performance patterns are found for hires used to establish new activities (divisionary growth) versus hires used to provide capacity in existing activities (non-divisionary growth). In this sample, early divisionary growth is linked to significant performance benefits, while early non-divisionary growth is linked to a higher chance of failure. Mechanism tests show evidence that early divisionary growth aids process development, setting the stage for later non-divisionary growth.

Strategic Mutual Embeddedness: How Suppliers Escape Value Chain Lock-in

STR
Zhang Qi (Renmin University of China); Guowei Lai (Xiamen University); Bing Chen
How do specialized suppliers achieve capability upgrading when geopolitical disruption transforms lead firms from partners into adversaries? GVC theory emphasizes vertical pathways through relationships with lead firms; when technological blockade forecloses these relationships, existing theory predicts stagnation. We identify an alternative: horizontal breakthrough through deep collaboration among complementary suppliers. A longitudinal case study of two Chinese automotive electronics suppliers (2009-2022) reveals how firms facing complementary forms of value chain lock-in constructed a governance arrangement we term Strategic Mutual Embeddedness—bidirectional capability embedding that generates coupled upgrading trajectories unavailable to either firm alone. We make three contributions. First, we extend GVC upgrading theory by identifying horizontal pathways that bypass lead firm gatekeeping. Second, we theorize Strategic Mutual Embeddedness as a distinct governance mode, introducing complementary lock-in as its structural precondition. Third, we qualify the threat-rigidity hypothesis: technological blockade enabled innovation by foreclosing existing pathways while providing institutional resources for constructing alternatives. Keywords: value chain upgrading, horizontal collaboration, strategic mutual embeddedness, geopolitical disruption, threat-rigidity, interorganizational governance
