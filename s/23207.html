Beyond the Liability of Foreignness: Cultural Distance Enhances Immigrant Entrepreneurship (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Johannes Kleinhempel (Warwick Business School); Robert J. Pidduck (Old Dominion University)
Does cultural distance constrain or foster immigrant entrepreneurship? On the one hand, culturally distant immigrants could be less likely to engage in entrepreneurship due to institutional unfamiliarity and heightened discrimination. On the other hand, larger cultural distances could enhance scheme switching and opportunity recognition and thereby increase immigrant entrepreneurship. Adjudicating between these perspectives is difficult because dyadic cultural distance effects are often confounded with origin- and destination-country characteristics. Using data on 11,813 first-generation immigrants from 83 origin countries residing in 30 destination countries, we focus on the cultural distance variations across 1,018 origin-destination dyads while holding constant origin and destination country characteristics. We find that cultural distance is positively associated with immigrant entrepreneurship: immigrants from culturally more distant origins are more likely to be entrepreneurs than immigrants whose country-of-origin culture is more proximate to the country-of-destination culture. Importantly, this association is not driven by discrimination-driven “push” entrepreneurship, or by geographic, institutional, or economic distance. We also show that munificent destination-country institutions strengthen the positive cultural distance–entrepreneurship association: it is more pronounced under supportive migrant integration policies and in culturally loose countries. Overall, our findings reposition cultural distance as an asset rather than a liability for entrepreneurship.

Doing Business for Good or Doing Good for Business? Entrepreneurial Diasporas’ Philanthropy

ENT
Sarika Pruthi (San Jose State University); Susanna Khavul (San Jose State University)
Embedded in dual country contexts, high technology entrepreneurial diaspora are positioned as new development actors and philanthropists. Studies have explored immigrant and immigrant-owned small businesses’ engagement in social initiatives. However, with few exceptions, research is mainly focused on social responsibility in the country of residence (CoR) and diaspora business investing in the country of origin (CoO). Philanthropy among highly technology entrepreneurial diasporas is less understood, with a relative neglect of the micro mechanisms and individual social networks for channeling diasporas’ giving. Based on a grounded theory approach using 20 case studies, we find that high technology entrepreneurial diasporas give in both the CoR and CoO, strategically leveraging transnational social networks for inspiring their giving, identifying targets, and transferring resources. However, they are heterogenous in their (1) choice of CoR or CoO as a prime geographic focus, and (2) growth strategies for their philanthropy based on their motivations and perceived cultural norms, outcomes and impact of their philanthropy in the CoR and CoO. These findings contribute to the immigrant entrepreneurship and international entrepreneurship literatures.

National Conflict and Immigrant Entrepreneurship: Evidence from the US-China Conflict

ENT
Suzan Sim (IESE Business School)
Despite the current trend toward deglobalization, how national conflict reshapes immigrant entrepreneurship remains unclear. This study shows that national conflict can unexpectedly increase the funding of domestic startups founded by immigrants from the conflicting country as investment is redirected away from that country toward these startups. Leveraging the rapid escalation of the US-China conflict in 2018 in a difference-in-differences framework, we find that Chinese-founded US startups raised significantly more venture capital than White-founded US startups following the conflict. Consistent with the proposed mechanism, this effect is concentrated in technology sectors in which US investors had previously invested heavily in China, and we observe consistent results at the investor level. In addition, we show that Chinese-founded startups with ethnically integrated founding teams captured more of the redirected capital. Overall, this study advances our understanding of how geopolitical tensions reshape capital allocation and generate unintended advantages for immigrant entrepreneurs.

When Immigrants Outperform: Family, Wealth & Industry Contingencies in Entrepreneurial Performance

ENT
Omar Yousef Alrubaiaan (King Saud University); Mi (Kelly) Hoang Tran (Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute); Murali D.R. Chari (Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute)
Immigrant entrepreneurship has attracted growing scholarly interest, but research has largely emphasized business entry rather than performance after founding. Evidence on immigrant entrepreneurs’ post-entry outcomes remains limited and inconclusive. Addressing this gap, this study examines how social, financial, and industry contexts condition the relationship between immigrant status and entrepreneurial performance. Drawing on mixed embeddedness theory, we argue that family size strengthens immigrant entrepreneurs’ performance advantage, while greater wealth weakens it. We also propose that this advantage is more pronounced in blue-collar industries than in white-collar sectors. Using longitudinal data on 3,355 ventures from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (1997-2019), we find strong support for these arguments. Our findings advance mixed embeddedness theory and deepen understanding of when and why immigrant entrepreneurs outperform their native-born counterparts.

When Multiculturalism Helps, and Hurts: Contextualizing the Liability of Foreignness in New Ventures

ENT
Anastasia Veronica Graham Bailey (Rutgers University); Benjamin A. Campbell (The Ohio State University)
Immigrant entrepreneurs face liabilities of foreignness (LOF) when founding ventures in unfamiliar institutional environments. Integrating institutional theory with the knowledge-based view, we examine how multicultural features, co-founder nationality, language use, and industry globalization, shape venture performance for immigrant- and native-led firms. Using data from the U.S. Census Bureau’s Survey of Business Owners, we find that these features are associated with higher performance among immigrant-led ventures, while producing asymmetric effects for native-led firms: immigrant co-founders may extend LOF, whereas multilingual operations enhance performance. These findings highlight the context-dependent role of multiculturalism in mitigating or amplifying LOF across founder types.
