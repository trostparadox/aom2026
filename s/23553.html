Conceptualisation, Measurement, and Validation of Consumers’ Perceived Sustainability Concerns

TIM
Xiaofan Chen (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Emma Slade (University of Bristol Business School); Xiaojun Wang; Davit Marikyan (Newcastle University)
While autonomous artificial intelligence (AI) systems or autonomous technologies (ATs) have been suggested to promote sustainable development, insufficient research has been done to investigate the sustainability of the technologies themselves. Concerns are rising about the technologies’ negative impact on sustainability, such as energy consumption, deskilling, and job replacement. Based on the theoretical foundation of the three pillars of sustainability, this research conceptualises a multi-dimensional construct named perceived sustainability concerns (PSC) to capture consumers’ perceptions of the unsustainable consequences of AT usage. The construct consists of environmental concerns, social concerns, and economic concerns. This research also develops a measurement scale for the PSC construct and validates it in a nomological network. We find that consumers’ PSC generates considerable negative influence on AT acceptance and fully mediates the effect of personal norms on acceptance. Theoretical contributions and practical implications are also discussed.

Human-Centered Design for AR Shopping: Linking Interface Features to Motivation and Choice

TIM
Anjali Pathania (Central University of Jammu); Gowhar Rasool (Central University of Jammu); Syed Javeed (Central University of Jammu)
As online shopping becomes more immersive, Augmented Reality (AR) is transforming digital consumer experiences. While previous studies have highlighted AR’s ability to boost purchase intentions, little attention has been given to how specific AR features affect consumer motivation and decision-making. To address this gap, this research applies the Stimulus-Organism-Response (SOR) framework alongside Self-Determination Theory (SDT) to examine how core AR platform drivers interact with intrinsic motivation drivers such as autonomy, competence, and relatedness to influence consumer judgments, including willingness to pay and confidence in choice. Data were gathered from 468 participants who engaged with AR and non-AR e-tailing platforms. Using PLS-SEM and fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), findings reveal that interactivity and novelty significantly enhance motivational states in AR contexts, whereas vividness plays a stronger role in non-AR settings. AR quality was linked only to autonomy, indicating varied effectiveness across motivational dimensions. Additionally, product uncertainty partially moderated these relationships, underscoring the impact of contextual risk.

Personalized Emails as Digital Nudges: Balancing Engagement, Impulsivity, and Privacy

TIM
Sabine Khalil (Illinois State University); Bahae Samhan (Illinois State University); Logan Birchbauer (CNA)
Personalized email marketing has become a key driver of consumer engagement, utilizing data such as purchase history and browsing patterns to send targeted messages. Beyond boosting loyalty, personalization can also act as a digital nudge that encourages impulsive buying by triggering emotional responses and bypassing rational decision-making. This study investigates how value perception, timing, frequency, and privacy concerns influence the effectiveness of personalized emails in promoting engagement and impulsive purchase decisions. Using survey data from 437 consumers and multiple regression analysis, we identify the cognitive and emotional processes behind consumer responses. Results show that well-timed and balanced personalization enhances engagement and loyalty, whereas excessive frequency or intrusive targeting diminishes effectiveness and trust. The study contributes to the IS literature by demonstrating how personalization strategies interact with user thinking and feelings, offering insights for designing email campaigns that enhance engagement while respecting user privacy.

Predicting User Engagement in Live Video: A Method Based on Expectation Confirmation Theory

TIM
Yingjie Chen (Renmin University of China)
Existing research on live video recommendation primarily focuses on predicting the user's next click on a live video, neglecting the significance of user engagement. This paper proposes an algorithm based on Expectation Confirmation Theory (ECT) to predict user engagement by modeling user expectation and perceived experience. User expectation is determined by the historical experience of similar live videos, while the perceived experience depends on whether the target video aligns with the user’s evolving content preferences. To effectively model the evolution of user preferences, this paper employs a multi-head causal self-attention mechanism to capture user preferences and uses historical engagement sequences to control the mask matrix, capturing users' dynamic preferences. Finally, this paper integrates user expectation and perceived experience to predict engagement for each target video. To evaluate the performance of the proposed recommendation algorithm, experiments are conducted on a real-world live video dataset based on user's viewing behavior. The results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm outperforms baselines in both predicting user engagement and Top-N recommendation tasks. Moreover, this paper conducts several experiments to validate the robustness of the proposed recommendation algorithm and finally empirically tested the impact of ECT on user engagement.

The Conditional Value of Consumer Satisfaction: Innovation Choice Under Economic Recession

TIM
Jae Kyun Yoo (Fairfield University); Minjae Lee (Southern Connecticut State University)
Economic recessions reshape consumer expectations and amplify scrutiny, altering how firms’ capabilities translate into performance. This study examines how recession-driven shifts in consumer expectations intensify the vulnerability of firms with high preexisting consumer satisfaction and how their innovation choices shape the extent to which this capability becomes a liability. We argue that recessions heighten negative reactions to perceived performance deviations, making high pre-recession satisfaction—typically a strategic strength—especially vulnerable. Using data from the U.S. automobile industry spanning the 2008 financial crisis, we find that consumer satisfaction declines significantly during recession and that firms with higher preexisting satisfaction experience disproportionately larger losses. We further demonstrate that innovation strategy plays a decisive moderating role: incremental product redesigns, which preserve familiarity and signal stability, mitigate the recession penalty for high-satisfaction firms, whereas new product introductions do not offer comparable protection. The study provides a theoretical account of how economic downturns can invert the value of demand-side capabilities and identifies conditions under which incremental innovation outperforms more transformative approaches. Managerially, the findings offer guidance on how firms can align innovation portfolios with shifting consumer psychology to protect reputational assets and sustain performance during an economic disruption.
