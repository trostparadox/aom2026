Rethinking Job Demands–Resources Theory: Insights from Digital Transformation in Chinese SOEs

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 4:00PM–5:00PM
Philadelphia Marriott Downtown — Poster Wall 12
ODC

People

Authors: Yingqian Wang Manchester University; Natalie Cunningham The University of Manchester; Jaco Renken

Session overview

Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) theory is a widely used framework for understanding how workplace changes affect employee well-being and performance. However, most JD-R research is based on quantitative methods in Western contexts. Digital transformation (DT) in Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs) presents a unique context that questions core JD-R assumptions: it involves multilevel radical change, culturally embedded institutional arrangements, and systematic role identity reconstruction. This study asks: How do JD-R theory's core conceptualizations operate in the Chinese SOE DT process, and what new understandings emerge? We conducted an interpretive case study of a major SOE's Financial Shared Services Centre (FSSC) transformation. Our findings discuss JD-R theory's boundary conditions and propose a dynamic constructivist perspective emphasizing interpretive processes. First, we argue that DT, as a systematic organizational change, first influences JD-R changes at organizational and team levels, which are then directionally allocated across hierarchical positions, system roles, and age cohorts through power and cultural mechanisms. Second, we find employees retrospectively reclassify the same organizational changes as demands or resources as external contexts evolve; these classifications change over time during the DT process, challenging the static perspective of some JD-R studies. Third, we find classic Western "resources" like work autonomy are experienced as threats in SOE contexts due to high power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and accountability pressures, which rethinks the scale-based measurement approach to understanding JD-R outcomes. This study contributes to organization theory by revealing how cultural-institutional contexts shape the meanings of work characteristics, with implications for theory development and cross-cultural generalizability.
