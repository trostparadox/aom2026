Session overview

Quantitative research remains foundational to advancing management knowledge, yet even well-intentioned scholars can inadvertently tell misleading stories with statistics when inference is treated as a mechanical exercise rather than a disciplined form of judgment. In management research, p-values and conventional thresholds (e.g., p < 0.05) too often become rhetorical shortcuts for credibility, while uncertainty, effect magnitude, and alternative explanations receive less attention. This Professional Development Workshop (PDW) provides an applied, participant-centered toolkit for recognizing and preventing common inferential misuses that can inflate false positives, mask practically meaningful effects, and invite overconfident causal claims. The session blends short, high-impact segments with live demonstrations and guided small-group activities. Participants will examine six recurring “misuses” that appear across the quantitative literature: (1) p-hacking and undisclosed analytic flexibility; (2) the “cult” of 0.05 and optional stopping; (3) statistical significance versus practical significance; (4) “not significant” does not mean “no effect”; (5) correlation does not imply causation; and (6) Simpson’s paradox, in which aggregate patterns reverse once we account for meaningful subgroups. Rather than simply critiquing these pitfalls, we emphasize constructive alternatives: transparent analysis plans, effect-size and confidence-interval thinking, power-aware interpretation, robustness checks, and careful causal language. Designed for doctoral students and early-career scholars (but welcoming to all AOM members), the PDW aims to strengthen methodological literacy that supports more credible theory testing, more informative manuscript writing, and more responsible evidence-based recommendations. Participants will leave with an “Inference Integrity” checklist and sample language for reporting results in a way that is both statistically precise and substantively meaningful.
