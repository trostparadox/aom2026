Job Hopping as a Predictor of Selection Decisions, Turnover, and Performance

HR
Jake T. Harrison (University of Iowa); Emily D. Campion (University of Iowa); Chad Van Iddekinge (University of Iowa); Michael A Campion (Purdue University); Carter Gibson
Despite its prevalence in the modern world of work, the phenomenon of job hopping remains poorly understood. We conducted three studies to clarify what job hopping is, how it is perceived during the hiring process, and how it relates to work outcomes. In Study 1, human resource professionals (N = 61) indicated that they generally view job hopping unfavorably because it can signal low commitment or low performance. In Study 2, we designed and implemented an experimental hiring scenario (N = 85) and found that hiring managers consistently ranked job-hopping applicants lower than non-job-hopping applicants, and that this occurred through the partial mediating effects of lower expected commitment and performance. We also found that evidence of career progression buffered negative perceptions of job hopping, while occupational changes exacerbated negative perceptions of job hopping. In Study 3, operational data on job applicants (N = 30,640) showed that job hopping behavior related to fewer job offers, higher turnover, and lower job performance. In addition, a measure of job hopping provided incremental validity in the prediction of these outcomes over commonly used selection procedures. Together, these studies show that although there are caveats, job hopping generally presents perceived and real risks to organizations.

'Potential' as an Organizational Object: Talent Assessment, Selection and Development Practices

HR
Manuel Baeriswyl (University of Lugano); Jeanne Mengis (Università della Svizzera Italiana); Davide Nicolini (Warwick Business School)
Organizations increasingly orient their focus toward identifying and developing employees’ ‘potential’ rather than solely managing them based on their performance. Yet, we know very little about what a focus on potential entails, how it is organized and how it is made actionable in practice. Drawing on an 18- month ethnographic study of a national elite team sports organization and its partner clubs, we explore how practitioners assess, select and develop athletes’ potential through the enactment of a potential-focused strategy. We find that potential is dynamically made into a tangible organizational object through a bundle of practices that make it reportable, allow its development and buffer the impact of poor performances in organizational activities. We show that, despite a focus on potential, athletes’ performances are central to the dynamic enactment of a focus on potential for the organization, rather than opposed to it. We shed light on crucial challenges faced by the organization and its members tied to accountability, visibility, and reflexivity created by a focus on potential and possible opportunities tied its development through organizational practice. We link our discoveries to current debates on talent management, performance evaluation and organizational attention.

Do High-Performance Work Systems Promote or Inhibit Employee Turnover Intention? A Meta-Analysis

HR
Haobo Tian (Peking University); zhang zijian (Shandong University)
Although prior research has primarily shown the beneficial effects of high-performance work systems (HPWSs), emerging findings provided some inconsistent findings on the impact of HPWS on employee turnover intention, with unclear underlying mechanisms and boundary conditions. To elucidate whether and how HPWSs serve as assets or liabilities, we employe job demands-resources (JD-R) theory to investigate three distinct mediators (job resources, challenge job demands, and hindrance job demands). Furthermore, we examine the contextual boundaries of HPWS effectiveness by investigating where (i.e., national culture) these effects vary. To provide a more comprehensive explanation, we adopted a meta-analytic review to analyze 1,111 independent correlations (N = 1,576,159) from 811 articles. The results indicate that HPWSs can reduce employee turnover intention through enhanced job resources and reduced hindrance job demands, while paradoxically increasing turnover intention through heightened challenge job demands. Comparative mediation analyses showed that job resources have more powerful explanatory power in linking HPWSs to employee turnover intention than job demands. Moreover, moderation analyses suggested that the negative relationship between HPWSs and hindrance job demands is attenuated in low individualism cultural contexts.

Fast Track or Exit Ramp? Why Mistimed Promotions Spark Turnover

HR
Jay Hardy (Oregon State University); Chase Thiel (University of Wyoming)
Organizations often promote with retention goals in mind. Yet, exits in months following promotions are paradoxically common. In this paper, we argue that a promotion’s timing affects whether it stabilizes attachment or accelerates exit. Specifically, we propose a “Goldilocks Framework” governs the relationship between promotion timing and post-promotion turnover, such that both early and late promotions generate higher risk than those that occur within the normative window. In Study 1, analysis of a large field sample of supervisory promotions (N = 11,422) showed that turnover risk was indeed higher following both faster- and slower-than-typical promotions. To adjudicate among plausible explanations for this finding, we organized rival theoretical explanations into three psychological force families, whose theoretical salience varies with timing. Then, in Study 2, we leveraged a novel creative destruction-inspired design (N = 850) to compare the relative explanatory strength of mechanisms within each family side-by-side. Results suggest that exits following faster-than-typical promotions were best explained by underdeveloped organizational embeddedness; off-schedule promotions by reduced organizational trust; and slower-than-typical promotions by interpersonal justice. Strong job markets further amplified readiness and disembeddedness pathways. Collectively, these findings clarify how and when misaligned timing contributes to increased post-promotion turnover risk, offering actionable levers for mitigation.

From Lateness to Leaving: Distinct Withdrawal Profiles and Transitions Predict Turnover

HR
Rakoon Piyanontalee (The Pennsylvania State University); Yea Hee Ko (Singapore Management University)
Research on the progression of withdrawal model suggests that employees disengage from work by moving from relatively minor acts (e.g., lateness) to more consequential ones (e.g., absence) and ultimately to voluntary turnover. Although prior work documents positive links among these behaviors, evidence is largely variable-centered and static, leaving open whether withdrawal unfolds through distinct behavioral configurations and whether escalation, as opposed to stable withdrawal, uniquely predicts impending exit. Using 8 years of factory attendance records, we apply latent profile analysis to identify systematic combinations of lateness and absence behaviors and latent transition analysis to capture movement among withdrawal profiles over time. Results reveal three distinct attendance-withdrawal profiles (Stable Attenders, Chronic Latecomers, and Volatile Absentees) that differ in both average levels and variability of lateness/absence. Survival analyses show that membership in more withdrawal-intensive profiles is associated with substantially higher subsequent turnover risk. Further, employees who transition into more withdrawal-intensive profiles exhibit higher turnover risk than those who remain stably withdrawn, indicating that dynamic escalation provides incremental predictive power beyond static withdrawal levels. Together, these findings offer a view of employee withdrawal behaviors as a multi-faceted, dynamic construct, not simply captured by linear temporal progression.
