When Career Potential Goes Unrealized: Toward A Theory of Constrained Career Agency

CAR
Surendra Babu Talluri (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Careers research has long emphasized individual agency as a key driver of career success, showing individuals as proactive actors who shape their careers through goal setting, skill development, and adaptability. Yet an important question remains insufficiently explained. Many individuals who are motivated, capable, and proactive still fail to realize their career potential. Existing theories offer only partial explanations of this phenomenon. Agency-centered perspectives assume that effort and proactivity reliably translate into favorable outcomes, whereas structural and inequality-based accounts emphasize constraints but often treat individuals as passive recipients. To address this critical question, we develop a theory of constrained career agency that provides an integrative perspective of how agency and constraint jointly operate in the process of career enactment. By shifting attention from whether individuals exercise agency to whether and when agency can take effect, our theory reconceptualizes unrealized career potential as a misalignment between agentic action and opportunity structures, offering a process-oriented framework for understanding career divergence under conditions of constraint.

Challenged or Hindered Under Career Plateau? The Double-Edged Sword Effect of Underdog Expectations

CAR
Wenjing Cai; Li Lin (Nanjing Agricultural University); Sergey Gorbatov (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Employees who perceive that others underestimate their competence, known as underdog expectations, often display paradoxical reactions: some become more motivated to excel, whereas others disengage or behave unethically. Using an exploratory, sequential mixed-methods design, this research examines how underdog expectations shape employee unethical behavior. In Study 1, we conducted in-depth interview to identify the emotional, cognitive, and behavioral responses associated with being underestimated, thereby laying the groundwork for the subsequent quantitative study. Drawing on transactional theory of stress and coping (TTSC), we propose a dual-path model in which underdog expectations function as a socially evaluative stressor that elicits either challenge appraisals, which reduce time theft, or hindrance appraisals, which increase time theft. We further theorize that career plateau conditions the dual-paths by weakening the challenge appraisal and strengthening the hindrance appraisal. Results from a time-lagged field study provide empirical evidence that underdog expectations represent a double-edged social experience, that is, activating ethical self-regulation for some employees while provoking disengaged deviance for others, depending on their career resource context. By foregrounding TTSC, this study extends the underdog expectations literature and advances understanding of the ethical consequences of being underestimated at work.

Constrained by the Clock: How Career Scripts Shape the Evolution of Career Values

CAR
Yihui Lin (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics)
We examined how career values—authenticity, balance, and challenge—evolve among Chinese millennials in private-sector internet firms to understand how institutionalized norms shape career-stage specific priorities. Drawing on the Kaleidoscope Career Model (KCM) and career script theory, we conceptualize scripts as meso-level interpretive frameworks that translate macro logics into micro value priorities. Employing a qualitative method, we conducted and analyzed 34 semi-structured interviews with millennial employees from two private internet firms, using abductive thematic analysis across early (0–7 years), mid (8–15 years), and late (15+ years) stages. We find that early careers center on challenge as legitimacy—rapid learning, mobility, and visible progress—while authenticity is secondary and work–life balance (WLB) emerges reactively under strain. During mid-career, WLB becomes primary amid burnout and caregiving responsibilities; a widely internalized age-35 norm recasts challenge toward stability and risk management, and authenticity is consciously deferred yet resurfaces at moments of value misfit. The participants projected that late careers are anchored in non-negotiable WLB, expressing authenticity as legacy and generativity, and reframing challenge as selective, purpose-driven engagement. Together, these results indicate that career values are socially constructed and dynamically mediated by career scripts that channel institutional pressures into evolving value hierarchies across stages. The study extends KCM by specifying a socio-cultural mediation mechanism, refines career script theory as dynamic and agentic over time, and informs stage-appropriate, culturally resonant career development in high-pressure, collectivist contexts.

Not All Away From Work Are Equal: Away Legitimacy, and Promotion in Organizations

CAR
Lingling Zhang (Nanjing University); Jia Li (Wake Forest University); Yunhui Huang (Nanjing University Business School); Julie Holliday Wayne (Wake Forest University)
Employees’ short-term absences from work for personal or work-related reasons (away from work; AFW) are common in organizations. However, prior research has focused primarily on single forms of long-term leave (e.g., maternity leave), offering limited insight into how short-term absences shape promotion outcomes. Using a multi-state hazard model and secondary data from a large service organization with a multi-tiered hierarchy, we examine how the legitimacy of different types of AFW affects promotion outcomes. We find that AFW functions as an observable signal that managers interpret when assessing promotability, the signal value of AFW is highly context dependent. AFW taken because of mandatory company training, which employees are required to attend, is considered legitimate and does not affect promotion prospects. In contrast, other types of AFW, including personal AFW and non-mandatory work-related AFW, are considered illegitimate and significantly reduce the likelihood of promotion. Moreover, the negative effects of AFW are weaker at higher organizational ranks. By revealing the signaling role of AFW and its contextual and hierarchical contingencies, this study extends signaling theory to intra-organizational career dynamics and highlights potential sources of bias in promotion decisions.
