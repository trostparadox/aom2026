Session overview

Whilst organizations across the globe increasingly recognize neurodiversity as an important dimension of workplace diversity, disclosure remains a central and unresolved challenge in efforts to create neuroinclusive workplaces. In fact, current estimates suggest that one person out of five is neurodivergent, yet many neurodivergent employees choose not to disclose their neurotype at work due to concerns about stigma, bias, and negative career consequences. Disclosure thus represents a critical tension: it can enable access to reasonable accommodations and support, but it can also expose individuals to discrimination, misunderstanding, or identity-based marginalization, leading many to engage in concealment or ‘masking’ behaviors with detrimental consequences for wellbeing and performance. The aim of this panel is to move beyond simplistic debates about whether employees should disclose and instead interrogate the conditions under which disclosure becomes necessary, risky or redundant. We aim; i) to examine the opportunities and challenges associated with neurodiversity disclosure across organizational contexts, ii) to critically reflect on the role of HR, leadership, and organizational practices in shaping the ‘inclusive work design by default’ work environments, and iii) to identify the future research agenda that advances ethical, inclusive, and evidence-based approaches to neurodiversity at work.
