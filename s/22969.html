Converting Conviction: Strategic Concealment and Fundraising Success Among Crisis Pregnancy Centers

OMT
Alessandro Piazza (Rice University); Filippo Carlo Wezel (USI Lugano, Switzerland)
How do organizations sustain moral contestation? While research has documented the role of organizations in fueling polarization, less understood are the strategies these organizations deploy to attract the financial resources necessary to maintain their activities. We theorize that organizations engaged in moral boundary work can use strategic concealment—not merely to hide from hostile audiences, but to proactively attract resources from sympathetic ones. We distinguish backstage concealment, which cloaks moral agendas in professional or technical authority, from frontstage concealment, which positions organizations at symbolic boundaries to intercept target audiences. We develop and test these ideas in the context of crisis pregnancy centers (CPCs), nonprofit organizations dedicated to discouraging abortion that collectively raised over $1.5 billion annually by 2019. Using longitudinal data on 1,788 CPCs in the United States from 2010 to 2019, we find that both backstage concealment (offering ultrasound services) and frontstage concealment (locating near abortion clinics) significantly increase donor contributions. The effectiveness of these strategies, however, depends on community context: backstage concealment generates stronger returns in more educated communities, while frontstage concealment proves most effective in communities with stronger religious opposition to abortion. Contrary to expectations, backstage concealment also yields greater returns in high-opposition areas—suggesting that concealment may operate through affirmation of existing beliefs rather than persuasion alone. By tracing how concealment strategies convert moral conviction into organizational resources, this study illuminates the financial infrastructure that sustains polarization.

Bricolage Dynamics and Strategic Renewal in Times of Prolonged Resource Scarcity

OMT
Guting Shen (University of Birmingham)
This study develops a process model that explains how organisational bricolage unfolds under conditions of prolonged resource scarcity and enables strategic renewal, drawing on a historical case study of the Sun Sun Department Store during wartime Shanghai from 1937 to 1945. While existing research largely recognises organisational bricolage as an effective response to resource scarcity, little is known about its evolution and long-term consequences when scarcity persists. Our analysis reveals that bricolage neither recedes over time nor remains unchanged under prolonged resource scarcity, but unfolds through three successive dominant forms: survival bricolage, value-enhancing bricolage, and repositioning bricolage. Transitions across these forms are shaped by environmental changes and the cumulative effects of prior bricolage practices. This study thus advances organisational bricolage research by delineating the evolving nature of bricolage rather than treating it as a temporary or static response. It also contributes to research on strategic renewal by showing how successive forms of bricolage generate enabling conditions that progressively support organisation-wide reconfiguration and closer alignment with an evolving environment, thereby offering an alternative pathway to strategic renewal.

When Controversies Hurt or Help: How Event Morality and Specificity Drive Peer Market Reactions

OMT
Nahuel Depino-Besada; Ekin Ilseven (Católica Lisbon School of Business & Economics); Mircea Epure (Pompeu Fabra University)
We examine how corporate controversies affect uninvolved industry peers by introducing a moral lens into spillover research. We theorize that event immorality triggers category-based condemnation, whereas event-firm specificity channels blame toward the focal firm and enables positive opportunity spillovers. Accordingly, we expect immorality (specificity) to worsen (improve) peers’ market reactions, with firm specificity attenuating the negative effect of immorality. We confirm our predictions through event studies on 531 U.S. publicly listed firms (32,385 firm-day observations) and relying on dictionary-based analyses of 3,556 news pieces on corporate controversies. Additional analyses distinguish severity from immorality, and show that, consistent with a morality-based punishment mechanism, negative spillovers are stronger for firms headquartered in Democrat-leaning states. By shifting the analysis from the firm to the event level, we are able to show that spillovers cannot be fully explained without accounting for factors such as immorality and specificity of controversies. Our findings also show that industries function as categorical judgment devices imbued with moral expectations, which can trigger industry-wide condemnations.

Competitive and Cooperative Market Regimes: Structural Transitions and Scalable Coordination

OMT
Amador Lopez (Autonomous University of Madrid); Juan Ramón Campos Blázquez (Autonomous University of Madrid); Luis Rubio-Andrada; Laura Vidal Serrano (Autonomous University of Madrid)
Market coordination is often conceptualized as a decentralized process in which autonomous actors align through competition and price-mediated interaction. While this view has generated powerful insights under conditions of limited interdependence, it becomes increasingly strained as markets scale and value creation depends on tightly coupled activities across multiple actors. Existing research has responded by documenting the coexistence of competition and cooperation, yet it continues to treat these dynamics primarily as firm-level strategies or relational choices, offering limited insight into how markets reorganize when prevailing coordination mechanisms become structurally inadequate. Our work develops a structural perspective of market coordination in which competition and cooperation operate as alternative coordination regimes. From this perspective, markets reorganize when existing coordination mechanisms lose viability, and artificial agents contribute endogenously to coordination under conditions of scale and interdependence. We argue that coordination regimes depend on the underlying architecture of interdependence, and that increasing non-separability can destabilize competitive coordination, triggering discontinuous transitions toward cooperative regimes. Cooperative coordination, in turn, can remain viable at scale even when global optimization is infeasible. Integrating insights from strategy, complexity economics, and organization theory, the paper introduces a lightweight formal mechanism that illustrates how changes in disagreement feasibility generate threshold effects separating coordination regimes. It further shows how distributed artificial agents can mediate these transitions by reducing effective coordination frictions, thereby enhancing the scalability of cooperative coordination without reintroducing centralized control.

Accommodation or Consolidation? Firms’ Nationalist Positioning In Response to Hostile Foreign Policy

OMT
Natalie Wen Wen (HEC Paris); Mia Raynard (The University of British Columbia); Chuchao Zhong (The University of British Columbia); Yasir Dewan (HEC Paris); Royston Greenwood (University of Alberta)
Research on geopolitics and organizations typically predicts that foreign hostility pushes firms toward nationalist alignment with the home country. We complicate this view by showing that firms’ nationalist positioning depends on whether foreign engagement remains viable or is foreclosed. When engagement remains viable, firms confront competing pressures from domestic and foreign audiences. When it is foreclosed, domestic audiences dominate. We test this argument by examining how Chinese listed firms adjusted their rhetorical nationalism in response to two U.S. policy instruments during the U.S.–China trade war: tariff escalation and export-control Entity List designation. Results show that tariff-exposed firms reduced nationalist rhetoric, a pattern we label accommodation, whereas Entity List-designated firms intensified nationalist rhetoric, a pattern we label consolidation. CEOs with Communist Party membership attenuated accommodation and amplified consolidation, while board directors with Mao-era socialization weakened the decline in nationalism and amplified attention to international economic friction following tariffs. Our findings advance understanding of how organizations navigate political embeddedness under geopolitical pressure.
