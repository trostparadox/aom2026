The Right Role in the Right Place: Idea vs. Tech Roles, Brokerage Benefits, and Creative Performance

OMT
Jehyun Chung (Seoul National University)
As technology is becoming an integral aspect of innovation and creative work, I investigate the difference in network structure and creative performance between Idea-based roles who focus on creating novel ideas, and Tech-based roles who focus on technical implementation tasks. I argue that Tech-based roles are more likely to occupy brokerage positions than Idea-based roles as they focus on technical skills that are more generally applicable across projects than ideas with specific identities. However, Idea-based roles benefit more in their performance from occupying brokerage positions, as non-redundant information enhances their idea creation tasks. I find support in my hypotheses with collaboration and performance data of 5 years in Game Jams, which are short-term competitions where developers collaborate to build a game of a given theme. This study contributes to the network literature by suggesting that role differences could systematically shape brokerage positions and their benefits particularly in technology-driven environments.

A Marchian Perspective on Exploratory Play as a Mode of Human–AI Interaction

OMT
Feichin Ted Tschang (Singapore Management University); Esteve Almirall
This paper develops a conceptual framework to explain how play functions as a mode of exploration in human–AI interaction. Building on March’s Technology of Foolishness and the organizational play literature, we theorize exploratory play as a cognitive and behavioral orientation that relaxes goals, rules, memory, and evaluative constraints in order to enable wide-ranging and itinerant search. We first reinterpret March’s framework in relation to contemporary theories of play and creativity, then illustrate its relevance through historical and contemporary cases of tinkering. We extend this framework to human interaction with generative AI, arguing that large language models introduce a novel environment for exploratory play by enabling rapid linguistic prototyping, iterative reframing, and safe experimentation. Rather than treating AI as a substitute for human creativity, we show how human intuition, imagination, and sensemaking remain central to guiding exploration and recognizing value. The framework offers a theoretical lens for understanding how human–AI interaction may increase the conditions under which unexpected insights and novel strategic possibilities emerge.

Selling the Unexpected: How Organization Manufactures Serendipity

OMT
Diansha Wang; Ziyun Fan (University of York)
This study introduces ‘manufacturing serendipity’ to explain how organizations strategically construct the felt experience of unexpected opportunity. Drawing on a qualitative case study of Chinese admission agencies (2004–2024), we demonstrate how serendipity is manufactured through three forms of strategic interplay of uncertainty and certainty: managed contrast, engineered compression and black-boxed fusion. These forms unfold across shifts in regulatory governance, from localized experimentation to technocratic re-regulation to moral governance, progressively transforming uncertainty into a commodified emotional asset. Our study contributes to serendipity research by theorizing uncertainty not as an external constraint, but as an intentionally constructed, organizational instrument for affective value creation, advancing a view of serendipity as an intangible and emotionally performative outcome that legitimizes organizational action in volatile and contested environments.

Coordinating for Innovation-Led Digital Transformation: Evidence from Large Industrial Legacy Firms

OMT
Aleksander Millang (Nord University); Marianne Terese Steinmo; Irina Isaeva (Nord University)
Digital transformation (DT) is rapidly reshaping industrial production systems worldwide, driven by advancements in data, connectivity, automation, and analytics. Large industrial legacy firms often position themselves toward DT but also struggle to harmonize the exploration and exploitation efforts required to develop digital innovations. This study examines five large legacy firms to explore how industrial firms coordinate innovation-led digital transformation. We find that in the initial phases, firms struggle to harmonize exploration and exploitation activities, which, however, are managed over time through a combination of informal and formal coordination mechanisms. Given these results, we provide guidance for theory and practice on how large legacy firms can transition digitally through a set of coordination activities that balance exploration and exploitation in digital innovation.
