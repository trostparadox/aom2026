How AI Capability Shapes Dual Pathways to Environmental Innovation and Greenwashing

STR
Hua Fang (Hanna) Liu (Baruch College, CUNY); Bryan W. Husted (Monterrey Institute of Technology); Shoeb Mohammad (Ontario Tech University)
As firms invest in artificial intelligence (AI) to enhance their sustainability strategies, questions remain about whether AI facilitates authentic environmental innovation or enables strategic greenwashing. Drawing on the resource-based view and signaling theory, we develop a dual-pathway framework in which AI capability can both improve environmental innovation—by enabling data-driven insights and operational efficiency—and intensify greenwashing, by empowering firms to craft credible yet potentially misleading environmental narratives. We further argue that board ideological heterogeneity moderates these relationships: ideological heterogeneous boards strengthening the alignment of AI with environmental innovation, while weakening the link between AI capability development and greenwashing. Using a multi-source panel dataset of large U.S. firms from 2005 to 2019, we find support for our hypotheses: AI capability is positively associated with both environmental innovation and greenwashing; importantly, these relationships are strengthened and weakened when boards exhibit greater ideological heterogeneity, respectively. By integrating AI capability with governance heterogeneity, this study offers a more nuanced understanding of how digital transformation intersects with sustainability and corporate governance.

Academic Performance and Entrepreneurship: Scientists and Engineers During Japan's industrialization

STR
Shotaro Yamaguchi (University of Wisconsin-Madison); Takuya Hiraiwa (University of Maryland College Park); Serguey Braguinsky (University of Maryland College Park)
Although an entrepreneur’s human capital is widely recognized as shaping entry into and survival of entrepreneurship, we lack evidence on whether science and engineering (S&E) skills acquired at university—rather than degree credentials alone—truly matter. We address this gap using an employer–employee matched dataset covering nearly the universe of S&E graduates from Tokyo Imperial University (1890–1918 cohorts), tracking their careers through 1939 and linking individual academic performance to firm founding and survival. We uncover substantial heterogeneity even among the elite university graduates: those with stronger academic performance are more likely to found growth-oriented firms and less likely to enter local self-employment. Their academic performance also strongly predicts the survival of the firms they founded for nearly 100 years. Academic performance also strongly predicts firm survival for nearly a century. Analyses of potential mechanisms suggest that high-performing founders are better able to attract other S&E graduates in early stages, contributing to long-term survival. Notably, a direct effect of founders’ academic performance on survival persists even after accounting for these mediators.

Business Models as Boundary Objects for Interdisciplinary Sustainability Research

STR
Philip Rodak (University of St.Gallen)
Many political and academic stakeholders call for interdisciplinary research on environmental sustainability. However, scholars from different disciplines often struggle to integrate their knowledge due to the absence of a shared conceptual framework and “common language”. We argue that the business model concept from the strategic management domain can serve as a powerful boundary object that helps to bridge disciplinary silos in sustainability research. To support this argument, we conduct an integrative literature review covering 104 peer-reviewed articles published in leading academic journals of several disciplines (political sciences, psychology, and sociology) between 2000 and 2024. In doing so, we synthesize the fragmented research landscape on environmental sustainability scattered across disciplines. We simultaneously demonstrate how the three core dimensions of a business model – (1) value proposition; (2) value creation and delivery; (3) value capture – can accommodate insights from multiple disciplines so that the business model concept provides a point of convergence for scholars across disciplines. We further use the business model concept to identify fruitful avenues for future multidisciplinary collaborations involving management scholars, political scientists, sociologists, and/or psychologists. Finally, we discuss how the use of the business model concept for interdisciplinary research can enhance managerial decision-making.

Tipping the Balance: NCAA Labor Restrictions, Mobility, and Productivity (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Nathan J. Ashby (The University of Texas at El Paso); Miguel A. Ramos (The University of Texas at El Paso)
The exogenous removal of labor restrictions precipitated by NCAA v. Alston has provided a unique natural experiment for analyzing the impact of demonopsonization on market concentration. Using basketball tournament data between 2003 and 2024 we find evidence of increased concentration in performance among higher-tier programs. The women’s program estimates, which are larger in magnitude than the men’s, probably result from a wider dispersion in market demand and resource availability predating the reforms. We also analyze parity in regular-season conference play and fail to detect a relationship between the changes and dispersion at the highest level, but we do find a relationship for lower-tier conferences, a result we attribute to significant player transfers. These findings are important not only for measuring the effects of removing mobility barriers but also for providing valuable information on the broader economic impact as universities adjust to evolving financial realities of this major change.
