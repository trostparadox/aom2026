Ecosystems vs. Integration? How Consensus Shapes Innovation Governance During Technological Changes

STR
Taiyi Cheng (China Europe International Business School); Wei Yang (China Europe International Business School); Brian Wu (University of Michigan)
When engaging with complex, interdependent technologies, firms face a major governance challenge: should they organize innovation through ecosystems of specialized complementors, or should they internalize interdependencies through vertical integration to achieve superior adaptation in rapidly changing regimes? Prior research offers conflicting predictions, highlighting the flexibility and breadth of ecosystem search yet also the coordination advantages of concentrated authority. We address this tension with a formal simulation model that represents the trade-off between vertical integration (governing interdependencies within firm boundaries) and ecosystems (governing interdependencies across firm boundaries), highlighting the role of consensus in accelerating ecosystem efficiency in technological changes. More specifically, we find that holding total interdependencies required for value creation constant, ecosystems are slightly superior for incremental adaptation but are less effective than vertical integration during radical change when consensus is weak. Our models further examine the role of two distinct types of ecosystem consensus in shaping ecosystem efficiency during adaptation to radical changes. We show that cognitive consensus, shared expectations about future technological trajectories, increases ecosystem efficiency by aligning the direction of distant search. Incentive consensus, representing shared expectations about value capture, determines whether partners implement risky, ecosystem-specific moves. Our results are organized into a dual-consensus typology of regimes of governing innovation complexity to understand the efficiency of ecosystem adaptation during technological change. These results reconcile competing views of ecosystem versus integration and specify when ecosystem organization enhances adaptive efficiency under technological change.

Superseding the Firm: A Knowledge-Based View of Ecosystems

STR
Sergei Mozheiko (The Hang Seng University of Hong Kong)
When labor and knowledge are divided across distinct agents, the question arises of how to coordinate them in the most efficient and economical manner, potentially by bringing dispersed knowledge to a single point of integration to solve complex problems. From the knowledge-based view, managed firms are understood as such points of knowledge integration, as they function as institutions that enable rich and nuanced interactions among specialists in close proximity. However, administration, planning, and control impose increasing burdens on managers when complexity—arising from both knowledge diversity and the frequency of interactions—grows beyond their cognitive capacities. In the industrial era, this complexity was primarily addressed through hierarchical layering and the multidivisional form. With the advent of the information era, however, this trajectory has shifted, increasingly favoring knowledge integration across firm boundaries. I theorize how contemporary “business ecosystems” constitute a new kind of institution, distinct from both markets and firms, by reshaping interdependencies among dispersed knowledge holders in ways that enable knowledge integration while incurring lower levels of complexity. In doing so, I revisit the knowledge-based view in light of this relatively new phenomenon and contribute to the literature on ecosystems and organizational modularity by examining it from a new angle.

Innovation Ecosystem Alignment: The Co-Evolution of Meta-Organizational Identity and Attention

STR
Xinyue Li (Tilburg University); Mohammad Taghi Ramezan Zadeh (Amsterdam Business School)
A central challenge in ecosystem literature concerns how alignment is achieved among autonomous yet interdependent actors in the absence of formal authority. To address this challenge, we conceptualize innovation ecosystems as meta-organizations and explore how meta-organizational identity functions as an informal governance mechanism that enables ecosystem orchestration and alignment. Drawing on the Attention-Based View (ABV) and organizational identity literature, we theorize how meta-organizational identity formation shapes the distribution of managerial attention and, in turn, facilitates alignment within an innovation ecosystem. We conduct a longitudinal, in-depth single-case study of the innovation ecosystem surrounding the Port of Rotterdam Authority in the Netherlands from 2015 to 2025. Our analysis reveals a co-evolutionary process through which meta-organizational identity formation and collective attention mutually shape one another as the ecosystem transitions from emergence to development. We show how orchestrators deploy ecosystem narratives and identity framing to influence members’ sensemaking and attention allocation, while sustained patterns of collective attention reinforce the evolving meta-organizational identity. By extending ABV to post-Chandlerian forms, this study advances research on ecosystem governance and offers practical insights for orchestrators navigating complex adaptive systems.

How a State Actor Formed an Ecosystem for Nascent Technologies in the Middle of the Himalaya. (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Olivia Deuschle (Saïd Business School, University of Oxford); Paulo Savaget (University of Oxford)
Research on innovation ecosystem formation typically assumes the presence of at least one of three enabling resources: dense networks of co-located partners, substantial financial capital or deep domain expertise. We know far less about how actors lacking all of these enablers nonetheless resourcefully build innovation ecosystems by leveraging place-based features. We examine an extreme case in which a state actor in Bhutan formed an ecosystem for nascent technologies in the middle of the Himalayas. Our findings reveal a three-stage process model: (1) attracting local actors through place-grounding; (2) attracting global actors through place-recasting; and (3) bridging local and global actors through place-embedding. Through this process, geographic features that are commonly conceptualized as constraints on innovation—such as being surrounded by mountains, rivers or dense forest cover—are strategically recast as assets for ecosystem formation. We make three contributions. First, we show how innovation ecosystems can be built when financial capital, technological expertise and partner density are initially absent. Second, we demonstrate how the unique features of a place can be strategically mobilized to attract partners and incentivize collaboration, challenging the tendency to treat geography as a static backdrop rather than a resource. Third, we show that orchestration capabilities need not be pre-given but can emerge over time, clarifying how and when a focal actor becomes an orchestrator.
