Contingent Returns to Interpretive Capacity: How Institutional Ambiguity Reshapes Immigrant Business

ENT
Gal Blatman (University of Southern California); Alfred Marcus (University of Minnesota)
Drawing on entrepreneurial cognition and institutional theory, we examine when interpretive capacity (IC) constrains rather than enables immigrant business ownership. We develop a capacity-first account in which IC, defined as the ability to read and integrate multiple institutional cues, typically increases the likelihood that immigrants become business owners, but its payoff depends on the coherence of the policy regime. We introduce residual institutional ambiguity (RIA), an entropy-based measure of persistent misalignment across enforcement, integration, and benefits policies that is distinct from overall policy restrictiveness. Using 2000–2020 American Community Survey microdata linked to state-level immigration policies, we show that IC is strongly associated with business ownership in coherent environments, but that this relationship is significantly attenuated when policy signals are contradictory. Co-ethnic density further compresses the returns to IC under high RIA, as collective sensemaking and conformity pressures amplify the perceived institutional hazards of business ownership. Our findings challenge the assumption that cognitive resources uniformly promote business ownership and instead specify the boundary conditions under which judgment inhibits action. We demonstrate that the returns to interpretive capacity depend on institutional coherence and argue that capacity-building efforts and policies to promote business ownership are most effective when combined with consistent institutional signals.

Entrepreneurship Beyond Equilibrium: How Labor Strikes Impact New Business Formation

ENT
Wrenford Thaffe (State University of New York College at Oswego)
We explore how labor conflicts affect entrepreneurial activity by conceptualizing strikes as exogenous shocks that push local labor markets away from equilibrium. Using panel data featuring 2707 work stoppage events across 353 counties in the United States from 2005-2023, we apply the thermodynamics of entrepreneurship perspective with Prigogine’s theory of dissipative structures to develop a nonlinear, regime-based account of new business formation. We argue that intense work stoppages indirectly shape entrepreneurship by reconfiguring how economic resources and labor market conditions impact new business formation. By employing a county fixed-effects panel threshold model, we identified a critical threshold in work stoppage intensity beyond which the effects of various economic factors (e.g., retirement savings, unemployment rate, and GDP) on new business density systematically change. Our findings further establish entrepreneurship as an emergent property of disequilibrium conditions. We also contribute to management theory by demonstrating that labor conflicts function as systemic shocks that alters the underlying opportunity structure for entrepreneurial action.

Navigating the Entrepreneurial Process in Emerging Markets Through Exploration and Exploitation

ENT
Indu Ramachandran (Texas State University); Joshua J. Daspit (Texas State University); Vishag Badrinarayanan (Texas State University)
The majority of research on entrepreneurial process is studied within the context of developed nations or developed nations entering emerging markets (international entrepreneurship), with limited attention given to the entrepreneurial processes of incumbent firms in the emerging markets. Using a learning perspective, this paper seeks to conceptually understand how firms navigate the entrepreneurial process in emerging markets. Specifically, this paper suggests that the firm’s ability to explore and exploit new knowledge influences the entrepreneurial process. Research has underscored the importance of delineating exploration and exploitation into their internal and external components to better understand how firm performance can be increased, and how these can help entrepreneurial ventures successfully develop capabilities. We extend this line of thought to suggest that the internal and external components of exploration and exploitation have differing influences on the entrepreneurial process. Additionally, we propose that institutional factors within emerging markets (namely state of rule of law, informal business networks) influence the extent to which internal and external exploration/ exploitation relate to the entrepreneurial process. The framework offered conceptualizes how firms use exploration and exploitation capabilities to navigate the entrepreneurial process in emerging markets.

New Ventures and Investment in Employee Welfare and Training in Emerging Economies

ENT
BIBEK BHATTACHARYA (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Ashneet Kaur (Indian School of Business); Sudhanshu Maheshwari (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Sai Chittaranjan Kalubandi (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Investing in employee welfare and training has garnered significant attention as a strategic imperative for organizations, particularly within the realm of internal corporate social responsibility (CSR). This study delves into how new ventures and incumbent firms in emerging economies, specifically India, vary in their approach to investing in employee welfare and training. Utilizing a dataset of Indian firms spanning from 1989 to 2022, we explore the impact of financial constraints and the introduction of creditor reforms on these investment patterns. Our findings indicate that new ventures allocate a higher proportion of their resources to employee welfare and training compared to incumbent firms. Furthermore, this propensity is amplified following the 2016 introduction of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), which eased access to credit. This study contributes to the literature by highlighting the critical role of financial accessibility in shaping CSR strategies and underscores the importance of supportive policy frameworks in fostering entrepreneurial ecosystems in emerging markets.

Why Spatial Tax Incentives Fail to Stimulate Entrepreneurship in Multicultural Markets

ENT
Onome Ighoavodha (Johnson County Community College); dt ogilvie (Rochester Institute of Technology)
This study examines why spatially targeted tax incentives fail to stimulate entrepreneurial activity in multicultural urban markets. Using the Opportunity Zones (OZ) program as an empirical setting, the analysis investigates whether place-based investment incentives translate into new business formation and inclusive entrepreneurship outcomes. Focusing on Houston, Texas, the study uses a Difference-in-Differences design comparing 12 OZ-designated census tracts with 12 matched non-designated tracts to assess effects on employment, overall business formation, and Black-owned business activity. Drawing on data from the American Community Survey PUMS, County Business Patterns, and the Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics program, the results indicate that OZ designation is associated with modest employment gains but no statistically significant increase in entrepreneurial activity or Black-owned business formation. The findings suggest that capital-oriented tax incentives may generate labor market effects without fostering local entrepreneurial entry, especially in multicultural urban neighborhoods. This study contributes to entrepreneurship research by identifying boundary conditions under which place-based incentives fail to catalyze inclusive entrepreneurship and by highlighting the importance of complementary ecosystem-building mechanisms in racially diverse markets (Kaye & Greenlee, 2024; Clausing, 2025; Corinth et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2024).
