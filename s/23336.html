Interpreting Uncertainty: Performance Feedback, Attribution, and Growth Option Exercise in IJVs

STR
Yunok Cho (Southern Methodist University); Seung-Hyun (Sean) Lee (The University of Texas at Dallas)
How do firms respond to uncertainty when exercising a real option? While real options theory predicts that uncertainty discourages irreversible commitments by increasing the value of waiting, firms often respond heterogeneously to similar levels of uncertainty. Drawing on behavioral theory of the firm and attribution theory, we argue that this heterogeneity arises because managers interpret uncertainty through the lens of performance feedback. Specifically, we theorize that international joint venture (IJV) performance feedback moderates the relationship between uncertainty and IJV acquisition by shaping managers’ attributions regarding their capability to manage uncertain environments. Positive performance feedback weakens the deterrent effect of uncertainty on acquisition, whereas this attribution-based moderation is attenuated when controllability is constrained by minority ownership, business group–affiliated partners, or acquisition-based IJVs. Using longitudinal data on 275 IJVs operating in Korea between 2007 and 2019, we find support for these arguments. Our study extends real options theory by incorporating attribution-based behavioral mechanisms and advances performance feedback research by highlighting how feedback reshapes firms’ sensitivity to uncertainty in post-entry investment decisions.

Temporal Conditions of Plural Sourcing Strategies in the Electric Utility Industry

STR
Shaohua Lu (Santa Clara University); Jennifer Woolley (Santa Clara University)
This study examines how firms’ sourcing strategies evolve over time and investigates the mechanisms that drive transitions from reliance on a single governance mode to plural sourcing. While prior research has largely conceptualized sourcing decisions as a static make-or-buy choice, a growing literature recognizes plural sourcing as a distinct governance arrangement. However, little is known about how firms arrive at plural sourcing or how sourcing strategies unfold over time. Adopting a temporal perspective, we analyze the sequencing of sourcing decisions using a longitudinal dataset of U.S. electric utilities sourcing wind power from 2005 to 2015. Our analysis shows that initial wind power procurement is primarily driven by external pressures, including policy mandates and social activism. In contrast, transitions toward plural sourcing are shaped largely by firm-specific resources, asset configurations, and demand uncertainty. These findings demonstrate that the determinants of internal production and external procurement are asymmetric and temporally distinct. By highlighting the dynamic processes underlying governance mode choices, this study advances theory on plural sourcing and firm boundaries, while offering implications for managers and policymakers in the renewable energy sector.

Investor Protection and the Architecture of Hierarchical Business Groups

STR
Magi Trobat-Fullana (University of the Balearic Islands); Bartolome Pascual-Fuster (University of the Balearic Islands); Elitsa Rumenova Banalieva (Northeastern University)
Why aren’t Korean chaebols deep (Almeida et al., 2011)? The strategic hierarchical design of business groups (BGs) remains underexplored in corporate governance research. This paper extends the theory of pyramidal formation by examining how and to what extent hierarchical structures grow. We document an incentive to transfer and accumulate wealth at the upper layers of the BG hierarchies. We distinguish between two wealth-transferring mechanisms: the dividend and the intra-group transaction mechanisms (IGTM). We propose that the dividend mechanism potentially entails timing opportunity costs and dividend repatriation taxes, which discourage hierarchical depth expansion. Then, we find that the hierarchical depth is contingent on the wealth-transferring mechanism used by controlling shareholders to accumulate wealth at the upper echelons and that the mechanism choice depends on institutional investor protection. When minority shareholders are institutionally empowered, the dividend mechanism prevails, and controlling shareholders design shallower hierarchical structures to minimize this mechanism’s costs. In contrast, when controlling shareholders are free to use the IGTM, they can quickly and cheaply transfer wealth from the bottom to the top of the hierarchy, allowing them to build deeper hierarchical structures. Following the idea of minimizing the costs entailed in the dividend mechanism, we find that affiliates facing lower dividend taxation are more likely to become the parent firm of subsequent affiliates, especially in environments where empowered minority shareholders can enforce the dividend mechanism.

We’re One in a Million! How Inflated Asset Specificity Perceptions Undermine Competitive Advantage

STR
Russell Coff (University of Wisconsin-Madison); Libby Weber (Michigan State University)
This theory paper explores managers’ systematic asset specificity perception bias. That is, competitive advantage framing, organizational identification, false uniqueness, and confirmation biases encourage managers to invest excessively in idiosyncratic capabilities. While Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) traditionally assumes asset specificity is known ex-ante, managers’ boundedly rational perceptions actually shape capability investments. This bias may lead firms to internalize some activities that could be conducted in an alliance. For external transactions, this bias elevates the specificity required in the task description and constrains the search to suppliers likely to make idiosyncratic investments. Thus, whether activities are conducted internally or through hybrid transactions, they tend to reflect excessive specialized investments. Over time, individual capability investments accumulate, become increasingly co-specialized, and are connected through a web of idiosyncratic routines. Such bias-driven processes form a distinct basis for core rigidities if other firms actually have similar needs and the market develops more efficient solutions. In contrast to prescriptions in the extant literature, efforts to step up innovation will likely worsen the problem if the focus remains on idiosyncratic co-specialized solutions. This paper expands our understanding of biased specificity perceptions and their negative impact on competitive advantage.

MNEs Decisions During Armed Conflicts

STR
Chi Nguyen (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Valerie Duplat (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Oli Mihalache (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Jean Luc Arregle (emlyon business school)
When armed conflict breaks out in a host country, multinational firms operating there face growing pressures that can make continued market presence difficult. Yet during the 2022 Russia-Ukraine war, many multinational firms continued operating in Russia despite sanctions, regulatory scrutiny, and public calls for exit. This study examines how firms manage to stay in conflict-affected markets by deploying buffering strategies that mitigate political and societal pressures from their home countries. Using the 2022 Russia-Ukraine war as context, we analyze a preliminary sample of 174 publicly listed US firms that maintained operations in Russia after the invasion. Drawing on resource dependence theory, we see domestic corporate political activity (CPA) as a political buffer that helps firms manage dependence on home governments during wartime. We also theorize that maintaining subsidiaries in a country opposing the conflict-affected market can serve as a reputational buffer aimed at societal audiences. Using ordinal logistic regression, we find that domestic CPA significantly increases the likelihood of firms staying in Russia. While subsidiary presence in a strategic opposing country has no direct effect, it increases the likelihood of staying only when firms are highly visible to the public, highlighting the moderating role of a firm's public visibility. These findings shift attention from why firms remain in war-affected markets to how they actively buffer against political and societal pressures, contributing to research on multinational strategy under conditions of geopolitical conflict.
