Attuned to the Archive: Forms of Affective Intensification in Political Organising

OMT
Anna de la Torre Fornell (Venice School of Management - Ca' Foscari University); Monica Calcagno
As contemporary societies face times of growing political disengagement, with collective action often being viewed as ephemeral and precarious, understanding how processes of political organising are sustained over time remains a critical endeavour for both scholars and activists. The present paper investigates their durability, drawing on theories of affect and the concept of affective intensification. Methodologically, we adopt an ‘affective ethnographic history’ approach to examine a citizen assembly in Marghera, the mainland neighbourhood area of the Italian city of Venice, which self-organised against chemical hazards between 1998 and 2013. Attuning to the archival material from the local documentation centre, three mechanisms of spatio-temporal intensification – juxtapposing, nearing, and accelerating – emerge to sustain the process of political organising. The paper contributes to the literature on affective organising by expanding our understanding of durability and affective intensification.

Infrastructuring The Politics Of Liminality In The Nordics: Making Lines Into Zones At The Borders

OMT
Elena Raviola; Bengt Andersen; Ursula Plesner (Copenhagen Business School); Bertil Rolandsson (University of Gothenburg)
This paper focuses on borders as an urgent and consequential object of politics and organizing. More specifically, it investigates how the politics of liminality that manifest at the national borders is infrastructured. While liminality has often been studied as an experiential or symbolic condition, we shift the analytical focus to how it is materially and organizationally produced through processes of infrastructuring. Drawing on an ethnographically-inspired, multi-sited study of the Norway–Sweden and Sweden–Denmark borders, we investigate how legal, technological, and organizational infrastructures work together to materialize the evolving politics of liminality and transform borders from lines of passage, primarily aimed at filtering migration, into zones of (datafied) transition, primarily aimed at filtering criminality. We analyze three interrelated processes: the fabrication of law, the implementation of epistemic technologies, and the sharing of formal and informal organizing across borders. Our study shows how the contemporary infrastructuring of politics of liminality (at the borders) implies a spatial and temporal expansion of liminality in three main facets. First, through the practical fabrication of law, the space of liminality is expanded into zones where certain legal categories are suspended. Secondly, through the datafication of passages, the time of liminality is expanded because data persist, are shared and can be reanalyzed retroactively. Liminal transition might thus also be reversed. Thirdly, the spatial and temporal expansion of liminality transforms it into space-time moments in which it appears in unforeseeable ways. The paper contributes to organization studies by developing an infrastructural perspective on liminality as a political and organizational achievement and by inviting further attention of organizational scholars to borders as an urgent and highly consequential political phenomenon.

“I Contain Multitudes”: The Strategic Value of Multivocally Resonant Language

OMT
Natalia Panchieri (University of Bologna); Simone Ferriani; Stefano Li Pira (University of Bologna)
Organizations pursuing future-oriented strategies must secure favorable evaluations from heterogeneous stakeholders, yet these audiences apply incompatible evaluative schemas and often encounter the same messages in shared communicative spaces. How can a single message generate positive evaluation across such diverse audiences when the future it depicts does not yet exist and thus lacks empirical grounding? We introduce multivocal resonance (MR)—an audience-level interpretive pattern through which the same linguistic expressions elicit broadly favorable evaluation across heterogeneous audiences. We theorise that MR is enabled by semantic folds: words that are simultaneously central within multiple distinct semantic communities, allowing different audiences to recognize the language as legitimate while elaborating it richly through their own evaluative vocabularies, yet along systematically divergent associative pathways. Distinguishing interpretive diversity from interpretive utility, we argue that ambiguity is necessary but insufficient for positive cross-audience evaluation: language must also support audiences’ connection-making work. Finally, we specify boundary conditions—institutional complexity, audience interdependence, and temporal stage—that moderate when MR yields favourable evaluation. The framework advances research on imaginaries, strategic communication, and evaluation by specifying when a single message can mobilize distributed support without requiring convergence on shared meaning.

Performing Historical Resources: Implications for History and Collective Memory

OMT
Jeremy Aroles (University of York); John Stewart Hassard (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester); Bill Foster (Not Associated); Yin Liang (Newcastle University)
Our paper seeks to examine how organizations mobilize resources to produce, rewrite and alter their respective pasts, thereby shaping collective memory. We here ask: How do organizations perform their historical resources? We first theorise the processes through which organizational historical resources are performed as real, social, commercial or political objects by presenting a typology that fleshes out their key features. We show how they differ vis-à-vis the processes underlying their performance, their value, their expertise(s) and their longevity. We then explore how history is articulated and produced through each of these four types of objects. Our paper provides a detailed appreciation of how various types of historical resources can be performed to enact different framings of the past. In so doing we pave the way for further empirical research into the organizational consequences of such processes.

Substantive and Subjective: A Multi-Method Investigation of B Corp Certification on Customer Ratings

OMT
Kunyuan Qiao (Northeastern University); Wesley Sine (Cornell University); Tejas Ramdas (Cornell University); Mary Ross
We examine how B Corp certification affects customer satisfaction with a multi-method approach combining archival analysis of a matched sample of 940 firms, a regression discontinuity design around the 80-point cutoff for B Corp certification, and controlled laboratory experiments. Our archival analysis demonstrates that certified B Corps achieve 16-22% higher customer ratings than conventional firms, with this relationship operating through enhanced employee satisfaction. Firms with stated social missions lacking formal certification (“B Corp-like”) show significantly weaker effects, highlighting the value of third-party verification and governance mechanisms. Critically, the employee satisfaction mechanism mediates 11.5% of total certification effects on customer ratings, revealing substantial unexplained variance. Complementary controlled experiments isolate subjective cognitive processes: participants rate identical products associated with certified B Corps significantly higher, particularly in contexts where ethical considerations are salient. Our study advances institutional theory by shifting the attention on the traditional substantive-symbolic dichotomy to substantive organizational improvements and subjective cognitive engagement, both of which produce measurable stakeholder benefits. Our findings also extend signaling theory by demonstrating that certification signals create value beyond reducing information asymmetry about organizational quality—the signals themselves directly influence stakeholder evaluations through cognitive processes even when actual product quality is held constant experimentally.
