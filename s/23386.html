Everything Everywhere all at Once: Dynamics in Intellectual Property for Regulation of Uncertainty

STR
Jorge Fuentes; Michael Leatherbee (Not Associated)
This article introduces the concept of Dynamic Intellectual Property (Dynamic IP) as a strategic framework for managing intellectual property (IP) in uncertain and innovation-driven environments. Building on research in dynamic capabilities, strategic entrepreneurship, profiting from innovation, and intellectual property management, the study reconceptualizes IP not as a static legal instrument but as a strategic evolving capability. The framework integrates three strategic phases —opportunity-seeking, advantage-seeking, and sustainability-seeking— with three strategic logics of appropriability —IP options logic, IP leverage logic, and IP portfolio logic— within a cyclical process of competitive advantage renewal. Through this cycle, firms can experiment and explore under uncertainty, strengthen appropriability regimes to capture value from growing competitive advantages, and extract long-term profit from those competitive advantages once consolidated while discovering new opportunities. Dynamic IP positions IP simultaneously as an adaptive protection mechanism and a regulator of uncertainty, thereby enabling the emergence of new business opportunities.

Still Waters Run Deep: Data-Digital Technology Coupling and the Unleashing of Data Network Effects

STR
Jia Wei (Zhejiang University); Fuyicheng Lou (Zhejiang University); Shiwen Liu (Zhejiang University)
In the digital economy, Data-Derived Enterprises (DDEs) face a severe “Cold-Start Problem,” trapped in a vicious resource cycle where a lack of data hinders algorithm optimization, while inferior algorithmic performance precludes the acquisition of user data. Although extant literature emphasizes the critical role of data in unleashing network effects, few studies reveal how to break this paradox in the absence of initial data resources. Based on a longitudinal single-case study of Momenta, a Chinese autonomous driving unicorn, we develop a process model of the coupling between data and digital technologies. Our findings reveal that firms evolve through three phases to mitigate the cold-start problem and ultimately unleash data network effects: (1) Architectural Coupling, which “channels” data via business architecture alignment and embedded digital probes; (2) Processual Coupling, which “agitates” data flow through technology-driven curation and data-driven algorithm envelopment; and (3) Permeation Coupling, which facilitates the co-evolution of data and technology via end-to-end large models, enabling data and digital technologies to “run deep and wide.” By deconstructing the dynamic coupling mechanisms between data and digital technologies, this study challenges the traditional view that “data stock” is a prerequisite for unleashing data network effects. Instead, it highlights the pivotal roles of “high-quality matching” and “coupling mechanisms” in unleashing data network effects, thereby contributing to digital innovation theory.

More Contribution, Less Control? How Should Startups Collaborate in Open Knowledge Communities?

STR
Bradley Byounghoon Koh (INSEAD); E. Dogukan Yilmaz; Vibha Gaba (INSEAD)
New ventures increasingly collaborate with open-source communities to access external knowledge and accelerate growth, yet such collaboration poses a fundamental challenge because firms and OSCs operate under differing organizing principles. A central question is how new ventures can effectively coordinate with decentralized OSCs that offer high autonomy and freedom while maintaining the venture’s strategic focus. We address this tension by examining how distributing decision rights to external contributors shapes knowledge collaboration across organizational boundaries. Theoretically, we argue that decision-rights allocation functions as a boundary-spanning coordination mechanism that aligns firm-level objectives with community-based modes of organizing. Using a panel dataset of 15,984 U.S. software startups founded between 2011 and 2023, comprising 379,963 firm–quarter observations, we show that distributing decision-making authority to external contributors is positively associated with the likelihood of receiving first-round venture capital funding. This relationship is significantly stronger when ventures possess greater internal capacity to absorb external contributions. Additional analyses reveal diminishing returns to extensive decision-rights distribution, underscoring coordination limits to decentralization. These findings advance research on organization design and entrepreneurship by explaining how ventures learn to coordinate with fundamentally different organizing forms rather than simply accessing external knowledge.

Data Resource Disclosure and Interfirm Dependence: Evidence from Trade Credit Relationships

STR
QIURU YIN (Tianjin University)
Data has increasingly emerged as a critical strategic resource shaping firms’ competitive positions and interorganizational relationships. Recent research in international business conceptualizes this as “data-based power,” arguing that the ability to capture, analyze, and apply data on a global scale forms a self-reinforcing feedback loop that underpins the dominance of big-tech multinational enterprises, fundamentally altering traditional competitive dynamics. Beyond enabling internal analytics and innovation, data is now frequently disclosed through corporate reports and communications to satisfy demands for transparency from a range of stakeholders. This disclosure is increasingly governed by stringent global standards, such as the EU’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), which mandates third-party assurance for sustainability data, effectively raising the credibility and strategic weight of disclosed information to the level of financial reporting. While prior research has largely examined disclosure’s role in reducing information asymmetry in capital markets, less is known about how visibility of strategic resources affects firms’ relationships with key exchange partners such as suppliers and customers. Emerging literature from operations and supply chain management sheds light on this gap, demonstrating that data sharing between supply chain partners (e.g., through shared mobile applications) can create “networked assets,” leading to synchronized performance, risk contagion, and convergent innovation patterns among connected firms. Furthermore, studies show that qualitative disclosures, such as customer risk factors in annual reports, can trigger strategic recalibrations upstream, prompting suppliers to pursue breakthrough innovations to mitigate dependence and seek new markets. The study of disclosure in these contexts is important because interfirm dependence often constrains or enables firms’ access to vital resources and shapes relational outcomes beyond what is captured in financial markets. Therefore, corporate data disclosure transcends mere transparency; it is a strategic act within the resource-dependent networks firms inhabit, capable of redistributing power, managing risk, and reconfiguring competitive relationships.
