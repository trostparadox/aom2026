Now or Never – Or Just Not Yet? A Regret-Regulation Framework of Opportunity Evaluation

ENT
Katrina Brownell (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Thomas J. Dean (Colorado State University)
Scholars have primarily examined regret in entrepreneurship through a retrospective lens, which overlooks the influence and importance of regret as an anticipatory influence on decision-making and action. By integrating affect-as-information and regret-regulation theories, we develop a regret regulation framework of opportunity evaluation that explains how entrepreneurs manage both anticipated regrets of action (errors of commission) and inaction (errors of omission) in ways that render outcomes psychologically defensible in hindsight. We identify three mechanisms through which anticipated regret shapes evaluation – evaluative intensity (the extent and rigor of evaluation activities), incrementalism (the magnitude of commitments made during evaluation), and sufficiency (the amount of evidence required to justify advancement) – with three distinct outcomes: advancement toward action, abandonment of the opportunity, and deferral, a state in which entrepreneurs neither act nor abandon but remain in a state of prolonged evaluation. Collectively, our framework elevates the importance of anticipatory influences on decision making by showing how anticipated regret organizes evaluation around post hoc defensibility and establishes deferral as a theoretically meaningful outcome of opportunity evaluation.

Persist, Perish, Pivot, or... Preserve? Individual-Opportunity Fit Approach

ENT
KANAN ASIF (Butler University); Michael P. Ciuchta (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Ann Kronrod
Entrepreneurs pursue and persist with opportunities based on their beliefs about the feasibility and desirability of the opportunities. However, existing research does not identify what entrepreneurs do if an opportunity remains feasible but no longer desirable, or desirable but no longer feasible. Furthermore, existing research views the choice between persistence, pivoting, or opportunity abandonment through a binary lens: persist or pivot/abandon. In this research, we introduce a fourth choice, i.e., opportunity preservation, suggesting that some entrepreneurs temporarily abandon but preserve their opportunities and reanimate them later once the situation becomes more favorable. Building on the notion of opportunity preservation, we integrate dormancy theory and person-environment fit theory and develop a 2x2 decision matrix. This matrix predicts entrepreneurs' choice between persistence, perishing (opportunity termination), pivot, or preservation based on their perceived fit with these opportunities. Using a survey with 410 entrepreneurs and an experiment with 300 entrepreneurs (1200 decisions), we found empirical support for the conceptualization of opportunity preservation and for the decision matrix proposed. Our decision matrix offers a more nuanced understanding of entrepreneurial decision-making, thereby integrating and predicting these decisions within a unified framework. This dissertation also makes important contributions to the literature on entrepreneurial opportunities, the theory of entrepreneurial action, and pivoting.

To Pivot or Adapt? Comparative Opportunity Evaluation Under Commitment

ENT
Jens Schueler (University of Bayreuth); Matthias Baum (University of Bayreuth); Brian S. Anderson (University of Kansas)
Following aspirational failure, whether entrepreneurs pivot or adapt may depend on how they evaluate the relative predictability of their strategic options. We apply an opportunity-evaluation perspective to disentangle how four comparative cues—customer demand, capability fit, operational complexity, and perceived control—shape the likelihood of pivoting under high commitment. Contrary to portrayals of pivots as abrupt or threshold-driven events, we argue that entrepreneurs rely on a cue-by-cue evaluation of these signals, influenced by their dispositions toward control, persistence, and future outcomes. In a metric conjoint experiment (n = 2,144 decisions nested within 134 entrepreneurs), we find that each cue independently increases the likelihood of pivoting when it is more predictable for the reorientation option, and that decision makers’ dispositions toward control, persistence, and future outcomes selectively moderate these effects. A Monte Carlo simulation replicates this pattern.

Who Perceives Entrepreneurial Opportunities During Disruptive Events?

ENT
Zonghui Li (Missouri State University); Emma Su (University of Dayton)
This study examines why entrepreneurs differ in their opportunity perceptions during a crisis, specifically the COVID-19 pandemic. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we hypothesize that entrepreneurial traits associated with resource investment, including opportunity-driven mindset and creativity, enhance entrepreneurs’ perception of opportunities during the pandemic. In contrast, traits linked to resource protection, including long-term orientation and proactivity are expected to diminish opportunity perceptions under crisis conditions. An empirical analysis of 7,867 observations from the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) Adult Population Survey (APS), using hierarchical linear modeling (HLM), provides support for our hypotheses. This study contributes to the growing body of literature on the impact of disruptive events and deepens our understanding of the relationship between entrepreneurs’ motivation to protect and preserve resources and their perception of entrepreneurial opportunities during a crisis.

“Actualizable” Entrepreneurial Opportunity: Formal Modeling Using Discrete-Time Real Options

ENT
Gurdeep Singh Raina; Anup Menon Nandialath (University of Wisconsin-La Crosse); Arvin Sahaym (Washington State University)
How entrepreneurial opportunities are actualized under uncertainty remains poorly understood. We develop a formal model showing that opportunity outcomes depend on the interaction and sequencing of internal validation and evolution (IVE) and external endorsement and tangible support (ETS). Conceptualizing opportunities as option-like processes, we demonstrate that validation does not universally create value: under high uncertainty, premature validation can erode option value, while external endorsement may arise rationally even without robust validation. We further show that signal precision can outweigh signal strength in stakeholder evaluation. We use illustrative cases to highlight distinct IVE–ETS trajectories. Overall, our framework reframes opportunity development as a contingent sequencing problem rather than a linear validation process.
