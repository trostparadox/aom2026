Session overview

Technologies increasingly mediate how people interact, coordinate, and make sense of work. Across organizational settings, digital systems such as artificial intelligence, algorithmic decision tools, and platform infrastructures are reshaping not only tasks and workflows, but also the interactional conditions under which work is accomplished (Barley, 1986; Orlikowski, 1992; Leonardi, 2011). However, much of the literature on technology and work has analyzed this technological change either as an economic or organizational phenomenon, focusing on aggregate labor-market outcomes or high-level design choices (e.g. Autor, 2015; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). In this symposium, we aim to refocus attention on the Chicago School tradition of sociology, which treats social order as emergent from interaction and emphasizes close empirical engagement with how people experience and respond to changing conditions at work (Park, 1925; Hughes, 1958; Blumer, 1969), as well as more recent work that follows these general tenets (Barley, 1990; Suchman, 2007; Bailey et al., 2022). Building on this tradition, the present symposium brings together four papers that examine how technologies mediate interaction at work, revealing how coordination, relationships, and opportunities are dynamically produced through situated practices rather than determined solely by technological capabilities or organizational design. Together, the papers illustrate how Chicago School–inspired perspectives can deepen contemporary debates on automation, AI, and digital transformation by centering the micro-processes through which social order is produced and contested at work. They show how technologies open and close developmental opportunities, generate new relational cleavages, reshape task-level work in practice, and enable alternative forms of agency within bureaucratic systems. In doing so, the symposium highlights the continued relevance of interactionist and ethnographic approaches for understanding technological change in organizations.
