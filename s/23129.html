The Role of Attributed Motives in Subordinate Reactions to Leader Perfectionism

MOC
Jiyu Li; Zhenyuan Wang (East China Normal University); Jun Gu (Macquarie University)
Although existing research suggests that leader perfectionism has a negative impact on organizations and their members, this leadership style is still common in practice and can have positive and motivating results. We draw on attribution theory with the dual-strategies theory of social rank to examine how employees characterized by altruistic attribution and self-interest attribution show different cognitive and behavioral reactions to leader perfectionism. To test our research hypotheses, we conducted a scenario experimental study (Study 1) and a three-wave lagged survey (Study 2). The results showed that when employees exhibit high levels of altruistic attribution, leader perfectionism is more likely to increase their perceived leader prestige, thereby increasing manager-directed OCB. Conversely, when employees possess strong self-interest attribution, leader perfectionism tends to lead to perceived leader dominance, which is associated with more manager-directed CWB. These findings illuminate the delicate relationship between leader perfectionism and employee cognitive and behavioral responses, and deepen our understanding of effective management practices within organizations.

How the Empowering Effects of Mindfulness Meditation Improve Work Performance and Civility

MOC
Andrew Hafenbrack (University of Washington); Joy Youn Soo Kim (University of Washington); Eric (Shaoxue) Wu (Beijing Jiaotong University); Adam Galinsky (Columbia University in the City of New York)
Does mindfulness meditation improve performance at work? Although research offers suggestive evidence, past studies finding positive associations have been exclusively correlational. Furthermore, other research suggests that mindfulness meditation can impair productivity by producing more passive employees. To make sense of these findings, we offer a new conceptual model that explains how and why mindfulness meditation can improve work-related outcomes. We propose that mindfulness meditation improves work performance, civility, and leadership potential by increasing feelings of power and control. To test our Empowerment Model of Mindfulness Meditation, we conducted three mixed-method experiments (N = 998). Two laboratory studies found that mindfulness increased a sense of power because it boosted control over one’s thoughts; furthermore, this increased sense of power predicted better job application performance. An eight-week field experiment (Experiment 3) with 345 employees and supervisor-rated outcomes found that random assignment to a mindfulness meditation condition increased performance, civility, and leadership emergence relative to a therapeutic writing condition and a no-intervention-control condition. Serial mediation analyses revealed that mindfulness meditation increased leadership emergence by first boosting control at work, which improved performance and civility. The findings reveal how mindfulness practices can make organizations stronger and put employees on the path to leadership.

The Erosion of Voice Endorsement: How Leader Tenure Shifts Power Beliefs

MOC
Yaqian Zhang (Zhongnan University of Economics and Law); Qing Lu (University of Khorfakkan)
Historical records and business cases consistently suggest that leaders’ endorsement of subordinate voice varies as their time in office increases, yet scholars have paid limited empirical attention to this phenomenon. Drawing on the leader identity construction theory and the lay theory of power, we examine how leader tenure influences voice endorsement through shifts in power beliefs, positing that tenure operates via leaders' advancement through subjective occupancy stages. Across a scenario-based experiment (Study 1, N = 214) and a two-wave field survey (Study 2, N = 250), we find that as leaders advance in their roles, collaborative power beliefs decrease while coercive power beliefs increase, which in turn diminishes voice endorsement. Moreover, selection mode and leader mindfulness moderate these effects: leaders elected by subordinates (as opposed to those appointed by upper management) exhibit a slower decline in collaborative beliefs, whereas mindful leaders, by being more attuned to their power dynamics, accelerate the shift toward coercive power beliefs. This research advances a dynamic understanding of leader receptiveness to voice and highlights mechanisms to mitigate leadership rigidity over time.

The Double-Edged Effect of Power-Status Inconsistency on Leaders’ Developmental Feedback

MOC
Lu Liu (Nanjing University); Ruhong Liu (Yantai University)
Drawing upon an integrated framework of objective self-awareness theory and the dual perspective model of agency and communion, this study examines the mechanism and boundary conditions through which high-power and low-status inconsistency influences leaders’ developmental feedback. Using matched data from 518 leaders and team members nested within 90 work teams, we conducted an empirical test. The findings reveal a double-edged effect of high-power and low-status inconsistency on developmental feedback. In a performance climate, high-power and low-status leaders develop a low level of agentic self-concept, which in turn negatively affects their developmental feedback. In a mastery climate, high-power and low-status leaders develop a low level of communal self-concept, which in turn positively affects their developmental feedback. By uncovering the mechanism through which high-power and low-status inconsistency shapes developmental feedback, this study explicates the dual self-awareness process of individuals experiencing hierarchical inconsistency within the agentic-communal social-cognitive system, offering insights for organizations seeking to optimize team motivational climates to unlock leadership potential.

How Do Employees React to Hierarchical Inconsistency? Emotional Mechanisms of Envy and Pride

MOC
Ruhong Liu (Yantai University); Lu Liu (Nanjing University)
As digital technologies reshape organizational operations and value creation, power structures increasingly diverge from knowledge distributions, leading to the widespread emergence of low-power and high-status inconsistency among employees. Prior research provides inconsistent explanations of behavioral responses to hierarchical inconsistency, highlighting both adaptive and maladaptive outcomes. Drawing on envy subtypes theory, this study used an experiment and a survey to examine upward social comparison processes in which low-power and high-status employees compare themselves with team leaders. The findings show that perceived leader authentic pride links low-power and high-status inconsistency to benign envy and subsequent feedback seeking. Conversely, perceived leader hubristic pride connects low-power and high-status inconsistency with malicious envy and increased knowledge hiding. Conceptualizing low-power and high-status inconsistency as an upward social comparison context clarifies employees’ emotional experiences and coping behaviors in inconsistent hierarchies. The study proposes an integrative framework that captures both promotive and inhibitive effects of low-power and high-status inconsistency. Practically, the results underscore the role of leader emotional expression management in activating the constructive potential of low-power and high-status employees.
