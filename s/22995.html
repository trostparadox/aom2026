Hide Quality Through Quantity? Geopolitical Tension and Corporate Innovation (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Dengke Jin (Sun Yat-Sen University)
Prior research has not reached a consensus on how geopolitical tensions influence firm innovation. Leveraging the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security’s Entity List as an exogenous policy sanction, we examine how Chinese firms respond to technology sanctions through the lens of resource dependence and signaling theories. We argue that sanctioned firms strategically expand their innovation quantity, primarily by increasing patent applications, to convey a positive signal of technological resilience to external stakeholders and to hide the negative signal that corporate innovation quality fails to improve. Further analyses show that this innovation quantity expansion becomes weaker when institutional investors hold stronger confidence in the firm, but intensifies when firms exhibit higher levels of internationalization. Moreover, when top executives have academic experience, firms are less inclined to rely on quantity signals, and their innovation quality improves either. Based on 46 Chinese listed firms that were added to the Entity List between 2018 and 2022, we constructed a panel dataset of 92 firms from 2016 to 2023 which results 716 observations, and the time-varying DID analysis supports these arguments. This study advances the literature on geopolitical tensions by highlighting firms’ strategic innovation behaviors under technology sanctions and extends the application of signaling and resource dependence theories to the broader context of international political economy.

Navigating a Fragmented Global Environment: The Impact of Political Polarization on Firm Innovation

IM
Sung Hoon Lee (NEOMA Business School); Alfonso Carballo-Perez (NEOMA Business School); Margherita Corina (NEOMA Business School)
Political polarization is a growing global trend that increases turbulence and uncertainty for multinational firms. Despite its rising importance, the mechanisms through which polarization affects firms remain underexplored. This study focuses on the effect of polarization on the innovation performance of multinational firms, arguing that polarization tends to hamper the firms’ ability to absorb and recombine external knowledge, inhibiting firms’ access to domestic and foreign knowledge and thus limiting the pool for recombination. We explore this further by examining how the effect of polarization on innovation changes due to firm- and country-level factors, including international diversification, firm size, and national adoption of industrial robots.

When Geopolitics Enters the Boardroom: Shareholder Exposure and Innovation Oscillation

IM
Yi Yang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Ning Fang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Patrick Peng Wang (Beijing Normal-Hong Kong Baptist University); Chenguang Hu (Beijing Normal-Hong Kong Baptist University)
Geopolitical tension has been recognized as a defining feature of the contemporary business environment. However, in research on corporate governance and innovation, the impact geopolitical tension exposure has on firms’ innovation dynamics is not well understood. Through integrating a geopolitics perspective and corporate governance, we develop a framework to explore how shareholder-exposed geopolitical tension influences the stability of innovation strategy. We suggest that shareholder-exposed geopolitical tension is positively associated with innovation oscillation due to the transmission of conflicting geopolitical mandates into the boardroom. Drawing on resource dependence theory, we further reveal a nuanced boundary condition: while firm knowledge breadth, amplifies oscillation by lowering the costs of reactive switching, firm network centrality and political connections buffers this oscillation by providing alternative legitimacy and information. Our study contributes to the geopolitical view of the firm by illuminating the internal transmission mechanisms of rivalry and the heterogeneity of firm responses to geopolitical pressure.

How Firms Adapt R&D Investment Strategies Amid Geopolitical Uncertainty: Moderating Role of Geograph

IM
Chun-Ping Yeh (National Taiwan University of Science and Technology); YI CHI HSIAO (National Central University)
Geopolitical risk (GPR) significantly influences corporate decision-making, particularly in research and development (R&D) investments, yet existing literature presents conflicting views on its impact. While some argue that heightened GPR discourages innovation by increasing uncertainty and investment hesitancy, others suggest it can incentivize firms to intensify R&D efforts as a strategic response. To reconcile these perspectives, we draw on strategic growth option theory and propose that although GPR can drive firms to increase R&D investment, this effect is weakened when firms have high geographical dispersion due to elevated operational and transactional costs. Using a fixed-effects model on an unbalanced panel data of 1,412 firm-year observations from Taiwan’s top 100 publicly listed manufacturing firms (2006–2021), we find that while global GPR positively influences corporate R&D investment, geographical diversification negatively moderates this relationship. Our study contributes to the international business and geopolitical risk literature by offering empirical evidence from a geopolitical hotspot and highlighting geographical dispersion as a key contingency in the GPR-innovation link. These insights provide practical implications for firms, suggesting that leveraging geopolitical uncertainty for innovation is more effective when business operations are less geographically dispersed

Geopolitical Volatility and International R&D Investment in Third Countries

IM
Wenyu Zhong (South China University of Technology); Xiaoxia Tan (Guangdong University of Technology)
The escalating global geopolitical tensions have shifted the logic of R&D internationalization from seeking innovation resources to managing geopolitical uncertainty. Drawing upon real options theory, this study investigates the impact of (US-China) geopolitical volatility on the international R&D investment of Chinese firms in third countries. While prior research emphasizes the deterrent effect of bilateral tensions, we argue that geopolitical volatility increases the switching option value of R&D portfolios, thereby incentivizing firms to expand their R&D footprint in third countries as a strategic hedge against uncertainty in a conflict context. Our findings reveal that heightened geopolitical volatility significantly promotes R&D investment by firms in third countries. Furthermore, we explore how country-level ties between the third country and the geopolitical rival country (US) moderate this relationship. The positive relationship is attenuated when the third country maintains close military relations with the US. However, contrary to our prediction, geographic proximity between the third country and the US weakens this effect, suggesting that distant markets are prioritized as superior diversification tools for hedging localized geopolitical risks. Moreover, we examine the moderating role of a firm’s international investment portfolios: a pre-existing investment presence in the third country weakens this effect, whereas the motivation for R&D expansion is significantly amplified for firms with a high concentration of investment in the rival country (US). Our study extends real options theory to the geopolitical realm and provides micro-level evidence on how multinational enterprises respond to turbulent environments through strategic diversification amidst the wave of political de-risking.
