Beyond the Glass Ceiling: Women, Leadership, and Advancement

Moderator: Jackson Lu Massachusetts Institute of Technology

Session overview

This session explores women's advancement into leadership and the organizational conditions that facilitate or constrain it. Focusing on executive careers, leadership representation, pay gaps, and institutional arrangements, the papers reveal how gender inequality persists even when women gain access to top roles. Collectively, they offer new insight into the structural, symbolic, and career-related mechanisms shaping women's progression to positions of authority.

Papers (5)

The Bamboo Ceiling in the UK: Ethnicity–Gender Intersectionality, Risk Tolerance, and Leadership

DEI
Jackson Lu (Massachusetts Institute of Technology); Qian Li (Beijing Foreign Studies University); Shuang Liang (Beijing Foreign Studies University); Wanqin Mu (Beijing Foreign Studies University); Shiyang Gong (Beijing Normal University)
In the UK, Asians are often considered the “model minority” with few hardships due to their strong educational performance and economic success. We question this perception by uncovering a “Bamboo Ceiling” in leadership attainment for certain Asian subgroups in the UK. Whereas many researchers and practitioners treat Asians as a monolith, we theoretically and empirically distinguish between ethnic Chinese (the largest East Asian group) and ethnic Indians (the largest South Asian group). In Study 1, we analyzed CEOs and CFOs of the Financial Times Stock Exchange (FTSE) companies—the largest publicly listed companies in the UK—over a 14-year period (2011–2024). There were virtually no ethnic Chinese leaders, while ethnic Indians did not differ significantly from Whites in leadership representation. In Study 2, we analyzed a large, nationwide dataset from the UK Biobank (N = 322,734) to further investigate the moderating role of gender and the mediating role of risk tolerance. While ethnic Chinese overall experienced a Bamboo Ceiling (thus replicating Study 1’s results), this main effect was moderated by gender: Ethnic Chinese men were significantly less likely than ethnic Indian and White men to attain leadership positions as a function of lower risk tolerance; by contrast, ethnic Chinese, Indian, and White women did not differ significantly and were each disadvantaged relative to their male counterparts. These effects were robust across survey-based and polygenic measures of risk tolerance. Overall, our findings highlight the potential disadvantages of low-risk-tolerant individuals in leadership attainment and underscore the need for organizations to provide more equitable and inclusive pathways to leadership.

The Double-Edged Effect of State Ownership on Private Firms’ Executive Gender Gaps

DEI
Xiuyuan Fang (Fudan University); Jinxuan Wu (Fudan University)
Drawing on institutional theory, this study examines how state ownership, which embodies state logic and bureaucratic logic, influences the executive gender pay gap and gender promotion gap in private firms. Using data of Chinese privately owned listed firms from 2014 to 2022, we find a double-edged effect of state ownership: it narrows the executive gender pay gap but widens the executive gender promotion gap. Furthermore, integrating the legitimacy perspective, this study examines the moderating effects of institutional pressures (media supervision as a form of normative pressure and female political leaders in local government as a form of cognitive pressure). Results show that media supervision amplifies the positive relationship between state ownership and the executive gender promotion gap. In contrast, female political leaders in local governments weaken the negative relationship between state ownership and the executive gender pay gap. This study reveals how inter-organizational relationships transmit institutional logics by introducing an institutional logics perspective to explain gender inequality, and contributes to the understanding of how institutional logics and institutional pressures interact to produce complex gender equality outcomes in organizations by integrating the institutional logics perspective with Scott’s three institutional pillars.

Leading with Ego: Gendered Consequences of Narcissism in Corporate Acquisitions

DEI
Ole Moritz Meißner (University of Groningen); Lipeng (Gary) Ge (University of Groningen)
This study examines how CEO narcissism affects post-acquisition performance and whether these effects differ by CEO gender. Prior studies have largely viewed narcissism as a double-edged sword, with evidence of both positive and negative impacts of CEO narcissism being documented. Drawing on social congruity theory, the study highlights that when female CEOs possess narcissistic traits that are inconsistent with their communal role expectations, such inconsistency triggers intensive scrutiny over female narcissistic CEOs’ acquisition behaviors. Thus, compared to male CEOs, female CEOs are able to leverage the positive aspect of narcissism and enjoy better acquisition performance. Using a sample of U.S. acquiring firms between 2007 and 2022, this study analyzes the relationship between CEO narcissism and post-acquisition performance. The results, however, indicate that CEO narcissism is positively associated with post-acquisition performance. Regarding gender differences, this study reveals pronounced gender asymmetries: narcissism is associated with less favorable post-acquisition performance for female CEOs, whereas positive effects of narcissism are observed for male CEOs. Overall, the findings suggest that the performance implications of CEO narcissism differ across different gender groups. This study contributes to the literature by highlighting the importance of gender in shaping how executive traits translate into superior acquisition outcomes.

Women’s Representation in Top-Income Positions and Gender Pay Equity in Firms

DEI
Gonzalo Valdes (Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile); Lorena Reyes; Andrea Bentancor; Tomas Reyes
This study examines how female representation in high-income positions within firms influences women’s wages and organizational gender pay gaps in the Chilean private sector. Drawing on perspectives on gendered organizations, the analysis uses matched employer–employee administrative data from the Chilean Unemployment Insurance system covering the period 2006–2023 and nearly four million salary observations. We estimated fixed-effects regression models that include individual- and firm-level variables. We operationalized female representation as the proportion of women within the top income quartile and within the top five percent, allowing comparison between a broader and a more stringent definition. The results suggest that greater female representation in high-income positions is associated with higher wages for women and with smaller organizational gender pay gaps. On average, a 10- percentage-point rise in the share of women in top positions increases women’s wages by about 2%. This effect is observed across the entire wage distribution when representation in the top five percent is considered. Female representation in top positions also narrows organizational gender pay gaps by up to 8%. Overall, the findings provide large-scale evidence from a developing economy context that women’s representation across upper levels of the wage hierarchy plays an important role in shaping equity-related wage outcomes within organizations.

Beyond Compliance: Inclusion or Deviance after a Board Gender Diversity Comply-or-Explain Policy

DEI
Jannine Poletti-Hughes; Beatriz Martínez (Public University of Navarra); Isabel+34948169398 Abinzano (Public University of Navarra); Siri Ann Terjesen (Florida Atlantic University)
This study investigates whether comply-or-explain gender diversity reforms promote meaningful inclusion on corporate boards and how firms deviate from governance norms following reform. Using 42,767 firm-year observations from 47 countries and a difference-in-differences design, we distinguish representation from inclusion through a five-dimension index capturing pre-existing female presence, reaching three or more women, participation in key committees, presence of a female CEO/Chair, and increases in female directors without expanding board size. We find that while reforms increase female representation, their effects on inclusion depend on national gender equality and firm visibility. Two deviant behaviors emerge: reduced under-conformity only in more gender-equal countries, and widespread reluctant compliance—expanding boards to add women without shifting power—which may ultimately limit the intended benefits of reform.
