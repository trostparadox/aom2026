The Dark Side Of Differentiation: Attributional Responses To Coworkers’ I-Deal & Their Fallout

SIM
Fauzia Syed (Rabat Business School, International University of Rabat); Saima Naseer (Brock University); Fatima Shamim
Drawing on attribution theory, the current study examines how coworkers’ perceptions of idiosyncratic deals (I-deals) affect emotional, cognitive, and behavioral outcomes. Specifically, we examined how bystanders’ perceptions of I-deals promote counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs) and job performance through the underlying dynamics of resentment and, further, through detachment and emotional exhaustion. We further tested how these indirect relationships are contingent on psychological entitlement in a moderated-mediation study. The current study utilized a time-lagged research design with data (n=280 dyads) from employees and their peers working in service-sector organizations in Pakistan at four-time intervals, with a one-month lag between each measurement point. The findings confirm the mediating effects of co-workers’ I-deals on employee outcomes via the creation of resentment, followed by two parallel mechanisms: psychological detachment and emotional exhaustion. Moreover, these indirect effects are stronger when high levels of psychological entitlement exist. Overall, our study sheds light on key mechanisms and boundary conditions through which co-workers’ I-deals negatively affect an individual's behavioral outcomes. Our study adds to the literature on I-deals and organizational behavior and extends Attribution theory by identifying emotions and recovery failures as core mechanisms linking co-workers’ I-deals to behavioral outcomes.

When Do Multi-Stakeholder Initiatives Work? Coalition Building and Substantive Social Actions (WITHDRAWN)

SIM
Rakesh B. Sambharya (Rutgers University-Camden); Deeksha Singh (Rutgers University)
Firms increasingly participate in multi-stakeholder initiatives (MSIs) to address social and environmental challenges that are difficult to govern through markets or regulation alone. Despite their prevalence, evidence on whether MSIs generate meaningful outcomes remains mixed, in large part, because firms that lead MSIs often fail to undertake substantive actions. We address this gap by recognizing MSIs as organizational coalitions and examine how lead firms’ coalition-building efforts link with substantive outcomes. Advancing a framework that identifies three coalition building practices: self-restraint, attention to disadvantaged members, and ongoing monitoring and development, we use longitudinal data on the palm oil industry to analyze how lead firms’ coalition building efforts tie to their adoption of sustainable oil, an observable indicator of substantive actions. Our findings suggest that lead firms that visibly constrain their own discretion by limiting self-promotional signaling as well as support disadvantaged coalition members are often those that commit to substantive actions. The findings suggest that participation in collective governance arrangements does not uniformly enhance outcomes; rather, performance-relevant effects depend on lead firms’ commitment to coalitional relationships.

Committee-Level Dispersion in Board Governance: Evidence from Stakeholder Oversight

SIM
Eunyoung Park (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Sam Garg (ESSEC Business School); Xinhao Qiao (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Boards increasingly oversee stakeholder-related issues spanning multiple functional domains, yet how they structure such cross-cutting oversight remains understudied. We theorize that committee-level dispersion versus concentration of stakeholder oversight—the extent to which stakeholder oversight is distributed across standing committees—shapes boards’ capacity to process and respond to stakeholder demands. Analyzing S&P 500 firms’ committee charters, we find that greater dispersion is associated with stronger environmental and social performance. Dispersion aligns the governance structure with the multidimensional nature of stakeholder issues by embedding oversight into boards’ core decision arenas, broadening attention across functional frames, and enhancing information processing. Moreover, dispersion is most effective when accompanied by integration mechanisms: dedicated stakeholder committees provide formal coordination, while directors serving on multiple committees enable relational integration. Our study identifies committee-level dispersion as a core architectural dimension of board governance, highlighting how the distribution and integration of oversight responsibilities shape boards’ responses to cross-cutting stakeholder issues.

Reconfiguring Internal Legitimacy Through Exogenous Cyber Incidents

SIM
Fernando Ressetti Pinheiro Vianna (Technological Federal University of Paraná); Rafael Alcadipani Da Silveira (FGV EAESP)
Cybersecurity has become a critical social and organizational concern as digital infrastructures increasingly expose organizations to severe financial, operational, and reputational risks. Despite widespread recognition of its importance, cybersecurity departments often struggle to secure sustained internal legitimacy, as their preventive and anticipatory claims remain weakly prioritized until major incidents occur. This raises an important question: how is cybersecurity’s internal legitimacy constructed, contested, and reconfigured over time, particularly in response to exogenous critical events? To address this question, we conducted a qualitative study based on 40 interviews with cybersecurity professionals—including operational specialists, managers, consultants, and Chief Information Security Officers—conducted in two waves between March and October 2025. Our analysis reveals a three-stage process. First, cybersecurity departments engage in continuous internal legitimacy work through policies, training, and vulnerability demonstrations, yet these efforts rarely translate into sustained enactment. Second, internal legitimacy remains persistently fragile, as cybersecurity claims are acknowledged but weakly prioritized amid competing organizational agendas. Third, exogenous cyber incidents function as high-salience shocks that abruptly reconfigure internal legitimacy, temporarily transforming contested claims into organizational imperatives. However, this event-driven legitimacy proves powerful yet unstable, often generating cycles of heightened commitment followed by retrenchment. Overall, our study advances SIM research by conceptualizing internal legitimacy as an event-sensitive evaluative process and showing how legitimacy judgments shape organizational prioritization, responsibility, and action in the face of persistent technological risk.

Symbolic Stewardship: Policy–Behavior Decoupling in Investor Proxy Voting

SIM
Siyana Gurova (ZHAW School of Management and Law)
Institutional investors increasingly signal sustainability commitments through proxy-voting policies, yet the alignment between this rhetoric and actual voting remains empirically untested. This paper introduces a policy-behavior gap measure with two components: direction, capturing systematic under- or over-implementation of stated commitments, and magnitude, capturing the absolute size of misalignment. Policy text is mapped onto a continuous scale of stated stewardship using latent semantic scaling. Bayesian ideal-point models on ESG-relevant shareholder proposals estimate revealed stewardship from house-level votes on a commensurable scale. The gap is computed as the residual between revealed and stated stewardship and decomposed into sign and absolute value. Cross-sectional regressions then explain variation in each gap measure across investors. The analysis covers 205 institutional investors with matched policies and voting records in the 2025 proxy season. Asset owners show more positive directional gaps (quiet stewardship) and smaller absolute gaps (tighter coupling) than asset managers. Europe-based investors also exhibit more positive directional gaps and smaller absolute gaps than U.S. investors. Investor size predicts more negative directional gaps but not larger absolute gaps, pointing to incentives rather than capacity. The findings clarify when disclosed voting policies operate as credible commitments versus low-cost symbolism in institutional investor stewardship.
