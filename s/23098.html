The Hidden Costs of Hybrid Work: How Daily Work Locations Shape Employee Extra-Role Behaviors

HR
Jian Shi (Renmin University of China)
Hybrid working has become the “new normal”, yet its consequences for employees’ day-to-day work behaviors remain insufficiently understood. Drawing on various social psychological theories and the emerging literature on hybrid working, we examined how working remotely (versus on-site) affects employees’ engagement in both formal role responsibilities (i.e., in-role behaviors such as task performance and withdrawal behavior) and discretionary behaviors beyond one’s job descriptions (i.e., extra-role behaviors such as helping and creative behavior). We conducted a daily diary study among hybrid workers in the U.K., capturing within-person variations in work location and work behaviors across multiple days. As expected, our findings revealed that employees engaged less in extra-role behaviors on days working remotely (versus on-site), while in-role behaviors remained stable across work locations. We further showed that these reductions in helping behaviors were explained by employees feeling less engaged and less identified with their team on remote-working days, and that decreases in creative behavior were accounted for by lower team identification alone. Contrary to our predictions, we found no evidence that identity leadership mitigated these effects. We discuss implications for the design of hybrid working policies to sustain positive work behaviors.

Remote Work as a Signal: Unpacking the Dynamics Between Sustainability Values and Commitment

HR
Luca La Mantia (Politecnico di Milano); gabriele boccoli (Politecnico di Milano); Elia Rigamonti (Politecnico di Milano); Luca Gastaldi (Politecnico di Milano); Mariano Corso (Politecnico di Milano)
Many organizations are gearing up to meet sustainability challenges by integrating corporate social responsibility practices into their processes. However, if these interventions are not implemented by employees who are truly committed to the organization, or are not accompanied by real changes in the values that determine employee behavior, they risk remaining ineffective. We test whether congruence between individual and organizational values related to sustainability fosters employees’ affective commitment towards the organization, and if a strong commitment and identification with it lead employees to experience greater value congruence. These two relationships are explored respectively from the lens of Person-Organization Fit Theory (P-O Fit) and Social Identity Theory (SIT). We further extend this theoretical foundation through Signaling Theory, positing that the effectiveness of the cultural shift that embeds sustainability values depends on the organization’s credibility to put them into practice. Organizations can promote remote work policies, which positively affect several sustainability outcomes, to signal their serious intention in pursuing and implementing their sustainability values. A longitudinal survey of 811 employees from an Italian multi-utility company reveals that value congruence only enhances affective commitment in employees who work remotely, suggesting that alignment alone does not foster commitment without credible organizational signals. However, high affective commitment promotes employees’ internalization of corporate sustainability values, and it is stronger in workers who do remote work. Findings align with SIT while challenging traditional P-O Fit assumptions, and posing remote work as a relevant catalyst in sustainable transition.

On-Site Working Versus Teleworking and Daily Work Goal Progress

HR
Xinyu Zhang (Beijing Technology and Business University)
Although hybrid work is increasingly prevalent, how daily work location shapes employees’ work goal attainment remains insufficiently studied. Drawing on self-determination theory, we theorize that the working location each morning (on-site vs. teleworking) shapes subsequent work goal progress by morning perception of job control and authenticity. We tested our theoretical model in two complementary studies: a 10-work-day experience-sampling study (Study 1: N = 185 Chinese employees, 1,640 day-level observations) and a scenario-based experiment (Study 2: N = 193, U.S. sample). Across both studies, daily teleworking (versus on-site work) predicted higher morning job control and authenticity; these, in turn, sequentially promoted daily flow and same-day work goal progress. Perceived leader support counteracted location-based need frustration, helping employees maintain their morning perception of job control and authenticity and thereby sustaining their subsequent flow and daily goal progress. Collectively, these findings offer valuable insights for both theoretical understanding and practical applications in managing hybrid workers.

How Identity Integration Shapes Daily Telework Effects on Procrastination

HR
Xinyu Zhang (Beijing Technology and Business University)
With hybrid work becoming widespread, organizations need clearer insight into how alternating between home and office shapes employees’ social experiences and daily self-regulation. Guided by role identity theory, we investigate when and why teleworking fosters counterproductive delay. We conducted two studies. Study 1, a scenario experiment (N = 236), manipulated work location (teleworking vs. on-site) and identity integration (high vs. low) to establish causal paths among the focal variables. Study 2 adopted a ten-work-day experience-sampling design with 161 employees engaged in hybrid arrangements, generating 1,436 daily observations that capture within-person fluctuations across successive remote and office days. Results from both studies converge: teleworking compared to on-site working increases daily loneliness for employees low in identity integration; daily loneliness, in turn, predicts higher same-day procrastination; and loneliness mediates the interactive effect of work location and identity integration on daily procrastination. These findings advance telework research through an identity lens and offer practical guidance for cultivating productive remote workforces.

Remote and Hybrid Work as an HR Design Challenge: An Integrated Framework for Employee Well-Being

HR
Rania Shkairat
Remote and hybrid work have become persistent attributes in contemporary organizations, however research on their implications on employee well-being shows inconsistent findings. Prior investigations elucidate various advantages, including autonomy, flexibility, and risks, such as social isolation and the nebulous work–non-work boundaries; however, they do not provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how these arrangements influence well-being. This paper addresses this gap by reframing remote and hybrid work as an HR design issue rather than simply a location decision. Using the Job Demands–Resources model, strategic boundary control theory, and social connection, we present an integrated framework that examines how these arrangements affect well-being through psychosocial work conditions, boundary control processes, and social connection. We also explore how HR practices and leadership influence these dynamics. This paper advances the HR literature by synthesizing diverse research streams and providing practical recommendations for designing remote and hybrid systems that enhance employee well-being.
