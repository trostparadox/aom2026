The Hidden Barrier: Contagion Psychology and Resistance to Remanufactured Goods in B2B Supply Chains

OSCM
Fayyaz Khan (Al Yamamah University)
The circular economy demands that organisations embrace remanufactured components, yet adoption in business-to-business markets remains stubbornly low despite favourable economics and equivalent technical specifications. This paper argues that a critical barrier has been systematically overlooked: the psychological contamination beliefs that procurement professionals carry but cannot openly acknowledge. Drawing on Rozin and Nemeroff's law of contagion (the ancient intuition that objects retain the "essence" of previous owners), I develop a theoretical framework explaining why organisational buyers resist remanufactured goods even when rationality dictates otherwise. We introduce the concept of professional masking, whereby procurement managers suppress visceral disgust responses but unconsciously channel them into inflated technical risk assessments. Using a theory adaptation methodology, I translate contagion psychology from consumer research into the B2B domain, identifying boundary conditions and mechanisms unique to organisational buying contexts. The paper advances seven propositions addressing contamination mechanisms, moderating conditions including component criticality and brand association risk, and mitigation strategies centred on transformation framing. We contribute to behavioural operations management by extending the field beyond cognitive biases to encompass visceral and emotional influences on supply chain decisions.

The Effect of Transfer Payments on Service Level Contract Preferences and Performance

OSCM
Jose Benedicto Duhaylongsod (IESEG School of Management); Yingshuai Zhao (University of Cologne)
This paper investigates supplier preferences and production decisions under flat penalty versus bonus service-level contracts in supply chain settings. While both contract types are mathematically equivalent when parametrized to yield identical expected profits, behavioral factors may drive systematic differences in decision-making. We develop a reference-dependent behavioral model that predicts that suppliers will choose suboptimal production quantities under both contract types and will show a systematic preference for bonus over penalty service-level contracts despite their profit equivalence. We test these predictions through two complementary experimental studies. Study 1 examines production decisions under both contract types and shows that production quantities are below profit-maximizing levels but are statistically equivalent across contracts. Study 2 investigates contract preference, revealing a significant preference for bonus contracts. The results are both consistent with the predictions of the behavioral model. In addition, our behavioral model outperforms the alternative models in explaining actual decisions. These results provide insights for managers designing supply chain contracts in settings where failure to meet service levels can lead to operational disruptions, highlighting how contract framing influences both strategic choices and operational performance.

Should I Change My Physician’s Gender? The Role of Ambiguity in Patient-Physician Gender Concordance

OSCM
Xiaodan Shao (Nanyang Technological University); Vivek Choudhary (Nanyang Technological University); Anand Gopal (University of Maryland College Park)
Matching patients with physicians remains an important part of the healthcare system. Literature has established that patients tend to have strong preferences for physician attributes such as gender, race, education, and experience, amongst which gender is particularly significant in driving these preferences. Patients often prefer physicians of the same gender (i.e., gender concordance) to the extent that they are willing to postpone procedures or incur additional costs. This preference for gender concordance enhances operational efficiency by improving health quality and reducing unnecessary resource utilization through improved patient-physician alignment. However, major societal events that force sudden changes in healthcare delivery and access can disrupt preference, regarding physician gender. This study examines how the heightened ambiguity during the lockdown influences patients’ adherence to or deviation from their typical gender-based physician choices. Drawing on an ambiguity aversion lens, we employ a differences-in-differences model to examine whether heightened ambiguity during lockdowns creates tension for patients between adhering to gender preferences and prioritizing empathetic care in choosing physicians. The findings indicate ~11% decrease in gender concordance for female doctors post-lockdown, driven primarily by male patients increasingly choosing female doctors, while female patients’ preferences remain unchanged. This shift may be attributed to the perception that female physicians exhibit greater empathy through spending more time with patients, making them a preferred choice amid the ambiguity introduced by the lockdown. To address these imbalances, policymakers and healthcare leaders may increase the supply for female physicians by enhancing recruitment efforts, expanding medical school admissions, and implementing retention strategies in high-demand specialties. Healthcare systems can adopt flexible workforce planning and tailored training to accommodate both short-term and long-term shifts in gender-based preferences. Encouraging male doctors to adopt consultation techniques commonly associated with female physicians may help mitigate abrupt preference changes during future crises.

Who Pays the Cost: The Effect of Hospital Occupancy on Racial Health Disparities

OSCM
Rachna Shah (University of Minnesota); Minjeong Kang (University of Minnesota Twin Cities)
Racial disparities in healthcare outcomes persist even within facilities, yet little is known about how operational conditions shape equity in care delivery. We examine whether operational workload differentially affects discretionary clinical decisions across patients by race, focusing on epidural analgesia use in labor and delivery units. Using inpatient discharge data from New York State and exploiting quasi-random variation in hospital occupancy due to unpredictable labor onset, we examine whether capacity pressure affects epidural use differently by patient race and hospital affiliation

The Impacts of Supplier Cybersecurity Risk on Firm’s Bargaining Power with Customers: Evidence from

OSCM
Caishi Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Hanxiang Ma (Yunnan Minzu University); zhiyi Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); YUANXIN PANG (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Purpose – This paper aims to reveal the impact of supplier cybersecurity risks on shaping a firm’s bargaining power with its customers. Design/methodology/approach – From the perspective of resource dependence theory (RDT), this study constructs a theoretical framework to examine how supplier cybersecurity risks affect a firm’s bargaining power with its customers. Using publicly disclosed data on the top five suppliers of Chinese listed firms, this study builds a supplier–firm–year panel dataset to capture supply chain linkages over time. A textual analysis approach is employed to develop a measure of supplier cybersecurity risk. Findings – The empirical results reveal that supplier cybersecurity risk significantly weakens a firm’s bargaining power with its customers, a finding that remains robust across multiple tests. The mechanism analysis indicates that supplier cybersecurity risk reduces firms’ bargaining power by undermining their production efficiency and hindering their innovation performance. The moderation analysis further demonstrates that corporate social responsibility (CSR) and information security management systems (ISMS) can mitigate the negative spillover effects of supplier cybersecurity risk on firms’ bargaining power. Moreover, the adverse impact is found to be more pronounced when the geographic distance within the supply chain is greater and the cybersecurity legal environment is less developed. Originality/value – This paper reveals the impact of supplier cybersecurity risks on shaping a firm’s bargaining power with its customers.
