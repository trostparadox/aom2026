Minority Investor Protection, Family Control, and Board Composition

STR
Longwang Fu (City University of Hong Kong); Miao YUN (City University of Hong Kong); Dong Hyun Shin (City University of Hong Kong); Long Wang (City University of Hong Kong)
While prior research has extensively examined the power dynamics between controlling family owners and non-family shareholders by focusing on large blockholders, it remains unclear whether and how non-family minority investors can also play a role in shaping such power dynamics. Drawing on the literature on socioemotional wealth (SEW) preservation together with state influence in emerging economies, we theorize that once family firms are subject to a regulatory reform that empowers minority investors, they will perceive a substantial threat to family control and then strategically adapt their board composition to preserve SEW. Specifically, rather than directly increasing family representation on boards due to legitimacy constraints, they may demonstrate a significant cutback on non-family directors while no change for family ones, to indirectly sustain and even strengthen family control and influence. This effect is strengthened for firms that hold a greater state ownership or have received a larger amount of government subsidies before the reform. We also find that this effect is driven primarily by the suppression of non-family directors on key board committees (audit, compensation, and nomination). We corroborate our propositions using a quasi-natural experiment in China. Our study contributes to the literature on the controlling family’s SEW preservation, the governance effectiveness of regulatory reforms, and state influence in emerging economies.

Ownership Identity and Employee Relations: How Family Control Shapes Litigation

STR
Ahmet Sezer (Bocconi University); Jeroen Neckebrouck (IESE Business School); Mario Daniele Amore (Bocconi University)
Using UK Employment Tribunal decisions linked to population-level data on firms’ ownership, governance, and financial conditions, we analyze how corporate ownership affects the likelihood of employee lawsuits across major claim types. Our results reveal that firms controlled by family owners face a 15% lower probability of employee lawsuits. This reduced likelihood applies to multiple discrimination claim types (e.g., sex, race, and age discrimination) as well as non-discrimination disputes (e.g., unfair dismissal, breach of contract, and unlawful wage deductions). Extending the analysis to ownership structure more broadly, we show that, independently of family control, greater ownership concentration is associated with higher lawsuit risk, higher managerial ownership is associated with lower risk, and outside-investor ownership is largely unrelated to lawsuit incidence. The protective association of family control is stronger when families hold larger ownership stakes and more board seats, and when firms are larger and older; it weakens and can reverse in years with workforce reductions. Overall, these results highlight how ownership identity and control rights are associated with the likelihood of formal legal disputes.

Charismatic Leadership or Charismatic Authority? A Double-Edge Sword for Entrepreneurs

STR
Grace Tien (Northwestern University); Edward J. Zajac (Northwestern University)
A founder’s charisma has typically been seen as a desirable entrepreneurial asset whereby the founder’s behaviors, particularly the crafting of narrative strategies emphasizing the founder, can build a positive connection with employee followers and other important external constituencies (e.g., investors). In this study, we revisit this sunny characterization and suggest instead that as storytelling of an entrepreneur’s charisma increases, so does the likelihood that an external audience will conflate charismatic leadership with charismatic authority, resulting in more negative reactions. We suggest this is particularly likely in a more authoritarian cultural context (i.e., the Chinese venture market), and we consider this issue empirically by providing in-depth qualitative analysis of when and why founder charisma is likely to be seen less as furthering an entrepreneurial growth opportunity and more as a potential ideological threat, particularly by state affiliated investors. We also highlight how charismatic founders seemed unable/unwilling to recognize and counter this potential double-edged sword of charismatic leadership, continuing their narrative strategy’s primary emphasis on founder personality/background, rather than technology/product differentiation. We conclude by considering the implications of our perspective and findings for future research on charismatic leadership among entrepreneurs and work on cultural entrepreneurship.

Manage Professionalization Challenges in Founder-Dominated Firms: A Multi-Case Study

STR
Neng Liang (China Europe International Business School); Shu Lin
Transformation from founder-centric startup to professional management is a key stage in the growth of new firms and it entails significant changes in the firm’s leadership structure and management practice. Because most entrepreneurs are not good managers, this transformation is often achieved through founder CEO success (FCS) in the West, where most founders surrender control to professional CEOs either voluntarily or involuntarily. In China, however, most founders retain executive control after professional CEOs are appointed. In such a context, how can the CEOs professionalize the company’s prevailing management practice where the founders are still in control? What strategy or strategies those successful CEOs developed and pursued in the process? To explore these questions, we conducted an inductive multi-case study of FCS in eight private Chinese firms. Our findings show that the prerequisite of effective FCS is a long dating period to avoid joining firms with fundamental business and leadership deficiencies. After joining the firm, effective CEOs employed a three-pronged strategy: remember who the boss is, lead as a co-pilot and a partner, and create a shared-pathway forward. This study provides a “up close,” contextualized “thick description” of the FCS process in China, with firsthand information not available from other sources. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such study of FCS in China. Research and managerial implications are also discussed.
