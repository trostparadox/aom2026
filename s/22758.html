When Expediency Feels Contaminating: How Employees Cleanse After Cutting Corners

OB
Lijing Zhao; Benjamin G. Perkins (University of Alabama); Sunghyuck (Ryan) (Ryan) Mah (Seoul National University)
Employee expediency, or the use of unethical practices to expedite work for self-serving purposes, has become increasingly common in contemporary organizations. Yet research has focused almost exclusively on its antecedents, leaving the consequences of expediency poorly understood. Drawing on moral self-regulation theory, we investigate how stewardship behavior cleanses employees from feelings of moral impurity after engaging in work expediency and the role psychological entitlement and supervisor negative moral feedback play in this moral cleansing process. We test our model in three studies: an online experiment of 156 working adults, a multi-wave field study of 167 full-time professionals, and a multi-wave, multi-source field study of 294 supervisor-subordinate dyads. Results from these studies supported our moral cleansing theoretical model and revealed that psychological entitlement weakens the relationship between expediency and moral impurity while supervisor negative moral feedback strengthens it. By identifying moral impurity as a distinct mechanism of moral self-regulation and integrating individual differences with social factors as joint influences that shape the moral meaning of expediency, this research contributes to a more complete understanding of how employees navigate subtle moral violations in contemporary organizations.

The Paradoxical Effect of Unethical Pro-Family Behavior: Moral Cleansing Versus Moral Licensing

OB
Kun Xie (Tongji University); Jinlian Luo (Tongji University); Zhu YAO (Hunan University)
The prevalence and adverse consequences of unethical pro-family behavior (UPFB) in organizational settings have been increasingly recognized in emerging research. However, little is known about the paradoxical psychological mechanisms of UPFB that shape employees’ subsequent behaviors across work and family domains. By integrating moral self-regulation theory with work-family interface literature, this study develops and tests a theoretical framework that explains UPFB’s inherent moral paradox. Specifically, we propose that UPFB engagement simultaneously induces organization-oriented shame (activating moral cleansing) and family-oriented psychological entitlement (triggering moral licensing), which lead to increased workplace exemplification and heightened family incivility respectively. Moreover, employees with stronger work-family centrality experience intensified organization-oriented shame and greater family-oriented psychological entitlement when engaging in UPFB, thereby strengthening the indirect effects on subsequent domain-specific behaviors. Our propositions are validated by the findings of a rigorous multi-source study (employee, colleague, family member) and three-wave field study. This study provides a better understanding of the complex moral self-regulation processes that employees experience at work-family, and discusses the theoretical and practical implications, limitations, and future directions.

Resolute or Resigned: Follower Reactions to Leader–Follower Ethical Disagreement

OB
Alexander Lyle Effinger (Central Michigan University)
While existing research on ethical conflict has demonstrated numerous negative outcomes for employees facing dissensus regarding matters of right and wrong, the current research shifts the focus from the consequences of ethical conflict to the processes through which employees cope during such episodes. Specifically, I examine leader–follower ethical conflict to understand the intrapersonal and interpersonal processes followers use to navigate ethical disagreement. Across two studies, utilizing a critical incident technique and an experimental vignette method, I investigate how leader–follower ethical disagreement induces moral stress within the follower, thereby motivating follower moral agency. Further, I explore individual differences that steer followers toward functional versus dysfunctional responses. Results suggest that while ethical disagreement triggers moral stress—which leads to ethical voice and job withdrawal—individuals with higher moral self-efficacy are significantly less likely to withdraw. By illuminating these processes, this research advances our understanding of how ethical conflict motivates employee behavior and how organizations can empower staff to facilitate more constructive employee behaviors and outcomes in the face of ethical disagreement.
