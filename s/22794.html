From Template to Processual Convergence: Insights from the French Art Ecosystem

MH
Nathan POTIER (Université Paris Nanterre)
By adopting a historical methodology grounded in neo-institutional theory, this paper examines how the French art ecosystem’s transformations from 1816 to present modified institutional isomorphic pressures on artists, reshaping their business models. Analyzing legislative documents, artists’ writings, and focus group data, we trace three distinct periods characterized by different isomorphic configurations: the Neo-Classical era (1816-1881) with maximum institutional control, the Modern period (1881-1981) with market-driven normative pressures, and the Contemporary period (1981-present) with fragmented institutional landscapes. Our findings reveal a paradox: despite institutional fragmentation and weakened isomorphic mechanisms, contemporary artists exhibit convergence in adaptive capabilities while diverging in outputs and structures. We theorize this as processual isomorphism—a mutation where institutional pressures shift from template convergence (similarity in structure/outputs) to capability convergence (similarity in adaptive processes). Under conditions of institutional fragmentation, environmental volatility, and intensified competition, isomorphic forces redirect from prescribing content to shaping meta-competences. This reconciles isomorphism and institutional complexity literatures: fragmented institutions generate convergent pressures toward polyvalence implemented diversely. Empirically, we characterize contemporary artists as autonomous entrepreneurs operating as organizations, emphasizing the relevance of studying them through management sciences.

The Historical Literature Review Method in Management and Organization Studies

MH
Matteo Cristofaro (University of Rome Tor Vergata); Mairi Maclean (University of Bath); Charles Edward Harvey (Newcastle University Business School); Jeffrey Muldoon (Southeastern Louisiana University)
This article introduces the Historical Literature Review (HLR) as a structured and theory-generative methodology for reconstructing the temporal evolution of concepts, constructs, and scholarly debates in management and organization studies. While existing review types—systematic, narrative, integrative, bibliometric, and critical—have advanced knowledge synthesis, they lack methodological tools for tracing how ideas emerge, shift, and stabilize across historical contexts. The HLR addresses this gap by combining historical contextualization with interactive reflexivity, enabling scholars to interrogate the epistemic, institutional, and socio-political forces that shape knowledge production over time. We propose a six-step iterative framework. Each step guides scholars through temporal framing, corpus construction, intellectual genealogy, and abductive theorizing. The HLR does not treat history as background, but as a method for generating conceptually innovative and contextually grounded theory. By repositioning the literature review as a site of historical theorizing, this article offers both a methodological contribution and a call to reimagine the role of temporality in management scholarship.

Regulation as Legitimizing Deviant Practices

MH
Simon Billinger; Rasmus Nykvist (Linköping University)
Organizations are increasingly exploring new market opportunities and pursuing higher returns by operating outside the regulatory framework. Previous explanations that explore change resulting from rule evasion focus on the lack of timely regulatory interventions to prevent the deviant practices from gaining in popularity. But what if there is a regulatory intervention? In this paper, we explore when and how regulatory interventions aimed at preventing rule evasion may inadvertently result in an increased usage of the very practices they sought to curtail. We use a micro history approach, relying on a unique and extensive set of archival material to study rule evasion in the form of the Swedish “grey credit market” between 1955 and 1985. Drawing on previous scholarship in practice-driven institutionalism, rule evasion, and legitimacy we find that regulatory intervention may reduce the ambiguity often associated with evasive practices, and that by clarifying the rules for compliance, deviant practices are bestowed with increased regulatory legitimacy. In combination, the result is that the deviant practices become increasingly viewed as a permanent feature of the market, enabling their diffusion throughout the organizational field.

Organizational Repair Focused Deinstitutionalization: A Historical Case Study

MH
Michael Heller; John Stewart Hassard (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester); Michael Rowlinson (University of Exeter)
In this paper we present the new theory of organizational repair focused deinstitutionalization. This is based on Crawford et al.'s (2024) concept of repair focused deinstitutionalization and demonstrates how organizations can repair institutions which are no longer relevant or fit for purpose due to environmental changes. We demonstrate the theory through a historical case study of the deinstitutionalization of company magazines in the UK in the 1960s and 1970s and their replacement by internal communication.
