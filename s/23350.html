When Low Performance Takes Root: Deadwood and Delay at the U.S. Patent Office

STR
Christina Angie Nguyen (Fordham University)
Organizations rely on accountability systems to maintain productivity. Yet when both sanctions for low performance and incentives for high performance are weak, employees may scale back their discretionary effort. This erosion of commitment undermines the informal norms of cooperation that support group performance. I develop and examine the concept of "deadwood," a colloquial label for employees who shift from adequate productivity into a sustained period of low productivity but persist in the organization, often protected by tenure or other safeguards. Using administrative and personnel records from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office and the U.S. Office of Personnel Management, I find that deadwood-heavy groups face longer processing delays among non-low performers and higher exit rates, particularly of highly educated examiners. These delays became especially pronounced after Alice Corporation v. CLS Bank International, an institutional shock that disrupted examination routines and exposed the vulnerability of deadwood-heavy groups. These results suggest that entrenched low performers not only depress peers’ output, potentially through weakened morale, but also hinder adaptation when organizations are under pressure.

Navigating the Stability Paradox: The Shifting Nature of Complementarities in Team Design

STR
Shemuel Lampronti (University of Padua); Stefano Li Pira (University of Bologna)
A central tension in organizational design lies between stability, which fosters coordination, and flexibility, which enables adaptation. This paper investigates how the interplay between leadership stability and team member stability affects performance, and how this relationship is moderated by team skill. We argue that these two forms of stability function as complements for low-skill teams but as substitutes for high-skill teams. For low-skill teams, stability in both leadership and membership mutually reinforces the development of essential coordination capabilities. For high-skill teams, however, this same combination can breed inertia and lead to competency traps; in this context, a blend of stability and flux—substituting one form of stability for another —provides both continuity and the beneficial variety needed for adaptation. Using a longitudinal dataset of Venetian sailing regattas, we test this contingent relationship. Our findings demonstrate that complementarities in organizational design are not static but are contingent on the level of team capability. This provides a critical micro-level mechanism that links team composition to broader theories of organizational adaptation, exploration-exploitation, and competency traps, advancing a more dynamic understanding of organizational fit.

Corporate Purpose in Motion: An Evolutionary Framework and Typology for Purpose Enactment

STR
Raffaella Montera (Sapienza University of Rome); Nicola Cucari; Salvatore Esposito De Falco; Carlos Rey (International University of Catalonia)
Corporate purpose is both celebrated as a strategic compass and criticised as rhetoric, creating ambiguity about how it forms, adapts, and endures. This paper reframes purpose as an evolutionary and coevolutionary construct unfolding through cycles of variation, selection, and retention across three dimensions: genetic, relational, and phenotypic. The genetic dimension reflects an organisation’s historical identity and codes; the relational dimension captures stakeholder embeddedness and legitimacy negotiation; and the phenotypic dimension encompasses observable strategies, narratives, and practices. Building on this, we propose the purpose alignment grid, a diagnostic matrix with two axes: genotype–phenotype alignment and inside-out versus outside-in stakeholder orientation. The grid identifies four ideal-types of purpose states - aspirational, authentic, decoupled, and detached - while clarifying mechanisms that shift firms along virtuous or vicious pathways. By integrating identity, legitimacy, and coevolutionary theories, the model explains why some initiatives consolidate into resilient trajectories while others drift into symbolic compliance. It also specifies coupling mechanisms that embed purpose into strategy, business models, and governance. This study contributes a dynamic, integrative theory of corporate purpose, offering scholars a framework to move beyond static definitions and practitioners a tool to diagnose misalignments, calibrate ambiguity, and embed purpose authentically for long-term legitimacy and competitiveness. Keywords: Corporate purpose; corporate purpose typologies; evolutionary perspective; genotype; relational dimension; phenotype

Unjangling Opposing Simultaneity: A Review of Contradiction, Persistence, and Interdependence

STR
Alan Desai (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Devi R. Gnyawali (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Organizations face persistent, interdependent, and contradictory demands simultaneously. Different streams discuss such situations with their own terminologies, often in confusing ways, leading to a jangle effect and a lack of construct clarity. We conduct a systematic review of the literature, identify similarities and differences, develop “opposing simultaneity” (OS) as an integrative meta-construct, and propose a research agenda. Three questions guide this review: How do different literatures diverge and converge when discussing contradictory demands and organizational responses? What approaches exist to navigate contradictory demands? How do different navigational approaches produce different organizational outcomes? We define OS as the coexistence of contradictory yet interdependent poles that persist. We outline the meta-construct of OS, develop a taxonomy that integrates the literature, examine its effects in greater depth, and explore how different OS approaches lead to differential outcomes. Our theoretical insights advance scholarship by revealing that success requires: the cognitive capabilities to hold contradiction without defensive resolution, structural arrangements permitting specialization & coordination, relational capabilities maintaining trust across competing interests, and temporal capabilities sustaining dynamic equilibrium. We illuminate outcomes of surviving, thriving, or declining and explain how outcomes differ depending on the framing of OS, structural responses, and navigation of emphasis across poles.
