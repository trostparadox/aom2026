A Multilevel Framework for Understanding Racialized Exclusion in Organizations

OMT
Saran Anika Nurse (Kean University); Jay O'Toole (Old Dominion University); Tanja R. Darden (Towson University)
Why do organizations that claim inclusivity reproduce racial exclusion? Drawing on a qualitative case study of a statewide entrepreneurial support organization, we introduce theory that explains a multilevel process through which race-neutral ideologies and standardized practices unintentionally sustain inequality to explain the Paradox of Inclusivity (POI). Integrating Racialized Organization Theory with Attribution Theory, we show how macro-level colorblind ideologies shape meso-level routines that misalign with racially minoritized entrepreneurs’ (RMEs) structural realities. When RMEs disengage, staff interpret this behavior through attribution bias, framing it as an individual deficiency rather than an institutional failure. These interpretations legitimate selective resource allocation and produce success narratives that reinforce race-neutral logics, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop. Our contributions are threefold: (1) we introduce a generalizable POI framework explaining how identity-neutral ideologies paradoxically reproduce identity-based inequalities; (2) we advance multilevel organizational theory by specifying cross-level handoff mechanisms that generate recursive racialization; and (3) we reframe RME disengagement as a structurally grounded response, shifting entrepreneurship research from deficit-based explanations toward organizational accounts of exclusion. We conclude by outlining implications for theory, practice, and policy to dismantle race-neutral organizational designs that perpetuate inequality.

Organizational Legating: How Historical KKKs Sustained Racial Exclusion in American Sundown Towns

OMT
Kunyuan Qiao (Northeastern University); Matthew O. Hunt (Northeastern University); Ashley V Reichelmann (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Why do some communities abandon historical patterns of racial exclusion while others perpetuate them? We introduce organizational legating—the process whereby organizations institutionalize and enforce community norms, creating social structures that persist through individual imprinting and residential sorting. Examining American sundown towns (communities systematically excluding people of color from the 1890s through 1960s), we argue that Second Wave Ku Klux Klan organizations (1915-1944) institutionalized and enforced racial exclusion, embedding it deeply into community structures. These structures imprinted adolescent residents with negative racial attitudes toward Black people. Imprinted individuals subsequently engaged in residential sorting—remaining in or relocating to sundown towns with strong historical KKK presence while avoiding those without such legacies. Consequently, Second Wave KKK prevalence significantly predicts contemporary residents’ negative racial attitudes in historical sundown towns. We find robust evidence for our hypotheses with a sample of 643 surveyed participants from historical sundown towns. Our study advances institutional theory by demonstrating how organizations may embedded legacies, whose effects are manifested through individual imprinting and residential sorting. We extend social structure and personality research by revealing that the proximity principle operates not only through socialization but also through selection, offering new insights into how historical structures shape present-day racial attitudes.

Appropriative, Legitimation, and Resistance Work in Contested Transitions

OMT
Muhammad Umar Boodoo (Warwick Business School); Miguel Macías (Warwick Business School, U. of Warwick)
Contested transitions create opportunities to reduce inequality yet often reproduce it. We develop a framework revealing how inequality persists through three forms of institutional work operating across distributive, procedural, and recognitive justice dimensions. Appropriative work by incumbents concentrates benefits; legitimation work by intermediaries manages contradictions through symbolic-material decoupling; resistance work contests exclusionary arrangements. These interact through escalation cycles and legitimation crises, creating innovation opportunities that institutionalize as green inequality or transformation depending on governance capacity.

An Inside-Out Perspective: How Wealthy Insider Social Change Agents Aim to Challenge Inequality

OMT
Patricia Helena Hein (Ivey School of Business); Carina Keller (EBS University of Business and Law)
Addressing economic inequality remains a pressing societal challenge, with the top 1% controlling a disproportionate share of wealth in the US and Europe. While prior research has examined income disparities and macro-level wealth concentration, little is known about how wealthy individuals perceive inequality or their potential role in promoting social change. This study explores the experiences, motivations, and strategies of wealthy social change agents (wealth above $5m) who challenge entrenched structures from within elite circles. Drawing interviews and observations of wealthy activists, who are members of wealth tax advocacy organizations, we identify a three-phase process of engagement: emulsion, sedimentation, and crystallization. These phases are bridged by transitions that involve engagement outside and inside elite networks, as individuals navigate moral dilemmas, self-affirmation, and resistance. Our findings reveal distinct trajectories of wealth activism: public engagement, peer-oriented influence, and discreet support, each carrying varying personal and reputational costs. By highlighting the heterogeneity of the wealthy and the intrapersonal dynamics that drive engagement, this research advances understanding of how those with substantial economic and social capital can act as catalysts for systemic change. We show that wealth activism can move beyond philanthropy to confront the structural foundations of inequality from within positions of privilege.

Pulling Back the Curtains: Gender and Medical Service Prices in Medicare Part B

OMT
Pooria Assadi (California State University-Sacramento); Shinjae Won (Ewha Womans University)
Organizations frequently embody competing institutional logics that place conflicting demands on individuals. We examine how gender shapes the micro-level resolution of such tensions and, in turn, influences macro-institutional outcomes. Drawing on gender role socialization and institutional theory, we argue that female and male professionals systematically differ in how they weigh pro-social against economic logics, especially when behavioral discretion exists. Using comprehensive Medicare Part B claims data from 2013 to 2022, we find that medical services provided by female physicians involve lower excess charges than those provided by male physicians. This gender gap narrows in solo practices, where individual autonomy and revenue-oriented selection are greater. At the specialty level, gender differences attenuate as female representation increases, consistent with shifting normative expectations. Finally, we show that services predominantly performed by male physicians subsequently receive larger upward adjustments in Medicare-allowed amounts, revealing how gendered micro-level practices cumulatively advantage male-dominated domains in institutional resource allocation.
