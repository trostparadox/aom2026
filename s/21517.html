Virtual Care for Diverse Older Adults: Rapid Review and Management Implications

Authors: Hongmei Xie Flinders University; Bruno Alencar Pereira Flinders University; Andreas Cebulla Flinders University; Peivand Bastani Flinders University; Madhan Balasubramanian Flinders University

Session overview

Background: Virtual care is expanding rapidly, yet inequities persist for older adults from ethnically, culturally, and linguistically diverse backgrounds. Evidence on how service design and delivery shape equitable access and sustained engagement remains fragmented. Objective: To synthesise determinants of virtual care user experiences among diverse older adults and derive implications for healthcare management to support inclusive implementation. Methods: We conducted a rapid review (WHO-informed) across Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, MEDLINE, and CINAHL with no date restrictions. Eligible English-language empirical studies examined clinical virtual care (telehealth/telemedicine, mHealth, patient portals, remote monitoring) and reported user-experience barriers/facilitators among ethnically diverse older adults; Indigenous-focused studies were excluded as they warrant a dedicated review using distinct frameworks and appraisal criteria. Screening was conducted in Covidence (title/abstract; full text). Findings were synthesised using thematic coding appropriate for mixed evidence. Results: Thirty-nine studies were included (5 qualitative; 34 quantitative). Although the search was global, eligible evidence was concentrated in the United States (n=36), with additional studies from Canada, Malaysia, and New Zealand, indicating a cross-national evidence gap. Five domains shaped user experiences: (1) structural/system barriers (infrastructure gaps, geographic/ethnic disparities, and potential provider-level barriers); (2) digital literacy and usability (skills, training access, interface design); (3) language access and communication (interpreter integration, linguistic concordance, trust); (4) cultural alignment and acceptability (cultural relevance, comfort, trust); and (5) psychosocial/perceived value (reassurance, perceived benefit, family-mediated support). Conclusions: Inclusive virtual care requires interpreter-enabled workflows, sustained technical assistance (e.g., digital navigator models), flexible modality options, and governance that monitors uptake and outcomes stratified by language and ethnicity.
