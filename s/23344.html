Misleading Science Maps: Firms’ Strategic Responses to Data Malpractice in Alzheimer’s Research

STR
Matteo Tranchero (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania); Christian Fons-Rosen (University of California Merced); Lee Fleming (University of California Berkeley)
Why do firms invest in basic science when they could rely on published academic research? We argue that one overlooked reason is the uncertain reliability of science itself. When basic science is required for product development, corporate control of the research process serves as a substitute for the low verifiability of findings produced externally. Firms retain in-house research capabilities to assess, produce, and validate the studies needed for their innovations, particularly in domains where science is less dependable. We investigate this argument in the context of Alzheimer’s preclinical research, where inappropriate image alterations provide an objective marker of unreliable data. Using a novel AI-based detection tool validated through manual review, we scan the entire field and document a rising trend of data issues in published science. Problematic cases are less frequent when firms lead academic-industry collaborations and are nearly absent in fully corporate-authored research. In domains where academic science is less reliable, firms are more likely to conduct basic research internally and to build their inventions on these in-house studies. Our results identify scientific reliability as a neglected determinant of the “research boundaries” of the firm.

Bilateral Uncertainty and Global Patenting Location Choices: A Real Options Perspective

STR
Yuxuan Zhao (Not Associated); Minyuan Zhao (Washington University in St. Louis); Zhenzhen Xie (Sun Yat-Sen University); Donghong Li (Tsinghua University)
Deteriorating relationships between home and host countries heighten uncertainty about future market access, policy regimes, and appropriability conditions. Such uncertainty has been widely viewed as discouraging multinational enterprises (MNEs) from market entry. At the same time, the same uncertainty may encourage the use of patents as low-commitment entry vehicles: they preserve upside potential by enabling firms to scale up activities if conditions improve, while limiting downside exposure to the sunk costs of patenting if conditions deteriorate. Using a firm–patent family–country–level dataset of patent filings by global automotive original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) from 2010 to 2023, we find empirical support for this argument. This study contributes to international business and global strategy research by reconceptualizing patenting as an institutional response to bilateral uncertainty, thereby extending the real options view of internationalization.

Forced to Pursue Science: Entity List Triggers Science-Seeking Innovation

STR
Junhan Wang (The University of Hong Kong); Xibao Li (Tsinghua University); Yanbo Wang (The University of Hong Kong)
Science plays important roles in driving technological innovation, as evidenced by the United States (U.S.) and other OECD countries. While China has become a leading producer of scientific knowledge, Chinese firms’ engagement with science remains underexplored, especially amid rising geopolitical tensions. Examining the period from 2010 to 2022, we find that Chinese firms are increasingly leveraging science for innovation (evidenced by growth in patents that cite scientific literature, hereafter referred to as science-reliant patents) and participating in science production (reflected in rising domestic and international publications). Moreover, U.S. sanctions restricting Chinese firms’ access to proprietary technologies have accelerated their engagement with science, which is a global public good. Firms on the U.S. Entity List have accelerated their engagement in corporate science, broadened the temporal range of scientific knowledge sourcing, and deepened their utilization of both domestic and foreign sciences. Although China’s upward trajectory in corporate science is striking, a considerable gap in absolute levels of science engagement between Chinese and U.S. firms remains.

Conceptualising Business Resilience in the Context of Resource Scarcity

STR
Rosebella Achieng Nyumba (University of Wuppertal); Philipp A. Trotter
Business resilience is increasingly critical for organisational survival amid heightened uncertainty, yet existing research remains largely grounded in Global North contexts and single-shock settings. Consequently, prevailing conceptualisations inadequately capture the complex and persistent shock environments faced by firms in the Global South, where economic, political, environmental, and infrastructure-related disruptions frequently overlap. Moreover, although resources are widely recognised as central to resilience, limited attention has been paid to how specific resources enable firms to navigate different stages of resilience under conditions of scarcity. This study addresses these gaps through a systematic literature review of empirical research on businesses operating in the Global South. Adopting a process-based perspective, we examine how firms respond across the stages of anticipation, coping, and adaptation, and analyse the role of resources in shaping these responses. Our findings make two contributions. First, we introduce the concept of context-specific shock environments to explain the prevalence of interdependent, multi-faceted shocks. Second, we identify four interrelated resource categories that drive varying degrees of business resilience, highlighting how resource constraints limit proactive strategies and intensify reactive and adaptive responses. This study advances resilience theory and informs future research on organisational resilience in resource-constrained contexts.
