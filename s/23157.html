People Analytics at Work: Exploring Paradoxical Tensions in Employee and Executive Perceptions

CTO
Hannah Tilsch (LMU Munich School of Management); Anne-Sophie Mayer (LMU Munich School of Management)
People analytics (PA) is being increasingly deployed in organizations with the promise to enhance efficiency and strategic decision-making through insights generated from data on employees. However, realizing PA in practice is complex and raises significant ethical issues, such as employee surveillance. In this study, we explore how employees and executives perceive and respond to PA for skill management and workforce planning. Our findings reveal that employees and executives often do not enact a clearly positive or negative response, nor do they tell a straightforward story of perceived benefits and risks. Instead, individuals respond to PA in contradictory ways, simultaneously enacting conflicting and interdependent needs. Based on these findings, we inductively define four paradoxical tensions: (1) visibility and invisibility, and (2) accountability and autonomy from the employee perspective, and (3) generalizability and specialization, and (4) mobility and retention from the executive perspective. This study contributes to literature on PA and employee responses to technology at work by illustrating the nuanced complexity of individuals’ responses to PA and revealing persistent tensions faced in living PA.

Technological Complexity, Long Work Hours, and Employee Burnout: The Dual Role of Digital Leadership

CTO
Dilek Sahin (Düzce University); Abraham Stefanidis (St. John's University); Moshe Banai (Baruch College, CUNY); Yusuf KARASIN
This study examines the intricate role that digital leadership plays in the relationship between technology complexity and employee burnout, highlighting the mediating effect of long work hours. A questionnaire compiled of validated measures was translated from English and distributed to 420 healthcare professionals employed in two private and three public hospitals in Istanbul, Türkiye. We identified a direct positive relationship between techno-complexity and burnout; a positive association between health workers' perception of techno-complexity and long work hours; and a positive association between long work hours and burnout. Digital leadership reduced burnout but strengthened the positive relationship between long work hours and burnout. This complex set of relationships indicates that, while positive digital leadership may serve as a buffer protecting employees from burnout, it may also lead to increased expectations that result in employee resource depletion in situations where there is insufficient managerial support. Implications for practice are offered.

Hazard or Hassle? A Threat–Hindrance Split of Technostress Through Appraisals, Coping, and Outcomes

CTO
Romina Liza Kleiner (RWTH Aachen University); Susan Stead
As workplace digitalization accelerates, employees increasingly experience stress in response to technology use. Research on such technostress has identified numerous stressors and adverse outcomes but treats negative stressors as conceptually homogeneous. In contrast, general work stress research proposes distinguishing between hindrance stressors, which obstruct goal attainment, and threat stressors, associated with potential personal harm or loss. It remains unclear whether this distinction applies to technostress and whether these stressor types operate through different stress pathways. To address this gap, we conduct 32 in-depth interviews with professionals in marketing, consulting, and software development, using generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) as our technological context. Our findings reveal five hindrance techno-stressors and six threat techno-stressors, comprising established ones and novel ones specific to GenAI. We show that hindrance and threat techno-stressors differ systematically in their appraisals (including emotional responses), coping strategies, and outcomes: Hindrance techno-stressors primarily elicit prevention-problem-focused coping through hindrance appraisals, whereas threat techno-stressors evoke prevention-emotion-focused coping through threat appraisals, leading to more pervasive and long-term negative consequences. This study advances technostress and work stress research by characterizing differences between hindrance and threat stressors throughout stress pathways. Practically, our findings underscore the growing relevance of threat techno-stressors and the resulting need for interventions tailored to stressor type.

Always On, Never Off: The Impact of Compulsive Smartphone Use on Leaders’ Well- Being

CTO
Ryan Cheng (The University of Sydney); James Nicholas Donald (The University of Sydney); Jiesi Guo (Australian Catholic University); Helena Nguyen (The University of Sydney); Wiebke Doden (King's College London); Karyn L. Wang (The University of Sydney)
Unlike other employees, leaders often face the demand for high accessibility and responsiveness, making smartphones a central technological tool through which contemporary leaders coordinate, communicate, and lead on a daily basis. While this constant connectivity can help leaders remain effective and informed, it may also undermine leaders’ own wellbeing. In the present study, we examine the effects of leaders’ compulsive smartphone use (CSU) on their wellbeing. Drawing on self-determination theory, we propose that CSU undermines leaders’ daily fulfillment of basic psychological needs (i.e., autonomy, competence, and relatedness), which in turn undermines their hedonic well-being (job satisfaction) and eudaimonic well-being (work engagement and goal progress). We conducted a 10-day experience sampling study with 153 leaders in the United Kingdom. We found that CSU was associated with lower lunchtime autonomy, competence, and relatedness. These lower need experiences were then associated with poorer end of day well-being. Competence emerged as the most consistent pathway, mediating CSU’s indirect associations with all three wellbeing outcomes. Relatedness also mediated CSU’s indirect associations with job satisfaction and work engagement. The negative effects of CSU were also stronger on days when leaders experienced more frequent smartphone interruptions (eg., through notifications, prompts, alerts), particularly through competence. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications for leadership and workplace technology research, self-determination theory, and healthier digital work practices for leaders.

Virtual Workplace Rumor: Leaders as Role Models?

CTO
Melina Pfirrmann (Seeburg Castle University); Annika Marie von Gazali (Seeburg Castle University); Clemens Hutzinger (Seeburg Castle University)
In the virtual work context, the way leaders and employees interact and communicate with each other and how leaders act as role models is changing. Building on social learning theory, this research investigates how the valence and frequency of rumors spread by the leader in the virtual workplace affect the leader's role model function and whether this effect can be explained by characteristics of the leader such as warmth and competence. The study comprises two experiments, each with a 2x2 design. The first study (N 517) confirms the central assumptions that positive rumors and rarely shared rumors strengthen the role model function of virtual leaders, while frequent and negative rumors weaken it. The results of study 2 (N 540) also show that this effect can be explained by the perceived warmth and competence of the virtual leader. The spread of positive rumors leads to a perception of warmth and competence of the leader, and this assessment in turn reinforces the role model function of the leader. The results help organizations raise awareness of language and train and develop leadership skills, while also filling a gap in empirical research by focusing on rumors in the virtual work context.
