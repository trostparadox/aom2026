Session overview

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly reshaping work, not only by altering productivity but by transforming how work is experienced, governed, and defined (Autor 2015, Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017). While much existing research emphasizes automation and task substitution (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020), the papers in this symposium demonstrate that the consequences of AI depend critically on how AI is used, who uses it, and the institutional and occupational contexts in which it is embedded (Bailey and Barley 2020, Barley 2020). This symposium integrates four complementary papers that examine AI at work from psychological, occupational, and managerial perspectives. Wang, Doshi and Landis provide within-person experimental evidence showing that daily generative AI use changes how employees structure their work, increasing task crafting, perceived progress, and job demands, with stronger effects for less experienced and lower-ranked workers. Lee, Yin, Jia, and Wakslak show that passive reliance on AI undermines self-efficacy, psychological ownership, and meaningfulness, whereas active human-AI collaboration preserves these core motivational resources. Moving beyond the individual, Rahman, Kaynak, and Lee argue that occupational communities play a central role in shaping AI’s long-run effects, proposing a framework that explains when AI leads to augmentation, deskilling, or jurisdictional shifts based on occupational power and autonomy. Finally, Cappelli synthesizes evidence on algorithmic management, machine-learning decision systems, and large language models to show how AI is transforming managerial control, raising new challenges around “explainability”, fairness, privacy and accountability. Together, these papers advance a coherent account of AI as a sociotechnical force that reshapes work through intertwined psychological, occupational, and organizational mechanisms.
