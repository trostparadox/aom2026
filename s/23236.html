Birds of a Feather Flock Together? Extraversion Dissimilarity in Angel Investor–Entrepreneur Dyads

ENT
Wan-Chien Lien (National Central University); Jianhong Chen (University of New Hampshire)
In this study, we examine whether and when extraversion dissimilarity between angel investors and entrepreneurs influences investment outcomes. Drawing on the dominant complementarity theory, we argue that extraversion dissimilarity is positively related to the amount of follow-on funding. Moreover, we propose when angels have less investing experience, and entrepreneurs possess limited experience, the positive effect of extraversion dissimilarity on follow-on funding is stronger. Using a sample of 3,290 observation from 2010 to 2024, we found largely support for our hypotheses. Our study contributes to entrepreneurial financing research by being among the first to examine extraversion dissimilarity in investor–entrepreneur dyads, and to explore its performance implications along with boundary conditions.

Down But Not Out: When Angel Investors Escalate Commitment to Underperforming Firms

ENT
Alexander Narh Tetteh (University of Science and Technology of China); Dan K. Hsu (North Dakota State University); Qingxiong Weng; Magdalene Zeinab Ak Adams
Drawing on the escalation of commitment theory and pivoting literature, this study examines when angel investors (or angels) escalate their commitment to reinvest in firms they initially funded but underperform. Using a conjoint experiment with 2,384 decisions nested within 149 participants, we find that angels have sunk cost fallacy for non-financial losses, but not for financial losses. Also, we find that angels’ escalation of commitment to firms they funded but underperform is contingent on their preference for the entrepreneurs’ pivoting orientation. Specifically, for angels who incurred financial loss, entrepreneurs’ exploitative rather than explorative pivoting increases their escalation of commitment. Conversely, for angels who incurred non-financial loss, entrepreneur explorative rather than exploitative pivoting increases their escalation of commitment. We contribute new human factors, moral, and relational explanations for why angels may persist in underperforming firms despite being economically illogical to do so.

Entrepreneurs’ Use of Pygmalion Rhetoric and Investors' Funding Decisions: A Role of Charisma, Legit

ENT
Jim Toft; Jan Inge Jenssen (NLA University College); Salman bahoo (EDC Paris Business School)
An angel investor’s investment decision during an entrepreneur’s pitch is a complex process. This study examines the impact of Pygmalion rhetoric used by entrepreneurs on investors' decisions. Due to the subjective nature of pitch analysis, it is theorized that an entrepreneur’s charisma and legitimacy, along with a sustainable orientation of the business idea (signaling theory), serve as moderators in the framework. Analyzing 196 pitches to 13 angel investors in the Shark Tank US seasons (October 2020 to May 2022). We find that Pygmalion rhetoric has a positive influence on an investor's decision. An entrepreneur’s charisma has a moderating effect, but legitimacy does not. However, the sustainable orientation of a business idea positively moderates the investor decision. Our findings suggest that entrepreneurs who pair Pygmalion rhetoric with charisma and a focus on sustainability can significantly enhance their appeal to angel investors, who must discern whether such signals reflect genuine business potential.

Tell Me Lies, Tell Me Sweet Little Lies? Entrepreneurs' Legitimacy Lies and Investors' Dark Triad

ENT
Besart Kajdomqaj (University of St.Gallen); Petrit Ademi (University of St. Gallen); Dietmar Grichnik (University of St. Gallen)
While prior research considers legitimacy lies primarily from the entrepreneur’s perspective, we shift the focus to investors’ responses when such deception is noticed during venture evaluation. Drawing on signaling theory, we conceptualize legitimacy lies as deceptive signals evaluated along with venture signals. Using a conjoint experiment with business angels and venture capitalists, we find that legitimacy lies significantly reduce investors’ interest in ventures with high societal value, but not in ventures with high financial value. Moreover, we find that this legitimacy-lie penalty depends on differences in investors’ dark triad. Investors high in Machiavellianism and psychopathy show contrasting evaluations, pointing to a contingent penalty of deception in investment decision-making.

The Differential Role of Business Angels and Crowd-Investors on Startups' Borrowing Decisions

ENT
Vincenzo Capizzi (Università del Piemonte Orientale); Annalisa Croce; Francesca Tenca (University of Eastern Piedmont "Amedeo Avogadro")
This paper investigates the differential impact of Business Angels (BAs) and Crowd Investors (CIs) on the ability of early-stage ventures to obtain bank debt. We further examine how bank financing influences subsequent venture growth, depending on the type of equity financing received. Using a sample of 6,774 European ventures from PitchBook, we find that BA-backed ventures experience a significantly smaller increase in bank debt levels in the post-investment period compared to CI-backed ventures. Nevertheless, the marginal effect of debt on subsequent growth is significantly higher for BA-backed ventures, confirming the positive role of BAs in fostering the efficient use of venture debt. In additional analyses, we also show that this enhanced growth effect of debt is driven by BAs with focused portfolios and small syndicate investments, rather than by their investment experience. Our results contribute to the literature on investor heterogeneity and venture performance, underscoring that the non-monetary, value-added contributions of sophisticated investors are key determinants of an optimal capital structure and the growth effects generated by the efficient use of bank debt.
