From Underdog Expectations to Resistance: Affective Rumination and Job-Growth Mindset in Robot Use

OB
Jingxuan Jiang (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Chunyang Zhou (Nanchang University); Che Chen (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics Zhejiang College); Junbang Lan (Sun Yat-Sen University)
With the increasing prevalence of robots in contemporary workplaces, a key challenge for employees is effectively performing their work in the context of human–robot value co-creation. This study examines how underdog expectations regarding service robot usage (i.e., employees’ perceptions that others consider them unlikely to succeed with these technologies) influence behavioral resistance to robot use. Drawing on the transactional theory of stress, we propose that robot-related affective rumination mediates the relationship between such underdog expectations and resistance to service robot use. Moreover, employees’ job-growth mindset attenuates the effect of underdog expectations regarding service robot usage on robot-related affective rumination, indicating that higher levels of job-growth mindset buffer employees from the negative consequences of being perceived as underdogs in using service robots. We tested these hypotheses in a time-lagged field study, providing support for our predictions. Overall, we highlight the impact of underdog expectations regarding service robot usage, shedding new light on employees’ attitudinal and behavioral responses in augmentation-oriented artificial intelligence contexts.

How Robots Shape Who Leaders Think They Are: The Effects of Robotics on Role Identity and Leadership

OB
Yijie Zhang (Wuhan University of Technology); Jiaxin Lin (Tsinghua University); Liu-Qin (LQ) Yang (Portland State University)
As robotics technologies continue to advance, they are increasingly reshaping not only what work is done but also how work is organized and coordinated. Although prior research has begun to examine the implications of robotics for employees’ psychological states and behaviors, far less is known about how robotics influences leaders themselves. Drawing on leader identity theory, we develop and test a model in which different forms of human–robot interaction shape leadership behaviors by activating distinct leadership role identities. Specifically, we distinguish between robotic automation, in which robots execute tasks autonomously with minimal human intervention, and robotic augmentation, in which robots collaborate with humans to support task accomplishment. We theorize that automation strengthens task-focused leader identity, which in turn increases initiating structure, whereas augmentation strengthens person-focused leader identity, which in turn increases consideration. We further argue that leaders’ bottom-line mentality serves as an interpretive lens that conditions the extent to which robotics-related cues become identity-relevant. Results across two multi-wave field studies support our hypotheses. Overall, our findings advance understanding of how robotics reshapes leadership by identifying leader identity as a key psychological mechanism and clarifying why leaders respond heterogeneously to the technological environments.

Screen Time Goes Viral: The Contagious Effect of Observing Smartphone Use at Work

OB
Olympia Nakos (University of Georgia); Jessica Beth Rodell (University of Georgia)
Smartphone use in the workplace is ubiquitous, yet research has overlooked the social factors that may influence this behavior. Drawing on theories of self-control and contagion, we propose that observing workplace smartphone use triggers both direct and indirect and pathways that lead to personal smartphone use, ultimately impacting employee well-being and performance. Results from an observational study and a 10-day experience sampling field study demonstrate that observing smartphone use increases personal use and drives negative outcomes, offering insights for managing smartphone use and enhancing workplace functioning.

Polarized Moral Identity: Forces and Mechanisms of Technology-Assisted Dual Identity Processes

OB
Nikki Drader-Mazza (University of North Texas); Vidhi Jain (University of North Texas); Steven James Hyde (Boise State University); Samantha Jordan (University of North Texas); Rhonda K. Reger (University of North Texas); Virginie Lopez Kidwell (University of North Texas); Danielle Cooper (University of North Texas)
Digital tools create new arenas for self-conceptualization by offering affordances of greater ambiguity, anonymity, and unaccountability. Operating in these environments, however, is often driven by social stigma leading to the construction and maintenance of polarized moral identities whereby the socially stigmatized identity is concealed. Using the case of Ross Ulbricht and the rise and fall of Silk Road, we explore how polarized moral identities are formed and maintained, threatened, and ultimately reconstructed via personal, social, and structural forces and mechanisms. Integrating elements of grounded-theorizing with a methodological bricolage that triangulates large language model-assisted text analysis and independent human coding across a 20-year corpus, we develop a process model of how personal motives, social forces (stigma, scrutiny, and support), and structural features of technology combine to produce polarized-identity dynamics over time, extending theory on moral and dual identity processes. Overall, we demonstrate how digital affordances both enable and erode separation of polarized moral identities and offer a scalable qualitative approach for studying identity trajectories using a large corpus of archival data.
