The AI in HRM: AI as Social, Economic, and Psychological HRM Actor

HR
Sunghoon Kim (The University of Sydney); Hokyu Hwang; Ying Wang (Beijing Institute of Technology)
The rapid deployment of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in organizational life—from automated recruitment to predictive performance evaluation—has introduced a disruptive, non-human entity into the Human Resource Management (HRM) system. This trend necessitates a clearer conceptual understanding of agency and the definition of an HRM actor. To advance the discussion on this critical challenge, we first examine how HRM actors have been conceptualized in the HRM scholarship, conducting an extensive literature review (n=516). We thus identify and develop a three-fold taxonomy of HRM actors—Social, Economic, and Psychological—based on the primary activities actors perform in HRM contexts. We then apply this taxonomy, in conjunction with contemporary discussions of technology’s agency (assisting, augmenting, arresting and automating agency), to reflect on what it means to understand AI as a non-human HRM actor that supplements or substitutes human’s social, economic and psychological activities in HRM.

AI as a Lure or Just Hype? Employer Attractiveness for Future IT Professionals

HR
Pavel Kral (Prague University of Economics and Business); Petr Simacek (Prague University of Economics and Business); Vera Kralova (Prague University of Economics and Business); Martina Richterova
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly becoming embedded in organizational work, yet its role in shaping employer attractiveness remains poorly understood. Drawing on a sequential mixed-methods design, we examine what makes organizations attractive to future IT professionals and whether AI functions as a meaningful signal in this process. We first conduct in-depth interviews with digital-native IT students to identify salient determinants of employer attractiveness, and then test the resulting model using survey data from 806 respondents with PLS-SEM. Contrary to portrayals of AI as either a decisive lure or mere hype, our findings show that AI is perceived primarily as an inevitable feature of contemporary work rather than a direct driver of employer appeal. More broadly, organization- and information-related attributes, such as reputation, communication, and technological signaling, do not independently enhance employer attractiveness. Instead, candidates translate these determinants into assessments of work-related attributes, making the nature of job tasks the central basis of attraction. In particular, fear of task repetitiveness, along with career prospects and rewards and benefits, exerts the strongest influence on employer attractiveness. We contribute to research on employer attractiveness by identifying fear of task repetitiveness as a novel negative determinant and by theorizing how symbolic organizational and informational determinants are filtered through instrumental, work-related considerations. We further extend theorizing on AI in organizations by showing that core technologies affect employer appeal primarily through how they shape job content rather than through AI as a branding signal.

How Agile Practices Drive Radical Innovation: Tracing the Cognitive-Affective Dual Pathways

HR
Liang Han (Hunan University); Chen Ding (Dongbei University of Finance and Economics); Jialiang Pei; Weiqi Pan (South China University of Technology); Hui Lei (Hunan University); Mengting Su (Mahidol University)
While Agile Work Practices (AWPs) are widely recognized for enhancing team adaptability and efficiency, their impact on individual radical innovation remains unclear. Grounded in the Cognitive-Affective Personality System (CAPS) theory, this study investigates how team-level AWPs influence individual radical innovation through dual psychological pathways. Using multi-source, multi-wave data from 547 R&D employees and their leaders in 118 teams, we found that AWPs foster individual radical innovation by enhancing cognitive flexibility and by reducing work anxiety. Furthermore, individual AI usage plays a critical moderating role, acting as a double-edged sword. It strengthens the positive indirect effect via cognitive flexibility but weakens the negative indirect effect via anxiety reduction. These findings offer a nuanced perspective on the individual-level psychological mechanisms of AWPs and provide critical insights for managing innovation in the era of human-AI collaboration.

Behind the Algorithm: How AI Recruitment Disrupts Psychological Contracts

HR
Sunanda Nayak (FORE School of Management)
This study examines the psychological impacts of AI-driven recruitment processes within software organizations, highlighting the adverse effects on employees’ psychological contracts across diverse organizational contexts. Utilizing a qualitative approach, the research involved 91 semi-structured interviews (in two phases - within three months of joining and after one year of employment) with employees, revealing key themes such as cue extraction and interpretation, expectation setting, reciprocity assessment and adjustment, perceptions of fairness, transparency evaluations, sense of loss of connect, reciprocity expectation, organizational obligations, amongst others associated with AI recruitment. Further, the research develops a comprehensive process model that integrates sensemaking theory and social exchange theory to examine how AI-driven recruitment affects psychological contract formation as well as psychological contract breach development. This study contributes to existing literature by applying these two theories, providing insights into how employees navigate their experiences with AI recruitment and its implications for their employment relationships. Both theoretical and practical implications are discussed, providing a foundation for globally relevant recruitment strategies that can enhance employee trust and engagement.

The Double-Edged Sword Effects of AI-Driven Star-Nonstar Performance Gap Conve

HR
Zhijie Xing (Zhongnan University of Economics and Law); Jinzhao Qu (Hainan University)
Prior research suggests that star performers possess superior expertise and exert significant influence over non-stars. However, generative AI can take existing skill differentials out of the equation and enable non-stars to leverage AI to enhance their performance, which lead to the star-nonstar performance gap may convergence or even reverse, which raise an important but underexplored question: How do stars respond when their performance advantage is threatened by AI-empowered peers? Drawing on regulatory focus theory and implicit theory, we theorize that AI-driven star-nonstar performance gap convergence, depending on whether star employees with different mindsets (growth mindset and fixed mindset) perceive AI-driven star-nonstar performance gap convergence as “opportunity” or “threat”, trigger stars’ regulatory focus cognitions (i.e., problem-solving pondering and affect-focused rumination), and corresponding coping strategies (i.e., knowledge seeking and hiding from nonstar). In a scenario experiment of 161 participants, we found that AI-driven star-nonstar performance gap convergence will prompt star employees knowledge seeking from nonstar and stifle their knowledge hiding from nonstar via problem-solving pondering when star employees have a strong (vs. weak) growth mindset; AI-driven star-nonstar performance gap convergence will prompt star employees knowledge hiding from nonstar via affect-focused rumination when star employees have a strong (vs. weak) fixed mindset. Finally, Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.
