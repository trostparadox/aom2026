How Coopetition Dynamics Change as Industries Mature: Evidence from the U.S. Craft Brewing Industry

STR
Hyunsoo Kim (New York University); J.P. Eggers (New York University)
We explore how coopetitive preferences change as an industry matures by examining both the macro trends around within-industry collaboration formation and the micro behaviors of partner selection across multiple dimensions of strategic similarity in the U.S. craft brewing industry. Abductively studying multi-brewery collaboration beers from 2007 to 2023, we find that the rate of coopetition formation increases even as the industry matures. The nature of partner selection, however, fundamentally shifts in two theoretically important ways. First, firms prefer to buffer increasing competitive pressures by dramatically reducing partner similarity on one dimension while maximizing it on another, presumably to improve value creation opportunities. This highlights the strategic importance of multi-attribute trade-off management. Second, high-status incumbents abandon the collectivist "rising tide" logics in favor of self-preservation, clustering with highly similar local peers to pull up the ladder behind them in ways that reduce entry and accelerate competitor exits.

The Conspiracy of Optimism: Future Making and Coopetition in the New Space Economy

STR
Anna-Sofia Yanson (Università della Svizzera Italiana); CLELIA IACOMINO (Bocconi University)
In this paper, we seek to understand how interdependent rivals collectively make a shared future in the absence of a commercial market. Using 31 in-depth interviews with New Space actors across Europe, the United States, Japan, and India — including entrepreneurs, public agencies, major platform operators, and international regulators — we analyze how actors coordinate action in a sector characterized by extreme capital intensity, long technological horizons, and radical uncertainty about demand. Our analysis shows, first, that actors engage in ontological scaffolding by translating speculative technological possibilities into operationally actionable futures through narratives, public procurement, and regulatory categories. Second, we find that competitors enact collective future holding by pooling fragility and suspending premature closure — coordinating standards, cultivating redundancy, and protecting the credibility of the ecosystem so that no single failure undermines the plausibility of the sector as a whole. Third, we demonstrate that states discipline the future by anchoring long-horizon technological projects to sovereign time through mission programs, milestone-based contracts, and geopolitical commitments, encouraging firms to reorganize around underwritten futures rather than autonomous market demand. With this study, we extend research on future making and coopetition by showing that when markets do not yet exist, competitors cooperate not only to share costs or innovate, but to collectively uphold the legitimacy, temporality, and investability of the future itself.

How Third-Party Actors Coordinate Coopetition in Nascent Ecosystem Contexts

STR
Giulia Marcocchia (CY Cergy Paris Université); Audrey Rouyre
Platform-based ecosystems increasingly rely on the participation of competitors, making coopetition a central yet challenging dynamic during ecosystem emergence. While existing research recognizes the importance of coopetition in ecosystems, little is known about how such dynamics unfold at the early stages of ecosystem formation and how third-party actors influence their coordination. This paper addresses this gap by examining the role of third-party actors in shaping coopetition dynamics in nascent platform-based ecosystems. We draw on an in-depth qualitative case study of Automat, a Horizon 2020 European project aimed at developing a mobility data marketplace for connected vehicles. The consortium brought together heterogeneous actors, including competing automotive OEMs, service providers, IT firms, a cloud operator, academic institutions, and the European Commission as a central third party. Adopting an action research design, we analyze longitudinal data collected over three and a half years, combining observations from 18 consortium meetings with 20 semi-structured interviews. Our findings show that coopetition in emerging ecosystems is a multilevel and differentiated phenomenon. Horizontal coopetition among competing OEMs coexists with vertical coopetition between OEMs and service or technology providers, as actors simultaneously collaborate on shared standards while competing over data access and value capture. We further reveal that third-party actors play an evolving and situational role in coordinating these dynamics. In particular, the European Commission shifted from sponsor to situational orchestrator and mediator, using funding mechanisms, regulatory expectations, and temporal pressure to enable temporary alignment and mitigate tensions among ecosystem participants. This study contributes to research on coopetition and platform-based ecosystems by theorizing differentiated forms of coopetition during ecosystem emergence and by highlighting the dynamic role of third-party actors in enabling systemic coordination before ecosystem governance stabilizes.

When Does Multimarket Contact Enhance Firm Performance? Network Structure and Performance Feedback

STR
YungCheng Lai; JenJen Tseng
This study integrates the theories of social network and firm behavior (performance feedback), sequentially examining the relationship between multi-market competition (MMC) and firm performance. We adopt a behavioral theory of the firm perspective to explore how performance relative to aspirations influences competitive dynamics in multiple markets. The results show that the relationship between MMC and firm performance follows an inverted U-shape. Additionally, as the number of structural holes spanned by competitors in a firm's multi-market contact network increases, the positive effect of MMC on performance is weakened, potentially even turning negative at high levels of MMC. The findings also support the positive performance feedback hypothesis: when both the focal firm and its key rival experienced positive performance changes in the prior period (t-1), they are more likely to maintain mutual forbearance, resulting in higher performance under MMC. This research contributes to the literature by linking network structure and behavioral decision-making perspectives to better understand when and how multi-market competition can enhance or erode firm performance.

Is Perceived Trust Among Competitors Sufficient for Disruptive Innovation?

STR
AREZIGULI WUBULI; renfei Chen; ming Xia (xinjiang university); yang Li (xinjiang university); yuhong Ren (xinjiang university)
Collaborating with competitors has gained increasing prominence in recent years, particularly in emerging countries, as a strategy for disruptive innovation. This paper integrates coopetition theory with resource dependence and knowledge-creation perspectives to examine how perceived trust among competitors drives disruptive innovation, including both direct and indirect effects and boundary conditions. Our study used a survey approach to collect data from 223 high-tech manufacturing firms in China. The results indicated that trust significantly promoted disruptive innovation in coopetition through the mediating role of knowledge cocreation. Resource interdependence strengthened the effect of trust on knowledge cocreation, whereas market overlap enhanced knowledge cocreation to a point, beyond which excessive market overlap diminished the generation of disruptive ideas. Our findings advance the literature on coopetitive partnerships and offer managerial guidance on balancing cooperation and competition in the disruptive innovation process by highlighting the importance of resource interdependence and market overlap.
