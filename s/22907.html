Strength of the Relational State: Theorizing Concertive Compliance in Public Sector Governance

OMT
Ebony N. Bridwell-Mitchell (Harvard University); Marissa Combs (Harvard University)
How does the modern state accomplish its governance functions in complex public domains where neither market incentives nor formal rules alone suffice? Classic accounts emphasize bureaucracy and markets, but constructions of the state pay less attention to the administrative apparatus and everyday practices through which state governance is enacted. As a result, the literature has undertheorized how relationships operate not only within regulated organizations under weak forms of state governance, but also between state regulatory agencies and regulated organizations under strong forms of governance. We address this gap by introducing the concept of the relational state in which governance functions are carried out through relational practices and controls. Empirically, we examine the relational state in the context of school turnaround policy, where state education agencies (SEAs) confront the limits of top-down compliance directives and competitive performance incentives. Using a multi-site comparative case study, we show that SEAs rely on role-relational practices and controls, such as establishing interdependencies that generate bilateral goals. These practices bring schools and districts into closer concertive compliance, reflecting a negotiated normative consensus about appropriate work practices. Recognizing the relational state’s role in governance advances organizational theories of the state and how it operates as an institution. It also offers a framework for analyzing the organizational implementation of policy in complex public domains, such as education—a domain critical to all other sectors and to the viability of democratic societies.

Like It or Leave: Regional Logics, Controversial Policies & Turnover in Public-Sector Organizations

OMT
Hannah Grubbs (University of Oregon); Lauren Lanahan (University of Oregon)
A rich literature has examined the effects of firms or their leaders in taking stances on controversial issues; however, this same literature has offered less attention to the communities in which these organizations are embedded. In this paper, we explore how organization members may respond differently to the same controversial policy depending on their community’s prevailing logic. As a baseline, we hypothesize that when an organization becomes subject to a controversial policy, employee turnover will increase, and we argue that this effect will depend on the organization leader’s own political ideology. Next, we hypothesize that regional logics moderate these effects for two reasons—one having to do with local interpretation of the policy, and the other having to do with community embeddedness. To examine these assertions, we study employee turnover within Florida school districts in the wake of the 2022 Parental Rights in Education Act, colloquially known as the “Don’t Say Gay” bill. Utilizing a novel dataset of hiring and separation data from 2019 through 2023, we probe differences in employee turnover before and after the statewide legislation. Our results reveal that the policy increased turnover, and that this increase is driven by separations from districts in which the superintendent is a Republican and where districts are in Republican-affiliated counties. Our study demonstrates that what matters is not only the introduction of a controversial policy, but also the prevailing ideology of the organization’s leader and of the community where the employees live. We offer several managerial, practical and policy implications.

How Momentum Is Made and Unmade: Governance Dynamics in Policy-Oriented Projects

OMT
Rehema Sophia Msulwa (University of Oxford)
Policy-oriented projects are increasingly used to translate ambitious public policy objectives into large-scale interventions, yet many struggle to sustain forward movement over extended and politically contested trajectories. Existing research has typically conceptualised project momentum as a stable outcome or cumulative resource, offering limited insight into how momentum is generated, sustained, or reversed in contexts characterised by temporariness, fragmented authority, and institutional change. Drawing on a longitudinal qualitative study of the United Kingdom’s High Speed 2 (HS2) programme, this paper reconceptualises momentum as a governance-mediated, processual condition that is repeatedly reconstituted over time. Tracing the project across multiple phases, we show how shifting governance regimes generate distinct forms of momentum, enabling early acceleration, uneven stabilisation, and eventual selective unwinding. We develop a process model that explains how momentum evolves through cascading interactions between governance practices and institutional conditions, leaving residues that shape subsequent possibilities for action. By foregrounding governance as adaptive and evolving, this study advances understanding of momentum in policy-oriented projects and contributes to broader debates on temporality, coordination, and institutional change in complex, multi-actor settings.

Funded to Last? The Sustainability of Organizational Interventions: Evidence from the DRC

OMT
Anicet Fangwa Nantcho (McGill University); John-Paul Ferguson (McGill University); Joy Zhuoyue PANG (McGill University)
In a period of declining international development assistance, it is increasingly important to understand whether and how organizational change can be sustained after project funding ceases. We explore this by studying a multi-year RCT intervention to improve maternal and child healthcare in the Democratic Republic of Congo. We draw on organizational-learning and motivation theories to argue that the governance bundle implemented in this project, one that combines performance-based rewards with auditing & feedback to practitioners, should produce sustained benefits in health-center outputs after the project itself ends. We supplement the original project records with several years' data from the DRC's national health-statistics database, and leverage disruptions in project intervention to conduct a dose-response analysis. We find that, while all health-center outcomes decline from their peak levels of performance during the project intervention, the organizations that received the governance bundle persist in higher levels of output and care for at least 2.5 years after all external project funding and assistance ceased. We discuss the implications of such governance interventions for economic development projects and the study of organizations in the Global South.

Not One Compliance but Many: Authority Lense on Compliance in pluralistic organizations

OMT
Lali Giorgidze (University of Neuchatel)
Organizational compliance has been explored as a unified response to institutional demands, largely overlooking how pluralistic organizations enact compliance when authority is more polycentric. We conducted inductive and comparative case studies of 13 Swiss higher education institutions to study when and how organizations move beyond formal compliance with coercive pressures towards more substantive transformation in response to sustainability demands in national accreditation standards between 2015 and 2023. We develop four distinct compliance enactment types that are largely shaped by different authority configurations: Coordinated alignment, fragmented settlement, procedural enforcement, and negotiated customization. As we found, these types shape the extent to which compliance ranges from formal compliance with coercive pressures to substantive transformation. Shifting attention to how compliance is enacted in pluralistic organizations, our study seeks to make key contributions to the literatures on organizational responses to institutional demands, pluralistic organizing, and sustainability governance.
