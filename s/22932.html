When Is R&D The Answer to Underperformance?A Configurational Analysis from the Attention Perspective

OMT
Xi Li (University of Jinan); Yu Su (Shanghai University); Wenwen An (Guangdong University of Technology); Feng Zhang (South China University of Technology)
Whether firms choose to respond to underperformance through R&D investment remains inconclusive in existing studies. The attention-based view posits that firm decisions and behaviors result from the combined influence of internal and external contextual factors and firms’ attention to structural factors, which together guide the attention of decision-makers. In this paper, underperformance intensity and duration are taken as internal contextual factors; industry technological dynamism is taken as an external contextual factor; and firm ownership, top management team (TMT) regulatory focus, industry leadership, and unabsorbed slack are taken as four attention structural factors to explore how these factors jointly influence decision-makers’ choice to make R&D investments. Through fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) and data from Chinese A-share manufacturing listed companies, our findings reveal 8 paths (4 patterns) that lead to high R&D investment. Specifically, underperforming firms can achieve high R&D investment through diverse approaches, namely, resource-driven patterns, promotion focus-driven patterns, status-driven patterns, and SOE-specific patterns. By integrating the attention-based view with the configurational approach, this paper reconciles the classical paradox of responses to underperformance and enhances the explanatory power of performance feedback theory by incorporating additional valuable conditions that exert synergistic effects.

Executive Compensation Stickiness, Long-Term Focus, and the Radicalness of Corporate Innovation

OMT
Jiayi Song (Nanjing University); Zhengtang Zhang (Nanjing University); Xiaoran Hu; Yeshen Liu (Nanjing University)
Radical innovation is crucial for the long-term survival of firms, but it is often challenging to achieve for most firms. Drawing on the optimal innovation-motivating incentive scheme and the attention-based view, we investigate whether and how executive compensation stickiness motivates the radicalness of corporate innovation. Using a sample of 984 Chinese A-share listed firms involved in innovation from 2009 to 2016, our results show that firms can motivate the radicalness of innovation via the design of executive compensation stickiness. Meanwhile, we find that one way executive compensation stickiness promotes the radicalness of corporate innovation is through enhancing managers’ long-term focus of attention. Moreover, the motivating effect of executive compensation stickiness is particularly pronounced when the underperformance intensity is high, while underperformance duration does not appear to influence the relationship between executive compensation stickiness and the radicalness of corporate innovation. Our findings have important implications for research on executive compensation, attention, and innovation, as well as for regulators who intend to encourage radical innovation.

How Creative Re-Framing Happens: A Longitudinal Case Study of an Open Innovation Challenge

OMT
Cecilia Flores (Stockholm School of Economics); Santi Furnari
While research on open innovation challenges has focused on how teams innovate by addressing an already-framed problem, less attention has been devoted to how teams can innovate by creatively re-framing a problem. Triangulating 10-months of participant observation data with interviews, meetings’ video recordings and secondary data, we conducted a longitudinal case study of a technology-focused open innovation project and developed a process model of creative re-framing. Our model identifies four mechanisms -challenging and re-configuring the client relationship; embracing and unpacking the problem’s complexity- that together underlie the ebbs and flows of creative re-framing in an open innovation challenge. Our primary contribution is showing that the evolving relationship between the client organization issuing the problem and the open innovation team is an important source of creativity, potentially leading to creative re-framing. Implications for research on open innovation and collective creativity are discussed.

Innovation as Back to the Future: How Audiences Legitimate Retro-Innovation Through Iconic Templates

OMT
Antonio Paco Giuliani (University of Bologna)
Organizations increasingly innovate by returning to the past, reviving iconic forms, styles, and meanings to introduce products that look familiar yet promise novelty. Despite growing managerial enthusiasm for “retro” offerings, we know less about how such innovations become legitimate when their value proposition hinges on temporal ambiguity: they are new, but also deliberately old. Drawing on an abductive, longitudinal case study of Behringer’s launch of the RD-8 drum machine (an explicit contemporary reworking of the Roland TR-808) we analyze how producers and audiences jointly construct the legitimacy of retro-innovation in an online field. We show that legitimacy is built through iterative cycles in which the producer mobilizes an iconic template while audiences test (and contest) its fidelity, moral appropriateness, and categorical fit. We develop a process model centered on three audience tests (template fidelity, boundary work, and authenticity arbitration) and three producer responses (selective replication, strategic distancing, and temporal bridging). We further show how online arenas amplify and stabilize evaluative criteria through public evidence practices, turning legitimacy into an ongoing, inspectable accomplishment. Our theorizing extends research on legitimacy (Suchman, 1995), categorization (Zuckerman, 1999), media legitimation (Pollock & Rindova, 2003), and organizational identity dynamics (Ravasi & Schultz, 2006; Tripsas, 2009) by explaining how innovation can become legitimate through purposeful return and how iconic templates reorganize evaluative criteria in contested markets.

When is Tradition Both a Resource and a Constraint? The Differentiated Effect of Novelty Adoption

OMT
Andrea Lanza (Università della Calabria); Giusy Simone (University of Calabria)
In recent years, increasing interest among scholars has spurred the investigation of tradition as a resource or constraint, and its role in the adoption of innovation, addressing also those issues that hinder the advance of novelties and reinforce the status quo. The impact of innovation on tradition-led industries and products has received, instead, less attention; as a result, it seems quite undertheorized. Accordingly, this study examines the impact of novelty on a tradition-led industry and its related products. Our research setting is the Italian wine industry, in the context of which a novel classification, IGT (Indicazione Geografica Tipica-Typic Geographic Indication), was introduced in 1998, after a much-contested reform of the 1963 law that disciplined the system of wine Denominations. Our results show a differentiated reaction to adopting a novel category at the firm level, as products whose regulation allowed for change enjoyed better positioning over time, whereas other products, whose regulation was rigid and unable to change, suffered from worse positioning.
