Constellation Hybridity: Organizing Institutional Logics Across Networks for Indigenous Sovereignty

OMT
Vyas Sreenivas (Santa Clara University); Theodore L. Hill
How can organizations connect Indigenous communities to markets without sacrificing Indigenous sovereignty? We explore this question by examining two indigenous organizations: Keystone Foundation (India) and APY Art Centre Collective (Australia). Both developed constellations of legally separate but connected organizations, each embodying a distinct institutional logic. This architecture, which we term constellation hybridity, protects Indigenous governance through five mechanisms: logic containment, governance lock-in, selective coupling, trusted intermediation, and revenue flow accountability. Our findings show that when power asymmetries are profound and logics are fundamentally incompatible, sustaining hybridity may require distributing logics across organizations rather than balancing them within a single organization.

Toward a Theory of Entrepreneurial Production of Space: Rethinking Spatial Turn in Entrepreneurship

OMT
Indunil Prabodha Dharmasiri (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Despite the recognized importance of spatial contexts, entrepreneurship research treats them as passive backdrops through fragmented perspectives using incompatible terminologies. This prevents a comprehensive understanding of how space shapes and is shaped by entrepreneurial action. We address this theoretical fragmentation by developing an integrated framework reconceptualizing entrepreneurial space as a dynamic, contested, multifaceted, and affective element. Translating and extending Beyes and Holt's (2020) spatial twists framework to entrepreneurship, we reconceptualize space across four dimensions: site (placed and placing), contestation (power and spatial struggle), multiplicity (simultaneous spatialities), and poetics (imaginative and affective production). Our framework yields five formal definitions of spatial entrepreneurship broadly and four specific types (placed, contested, multiple-space, and poetic entrepreneurship) and eight testable propositions specifying when spatial production creates entrepreneurial opportunities and how spatial entrepreneurs create value. Our work contributes to the theory by providing a shared spatial vocabulary that integrates fragmented literatures, broadening value creation to include spatial efficiency, equity, and meaning, and generating empirically testable propositions for future research. By positioning space as both a medium and an outcome of entrepreneurship, our framework reveals spatial production as a fundamental entrepreneurial action, inviting scholars to adopt dynamic, critical perspectives on space in entrepreneurship.

Lessons from Australia's Black Summer: Configurational Pathways to Community Resilience

OMT
Jaco Fourie (UNSW Sydney); Ines Kuric (UNSW Sydney); Markus A. Höllerer; Graham Dwyer (Trinity College Dublin, The University of Dublin); Martin Kornberger (University of Technology, Sydney); A. Paul Spee (The University of Queensland); Renate Elisabeth Meyer (WU Vienna University of Economics and Business); Dennis Clemens Jancsary (University of Liverpool)
Despite increasing policy attention to community resilience, resilience outcomes following natural disasters continue to vary widely. This is primarily because both research and practice have tended to treat resilience drivers as discrete and additive, offering limited insight into how these drivers interact over time and within specific contexts. In the face of increasingly frequent and devastating disasters, such approaches risk reinforcing existing vulnerabilities and failure to mobilize critical local knowledge. In this paper, we examine the trajectories of four communities during the 2019-2020 Australian “Black Summer” bushfires to identify configurations of factors leading to positive, mixed, and negative resilience outcomes, and investigate how communities organize and interact with local leadership and formal emergency management to understand how different forms of collaborations contribute to resilience. Through our analysis, we offer insights for policymakers, emergency managers, and researchers. Pending more conclusive empirical evidence, we propose that resilience-building policy should account for the existence of multiple pathways to resilience.

Identity in Flux: Navigating the Intersection of the Entrepreneurial and Disability Identity

OMT
Sumit Kumar Jaiswal (Ahmedabad University); Priya Tewary; K V Gopakumar (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
This study explores how entrepreneurs with disabilities (EwDs) navigate the intersections of their entrepreneurial and disability identities. Drawing on semi-structured interviews with 30 EwDs across India and employing an identity-sensemaking lens, we find that EwDs constantly negotiate between strategically deploying their disability identity and confronting an attributed identity imposed by society. This tension highlights a complex interplay between individual agency and societal constraints. Moreover, the study reveals that the entrepreneurial identity acts as a catalyst for emancipation, for both the self and the wider community, suggesting that identity intersections can fundamentally mitigate or transform EwDs' outlooks and actions.
