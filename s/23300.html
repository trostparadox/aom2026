Augmenting Entrepreneurial Action: Human–AI Process Models of Causation and Effectuation

ENT
Jie Chen (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); Yiqian Lu (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); Heyang Sun (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); David Ahlstrom (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
This study advances entrepreneurial process theory by demonstrating how entrepreneurs can orchestrate predictive, generative, and agentic artificial intelligence (AI) systems across varying degrees of uncertainty. We propose two distinct yet complementary frameworks: a causal co-pursuit model designed for environments of measurable risk, and an effectual co-enactment model tailored for Knightian uncertainty. In stable contexts, the causal model illustrates how predictive AI functions as the primary analytical engine for optimization and forecasting. Conversely, the effectual model demonstrates how a distributed integration of generative, agentic, and predictive AI facilitates creative exploration, affordable-loss experimentation, and iterative control. Central to both models is the entrepreneur’s role in judgment and strategic interpretation. Furthermore, we outline the mechanisms that trigger transitions between these two logics, showing how AI systems can signal the need to shift strategies and support organizational learning. Beyond merely applying AI to existing frameworks, this research extends the ontological premises of causation and effectuation. We argue that AI transforms human foresight into algorithmic co-prediction and expands means-driven logic through technologically mediated control. By conceptualizing causation and effectuation as evolving human–AI hybrid logics, this paper offers a unified framework for entrepreneurial action in the digital age, with robust implications for theory, pedagogy, and policy.

Beyond the Hype: Building Strong Theory in AI-Entrepreneurship Research

ENT
Pedro Tonhozi De Oliveira (University of Oklahoma); Todd W. Moss (University of Oklahoma); Matthew Wood (University of Oklahoma)
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming entrepreneurial cognition, judgment, and action, yet scholarship at this intersection remains fragmented, descriptive, and loosely connected to core entrepreneurship theories. This mismatch risks allowing technological novelty—rather than solid theoretical reasoning—to shape the field’s development, leading to optimism bias, weak causal mechanisms, and uneven treatment of AI across levels of analysis. Using an abductive approach to synthesize the emerging AI-entrepreneurship literature, we map how AI is positioned across antecedents, processes, and outcomes and examine where these patterns align with—or overlook—foundational entrepreneurship theories. Our analysis reveals three theory-relevant issues that hinder cumulative theorizing: weak causal linkages, optimism bias, and the absence of integrative frameworks. To address these issues, we propose a multi-level framework that shows how AI enables and constrains key assumptions and mechanisms in entrepreneurship theory, along with a practical checklist for developing and testing strong theory at this nexus. Ultimately, this paper aims to guide AI-entrepreneurship research toward more disciplined theorizing, stronger causal explanations, and a more deliberate integration of AI into the intellectual core of entrepreneurship scholarship.

Developing Human Capability with Artificial Intelligence: An Entrepreneurial Opportunity Approach

ENT
Chien-Chi Tseng (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly reshaping entrepreneurial activity, yet existing research largely treats AI as a tool that improves efficiency, prediction, or execution. This perspective overlooks a more fundamental question: how does AI alter the human capabilities through which entrepreneurial opportunities are imagined, developed, and refined? Drawing on Joel Mokyr’s insight that technological techniques generate new phenomena that stimulate interpretive learning under uncertainty and further innovation, we develop a capability-based theory of AI-enabled entrepreneurial opportunity innovation. We conceptualize artificial intelligence as a capability-shaping infrastructure that transforms entrepreneurs’ learning rates, experimentation competence, evaluative judgment, creative recombination, and entrepreneurial understanding under uncertainty. Building a purely conceptual model, we theorize how AI-enabled human capability development influences opportunity innovation through expanded variation generation, accelerated experimentation, and altered interpretive processes, while also introducing risks of homogenization and capability traps when judgment is displaced by machine outputs. We further identify contestability of AI outputs, governance structures, uncertainty regimes, and venture stage as critical boundary conditions shaping when AI expands versus constrains entrepreneurial imagination. By repositioning AI from a task-level tool to a driver of capability development, this paper contributes to entrepreneurship theory by explaining how and when artificial intelligence enables sustained opportunity innovation, rather than merely faster execution.

Knightian Uncertainty, AI Augmentation, & the Paradox of Future Knowledge

ENT
David Matthew Townsend (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Judy Rady
Drawing on debates about the boundary conditions of AI in entrepreneurship, the purpose of this study investigates the impact of AI augmentation on the ability of entrepreneurs to address the problems of Knightian uncertainty: actor ignorance, practical indeterminism, agentic novelty, and competitive recursion. As epistemic technologies (i.e., knowledge-enhancing), prior research argues that AI systems can augment and enhance entrepreneurial decision-making through superior information processing and pattern recognition capabilities. In doing so, a growing volume of studies argue that AI systems provide superior methods for reducing key uncertainties inherent in entrepreneurial action processes. In contrast, other studies argue that no matter how intelligent AI systems become, they are still bound by the problems of Knightian uncertainty in dynamic, competitive marketplaces. Our study analyzes these competing theoretical arguments through a comprehensive agent-based model, which examines the impact of AI augmentation on 40+ million opportunity identification decisions spanning 200,000 agent histories. Our analysis illuminates a fundamental paradox of future knowledge where the superior information processing and pattern recognition abilities of AI systems creates a high-level innovation equilibrium trap in competitive marketplaces. Although AI augmentation enables entrepreneurs to quickly and efficiently identify, develop, and pursue high-quality opportunities, our analyses reveal that the diffusion of these tools amplifies the problems of Knightian uncertainty – particularly problems of competitive recursion. Based on these results, we conclude the study with a comprehensive discussion of future research opportunities regarding the contingent role of AI augmentation in opportunity identification processes.

Trust When Rules Run Out: Sacrifice, Moral Emotion, and AI Limits in Entrepreneurship

ENT
Chihmao Hsieh (SUNY Korea)
What uniquely human capacities remain valuable in entrepreneurship as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly advanced? This paper argues that trust in entrepreneurial collaboration depends on two capacities that AI fundamentally lacks: the ability to signal sacrifice, and the capacity to experience and signal moral emotion. When novel dilemmas arise that fall outside the AI’s training and the predefined constraints, it cannot signal sacrifice in ways that credibly sustain commitment because it does not incur personal costs—including irreversible investments or risks to self-identity. Furthermore, although AI systems may appear to behave morally, their reliance on externally programmed rules and data-driven optimization prevents them from bearing personal responsibility and experiencing moral emotions that support adaptive judgment. Both of these limitations are especially consequential under Knightian uncertainty, where novel ethical dilemmas routinely fall outside AI’s predefined constraints, creating moral blind spots; and AI’s defection thereof risks no psychological cost because there is no self-identity to threaten in the first place. Overall, AI’s inabilities to signal sacrifice and to engage in moral-emotional learning undermine trust formation during entrepreneurship.
