The Disruptive Effects of Remote Work on Psychological Safety and Voice

OB
Sean Martin (University of Virginia); Jeremy A. Yip (Georgetown University); Ayana N. Younge
The increasing shift to remote work presents multiple theoretical challenges to scholarly understanding about psychological safety, its antecedents, and its outcomes. Using Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we explore how remote versus in-person work arrangements moderate the relationships between contextual and individual antecedents of psychological safety and employee voice. Results from a multisource, three-wave study of employees in a mid-sized technology firm indicate that supportive leadership behaviors, a critical antecedent of psychological safety, are less impactful in remote work contexts, while remote work mitigates the negative impact of impostor thoughts on employees’ psychological safety. Our findings contribute to theory by suggesting that the contextual and individual antecedents of psychological safety are contingent upon whether employes are remote or in-person such that what might be beneficial in one environment, may be less important or even harmful in another. Practically, our work highlights the need for tailored strategies to cultivate psychological safety and voice in hybrid and remote environments, particularly for employees who may otherwise feel depleted by in-person interactions.

More is Better? Unpacking How Team Voice and Disparity Jointly Shape Information Elaboration

OB
Baidou Wu (Xi'an Jiaotong University); PAN FAN (Harbin Institute of Technology); Fengxiang Zhao (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Xiaoling Li (Huajue Zhihui (Xi’an) Technology Co., LTD)
Team voice is widely recognized as a key driver of team performance and innovation, yet little is known about how the quantity and distribution of voice jointly influence deep-level information processing. Drawing on social information processing theory, we examine the role of voice configuration, defined as the combined effect of team aggregate voice and team voice disparity, in shaping team information elaboration, and investigate team perceived justice as a mediating motivational mechanism. We tested our hypotheses using a three-wave longitudinal survey design and collected data from 554 employees across 71 teams in a large manufacturing company in mainland China. The results indicate that high aggregate voice enhances team information elaboration only when voice is evenly distributed, because this configuration fosters team perceived justice. Theoretical and practical implications for the team voice and information elaboration literature are discussed.

Safe to Talk: Psychological Safety and Conversational AI at Work

OB
Allen Samuel Brown (Carnegie Mellon University); Christopher Dishop (Auburn University); Andrew Kuznetsov (Carnegie Mellon University); Anita Williams Woolley (Carnegie Mellon University)
Conversational artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly used to support collaborative work, yet little is known about how such systems shape workers’ psychological experiences during interaction. We examine when and why collaboration with conversational AI enhances versus undermines psychological safety, a foundational condition for learning, communication, and interpersonal risk-taking at work. Drawing on psychological safety theory and self-determination theory, we argue that conversational AI is not inherently psychologically unsafe; rather, its effects depend on whether collaboration occurs under conditions of indirect evaluation. Across two preregistered experiments, we show that psychological safety is reduced when individuals collaborate with a conversational AI partner (vs. a human partner) in indirectly evaluative contexts, but not in non-evaluative contexts. We further demonstrate that this interaction operates through autonomy satisfaction. Providing causal evidence for this mechanism, a second study directly manipulates autonomy and shows that autonomy-supportive conditions eliminate differences in psychological safety between human and AI partners. These findings extend psychological safety theory to algorithmically mediated collaboration and highlight autonomy as a designable mechanism shaping human–AI interaction.

Supervisor-Directed Gossip and Employee Voice: Depletion Effects Under Abusive Leadership

OB
Julena M. Bonner (Utah State University); Alexander Romney (Utah State University); Zhiya (Alice) Guo (Utah State University)
Gossip is a pervasive aspect of organizational life and is frequently associated with negative consequences for individuals, teams, and organizations. While prior research has primarily focused on how gossip affects its targets and recipients, less is known about its downstream impact on the behaviors of those who engage in it—the gossipers themselves. Drawing upon the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we develop and test a gossiper-centric model examining how engaging in negative supervisor-directed gossip influences gossipers’ subsequent improvement-oriented communication. Across a sample of 207 full-time employees, results supported our prediction that gossiping about supervisors depletes employees’ psychological resources, which in turn reduces their likelihood of speaking up to suggest constructive changes or needed improvements. Furthermore, the level of abusive supervision moderated the depleting effect of negative supervisor-directed gossip, such that employees experienced less (vs. more) depletion when gossiping about highly (vs. less) abusive supervisors. These results contribute to the literature on gossip and voice and offer practical implications for managing informal communication and fostering open dialogue in the workplace.
