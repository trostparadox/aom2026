Entrepreneurship Education: A Double-Edged Sword for Employability?

ENT
Davide Hahn (University of Bergamo); Angelo Cavallo (Politecnico di Milano); Azzurra Meoli (University of Bologna); Evila Piva (Politecnico di Milano)
Employees endowed with entrepreneurial human capital (EHC) are widely recognized as crucial to organizational performance. Yet it remains unclear whether organizations are open to hiring employees who possess EHC. Prior research on founding experience suggests that, during hiring, recruiters often discount EHC because concerns about role fit and commitment outweigh appreciation of its functional value. However, we know far less about how these dynamics extend to entrepreneurship education (EE), an increasingly widespread and critical source of EHC, particularly for recent graduates entering the labor market. To address this gap, we examine how recruiters evaluate the employability of candidates with extracurricular EE and disentangle the competing roles of EHC value and misfit concerns. We do so by comparing EE against two counterfactuals—no extracurricular education and high-value, field-aligned extracurricular education—and by examining the moderating role of environmental dynamism. We test our hypotheses using a resume experiment with 186 professional recruiters, complemented by interviews to strengthen the face validity of our findings. Our results show that EE neither improves nor harms employability relative to no extracurricular education, but is penalized relative to high-value specialist education particularly in dynamic environments. These findings suggest that recruiters’ early-stage evaluations of EE reflect offsetting forces and that misfit concerns can prevail precisely where EHC should be most valuable. We contribute to research on EHC, EE, and early-stage hiring by clarifying when and why EE-based EHC is discounted in the labor market.

In-School and Extracurricular Entrepreneurship Education Before Career Choice.

ENT
Florian Saegebrecht (TU Dresden); Sarah Mueller-Saegebrecht; Thomas Niemand
Entrepreneurship education is widely studied as a driver of entrepreneurial intention, yet existing research predominantly focuses on university students. Much less is known about how entre-preneurial orientations are shaped before formal career choices are made. Drawing on the Theory of Planned Behavior, this study examines the effects of entrepreneurship education on entrepreneurial intention among secondary and vocational students. Using survey data from 197 students enrolled in secondary schools, grammar schools, and vocational schools, structural equa-tion modeling is applied to distinguish between in-school and extracurricular entrepreneurship education and to examine the mediating roles of perceived behavioral control and subjective norms. Due to high empirical overlap, attitude toward entrepreneurship and entrepreneurial intention are integrated into a single construct termed Attitudinal Entrepreneurial Intention. The results show that in-school entrepreneurship education directly strengthens attitudinal entrepre-neurial intention, whereas extracurricular entrepreneurship education primarily enhances perceived behavioral control. Subjective norms do not exert a direct effect. The study contributes by extending intention-based theory to the school context and providing quantitative evidence on entrepreneurial intention formation prior to higher education.

Relocating Entrepreneurship Education in the Yurt: A Processual Framework for Inclusive Redesign

ENT
Matteo Opizzi (University of Cagliari); Ludovica Moi (University of Cagliari); GIANLUCA PUSCEDDU; Francesca Cabiddu (University of Cagliari)
How can entrepreneurship education be redesigned to support entrepreneurial trajectories that do not conform to dominant startup-oriented, growth-driven, and individualistic models? While research on inclusive entrepreneurship education has expanded, it has largely focused on barriers, group-specific experiences, or program adaptations, leaving unexplored how education itself can be reconfigured as an institutional, symbolic, and relational infrastructure that enables inclusion. This paper examines how inclusive entrepreneurship education is enacted through the situated redesign of a program for unconventional entrepreneurs. Drawing on a single case study guided by engaged organizational ethnography, we trace how educational purposes, pedagogical arrangements, and learning relationships are iteratively reworked over time. Our analysis identifies three interdependent phases: a normative reorientation that redefines legitimate entrepreneurial purposes; a pedagogical reconfiguration that reshapes how entrepreneurship is taught through symbolic, linguistic, and material infrastructures; and the emergence of transformative learning dynamics that foster identity work, emotional safety, and collective legitimacy. Building on these findings, we advance a processual framework of inclusive entrepreneurship education that conceptualizes education as a flexible and relational infrastructure of legitimacy rather than a standardized delivery mechanism. We show that inclusion is not achieved by adding new audiences to existing models, but by reconfiguring the normative, pedagogical, and relational conditions through which entrepreneurship becomes imaginable, meaningful, and viable. By theorizing inclusive entrepreneurship education as infrastructural and processual, this paper shifts attention from categorical inclusion to legitimacy construction and offers design-oriented insights for supporting diverse entrepreneurial pathways.

Toward Diversity and Inclusion in Entrepreneurship Education: Using Words to Narrow the Gender Gap

ENT
Kimberly A. Eddleston (Northeastern University); Kristen Madison (Oklahoma State University); Chioma Okpalaeke (Oklahoma State University); Bryan D. Edwards (Oklahoma State University); Jerald Louis Wallace; Torsten Michael Pieper (University of North Carolina at Charlotte)
We investigate whether language is contributing to the pervasive gender gap in entrepreneurship. Drawing from person-environment fit theory, we theorize that the stereotypical masculine language used to describe entrepreneurship education activities signals to women and feminine individuals that they do not belong. Using experimental vignettes, we manipulated the descriptions of university entrepreneurship clubs and tested our theorizing with 275 business students. Findings indicate that masculine language triggers perceptions of “misfit” for female and feminine students, which lowers their interest in joining an entrepreneurship club. Whereas feminine and gender-neutral language helps dismantle masculine stereotypes of entrepreneurs, promoting inclusion and encouraging women and feminine individuals to see themselves as a “fit” with entrepreneurship. Our findings suggest that masculine language likely limits the quantity and diversity of potential entrepreneurs. Thus, our study highlights the power of words in promoting inclusion in entrepreneurship education, which may ultimately help close the gender gap in entrepreneurship.

Wartime Social Entrepreneurship Program: A Care-Centered Approach to Adolescent Wellbeing (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Sonia Vidal (Amsterdam Business School); Sanita Rugina (Maastricht University)
Education systems increasingly face crises - war, pandemics, climate change - that destabilize schooling models built on predictability, delayed rewards, and future labor-market preparation. In such contexts, education is expected not only to transmit knowledge but to sustain agency, hope, and purpose. While entrepreneurship education is often linked to experiential learning and agency, little is known about its role under prolonged violent conflict. This study examines a social entrepreneurship program for adolescents implemented in high schools during the ongoing war. Drawing on qualitative interviews with program coordinators, teachers, and students, we explore how entrepreneurship education can foster adolescent psychosocial resilience, civic purpose and future orientation amid disruption. We develop a dynamic model of wartime social entrepreneurship as a safeguarding infrastructure that merges entrepreneurial problem-solving with protective educational practices. We theorize this pedagogy as pedagogical safeguarding: a world-preserving educational infrastructure that protects adolescents’ developmental, existential, and civic capacities while enabling meaningful contribution during crisis. Safeguarding operates through three interrelated forms - symbolic, material, and temporal. Conceptualizing entrepreneurship education as pedagogical safeguarding, the study moves beyond skills training and advances research on youth agency and wellbeing in crisis, and extends entrepreneurship-as-world-making under conditions of war.
