Fight Hard or Pull Back? Dual Motivational Mechanisms of Problemistic Search

STR
Zhiling Lin (University of Wisconsin-Madison); Hart E. Posen
The extent to which the distance of problemistic search varies across organizations remains a central yet underexplored question in the performance feedback literature. Prior research in this paradigm has focused primarily on how performance shortfalls influence search intensity, offering limited insight into how organizations adjust search distance across knowledge domains. In this paper, we develop a motivational account of search distance by integrating regulatory focus theory with performance feedback research. We argue that performance shortfalls are constructed through distinct motivational orientations, promotion focus and prevention focus, which systematically shape organizations’ propensity for distant versus local search. Drawing on longitudinal Toxic Release Inventory data on U.S. manufacturing facilities from 1992 to 2020, our results reveal divergent problemistic search responses. Promotion-focused search expands search distance as performance shortfalls increase, whereas prevention-focused search contracts it. These findings challenge the implicit assumption that problemistic search follows a uniform pattern across organizations. By distinguishing search distance from search intensity and identifying the motivational mechanisms underlying problemistic search, this study advances performance feedback theory and sheds light on how organizations adapt search behavior in response to performance shortfalls.

Advance or Retreat? How Rivals’ Status Trajectory Affects a Firm’s Innovation Strategy

STR
Hongmei Xu (Guangdong University of Foreign Studies); Xiaoran Ni (Central University of Finance and Economics); Geng Cui (Lingnan University); Haiyang Li (Rice University)
This study examines how a firm responds to its rivals’ status gains (i.e., brand award). Drawing upon the social psychology perspective of interfirm competition, we make a distinction between emergent rising stars (i.e., first time recipients of a brand award) and entrenched superstars (i.e., multiple times recipients). We argue that rising stars and superstars affect the perceived competitive gap between the focal firm and its rivals differently. With the recognition of a rising star, the focal firm will frame it as an attainable yet pressing benchmark, thus increasing its innovation inputs for catchup. Conversely, the recognition of a superstar reinforces the perceived competitive gap, establishing an unattainable benchmark that prompts the focal firm’s strategic retreat and reduced innovation inputs. Leveraging a large-scale panel data of Chinese listed firms (2012–2020) and a novel text-based approach to identify competitors, we find robust support: Firms increase their innovation inputs following a rising star’s recognition but decrease their inputs after a superstar’s inclusion, with subsequent reductions in patent grants. Our study advances a psychologically grounded theory of competitive dynamics, demonstrating how rivals’ status trajectory systematically drives a firm’s strategic response.

Using Superstitious Heuristics in Strategic Decision Making: Process Outcomes and Contingencies

STR
Jing Liu (Northern Kentucky University); Weilei (Stone) Shi (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
Focusing on the phenomenon of using superstition as a decision shortcut in strategic decision making, we investigate the relationships between the use of superstitious heuristics and two process outcomes – decision speed and collective efficacy. We also explore three contingency factors – decision importance, executive influence, and environmental traditionalism – which capture the characteristics of the decisions, the decision maker, and the environmental context, respectively. Using survey data from a sample of Chinese top executives, we find that the use of superstitious heuristics is positively related to decision speed, and this relationship is strengthened by decision importance and environmental traditionalism. We fail to find support for an overall significant relationship between superstitious heuristics and collective efficacy; instead, we find that superstitious heuristics are positively associated with collective efficacy when the decision is of high importance or when the environment is highly traditionalistic. We further reveal that the moderating effect of executive influence on our two main effects is contingent on traditionalism.

Feedback, Decision Time, and Exploration Across Behavioral Tasks

STR
Stephan Billinger (University of Southern Denmark); Vithor Rosa Franco (Universidade São Francisco)
Exploration in uncertain environments depends critically on how individuals interpret feedback. Although many behavioral tasks are used to study exploration, it remains unclear whether common feedback properties predict exploratory behavior across structurally distinct paradigms. Using publicly available data from Anvari et al. (2024, Nature Communications), we examine whether shared feedback properties—initial feedback, average feedback, feedback variance, final outcomes, and decision time—explain exploration across five widely used tasks: the Alien Game, Multi-Armed Bandit, Optional Stopping, Sampling Paradigm, and Observe-or-Bet. Across regression analyses, a feedback distribution composite analysis, and network psychometrics, no single feedback metric consistently predicted exploration across tasks. Instead, feedback effects were strongly task dependent. Decision time emerged as the most consistent correlate of exploration across paradigms, although its direction varied by task, indicating a shared process-level role of cognitive effort rather than a task-general exploration tendency. Residual correlations between exploration measures were negligible once feedback structure and processing demands were accounted for, suggesting limited shared variance across tasks. Network analyses highlighted the Observe-or-Bet task as particularly central, consistent with its distinctive delayed-feedback structure. Together, these findings qualify strong claims of domain-general exploratory behavior and show that exploration arises from the interaction between task-specific feedback architectures and cognitive processing dynamics. We discuss implications for the measurement of exploration and for research on learning and decision making under uncertainty.

Revisiting Performance Feedback: Aspiration Levels, Capability Realization, and Risk-Taking Behavior

STR
Kyung Yul Lee (Hankuk University of Foreign Studies)
This study extends the behavioral theory of the firm by reconceptualizing performance feedback as a multidimensional construct that accounts for both performance relative to aspirations and performance relative to a firm’s underlying capabilities. We define four feedback categories: legitimated success (meeting aspirations with outcomes exceeding capability-based expectations), fragile success (meeting aspirations but underperforming relative to capabilities), potential-confirming failure (failing to meet aspirations yet performing above capability-based expectations), and structural failure (failing to meet aspirations and underperforming relative to capabilities). Using panel data on 224 team-seasons from the Korea Baseball Organization (2001–2025), we examine how each feedback type influences risk-taking decisions in the form of top leadership changes and workforce reconfiguration. Our analyses show that structural failures are associated with a higher likelihood of replacing the top leader, whereas legitimated successes correspond to greater leadership stability. Likewise, potential-confirming failures that exceed capability-based expectations trigger significant workforce reconfiguration, whereas both types of success entail minimal workforce changes. These findings underscore the importance of considering internal performance potential in feedback evaluation. By incorporating capability-based benchmarks alongside aspirations, this study provides a more nuanced understanding of when firms pursue strategic change versus reinforce the status quo.
