Hitting the Pitch Running: Immigration, Human Capital, and Job Performance of New Hires

OMT
Aaron Aujla (London School of Economics and Political Science)
When workers change organizations across countries, they must acquire new human capital, to reflect differences in the nature of work internationally. I theorize as to how the human capital (productive skills, knowledge, abilities, and other characteristics) which immigrant workers acquire in a new country is shaped by the organization in which they first work in that country. If the human capital which workers learn within an organization is to some extent firm-specific, the organization has a key — and underexplored — role to play in immigrants’ human capital acquisition. Since newly-arrived migrant workers lack a general understanding of the skills and knowledge required in a given national context, I argue they are uniquely receptive at this time to learning firm-specific capabilities which later impede performance in other organizations in that country. Using longitudinal data on 2,500 professional soccer players changing organizations within and between five European countries over seven years, I find that immigrant workers’ short-term performance in new organizations is paradoxically more negatively affected when they have already been working in the country previously (compared to when joining directly from abroad), and that this effect is exclusively driven by reduced performance on tasks which require firm-specific knowledge and skills. The performance decline is exacerbated when immigrants re-unite with former colleagues in the new firm. The results have implications for our understanding of when and how workers in global markets acquire new human capital, and the potential long-term consequences for job performance when immigrants remain in a country beyond their first firm.

Skills Under Threat, Family in Mind: Uncovering the Micro-Processes of Human Capital Renewal (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Seok-Hyun (Stephen) Hwang (The Hang Seng University of Hong Kong)
Technological change increasingly renders established skills obsolete, yet workers facing similar pressures often diverge sharply in whether and how they adapt. Existing research on human capital has documented the economic and organizational conditions associated with reskilling, but offers more limited insight into how adaptation becomes possible when skill renewal entails symbolic loss, uncertainty, and the prospect of starting over. This study addresses this question through an in-depth qualitative study of automotive technicians navigating the transition to electric vehicles in South Korea. The findings show that reskilling does not follow automatically from recognizing skill devaluation. Instead, adaptation becomes viable when technicians reinterpret skill threat through family-based obligations, particularly responsibilities associated with providing for dependents. This reorientation reshapes how workers evaluate the costs of inaction and enables engagement with reskilling despite persistent identity disruption. The study further identifies three modes through which this reorientation is enacted over time—oscillating adaptation, delegated adaptation, and hybridization—each reflecting distinct pathways through which workers sustain adaptation under uncertainty. By articulating the micro-level processes through which skill threat is translated into action, this study advances a process-oriented account of human capital adaptation and reframes family commitments as potential enablers of skill renewal during periods of technological change.

From Sigh to Sign: The Communicative Structuring of Grievances in Startups

OMT
Ji Young Jung (Ewha Womans University); Sang-Joon Kim (Ewha Womans University)
Startups are often described as flat organizations that promise autonomy and egalitarian interaction, yet members frequently experience discrepancies between these ideals and everyday organizational practices. This study examines how such discrepancies give rise to grievances and how grievances become collectively constructed through discourse. Drawing on a grounded theory analysis of 12,352 naturally occurring online reviews unobtrusively collected from four sampled startups on a digital platform, we conceptualize grievances as interpretive processes emerging from individuals’ experienced misalignments between expected and enacted flatness. Our findings show that organizational members rarely express grievances through direct complaints. Instead, they rely on a strategically ambiguous discursive term, which is a short, socially intelligible expression that condenses dissatisfaction while allowing multiple interpretations. This term enables grievances to circulate broadly without provoking overt conflict and, as they gain collective uptake, recursively shape members’ sensemaking, reinforcing shared perceptions of organizational misalignment. By theorizing the discursive and symbolic mechanisms through which grievances move from private interpretations to collectively institutionalized meanings, this study contributes to research on flat organizing, employee voice, and sensemaking, and demonstrates how organizational structures are continuously enacted and contested through everyday communicative practices.

Positive Social Capital and Resilience to Extreme Work Contexts

OMT
Jirs Meuris (University of Wisconsin-Madison)
Severe strain is a defining feature of extreme work contexts, yet its cumulative effects threaten workers’ health and well-being. This paper proposes that access to positive social capital exchanged through high-quality relationships underpins resilience to the physiological and psychological weight of working in extreme contexts while embedded in the setting and after returning home. Using a quasi-experiment combining pre- and post-surveys and administrative records from over 6,000 U.S. Army soldiers deployed to a combat zone in Afghanistan during 2012-2013, I observed a smaller increase in physical health problems and symptoms of depression that returned to baseline over time after returning home, a smaller increase in suicide ideation persisting after returning home, and a smaller increase in self-medication with alcohol and drugs that emerged after returning home among soldiers with access to more positive social capital during the deployment. These differences were greatest among soldiers who were exposed to more home stressors such as financial precarity or marital problems during the deployment. Altogether, this paper demonstrates that fostering positive social capital in work units is essential to human functioning in extreme work contexts and facilitating transitions out of these environments.

Work Autonomy and Resource Adaptation Under Market Shocks

OMT
Lingling Zhang (Nanjing University); Jia Li (Wake Forest University)
Exogenous market shocks are increasingly common, yet we know little about how individual workers adapt to such threats. While prior research has focused on firm-level responses to competitive disruption, this study examines how market shocks reshape individual-level resources and behavior. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we propose that worker autonomy and market context constitute critical boundary conditions that determine whether resource threats trigger adaptive investment or passive loss. We examine the entry of a ride-hailing platform as an exogenous market shock to incumbent taxi drivers in a large Chinese city. Using highly granular GPS and trip-level data and a regression discontinuity in time design, we analyze changes in drivers’ earnings, productivity, and search behavior. We find that the shock reduces average earnings, but that self-employed drivers, who possess greater autonomy, actively adjust their search strategies and experience significantly smaller resource losses. Moreover, the effects vary systematically across locations, indicating that contextual resource passageways shape individual outcomes. These findings extend COR theory to competitive market settings by identifying autonomy as a key determinant of resource convertibility and by demonstrating how contextual heterogeneity conditions resource loss dynamics.
