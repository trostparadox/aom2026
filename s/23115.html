What’s Next After Pointing the Finger?: Self-Serving Attribution in Workplace Injury

HR
Young Hoon (Gabriel) Jung (Montclair State University); Jihyun Eun (Salisbury University); Minjung Lee
How do managers and employees respond to workplace injuries? Self-serving bias from attribution theory posits that the two parties are likely to blame each other in such a negative organizational event. In this study, by examining the roles of industrial relations during the attribution process, we further investigate how such self-serving and biased attributions of managers and employees are manifested and subsequently reshape organizational settings in practice. Adopting the context of Korean industrial relations, which can be described in the three Ds—Distance, Duty, and Distrust—between managers and employees, we argue that such unique characteristics of industrial relations exacerbate self-serving attributions but simultaneously limit the actual behavioral responses available to both parties following workplace injuries. As a result, we find that managers may manifest their after-injury behavioral changes by excluding employees from participation in workplace training and development programs, whereas employees may tend to adopt a silent exit in the Korean context. However, we also find that the local embeddedness of the workplace attenuates the effect of workplace injuries on employees’ voluntary turnover.

Collision Between Work and Family Propelling Virtual Community engagement

HR
Yue Zhang (Peking University); Wenxue Zheng (Fuzhou University)
Drawing on the Work–Home Resources (W–HR) model and Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, this study examines why job demands, in interaction with family support or family undermining, drive employees into virtual communities through psychological detachment as a defensive resource conservation strategy. Using an online multi-wave survey and two scenario-based experiments, results support the proposed resource conservation mechanisms. Specifically, family support weakens the positive relationships between job demands and both virtual community engagement and psychological detachment, whereas family undermining strengthens these relationships. These findings reveal boundary conditions of cross-domain resource support and undermining between work and family in shaping virtual community engagement, thereby illustrating a nuanced triple-domain interaction process. By adopting a mesosystem perspective, this study advances understanding of resource–demand spillover across work, family, and virtual community domains and delineates the serial mediation through which individuals enact resource conservation strategies. Finally, we discussed implications, limitations, as well as future directions.

Task Interdependence Buffers the Negative Effect of Creative Time Pressure on Innovative Performance

HR
Yue Yuan (The University of Hong Kong); Zhiming Wu (Tsinghua University)
The subjective passage of time—the perception of how quickly or slowly time elapses—is a ubiquitous yet understudied phenomenon in workplace psychology, despite its profound implications for work efficiency. This study breaks new ground by investigating its role in innovation contexts, proposing it as the key mechanism linking creative time pressure to innovative performance. Grounded in the attention gate model and employing a mixed-methods approach—which combined an experimental study (n = 217) with a field survey (n = 381)—the findings revealed that creative time pressure negatively impacted innovative performance through the experienced passage of time. Crucially, however, perceived task interdependence served as a positive buffer, mitigating this adverse effect. The study contributed a novel theoretical framework that integrated time perception and team functionality perspectives to explain how creative time pressure shaped innovative outcomes. These insights offered practical guidance for managers in designing and optimizing time pressures in creative work, helping employees experience time more meaningfully and productively.

Desire to Learn and Knowledge Sharing Dynamics: The Mediating Role of Receiver Gratitude and Sense of Obligation

HR
Hussain Alhejji (Prince Sultan University); Saeed Turki Alshahrani (Al-Imam Mohammad Ibn Saud Islamic University)
The factors influencing when and how employees desire to learn, and why some actively engage in knowledge sharing (KS) while others remain passive, are not yet well understood. This study aims to examine the role of employee desire to learn in knowledge-sharing (KS) dynamics. We empirically explore how employees’ desire to learn shapes KS dynamics and how providers’ behavior influences receivers. Drawing from the theory of reasoned action and the theory of reciprocity, we examine how employees desire to learn shapes KS processes and how providers’ behavior influences receivers. We utilized data from 196 healthcare professionals in eight private hospitals in Saudi Arabia. Our results show that receiver knowledge-seeking behavior mediates the relationship between receiver desire to learn and provider KS behavior. We found that both receiver gratitude and receiver sense of obligation to share knowledge mediate the relationship between provider KS behavior and receiver KS behavior with others. This study contributes to knowledge management literature by showing the mechanism through which employee desire to learn contributes to both provider KS behavior and receiver KS behavior. In doing this, we highlight new insights into how learning motivation translates into continued KS within healthcare settings.

Gain Without Pain? The Physiological Impact of Work Time Reduction on Stress and Recovery

HR
Johannes Hueby (University of Münster); Julia Backmann (University of Münster); Felix Hoch (University College Dublin); Marika Platz (University of Münster); Matthias Felix Sinnemann (University of Münster)
As organizations increasingly adopt Worktime Reduction (WTR) models, specifically the four-day workweek, a critical debate has emerged: Does the temporal compression of work demands lead to detrimental work intensification, or does it serve as a structural resource for recovery? Drawing on the Effort-Recovery Model (ERM) and Strain-Based Spillover Theory, this study investigates the objective physiological impact of WTR on employee stress. We conducted a field study utilizing continuous physiological monitoring using Heart-Rate-Variability (HRV) to capture objective stress responses among N=87 employees across 21 organizations. Based on 10,401 daily observations analyzed via multilevel mixed-effects modeling, our findings challenge the prevailing intensification hypothesis. We found that the condensation of work hours did not result in a significant increase in daily physiological stress on workdays. Conversely, the intervention acted as a robust recovery mechanism: the additional free day associated with the WTR significantly lowered acute physiological arousal and generated a temporal buffering effect that reduced accumulated strain spillover into the weekend. These results suggest that WTR functions as a structural design intervention that intercepts the accumulation of physiological arousal without exacting the physiological cost typically associated with temporal density.
