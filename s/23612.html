Session overview

These papers examine how visible cues such as accent, attractiveness, gendered behavior, tattoos, and role stereotypes shape hiring and evaluation decisions. Together, they reveal how decision-makers rely on status signals and stereotypes in ways that can disadvantage candidates from marginalized groups. The session offers practical implications for designing selection processes that reduce bias and improve fairness.

Do You Even Know What You Are Talking About? How Accent Bias Impacts Career Outcomes

DEI
Gabriela Rodriguez (The University of Texas at Arlington); Scott Moore (The University of Texas at Arlington); Mauricio Mercado (Missouri University of Science and Technology)
Implicit leadership theory suggests that leadership evaluations reflect cognitive prototypes of who “fits” leader roles, while implicit stereotype theory highlights how social group cues can automatically activate biased judgments. In hiring contexts, sociolinguistic cues such as accent can therefore function as potent signals of group membership that shape leadership-relevant evaluations. Building on these perspectives, we develop a proposition-based model of how a Mexican Spanish accent influences interview, hiring, and salary recommendations for Latina leadership applicants. Specifically, we propose that a Mexican Spanish accent lowers employability outcomes indirectly by reducing perceived competence via prototype-based lack-of-fit inferences. We further argue that organizational context can disrupt this categorization process: when English–Spanish bilingualism is explicitly stated as a bona fide occupational qualification (BFOQ) in the job description, the accent can be recoded as competence-relevant, attenuating—or potentially reversing—the negative indirect effects of accent bias. By theorizing BFOQs as a boundary condition that can pivot implicit categorization and by centering Mexican women’s intersectional labor-market experiences, this paper extends scholarship on accent discrimination, leadership, and bias mitigation while identifying contextually actionable strategies for more equitable selection.

Now You See It; Now You Don’t: Influence of Individuation Information and Stereotypes on Selection

DEI
I.M. "Jim" Jawahar (The University of New Mexico); Lisa Marchiondo (The University of New Mexico)
Stereotypes are widely held and shape expectations about who is suitable for certain roles, thereby influencing selection decisions. Previous research indicates that less attractive individuals and women seeking male-dominated jobs are discriminated against more than attractive individuals and women seeking female-dominated jobs, respectively. We conducted two studies to test assumptions underlying this research, replicate previous research findings, and extend this stream by examining if an insight from Study 1 and social psychological research could be applied to the selection context to mitigate or even reverse these stereotypic effects. In Study 1, we took a novel approach to investigate if attractive individuals are still ascribed positive attributes by comparing correlations between attractiveness and attributes ascribed to strangers versus friends. Results indicated that for strangers (but not friends), decision-makers rely on an individual’s attractiveness to determine their qualities, evidencing the existence of attractive stereotypes. In Study 2, attractiveness and applicant gender were manipulated as within-subject factors and job sex-type (male- versus female-dominated job) and individuation information (presence or absence) as between-subjects factors. When no individuation information was provided, attractiveness and job sex-type stereotypes prevailed, but these effects reversed when individuation information was provided, such that the most qualified person was hired. These results indicate that provision of individuation information, diagnostic of an applicant’s suitability for a job, prevents decision-makers from relying on focal stereotypes. This insight could be used to mitigate unfair discrimination and facilitate DEI efforts. We discuss theoretical implications and practical significance of the results for job applicants and organizations.

Professional Women in Asynchronous Evaluation: The Role of Evaluator Gender

DEI
Celia Chui (HEC Montréal); Emma Y. Zhao (University of Virginia); Eileen Y. Suh; Rebekka Steiner (University of Bern)
As work increasingly shifts toward online and asynchronous interactions, it is important to understand how these mediums can reshape opportunities for women on the job market. Our research examines how women’s behavioral cues influence evaluations and hiring recommendations in asynchronous video interviews (AVIs) which are becoming increasingly prevalent in organizational hiring processes. These static, one-directional interactions magnify the signaling effects of behavioral cues at a critical juncture when initial hiring decisions determine who ultimately advances to obtain these positions. Focusing on professional women candidates, we examine three styles of behavioral cues along the agentic-communal spectrum: neutral-professionalism, friendly warmth, and feminine charm. Across two studies, one in the lab with students (Study 1, N = 401) and one online study with full-time professionals that have recruitment experience (Study 2, N = 582), we find that compared to neutral professionalism and friendly warmth behavioral cues, evaluators perceived the female applicant in the feminine charm cues condition as lower in morality and ability, which reduced hiring recommendations. Notably, we find that these effects were more pronounced in women evaluators than in men. We discuss the broader theoretical and practical implications for understanding different gender perceptions of women’s behavioral cues in hiring processes that are increasingly reliant on technology.

From Penalties to Authenticity: The Effects of Tattoos on Judgements and Hiring Depend on Status

DEI
Yipeng Wang (Not Associated); Laura Guillén (ESADE Business School - Ramon Llull University); Maria Kakarika (Durham University Business School)
Visible tattoos are increasingly common, yet evidence on whether they help or hurt candidates in hiring remains mixed. We reconcile this inconsistency by theorizing tattoos as a dual signal: they can simultaneously trigger a competence-based penalty and an authenticity-based benefit, with candidate status shaping these effects. Across two preregistered experiments using LinkedIn-style profile headers in a 2 (tattooed vs. not) × 2 (high vs. low candidate status) design, we find consistent support for this duality. In a U.S. population sample (N = 789) and a sample of U.S. hiring managers (N = 645), tattoos reduced perceived competence yet increased perceived authenticity; competence and authenticity, in turn, predicted hiring recommendations in opposite directions, such that the authenticity benefit was strong enough to completely offset the competence penalty. Crucially, candidate status moderated the authenticity pathway: tattoos yielded a stronger authenticity-based indirect benefit for high-status candidates, whereas this indirect benefit was weak or absent for low-status candidates. These findings advance research on tattoo perception, nonconformity, authenticity, and stigma in organizations by specifying how evaluators route the same cue into competing inferences, clarifying why tattoos sometimes appear penalized, neutral, or advantageous in hiring.
