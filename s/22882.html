The Mediating Role of Team Temporal Reflexivity and the Moderating Role of Team Goal Similarity

OB
Li Yu Kee (Peking University); Yiyong Zhou
While prior research establishes the impact of circadian rhythms on individual functioning, the implications of chronotype diversity for team dynamics remain largely unexplored. Drawing on the lens of team temporal mental models, this research examines how team chronotype diversity affects team outcomes through the regulatory mechanism of team temporal reflexivity (TTR). We further propose that this indirect relationship is moderated by team goal similarity. Testing a moderated mediation model with three-wave data from 62 project teams (N = 208), we found that TTR mediates the relationship between chronotype diversity and team performance (task performance and organizational citizenship behavior). Crucially, team goal similarity moderated this process: team chronotype diversity triggered TTR primarily when team goal similarity was low. Conversely, when goal similarity was high, the positive relationship between diversity and TTR was suppressed. These findings challenge the assumption that temporal diversity is inherently disruptive, identifying TTR as a critical mechanism for managing “energetic asynchrony” and highlighting the nuanced interplay between biological rhythms and social-cognitive structures in teams.

The Role of Skill Composition and Teams in the Online Gig Economy

OB
Ozlem Akekmekci (Radboud University Nijmegen); Andrea Monika Herrmann (University of Duisburg-Essen); Anna Nadolska (Radboud University Nijmegen); Gresa Latifi (Technical University of Munich)
This study examines how width of skills and unique skill combinations of gig freelancers who form a team rather than operating solo shape performance in the online gig economy. Using data from 9,369 freelancers on a major global platform, we find that both width and uniqueness of skill combinations are positively associated with performance, and these effects are amplified for the freelancers who do operate as a team rather than solo. These findings extend theory of skill economics by demonstrating that both generalists and unique skilled freelancers can be effective in the online gig economy, especially if they operate as a team. Keywords: Skills, Informal teams, Gig economy, Platform work, Freelancers, Team vs solo performance

Toxic or Transformative? The Double Life of Derailer Composition in Team Dynamics

OB
Younsung (Youn) Cho (University of Alabama); Daniel Gregory Bachrach (University of Alabama); Peter Harms (University of Alabama); Bryan W Hochstein (University of Alabama)
Despite the prevalence of personality derailers—subclinical "dark-side" tendencies—in organizations, research has largely treated them as static "bad apples" to be screened out, leaving a critical black box regarding their collective impact on team dynamics. Moving beyond this attribute-centric view, we adopt a relational lens grounded in social exchange theory to examine how derailer configurations shape emergent team communication networks and subsequent team learning. We conceptualize derailers not as inherent deficits, but as interpersonal coping strategies—Moving Away (withdrawal), Moving Against (dominance), and Moving Toward (compliance)—that fundamentally alter the costs, benefits, and reciprocity expectations of social exchange. Analyzing three-wave longitudinal network data from 90 self-managed teams, we demonstrate that derailer effects are configurational rather than uniformly toxic. Teams high in mean Moving Away developed sparse, asymmetric communication networks characterized by low reciprocity, constraining collective learning. Conversely, teams with greater heterogeneity in Moving Against exhibited functional dominance complementarity, fostering denser and more reciprocal communication that enhanced learning outcomes. By identifying reciprocity, rather than interaction volume, as the central structural mechanism linking personality composition to team learning, this study reframes dark personality in teams as a relational and emergent phenomenon and shifts theoretical attention from "who is in the team" to "how members are organized within systems of exchange."

Influential Teams: Characteristics That Make Successful Upward Influence

OB
Alex Hoang-An Pham (State University of New York at Binghamton)
While influence is a cornerstone of organizational behavior, scholarly inquiry has predominantly focused on individual-level tactics or formal top management structures, leaving a conceptual lacuna regarding the strategic influence of teams. This paper addresses this gap by developing a comprehensive framework that identifies the team characteristics that determine a collective’s capacity for successful upward influence. Drawing upon Social Exchange Theory and Resource Dependence Theory, the four characteristics, Cohesiveness, Performance, Leader-Member Relations, and Inter-team Relations serve as the primary drivers of influence success. Future research on how to conduct empirical studies to test the linkages between teams characteristics and influence outcomes, different types of influence, and the relationship of influential teams with their organization, other teams, and their employees has also been suggested.

Guided and Self-Regulated Reflexivity in Teams: An Integrative Meta-Analytic Framework

OB
Meltem Ceri Booms (KU Leuven); Bart De Jong (Durham University); Michaela Schippers; Jean-François Harvey (HEC Montréal)
Team reflexivity, the extent to which teams review and adjust their goals, strategies, and processes, has consistently been linked to team performance. Despite substantial scholarly attention, the field remains fragmented in two key respects. First, there is insufficient theoretical integration explaining why reflexive practices contribute to enhanced performance. Second, existing research has developed along two largely disconnected research streams: self-regulated reflexivity, which occurs organically within teams, and guided reflexivity, which is deliberately structured and guided by an external agent. This separation hinders cross-learning and theoretical advancement. To address these fragmentations, we develop an integrative model of team reflexivity, proposing that cognitive, motivational, and affective emergent states mediate its effect on team performance. We test this model through a meta-analysis of 167 primary studies (12,307 teams), synthesizing evidence from both reflexivity streams. Our findings confirm the mediating role of these emergent states and reveal similar cognitive and motivational mechanisms across both reflexivity types. These results enable theoretical integration and methodological complementarity between the two streams, offering a more comprehensive yet parsimonious understanding of the reflexivity–performance relationship. By clarifying mediating mechanisms, our model also advances the programmatic theory of team reflexivity and extends previous meta-analyses.
