The Role of Vicious Cycles in the Unraveling of Platform Ecosystems

STR
Mehdi Montakhabi (University of Oxford); Christopher L. Tucci (Imperial College London)
This paper examines how platform ecosystems, despite their potential for self-reinforcing growth through network effects, scale economies, and learning, can enter “vicious cycles” when opportunistic behavior by users or complementors undermines the platform's stability. Building on theories of network effects, ecosystem governance, and collective action, we develop a framework showing how individual self-interest and coordinated collective actions—such as off-platform coordination, legal workarounds, or flexible (generic) asset usage—can slow down a virtuous cycle and occasionally reverse it. We identify key contingencies, including asset perishability, platform liquidity, and flexibility of use, that moderate these outcomes, and demonstrate how partial breakdowns (e.g., fee renegotiations, user exits) can, under the right circumstances, escalate into broader systemic unraveling. By theorizing new mechanisms that link value capture strategies to platform decline, we contribute novel insights to the platform literature and extend existing models of ecosystem failure beyond technological design flaws to include emergent, user-driven dynamics rooted in the structural and evolutionary features of platform ecosystems.

Integration of Complementary Technologies in Platform-Orchestrated Ecosystems (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Yongzhi Wang (The Ohio State University); Gwendolyn Kuo-fang Lee (Not Associated)
A platform-orchestrated ecosystem brings together multiple sides of a market, connecting users, technology providers, and software application (app) developers. App developers decide whether and how to integrate technology providers’ complementary technologies. For example, complementary technologies such as software development kits (SDKs) and application program interfaces (APIs) in the ecosystem orchestrated by Apple are integrated by app developers at different rates. We posit that the rate is higher for app developers whose products do not require much social interaction among users. This higher rate of integrating complementary technologies is amplified under three conditions: (1) when the technologies have specific purposes and are provided by the platform owner (compared with those by third-party providers); (2) when the technology providers co-specialize with the app developers in the same product category; and (3) when the app developer specializes in a single product category (compared with a generalist spanning multiple product categories). We test our theoretical framework and find support for our hypotheses in the empirical context of Apple’s iOS App Store during the time-period of 2019-2021 when the Covid-19 pandemic shifted user interaction from offline to online.

From Enabling to Constraining: Orchestration and Governance in Ecosystem Evolution

STR
Wang Pin-Yu (National Chengchi University); Bo-Ling Yen (National Cheng Kung University)
This study advances strategy research on ecosystems by examining how orchestration operates as a dynamic governance logic across different stages of ecosystem evolution. Prior research has largely conceptualized ecosystem governance as a relatively stable configuration designed to address coordination challenges under uncertainty. However, less is known about how governance logics evolve as ecosystems scale and mature. Drawing on a longitudinal, multi-case analysis of platform ecosystems, we show that orchestration practices persist over time, but their governing logic systematically shifts from enabling to constraining as ecosystems develop. Practices that initially promote openness and flexibility increasingly function to manage interdependencies, enforce coordination, and stabilize participation as complexity grows. By theorizing orchestration as a phase-dependent governance logic rather than a static design choice, this study deepens understanding of ecosystem dynamics and highlights the strategic agency of platform leaders in reconfiguring governance over time.

Collaborative Value Creation and Appropriation in Platform Ecosystems

STR
Kenan Guler (Baylor University); Doug Miller (Rutgers University)
Platform ecosystems thrive on collaborative value creation, yet little is known about how participants manage interdependent relationships across different platform settings. We investigate how actor- and platform-level heterogeneity jointly shape complementor performance by examining the effects of historical exploration and exploitation behaviors of collaborating partners. Drawing on 16,551 video game releases between 1977 and 2017 by 1,405 video game developer companies and 402 video game publisher companies across 26 platforms, we find that extensive historical exploration behaviors negatively affect their present performance. We theorize that platform characteristics moderate this relationship through uncertainty resolution: platform complexity amplifies uncertainty, strengthening the negative effect, whereas platform generativity reduces uncertainty, making the relationship positive. Our findings advance ecosystem theory and offer practical insights for managing collaborations in platform ecosystems.

Coordination and Control Without Ownership: A Theory of Governance by Design

STR
Onyeka Obasi (Baruch College, CUNY); Evan Rawley (Carlson School of Management)
The theory of the firm characterizes the boundary of the firm as an equilibrium choice between market-based contracting and hierarchical governance, assuming that contracts are costly to monitor and enforce ex post. However, when economic activity is mediated by digital infrastructure, monitoring and enforcement can be embedded directly into the technical architecture of a contract ex ante. By designing the digital architecture of a transaction, a firm can effectively control a subset of a counterparty’s actions without ownership—a strategy we call “governance by design”. In this paper, we develop the concept of governance by design, demonstrating that three architectural design properties—modularity, traceability, and programmability jointly influence the contractability of a transaction by reducing ex post monitoring and enforcement costs. We further describe how design properties give rise to architectural mechanisms: interface design, data-driven orchestration, and governance-by-protocol, which provide contractual safeguards traditionally associated with hierarchy. By identifying the architectural conditions under which contractual governance becomes viable, even under high interdependence, the framework explicates why some platform firms are able to coordinate broad portfolios of interdependent services without ownership, while others remain narrowly scoped or vertically integrated.
