Cued to Coach: Using Signals and Skill Development to Integrate Conflict Coaching and Mediation

CM
Madeleine Alder (The University of Utah); Rachael Goodwin (Syracuse University)
Conflict coaching research has been limited and is often kept separate from other dispute resolution practices. Synergies between conflict coaching and mediation suggest that integrating the two practices could benefit the development of involved parties; however, minimal research has explored this integration. Through an inductive qualitative study of semi-structured interviews of practitioners with mediation and conflict coaching experience, we discovered that emotion- and power-related cues influence when and how mediators integrate and iterate conflict coaching in mediations. We propose a model based on a hybrid mediator-conflict coach role that outlines how these practitioners move through a process of identifying conflict patterns, observing cues, and helping disputants develop skills. This work advances understanding of conflict resolution practices, extends existing literature on conflict coaching and mediation, and introduces an original approach for practitioner training and use. These findings are particularly useful in workplace mediations, where skills-focused coaching techniques can benefit employee conflict management in long-term working relationships.

The Psychological Costs and Benefits of the Dual Pathways to Social Rank

CM
Nelly Arbel-Groissman (University of Maryland College Park); Yasha Spriha (Arizona State University); Jennifer Carson Marr (University of Maryland College Park)
People pursue social rank and influence through two primary pathways: dominance (coercion, intimidation) and prestige (competence-based respect and knowledge sharing). Although both strategies can be effective for attaining influence, research has focused largely on how others react to dominant versus prestigious actors, leaving open how these pathways shape the psychological states and well-being of the actors who deploy them. Integrating dual-pathways research with Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we develop an actor-centric account of rank pursuit and propose that dominance is more likely to trigger resource loss spirals, whereas prestige is more likely to trigger resource gain spirals. Our theory suggests that because dominance requires projecting strength, monitoring threats, and enforcing compliance, and often elicits adversarial interpersonal dynamics, it erodes social resources, heightens emotional exhaustion, and ultimately reduces well-being. In contrast, prestige involves demonstrating and sharing valued expertise, which tends to elicit appreciation, respect, and voluntary deference that replenish social and emotional resources, increase work engagement, and enhance well-being. We find support for our theory in a team-based time-lagged study and an experience-sampling study. In doing so, we reframe pathways to social rank as differing not only in their effectiveness for attaining influence, but also in their viability for sustaining rank over time without compromising psychological health.

Tighter Rules, Calmer Minds: Cultural Tightness Shapes Psychological Well-Being by Subjective SES

CM
Zaijia Liu; Krishna Savani (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Psychological well-being is shaped not only by individual characteristics but also by the cultural norms that structure everyday environments. While prior research has examined personal and structural predictors of mental health, the role of workplace culture remains underexplored. Drawing on cultural tightness and social class perspectives, we examine how unit-level cultural tightness (i.e., the strength and enforcement of local norms) affects mental health as a function of subjective socioeconomic status (SES). In a field survey of frontline employees in China (N = 324), tighter unit norms predicted lower depression and burnout among individuals with lower subjective SES, but not among those with higher subjective SES. A preregistered lab-in-the field experiment (N = 209) further demonstrated that tightness increased perceived certainty among low-SES individuals but reduced perceived autonomy among high-SES individuals. Mediation analyses confirmed that certainty and autonomy explained these differential effects on well-being. Finally, using data from the World Values Survey, we examined this relationship at the population level (N = 261,837) and found that higher national cultural tightness was associated with higher subjective well-being among individuals with lower subjective socioeconomic status, but not among those with higher subjective socioeconomic status. Together, these findings suggest that cultural tightness can benefit mental health for low-SES individuals by providing certainty but constrain well-being for those with high SES by limiting autonomy, highlighting the need for SES-sensitive workplace interventions.

A Resource Conservation Process Model of Sending Workplace Gossip

CM
Matthew Nadel (St. John's University)
Despite growing interest in workplace gossip, existing scholarship lacks a sender-centered model explaining how the decision to gossip for employees connects to particular workplace targets, receivers, and outcomes. This research introduces the Impacted Resources Model, a feedback-based framework grounded in conservation of resources theory that conceptualizes sending workplace gossip as a form of resource regulation. The model treats the gossip target as a distal trigger signaling perceived threats or opportunities to valued sender resources, activating gossip consideration. It distinguishes these antecedents from decision-making factors shaping enactment, including sender traits and attitudes, gossip sensitivity, and access to trusted receivers capable of fortifying resource caravans. Drawing on recognition-primed decision-making, gossip decisions are framed as rapid appraisals of resource risk and reward, the outcomes of which recalibrate subsequent gossip behavior. Practically, the model encourages organizations to teach thoughtful gossip that benefits all actors.

Advising in the Face of Constraints: How Resource Scarcity Shapes Advice- Giving

CM
Ibitayo Fadayomi (University of Chicago); Emma Levine
Across a pilot and three studies, we examine how perceived resource constraints shape advice-giving in the face of trade-offs between short- term needs and long-term goals. We find that advisors prioritize alleviating advisees’ immediate material constraints over promoting long- term outcomes, leading to systematically different recommendations for constrained versus unconstrained individuals. In our pilot, public health professionals believed resource-constrained communities should prioritize preserving scarce resources over engaging in recommended health behaviors. Study 1 shows that working professionals are more likely to recommend short-term financial gains over long-term career advancement when advising low-income (vs. not low-income) students, driven by prescriptive beliefs about what constrained individuals should prioritize. Study 2 shows that advisors push high-income advisees towards prioritizing greater long-term gains, but they give low-income advisees recommendations that focus on short-term gains, potential preventing constrained advisees from alleviating financial constraints in the long-term. Study 3 demonstrates that constrained advisees are more impacted by differences in advice, making these differences consequential for downstream decisions. Together, these findings suggest that perceptions of scarcity bias advice toward short-term considerations, potentially reinforcing existing social and economic inequalities.
