Stuck in Oil? Industry Stigma and Barriers to Cross-Industry Career Mobility

OMT
Keehyuk Ra (University of Washington); Shinjae Won (Ewha Womans University); Richard A. Benton (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
How does employment in a stigmatized industry shape workers’ career mobility? Drawing on categorical stigma theory, we argue that affiliation with a stigmatized industry signals cultural misfit and reputational risk to prospective employers, constraining workers’ ability to transition across industry boundaries, even when they hold transferable skills. Using a large-scale data base of workers’ career histories, we examine whether workers exiting the oil and gas industry—a core-stigmatized industry—face barriers to cross-industry mobility. Our analyses reveal that previous employment in oil and gas significantly reduces the likelihood of switching industries, net of human capital, occupational, and labor market controls. Analysis leveraging the Deepwater Horizon oil spill support a stigma-based mechanism rather than worker-side preferences. We further show that the mobility penalty intensifies when public disapproval of the industry rises and when prospective employers face heightened reputational risk. Our theory and findings extend research on industry stigma to the individual level and highlight how symbolic evaluations of industries create hidden structural barriers to career mobility.

One Exemplar to Rule Them All: Stigmatizing the U.S. Blockchain Voting Category via Exemplification

OMT
Gianlorenzo Meggio (Copenhagen Business School); Agnes Radziwon (University of Southern Denmark)
This work explains how an emerging category can be rhetorically stigmatized through exemplification. We investigate this process by conducting a longitudinal case study of the U.S. blockchain voting category (2012-2024). We document how this emerging category progressed from niche applications in private elections to adoption in the 2018 U.S. presidential election. However, as the category’s prominence grew, so did scrutiny - particularly from cybersecurity experts. Drawing on archival data and interviews, we develop a process model that illustrates how rational and moral claims against a category exemplar can trigger the diffusion of category stigma to a critical mass. Particularly, we identify three mechanisms (prefigurative questioning, substantiated dismissing, inductive generalizing) that drive this process by emotionally engaging public opinion. Our findings advance understanding of category stigma formation and diffusion, which has so far identified the use of category labels as a key enabler of stigma formation, by 1) highlighting exemplification as a new rhetorical vehicle that can be used to influence an emerging category’s social evaluation, and 2) illustrating how each evaluative dimension of stigma can be elicited and interact in this process.

How a Forced Reveal Reduced Flexibility Stigma in Workplaces with Caregiver Collectives

OMT
Debra Rowcroft (Yale School of Management); Alexandra Feldberg (Harvard Business School); Julia DiBenigno (Julia DiBenigno); Shiya Wang (Columbia Business School)
A pervasive “flexibility stigma” penalizes parents for working in non-standard ways to manage caregiving responsibilities. Existing scholarship suggests that parents use a variety of individual stigma-management practices to conceal their flexible work-use. This study draws on 244 longitudinal interviews with 80 working parents (2020-2025) to develop a process model exploring whether and how parents might openly and collectively engage in flexible work, reducing the perception of the flexibility stigma within their respective workplaces. We conceptualize the childcare crisis during Covid-19 as having created a “forced stigma reveal” that illuminated the support for flexibility in workplaces with contingents of similarly-situated caregivers, or “caregiver collectives.” Newfound recognition prompted a sense of linked fate among caregivers, emboldening engagement in two main “peer stigma reduction practices” that lessened perceptions of flexibility stigma over time: (1) acknowledging caregiving responsibilities as more situational and less controllable; and (2) working together to minimize caregiving responsibilities’ disruptiveness. In contrast, participants in workplaces with few, if any, other parents were unable to pursue peer stigma reduction practices and reported engagement in individual stigma-management tactics. This study contributes to our understanding of the flexibility stigma, uncovering how and when similarly-situated peers can collectively reduce the stigma attached to flexible work.

Stigma and Its Moments: Intermediaries’ Use of Rituals with Audiences In Stigmatized Activities

OMT
Aminat Muibi (University of Victoria); Stacey Fitzsimmons (University of Victoria)
Stigma research shows that taint can spill over from stigmatized industries to organizations and individuals, often treating transfer as an inevitable consequence of affiliation. Yet we know less about what unfolds in the interactional moments where evaluative audiences encounter boundary actors. Using an eight month multi sited case study of gambling shop attendants in Lagos, Nigeria, I examine how intermediaries use interaction rituals to resist stigma transfer. Drawing on 205 hours of observations, long interviews with attendants, semi structured interviews with gamblers and community members, and field artifacts, I identify four recurring co created rituals: socialization, care, empathetic solidarity, and material reciprocity. Sustained participation in these rituals produces symbolic anchoring, through which attendants become emotionally resonant figures seen as confidants and credible voices. Symbolic anchoring elicits visible audience support, including public defense, advocacy, and alignment during moments of tension. I theorize a recursive process in which ritualized interaction, symbolic anchoring, and audience support form a semi permeable barrier that inhibits stigma transfer from the stigmatized industry to intermediaries. The study advances stigma theory by specifying micro level mechanisms of stigma resistance and extends interaction ritual theory by showing how rituals can generate new relational possibilities in stigmatized settings. Keywords: Stigma Transfer, Intermediaries, Interaction Ritual Theory, Symbolic Anchoring, Audience Support

Stigma Coupling and Decoupling: How Practice Stigma and Social Control Co-Evolve Over Time

OMT
Wesley Helms (Brock University); Alessandro Piazza (Rice University); Sofiane Baba (University of Sherbrooke)
How do stigma and the social control of practices evolve over time? While stigma has long been understood as a mechanism of social control, organizational research has rarely examined how the two co-develop. We explore this through a historical analysis of cannabis regulation in Canada—a practice that transitioned from intense criminalization to growing societal acceptance. Our findings reveal that stigma and sanctions are initially tightly coupled when directed at marginalized clienteles (user groups) but begin to separate as the practice diffuses to more socially legitimate groups. Through analysis of three regulatory periods—targeting marginalized groups (1923-1960), countercultural movements (1960-1990), and medical users (1990-2001)—we identify mechanisms that reshape the relationship between stigma and social control. We show how intensified punitive enforcement can paradoxically broaden acceptance when it reaches diverse social groups, while the emergence of legitimate clienteles enables more accommodating regulation. This study contributes to organizational theory by advancing the concept of stigma decoupling, illustrating how social control of stigmatized practices evolves from purely constraining to enabling forms. We foreground the role of social control agents—both constraining and enabling—in shaping these shifts, offering insight into how regulatory regimes adapt as stigmatized practices spread beyond their initial user bases.
