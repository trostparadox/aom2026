When Signals Saturate: How Perceptions of Victim Signaling Influence Psych Safety and Ethical Egoism

CM
Logan Steele (Boise State University); Hsuan-Che (Brad) Huang (The University of British Columbia); Karl Aquino (The University of British Columbia)
Organizations have many channels for employees to report experiences of disadvantage, mistreatment, or harm. While such mechanisms aim to foster psychological safety, they can have unintended consequences when employees perceive that victim signaling has become prevalent in their workplace. To better understand these dynamics, we extend signaling theory by examining how receiver perceptions of signaling patterns—rather than an individual’s signaling behaviors—shape employee behavior. Specifically, we introduce perceptions of victim signaling (PVS), defined as perceptions that coworkers deliberately communicate claims of harm or disadvantage in ways that invite recognition and create expectations for organizational response. Critically, we shift the analytical focus from individual signaling acts to receivers’ perceptions of signaling as an environmental characteristic. Drawing on signaling theory and research on self-preservation, we propose that when employees perceive victim signaling as prevalent, it creates interpersonal uncertainty that reduces psychological safety—the extent to which individuals perceive it is safe to express themselves honestly. This reduction in psychological safety, in turn, motivates employees to prioritize self-protection and self-advancement, leading them to default to ethical egoism. Across four studies—three pre-registered laboratory experiments and a three-wave field survey—we demonstrate that PVS reliably reduces psychological safety, which in turn predicts increased ethical egoism. This relationship is particularly pronounced among Whites compared to non-Whites, though we find no significant differences between men and women. We discuss implications for theory and practice regarding organizational signaling dynamics, psychological safety, and workplace ethics.

The Effect of Supervisor Status Loss and Support on Unethical Pro-Supervisor Behavior (WITHDRAWN)

CM
Amber Johnson (University of Maryland College Park); Yingli Deng (Durham University); Anders Friis Marstand (Durham University Business School)
Supervisor status loss occurs when supervisors lose respect and admiration in the eyes of others. While status loss typically prompts subordinates to challenge a leader, we examine how and when this event can lead to unethical protection. Building on motivated moral reasoning theory, we argue that when a supportive supervisor experiences status loss, supervisor support creates social closeness that biases employees’ moral evaluations, this relational context activates moral disengagement, which allows employees to justify unethical behavior as an appropriate way to defend or protect the supervisor. We examine a moderated mediation model in which supervisor status loss predicts moral disengagement only under high supervisor support, and moral disengagement predicts unethical pro-supervisor behavior (UPSB). We tested our predictions across three studies: an experiment where we manipulated supervisor status loss in a recalled task (Study 1), a time-lagged field study with professionals (Study 2), and another experiment where we manipulated both supervisor status loss and supervisor support (Study 3). We pre-registered Studies 2 and 3. Analyses indicate that status loss is positively related to moral disengagement for highly supportive supervisors, but that this relationship is weaker when support is low. Moral disengagement, in turn, is positively associated with UPSB, producing a significant conditional indirect effect. Our findings show that the ethical implications of supervisor status loss depend on the broader relational context, and they reveal how moral disengagement helps explain employees’ willingness to engage in unethical acts for a supportive leader whose standing has been threatened.

When Abusive Supervision Silences Creativity? Understanding the Role of Workplace Loneliness (WITHDRAWN)

CM
Ashneet Kaur (Indian School of Business); Sudhanshu Maheshwari (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Mantasha Firoz (Management Development Institute Gurgaon); Aamna Khan (Indian Institute of Technology (ISM))
Abusive supervision has become increasingly prevalent in contemporary organizations, yet its implications for employee creativity remain incompletely understood. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, this study examines how abusive supervision undermines employees’ engagement in the creative process by eroding critical socio-emotional resources. We propose workplace loneliness as a central emotional mechanism linking abusive supervision to creative process disengagement and identify psychological safety as a contextual resource that can attenuate these harmful effects. Using a two-wave, time-lagged survey design with full-time employees from diverse service sectors (Wave 1: N = 323; Wave 2: N = 216), we find that abusive supervision is positively associated with workplace loneliness, which in turn diminishes creative process engagement. Moreover, psychological safety buffers the relationship between abusive supervision and workplace loneliness, weakening the indirect negative effect of abusive supervision on creative engagement. By foregrounding workplace loneliness as a critical socio-emotional pathway and highlighting psychological safety as a protective resource, this study advances understanding of how dark leadership behaviors impair creativity at its formative stages. The findings offer important theoretical contributions to the literatures on abusive supervision, creativity, and COR theory, while providing actionable insights for fostering psychologically safe environments that sustain employee creativity under adverse leadership conditions.

Patrolling the Powerful: The Reputational Consequences of Punishing Power-Holders

CM
William Fawson (London Business School); M. Ena Inesi (London Business School)
Existing research has shown that there are greater costs involved in punishing higher-power unethical perpetrators. In contrast, we investigate why doing so can generate superior reputational rewards for punishers. Drawing on costly signaling theory, we propose that punishing higher- (versus lower-) power perpetrators functions as a costly signal, leading audiences to confer higher moral character and greater downstream rewards on punishers. We find supporting evidence across five preregistered studies (N = 3,210 participants), where individuals are conferred higher moral character when they punish higher- (versus lower-) power perpetrators, and subsequently enjoy greater financial resources, trust, partner selection, and status. Overall, our work contributes to an improved understanding of how audiences judge the morality of punishment and introduces a dynamic that may motivate greater patrolling of the powerful when they commit unethical behavior.

Bragging About Third-Party Punishment Harms Moral Reputation Less Than Bragging About Helping

CM
M. Ena Inesi (London Business School); Jonathan Berman (London Business School); William Fawson (London Business School)
Prior research has shown that bragging about helping others undermines moral reputation, because it raises suspicion that the individual is self-promoting. In the present research, we consider whether bragging about third-party punishment—in which observers impose costs on wrongdoers—elicits a less negative effect on moral reputation than bragging about helping, across five preregistered experiments (total N = 3,974). We find that bragging (versus not bragging) about third-party punishing is less diagnostic of self-promotion than bragging about helping, since punishment conveys mixed signals about an individual’s character. We also find that bragging about third-party punishing is perceived as an attempt to inform others about wrongdoings committed by unethical perpetrators, providing cover for bragging when this information is highly relevant for audiences. In contrast, remaining silent about third-party punishing elicits backlash from audiences when disclosing this information would be highly relevant. Thus, the present research advances our understanding of the effects of bragging about third-party punishing, contributing to the self-promotion and third-party punishment literatures.
