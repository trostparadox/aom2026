Who Gets Flextime Matters: Individual Consequences and Organizational Dynamics

HR
Marvin Neu (University of St. Gallen); Heike Bruch (University of St. Gallen)
This study examines how unequal access to flextime within organizations shapes employee attitudes and organizational outcomes. Integrating social identity, justice, and HR process perspectives, we introduce the concept of organizational flextime access-rank correlation, capturing the extent to which flextime access aligns with hierarchical position within organizations. Using a multisource dataset of 21,220 employees across 116 organizations, we show that strong alignment between flextime access and hierarchy weakens, and can eliminate, the positive association between flextime access and job satisfaction. At the organizational level, the same alignment undermines bonding social capital, particularly when intergroup communication is high. These findings highlight that the consequences of flextime depend not only on its availability but also on how access is distributed within organizations.

The Double-Edged Sword Effect of Empowering Leadership on Employees’ Work-Family Interface (WITHDRAWN)

HR
Honglei Wang (Northeast Agricultural University); Shuqiu Wang; Yixuan Cao
Previous research has yielded inconsistent conclusions regarding the impact of empowering leadership on employee behavior. To address this gap, the present study develops an integrative model that simultaneously examines both the beneficial and detrimental effects of empowering leadership on work-family interface. Furthermore, the study explores proactive personality as a key moderating factor that determines whether empowering leadership leads to work-family facilitation or conflict. Data were collected through a time-lagged survey of 348 Chinese employees. Path analysis and moderated mediation analysis were conducted using Mplus 7.0 to test the hypotheses. The findings indicate that empowering leadership exerts double-edged sword effects through distinct affective pathways: enhancing work-family facilitation via positive affect and increasing conflict via negative affect. Importantly, these effects are contingent upon employees’ proactive personality. Specifically, empowering leadership promotes work-family facilitation only among employees with high proactive personality, whereas it leads to work-family conflict for those with low proactive personality. Drawing on affective events theory, this study enriches our knowledge of the double-edged effect of empowerment on employee work-family outcomes, and provides new insights into when and why empowering leadership can be beneficial for employee work-family life.

Employee Side-Hustle–Primary-Job Boundary Management: A Construal Level Theory Perspective

HR
Guohua (Emily) Huang (Hong Kong Baptist University); Bingjie Yu (Shandong University); Manuel J. Vaulont (Northeastern University); Cynthia Lee (Northeastern University)
Side hustles can enhance financial security and psychological growth but also introduce attention residue and resource drain, making a theory-driven process-based account necessary for both the individuals and the organizations where more and more employees are working on side hustles alongside their primary jobs. Based on construal level theory and boundary management theory, we propose that construal level (CL) shapes side-hustle–primary-job boundary management strategies (segmentations and aggregation) with CL flexibility and the flexible work arrangement of the primary job as two moderators to influence the impact of CL on boundary strategies which lead to side-hustle to primary-job enrichment/interference and in turn individual performance and wellbeing outcomes. Qualitative data from side-hustlers in the U.S. and China provided some evidence for the different strategies in managing the side-hustle–primary-job interface and the effects we posited. We further discuss quantitative approaches to test the proposed model. Theoretical contributions to the management literature and practical implications for both the individuals and the organizations involved are discussed.

When Care Fits Culture: How Family-Friendly Practices Shape Productivity, Turnover and Profitability

HR
Saehee Sam Kang (Florida State University); Songqi Liu (Georgia State University)
Despite widespread recognition that Family-Friendly Workplace Practices (FFWP) enhance employee well-being and work attitudes, evidence on whether and how they contribute to firm profitability is inconsistent and undertheorized. Addressing these gaps, we develop and test an integrated model that explains how and when FFWP affect firm financial performance. Drawing on the dual-pathway model and contingency theory, we propose that FFWP enhance firm profitability indirectly by increasing workforce productivity and reducing voluntary turnover, and that these relationships depend on the firm’s organizational culture. Using a time-lagged, multi-source dataset with objective performance outcomes, we find that FFWP improve financial performance through higher productivity and lower turnover. Moreover, these indirect effects are strengthened in organizations with a strong clan culture, characterized by collaboration, cohesion, and concern for employee well-being, but weakened in organizations with a strong market culture that emphasizes competitiveness and results. Our findings suggest that realizing the full financial potential of family-friendly workplace practices is contingent upon a supportive clan culture, whereas a competitive market culture may undermine these benefits.

The Work-Family Cost of Stop and Go: Effects of Daily Aversive Commutes on Dual-Earner Couples

HR
Ho Kwong Kwan (China Europe International Business School); Haixiao Chen (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Katherine Rong Xin (China Europe International Business School); Li Wang; Leyuan Xie (University of Alberta)
Drawing on the work-home resources model and crossover theory, this daily study examines how and when employees’ aversive commutes impair their spouses’ work-family balance via a sequential strain process. We propose that employees’ morning and end-of-work (evening) aversive commutes increase their end-of-work fatigue, which in turn, spurs their daily work-to-family conflict (WFC). Employees’ daily WFC then crosses over to negatively impact their spouses’ daily work-family balance. In addition, employee work-family multitasking alleviates the direct effect of employee WFC on spouse work-family balance and the indirect effects of morning and end-of-work aversive commutes on spouse work-family balance. By using experience sampling method of 14 days with a total of 105 dual-earner couples in China, we found that most of our proposed hypotheses were supported. Our research findings offer theoretical and practical implications to the literature on commuting, fatigue, and work-family interfaces.
