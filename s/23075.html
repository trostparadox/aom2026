A Normative Framework for Human Rights and Neuro-Inclusion

SIM
Ren Lovegood (University of Massachusetts Boston); Harry J. Van Buren (The University of Tennessee-Chattanooga)
This paper recontextualizes neurodiversity (ND) within broader disability politics, challenging approaches that disconnect it from systemic ableism and historical diagnostic biases. We demonstrate how historical frameworks, favoring male and Western archetypes, perpetuate misdiagnosis and exclusion for women and gender-diverse individuals. Building on how the concept of person-organization fit is at least partially rooted in assumptions that link worth to productivity to neuro-normativity, the paper highlights the material realities facing ND employees. Further expanding upon the complex co-occurring disabilities in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), we employ an intersectional lens to interrogate how gender, race, sexuality, and class constrain recognition and inclusion of ND in organizations. By critiquing the instrumentalizing “business case” for ND, this work advocates for organizations to move beyond conditional belonging and dismantle epistemic ableism to prioritize inherent dignity through management practices supporting the flourishing of all employees. We propose a normative human rights framework focused on the responsibilities of society, organizations, and individuals in fostering neuro-inclusion.

Insecure Jobs, Unsafe Homes: How Job Insecurity Fuels Domestic Violence

SIM
Xinxiong Wu (University of Wollongong); Rebecca Kechen Dong (University of Technology, Sydney)
Despite recognising domestic violence as a contemporary approach to societal challenges, our understanding of what workplace factors drive an individual’s violent behaviour at home remains underdeveloped. Drawing on work–family conflict theory, this study investigates whether job insecurity increases the likelihood of domestic violence and through which mechanisms this relationship operates. Using longitudinal data from 89,865 individuals in Australia, we analyse within-person changes in job insecurity and domestic violence over time from 2007 to 2023. The findings show that heightened job insecurity is associated with a greater likelihood of domestic violence, broadly defined to include harm directed toward family members and self-directed harm. Deteriorating mental health and increased financial strain mediate this relationship. Moreover, such effects are stronger for men and individuals without higher education. By conceptualising domestic violence as a family-domain outcome of work-related strain, this study extends work–family conflict theory into an ethically salient domain. The findings highlight that job insecurity generates social costs that extend beyond the workplace, shedding light on the importance of organisational practices and labour-market policies that reduce job insecurity to promote safety and well-being in the home.

Beyond Box-Ticking: Informal Practices of Governance Accountability in Institutional Voids

SIM
Folajimi Yesir Ashiru (Coventry University); Emmanuel Adegbite (University of Nottingham); Panagiotis Andrikopoulos (Coventry University); Jacob Wood; Matthias Nnadi (Cranfield University)
This study examines how professional investors process corporate governance information in Nigerian banks. Based on 27 interviews, our paper shows that investors systematically use informal channels such as networks, dialogue, and sociocultural and political signals to validate disclosures and construct qualitative governance ratings. Our study extends the corporate governance and institutional voids literature in practice today by highlighting the significant roles of informal channel mechanisms.

Corporate Philanthropy in B2B Markets: A Double-Edged Sword with Inverted-U Impact on Sales Growth

SIM
Wenting Zhu (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Bo Peng (Birkbeck - University of London); Vangelis Souitaris (City St George's - University of London); Zhigang Shou (Wuhan University); Hongxin Teng (Chongqing University)
Despite considerable attention in corporate ethics literature to whether corporate philanthropy pays off in terms of firm performance, few scholars have explored this issue from the perspective of organizational customers’ actual purchase behaviors. Drawing on instrumental stakeholder and signaling theories, we examine how corporate philanthropy levels may affect business-to-business (B2B) sales growth and how contextual factors may moderate this relationship. An analysis of panel data from 3,215 listed firms in B2B-dominant industries indicates an inverted U-shaped relationship between the level of corporate philanthropy and sales growth. Initially, philanthropy positively relates to sales growth, but beyond a certain threshold, it begins to hinder growth. Moreover, this curvilinear relationship flattens for firms with higher customer concentration and steepens for those that donate consistently over time. Subsequent scenario-based experiments confirm that increased integrity-based trust and decreased competence-based trust among organizational customers are two countervailing mechanisms underlying these findings. By revealing the dual-edged nature of corporate philanthropy within B2B markets, our study shifts the conversation in business ethics beyond the question of “whether philanthropy pays” to a more critical examination of its nuanced effects. These findings offer actionable guidance for managers to optimize philanthropic strategy in B2B markets.
