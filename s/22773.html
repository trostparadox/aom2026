When Representation Backfires: Female Managers and Gendered Promotion Outcomes Within Firms

SIM
Anqi Gong (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Jianfu SHEN (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Eddie HUI (City University of Hong Kong)
This study examines whether women’s presence in top management teams (TMTs) affects the career progression of female employees. Using a unique dataset that combines individual professional profiles with corporate TMT composition of Chinese listed firms, we find that female representation in TMTs worsens promotion outcomes for women compared to men. The negative impact is concentrated in contexts where female managers hold lower status within the TMT, such as occupying non-key roles, being underrepresented numerically, or having limited managerial authority. Further analysis indicates these negative results are likely driven by heightened intragender competition under tokenism and self-distancing behaviors caused by identity threat. Amid global efforts for leadership diversity, our findings warn that symbolic representation alone may unintentionally reinforce existing barriers to workplace equity.

Confucian Virtue Ethics and Credit Behavior: How Cultural Frameworks Shape Financial Commitment

SIM
Shaowei Zhang (Chongqing University); Fengwen Chen; Dan Yan (Chongqing University); Wei Wang (Chongqing University)
This study investigates how Confucian ethics—a virtue-based moral tradition emphasizing integrity (Xin), self-discipline, and commitment fulfillment— influences personal credit behavior. We conceptualize loan repayment as ethical choices involving promise-keeping rather than purely economic calculations, and theorize that Confucian culture cultivates moral character through three mechanisms: financial prudence (Zhi), self-discipline (Ke-ji), and social embeddedness (Ren). Analyzing over 4 million consumption loans from China’s consumer finance market and employing geographic measures based on historical temple distributions, we find that Confucian cultural exposure is associated with significantly lower loan delinquency. This relationship remains robust across alternative specifications and instrumental variable analyses. Mechanism analyses confirm that financial prudence, self-discipline, and social embeddedness mediate the culture-credit relationship. Heterogeneity analyses reveal stronger effects among individuals with lower education, males, older cohorts, and those in regions with weaker formal credit institutions. Supplementary analysis rules out credit avoidance as an alternative explanation. This study contributes to business ethics by demonstrating that cultural moral traditions function as informal ethical governance mechanisms, illuminating the complementary relationship between informal cultural institutions and formal regulatory structures.

Managerial Emotions and Emotional Regulation After Employee Wrongdoing: A Qualitative Investigation

SIM
Shetu Ranjan Biswas (Washington State University); Lily Yuxuan Zhu (Washington State University); Ken Butterfield (Washington State University)
Employee wrongdoing is emotionally consequential for managers, yet little is known about how managers experience and regulate their own emotions in these situations. Drawing on Gross’s (1998) process model of emotion regulation and affective events theory, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 34 managers to examine their emotional and regulatory responses to workplace transgressions. Managers reported 17 emotions, with anger and frustration most common, but also surprise, empathy, guilt, and other less studied reactions, indicating that wrongdoing often elicits a variety of co-occurring emotions. We identified 13 emotion regulation strategies, with physiological intervention and cognitive reappraisal most prevalent, alongside underexplored tactics such as social support, time delay, and labeling. Managers typically used multiple strategies, demonstrating that emotion regulation in the context of employee wrongdoing is complex and multi-faceted. The study extends research on moral emotions and emotion regulation by revealing a broader range of emotions and a more diverse set of regulatory strategies than suggested by prior research. Practically, our findings highlight the value of preparing managers for a variety of emotional reactions and equipping them with diverse regulation tools to support effective, professional responses to employee wrongdoing.

Social Investments and the Commodification of Community Conflict

SIM
Alexander Rustler (University of Oxford)
This paper resolves a puzzle in research intersecting nonmarket strategy and social movements: that social investments to local communities can avoid community conflict by alleviating grievances while they can also cause conflict by stimulating opportunistic behavior to exploit firms. Based on nine months of fieldwork in Peru’s mining industry, this paper examines how one of the world’s largest mining firms sought to manage violent community conflict through social investments. The analysis shows how social investments commodified grievances and turned the community’s use of violent conflict into a calculated transaction, in exchange for social investments. Over time, violent conflict became normalized as an “everyday” activity, rendering the use of social investments to quell conflict ineffective. The findings matter for theory and practice: social investments can fundamentally transform firm-community relations, beyond reducing grievances or stimulating opportunism, in ways that can institutionalize violent community conflict.
