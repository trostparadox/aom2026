Structuring Agency for Collective Action in a Large Public-Private Partnership Project

PNP
Liumiao Qin; Giorgio Locatelli (Politecnico di Milano - School of Management); Qinghua HE
Public-private partnerships (PPPs) aim to address grand challenges that exceed the capacities of any single actor, yet many struggle to meet their initial aspirations, experience member withdrawal, or encounter escalating conflict. Although prior research highlights cooperation and coordination problems as core impediments to collective action in PPPs, little is known about how actors exercise agency to address these problems over time. To address this gap, we conducted a 12-year longitudinal case study of an urban renewal PPP project. We developed a process model that explains how collective action problems and agency co-evolve over time. Our findings show that shifts in the relative severity of cooperation and coordination problems alter both the locus of agency and its mechanisms. Our process model elucidates how alignment between the temporal orientation and locus of agency produces cumulative effects that enable collaboration to advance and allow the collective capacity of managing interdependencies in goals, resources, and tasks to develop over time. We extend research on project-based organizing and processual perspectives on organizational design by explaining how sustained collective action in large PPP projects emerges as actors continually recalibrate their agency in response to evolving collective action problems.

Hybrid Governance and Investment Efficiency in State-Owned Enterprises: A Value Creation Perspective

PNP
Feigu Zhou (University of Montevallo); Yiqian Lu (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); Heyang Sun (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); James Vardaman (The University of Memphis); James Montgomery (University of Montevallo)
State-owned enterprises (SOEs) often struggle with investment inefficiency despite their economic significance. This study examines how hybrid governance in mixed-ownership SOEs enhances investment efficiency through the integration of complementary state and non-state resources. Moving beyond agency-based monitoring explanations, we adopt a value creation perspective emphasizing resource complementarity and the integration of dual institutional logics. Using panel data from 10,577 firm-year observations of Chinese A-share listed SOEs (2008–2021), we find that non-state shareholder governance significantly improves investment efficiency by reducing overinvestment. We further identify key boundary conditions under which hybrid governance is most effective, including lower levels of marketization, monopolistic industries, local administrative control, and constrained managerial power. Our findings show that the value of hybrid governance arises primarily from resource complementarity rather than monitoring alone, as non-state shareholders contribute market-oriented capabilities that synergize with state shareholders’ policy expertise. This study advances hybrid organization and public value theories and offers practical implications for SOE reform by emphasizing governance integration over ownership diversification.

Trust Ecologies and Co-Production in Public Governance (WITHDRAWN)

PNP
Hyunseok Hwang (Ajou University); Arnold Vedlitz (Texas A&M University)
This study investigates how multiple forms of trust shape citizens’ willingness to engage in two modes of co-production (i.e., co-investment and co-participation) in the context of climate-related public infrastructure. Drawing on survey data from 1,015 residents in the Austin metropolitan region in Texas, we theorize a multilevel “trust ecology” consisting of social trust, epistemic trust, and institutional trust in government and nonprofit organizations. Fractional logit, Poisson regression, and structural equation modeling reveal that social and epistemic trust consistently increase citizens’ willingness to co-produce. Institutional trust exhibits divergent effects: trust in nonprofits strongly predicts both co-production modes, while trust in government does not. Mediation analyses show that institutional trust serves as a mechanism translating social and epistemic trust into behavioral intention for co-production. Moderation analyses demonstrate that political ideology conditions several trust–participation pathways, particularly for behavioral co-production. These findings advance co-production theory by unpacking differentiated trust dynamics and offer practical insights for designing participatory infrastructure policies in polarized governance environments.

Adaptive Governance in Public-Private Partnerships: An Ecosystem Perspective

PNP
Lily Mergaliyeva (University of Law Business School); Dongmei Cao (Nottingham Trent University)
Scholarship on Public Private Partnerships (PPPs) has created frameworks for bilateral contracts and for contractual, two-party agreements but does not have a theoretical model for multiple actors working with high levels of environmental uncertainty. To help bridge that gap, we develop a concept called PPP adaptability – the ability of partnership structures to reconfigure participants, scope, governance rules, and resource allocation as environmental conditions change. We create a typology of four different PPP configuration types that vary depending on how many different types of actors are involved and how much uncertainty exists in their environment. Then we formulate three propositions that explain how the number of different types of actors involved in a PPP impacts the need for adaptation, how adaptations support the alignment of strategy among all parties involved and enable Orchestration Capability, and how together these two mechanisms impact the overall resilience of a partnership. This framework is an extension of PPP concept from being primarily focused on bilateral agreements to provide tools for understanding public private coordination in complex industries.

Investigating the Counterproductive Effects of Shared Governance Structures on Value Creation

PNP
Francesco Di Maddaloni; Luigi Mosca (Imperial College London); Andrea Tomo (University of Naples Federico II)
In the past few years, an exponential interest in collective decision-making processes across organizational and sectoral lines has emerged. Previous work on collaborative forms of governance has overlooked the microprocesses underpinning the value creation or untapped possibilities of the shared governance structures. To explore the mechanisms leading to value creation in collaborative forms of governance, we rely on a comparative case study of two UK’s infrastructure megaprojects and their mandated collaborations at the local community level: The Tideway sanitation project and the High-speed railway (HS2). We extend the literature on collaborative forms of governance by highlighting the dark side of these forms of collaborations and their counterproductive effects. We further provide managerial and policy implications by shedding new light on the mechanisms associated with missing opportunities for value creation. Our contribution opens implications for future research on cross-sector collaborations, collaborative governance structures, and the management of projects in the public sector.
