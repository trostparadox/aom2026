Emergence of Unicorns: A Boon or a Bane for Industry Peers

ENT
Harish Kamal (Indian Institute of Management Udaipur); Deepti Mishra (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
We investigate whether private firms attaining unicorn status (i.e., the unicorn event) impacts the valuations of their public peer firms. We employ a difference-in-difference design and find that unicorn events significantly increase the valuations of industry peers versus non-peers. These effects are stronger for firms that are smaller, younger, have higher intangible assets, operate in high-tech industries, have higher growth, and have greater financial flexibility. The findings are robust to identification concerns, alternative measures, placebo test, and sub-sample analysis. Overall, our results highlight that the emergence of VC-backed unicorn firms potentially enhances the perceived legitimacy of the sector, growth expectations, and resets valuation benchmarks, thereby positively affecting the valuations of industry peers, consistent with stakeholder salience theory.

European Unicorns: The Role of Team Human Capital Functional Diversity

ENT
Jannik Nolden (Technical University Munich)
New ventures on the “path-to-unicorn” face an exceptionally scaling challenge. Such new ventures must repeatedly solve new coordination, learning, and execution problems as complexity rises to reach a valuation above USD 1 billion. While unicorns have attracted substantial practical attention from venture capital (VC) investors, academic research still lacks clarity on the factors that drive success along the “path-to-unicorn” by securing the financing and valuation growth needed to reach unicorn status and their interaction. In broader entrepreneurship research, founder team human capital and functional diversity shows a strong positive association with new venture success. Our study addresses this research gap and examines how experience and education functional founder team diversity influences the likelihood of achieving unicorn status and the interrelationship among the functional diversity effects. We build on human capital theory and argue that founder teams with higher experience and education functional diversity increases skills a founder team can leverage and increase the likelihood to achieve unicorn status. We test our hypotheses using a European dataset of 925 ventures, comprising 445 unicorn ventures and 480 matched randomly selected ventures, compiled from PitchBook and linked to 2,165 individual founders using LinkedIn profiles. Our results show that founder team human capital distribution matters for unicorn attainment. Functional diversity of education is consistently positively associated with achieving unicorn status, whereas functional diversity of work experience shows only significant effects when combined with prior founder experience. First, different forms of functional diversity carry different implications for reaching unicorn status. Second, showing that the relationship between different functional diversity dimensions is non-additive and contingent.

Firstborn Gender, Perceived Social Status, and Individual Entrepreneurship: Evidence from China

ENT
Jiayi YANG (University of Wisconsin-Madison)
We examine whether having only daughters motivates entrepreneurial entry. In Confucian patrilineal societies, sonlessness is culturally constructed as a loss of social status. We argue that entrepreneurship compensates for this loss by offering a visible, individually controllable pathway to social status. We study this question in China, a context that prohibits sex selection and has long encouraged one-child families, thereby partly reducing endogenous child-gender sorting. Using data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS, 2010-2022), a nationally representative longitudinal survey, we estimate the effect of firstborn child gender, an identification strategy that is relatively more plausibly exogenous and yields a conservative estimate of the effect of having only daughters, on parents’ entrepreneurial entry. We find that having a firstborn daughter, compared to having a firstborn son, significantly increases the likelihood of entrepreneurial entry. This effect is attenuated by individual social status, and this attenuation operates only in subsamples with strong son preference. All three effects, the baseline daughter effect, its attenuation by social status, and its conditioning by son preference, are observed exclusively among fathers, with no detectable effects for mothers. Additional analyses provide no support for alternative explanations based on female socialization or differences in opportunity costs related to financial resources, effort, or time. This research highlights the importance of cultural context in shaping the entrepreneurial effects of having only daughters.

How an Organization’s Status and Its Identification With Peers Influences Its Response to Trends

ENT
Wei Xia (Rutgers Business School); H Kevin Steensma (University of Washington); Xiaoou Bai (Georgia State University)
We apply an identity lens to examine subtleties in how an organization’s status affects how strongly it identifies with its peers and thus how it responds to investment trends that spread amongst their peers. We observe over 54,000 entries by venture capital firms into 26 technology domains that emerged between 1995 and 2022. We find that low-status venture capital firms conform with trend participating peers whom they are not affiliated with by way of prior partnerships, whereas the numbers of affiliated trend participants and trend bystanders had no influence on whether a low-status firm entered the focal investment domain. In contrast, because high-status firms identify strongly with their affiliates, they conform with their affiliated peers both when those peers are trend participants and when they are trend bystanders. Furthermore, high-status firms respond to unaffiliated trend participants in a contrarian fashion: greater entry into an investment domain by unaffiliated peers decreases the likelihood of high-status firms’ entry into that domain as they attempt to distance themselves from unaffiliated trend-participating peers.

Social Exposure, Innovator-Consumer Homophily, and Inequality: Evidence from College Peers

ENT
Josh Feng; Elias Einio; Xavier Jaravel
We investigate the importance of social networks for the direction of entrepreneurship and innovation. Using quasi-experimental variation in the gender and socioeconomic composition among college peers in Finland, we show that exposure to lower-income peers increases the likelihood that an entrepreneur founds a firm in a necessities industry without affecting entrepreneurial income. Likewise, increased exposure to female peers increases entrepreneurial activities targeting female consumers. These effects are largest among groups over-represented in innovation: men and individuals from high-income backgrounds. We assess the macro implications of this heterogeneity in an endogenous growth model and find that differences in college peer composition explain around a quarter of the observed inventor–consumer homophily by gender, inducing a significant cost-of-living disadvantage for women. These findings show that innovators’ social experiences have a causal impact on the direction of innovation, independent of financial incentives.
