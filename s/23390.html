The Interplay Between Market and Non-Market Strategy: Evidence from Corporate Political Activity

STR
John Mawdsley (HEC Paris); Anthony Kim (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
Prior research documents how non-market strategy complements market strategy and influences competitive outcomes, yet little is known about how firms adjust their non-market engagement over time and under what conditions. Examining corporate political activity (CPA) as a salient form of non-market strategy—specifically, political donations to electoral campaigns—we investigate U.S. corporate law firms’ responses to the CPA of other critical market actors: clients and rival law firms. We find that firms increase their CPA engagement in line with their clients’ CPA engagement, suggesting CPA can manifest as a relational investment. However, this effect weakens when firms share a higher number of clients with rivals, indicating diminishing returns to client-related CPA when relational investments are feasibly weaker. Conversely, firms reduce their CPA engagement when rivals increase theirs, consistent with the declining influence of CPA in an increasingly crowded political arena. Interestingly, this negative association is not significantly influenced by the degree of shared clients. Our findings underscore the contingent and dynamic nature of CPA and its embeddedness within competitive structures. By showing how client relationships and rivalry shape CPA, we advance theory on the integration of market and non-market strategies and offer implications for managing strategic interdependencies in knowledge-based professional service markets.

Corporate Political Activity as Insurance Against Social Accountability

STR
Debtanu Lahiri (Nova School of Business and Economics); Nilanjana Dutt (Bocconi University)
Corporate political activity (CPA) is a central nonmarket strategy, yet its benefits are often assessed through firm performance outcomes that yield mixed evidence. We reconceptualize CPA as institutionally contingent insurance against social accountability. Drawing on new institutional economics, we argue that CPA’s core value lies less in shaping how rules are enforced: political engagement can expand access and increase the likelihood of regulatory leniency. This insurance logic creates a moral-hazard dynamic – by lowering expected enforcement costs in socially consequential domains, CPA can weaken deterrence and increase firms’ propensity to engage in corporate social irresponsibility (CSI). We further theorize three institutional contingencies that govern when CPA is most likely to operate as accountability insurance: political homogeneity, which expands the capacity for discretionary enforcement; institutional receptivity to business influence, which increases the legitimacy and decisional weight of firm engagement; and institutional disruption, which destabilizes enforcement expectations and upends institutional salience. Analyses of U.S. firms in the 2011–2020 period are consistent with these arguments, showing a positive association between CPA and CSI that strengthens under homogeneity and receptivity and attenuates during disruption. By shifting attention from market outcomes to enforcement and accountability, this study reframes CPA as a socially consequential strategy and clarifies how institutional context conditions its effects.

Local Externalities and Lobbying Strategy

STR
Sorena Rahi; Guy Holburn (University of Western Ontario); Kartik Rao (Trent University)
This paper examines how platform firms’ lobbying strategies reflect the geography of their local economic footprint and the distribution of stakeholder pressures. We predict an inverted-U relationship between a firm’s local market concentration and lobbying intensity. We argue that platform firms will intensify lobbying of policymakers as their activity becomes more concentrated within a district, but scale back once concentration increases beyond a threshold where increased visibility and stakeholder opposition reduce the expected returns to lobbying. We also predict that firms are less likely to lobby policymakers in districts where incumbents have a larger economic footprint. We find empirical support for our predictions using contact-level lobbying data and spatial listing data on Airbnb in Toronto (2016–2017). Our findings contribute to nonmarket strategy research by showing how spatial distribution of a firm’s economic footprint influences their political strategies.

Industrial Accidents and Nonmarket Portfolio Allocation: Evidence from U.S. State Political Markets

STR
Richard Devine (DePaul University)
Industrial accidents can ignite two legitimacy crises at once: regulators weigh tougher enforcement while communities demand visible contrition. Yet most research treats corporate political activity (CPA) and corporate philanthropy (CP) in isolation, leaving unexplained why otherwise similar spills spur a surge in CPA in one jurisdiction but CP in another. We theorize post accident responses as a portfolio allocation problem between two currencies of influence— CPA that buys access and CP that buys moral credit subject to institutional conditions. Building on political market logic and institutional pluralism, we distinguish political access price conditions (that alter the cost of access) from social receptivity efficacy conditions (that alter the legitimacy yield of public actions). We test the model with a panel linking more than 17,000 environmental accidents by large U.S. firms to jurisdiction specific CPA and CP. Both CPA and CP rise with accident salience and then fall, producing an inverted U-shaped relationship. Price conditions systematically lift and steepen the accident– CPA curve and lower and flatten the accident–CP curve. Efficacy conditions operate in the opposite direction by increasing the credibility of philanthropic remediation and reducing the incremental payoff to additional access. This study reframes nonmarket behavior as state contingent portfolio allocation, links macro institutional conditions to firm level spending, and identifies policy levers that can redirect resources from back channel politics toward visible public goods after industrial harm.

How Boards Decide to Lobby: Cognitive Microfoundations of Corporate Political Activity

STR
Philipp Benedikt Becker (Utrecht University); Coen Rigtering (Utrecht University); Patricia Klopf (Rotterdam School of Management)
Corporate lobbying expenditures vary substantially across firms, even within identical regulatory and industry contexts, suggesting that lobbying is shaped by more than objective regulatory exposure. We develop a cognitive perspective on corporate political activity that examines this variation by focusing on how boards of directors interpret and prioritize regulatory issues and thus lobbying. We theorize two cognitive microfoundations that shape board-level evaluations of lobbying as a strategic response: issue matter salience and regulatory domain familiarity. Issue matter salience reflects a bottom-up attentional process through which directors’ exposure to regulatory issues in interlocked firms elevates the perceived importance of prospective regulatory intervention. Regulatory domain familiarity reflects a top-down, schema-driven understanding of political and regulatory processes derived from directors’ prior exposure to lobbying. We further argue that stakeholder mobilization conditions how these cognitive mechanisms translate into lobbying expenditures by shaping how directors evaluate the strategic value of lobbying. By elevating external pressure and public scrutiny on a focal firm, stakeholder mobilization amplifies issue matter salience, increasing the likelihood that directors construe the focal issue matter as consequential, while tempering the influence of regulatory domain familiarity as informed directors reevaluate lobbying in highly mobilized environments. Using panel data on 3,398 firm-year observations of S&P 500 firms and focusing on environmental and social issues, we find support for the hypothesized relationships that is robust across multiple operationalizations of our key constructs. These findings highlight the role of board cognition in shaping corporate lobbying and advance a microfoundational understanding of variation in corporate political activity.
