Ceo Temporal Attention And Commitment Misalignment Under Institutional Complexity

STR
Diptikanta Panigrahi (University of Kansas); Sumanta Singha (Texas Tech University); Chang Hoon Oh (University of Kansas)
Why do firms frequently announce ambitious sustainability commitments yet struggle to deliver them? Drawing on the attention-based view (ABV) of the firm, this study conceptualizes commitment misalignment as a temporal gap between symbolic sustainability commitments and substantive execution that arises from trade-offs in executive attention. We argue that CEOs with a strong future-focused temporal orientation are more likely to generate misalignment because their emphasis on long-horizon aspirations expands symbolic commitments more rapidly than organizations can sustain the executional attention embedded in routines and short-cycle performance systems. We further theorize that stakeholder pressure and environmental dynamism amplify this tendency by increasing the salience of future-oriented expectations and fragmenting organizational attention across competing temporal demands. Once misalignment arises, organizations tend to engage in selective implementation, advancing only a subset of their initiatives. Using a multi-source longitudinal panel of large U.S. publicly traded firms, we find that CEO future focus is positively associated with commitment misalignment, particularly under high stakeholder pressure and environmental dynamism. In turn, misalignment reduces climate innovation intensity but increases innovation impact. These findings show that commitment misalignment reflects limits of organizational attention rather than simple symbolic decoupling and help explain why visionary sustainability commitments yield divergent innovation outcomes.

CEO Human Capital and Strategy by Doing: Exploring the Moderating Role of Financial Slack

STR
Jonathan Clift (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Marketa Rickley (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Moses Acquaah (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Mark Mallon (Florida Atlantic University)
Despite the increasing importance of strategic experimentation and learning through action on firm performance, CEOs’ influence on strategy through experimentation, rather than detailed planning, remains understudied. This paper integrates human capital theory with the strategy by doing perspective to examine how CEOs’ accumulated experiences shape organizational engagement in action-based strategy. Building on human capital theory, the paper distinguishes between CEOs’ general human capital, developed through diverse experiences, and firm-specific human capital, accumulated through tenure. The paper posits that general human capital enhances CEOs’ ability to adapt quickly and learn from experimentation, while firm-specific human capital constrains experimentation. The paper further examines financial slack as a boundary condition. Using a sample of S&P 1500 firms from 1992–2022, the findings show that CEOs’ general human capital is positively associated with strategy by doing, while firm-specific human capital is negatively related. Financial slack does not amplify the effect of general human capital; instead, it strengthens the negative influence of firm-specific human capital. These findings highlight the contingent role of resources in shaping strategic action and have significant implications for board decisions. The research advances understanding of the direct and conditional influence of CEO human capital on strategy by doing.

The U-Shaped Imprint: Military Service Intensity and CEOs’ Strategic Attention

STR
Zhenhua Li (Oregon State University); Ang Zhang (University of Cincinnati); Jeffrey Barden (Oregon State University); Jonathan D. Arthurs (Oregon State University)
How do formative institutional experiences shape the way top executives allocate their attention? Integrating the attention-based view with imprinting theory, we examine how CEOs’ military service influences the structure of executive attention across competing strategic domains. We theorize that military experience imprints durable cognitive filters that bias habitual attention allocation, but that the strength and direction of this imprint vary in nonlinear shape with service intensity. Specifically, we propose a U-shaped relationship between military service intensity and CEOs’ self-centered attention, such that moderate exposure broadens outward attention, whereas both low and high intensity reinforce inward focus through distinct imprinting mechanisms. We test these arguments using a large panel of U.S. public firms, combining biographical data on CEOs’ military backgrounds with text analysis of MD&A disclosures to measure relative attention to self-centered, competitor-centered, and market-driven domains. Employing fractional, compositional, and nonlinear modeling approaches, we find evidence consistent with the proposed non-monotonic imprinting effects. By conceptualizing executive attention as a bounded, compositional structure and demonstrating nonlinear imprint intensity effects, this study advances understanding of how formative institutions shape executive cognition and strategic attention allocation.

Different Peers, Different Moves: How CEO Machiavellianism and Narcissism Translate Shortfalls

STR
Kyeong-Seop (KS) Choi (Florida State University); Jianan Li (Towson University); Eric W. K. Tsang (The University of Texas at Dallas)
Why do similar performance shortfalls trigger acquisitions in some firms but not others? This study argues that performance feedback depends on how peers are defined and which executive traits govern attention to those comparisons. We conceptualize peer construction as an upstream, trait-tilted choice and examine how CEO narcissism and Machiavellianism interact with alternative peer frames and aspiration benchmarks. Using U.S. acquisition data and Cox hazard models, we compare administratively visible industry peers (SIC) with product-market peers derived from text-based networks (TNIC). Results show that narcissistic CEOs respond to shortfalls relative to SIC leaders, while Machiavellian CEOs respond to proximity to TNIC leaders. Edge-based aspiration thresholds further reveal that leader-oriented comparisons, not average standing, drive acquisition behavior. The findings reposition peer framing as a behavioral mechanism linking executive traits to strategic action.

Information Security, Firm Capabilities and CEO Regulatory Focus

STR
Joseph Simpson (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Karen Schnatterly (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Max Braun (LMU Munich School of Management)
Cybersecurity breaches impose substantial costs on firms, yet a paradox persists: organizations with apparent cyber-capabilities often experience breaches. We argue this reflects heterogeneity in cybersecurity approaches, whether firms emphasize threat assessment or prevention, and whether CEO regulatory focus implies distinct motivations for capability deployment. Developing novel measures of information security capabilities, we examine how prevention-focused versus promotion-focused CEOs shape both capability development and breach outcomes across 67,627 firm-year observations of publicly listed U.S. firms. Prevention-focused CEOs champion threat-focused and prevention-oriented security capabilities, whereas promotion-focused CEOs view cybersecurity as a lower priority. Critically, prevention focus moderates the protective returns of both capability types, functioning as a development and deployment mechanism that determines whether technical capabilities translate into reduced breach risk. Our findings integrate upper echelons theory and regulatory focus theory, revealing that cybersecurity protection depends fundamentally on the coherence between leadership motivation and capability development. This boundary condition highlights that the CEO's prevention focus is particularly important for typically invisible outcomes and in highly regulated domains.
