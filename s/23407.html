Unlearning Business: Critical, Postcolonial, and Radical Challenges to the MBA

Moderator: Khaled Shukran

Session overview

What if the very frameworks we teach in business schools reproduce the problems we claim to solve? This session brings together critical, postcolonial, and psychoanalytic perspectives to challenge the ideological foundations of management education: from the epistemic managerialism embedded in green MBA curricula, to how marketization is degrading Brazilian and Global South higher education, to the Lacanian libidinal structures that make change so resistant in academia. Rounding out the session is a provocative proposal to democratize the management classroom through deliberative mini-publics. Challenging, theoretically daring, and impossible to ignore.

Decolonising the Green MBA: Can Business Schools Unlearn Managerialism to Teach Climate Justice?

MED
Khaled Shukran; Marleth Judith Morales Marenco (University of Alabama); `Farhana Rahman (Putra Business School)
Through the lens of epistemic unlearning, this article establishes a conceptual framework for the reorientation of sustainability education in business institutions. The paper critiques how sustainability discourse frequently perpetuates epistemic managerialism, prioritizing calculative logic, depoliticized frameworks, and managerial detachment, drawing on Critical Management Studies (CMS) and Postcolonial Theory. In response, it introduces four interrelated constructs: decolonial pedagogy, epistemic managerialism, climate justice, and eco-reflexivity. Recognition, Reflexive Disruption, and Reworlding are operationalized within a recursive learning model that transcends knowledge accumulation and advances toward ontological transformation. The article makes three significant contributions: (i) it expands CMS critique to encompass epistemological terrains, (ii) it suggests reworlding as a framework for ethical and ontological reconstruction, and (iii) it provides eco-reflexivity as a basis for relational learning. The discussion includes the challenges of resistance and co-optation, as well as the implications for curriculum design, faculty development, and institutional governance. This work contributes to the ongoing discussions regarding the decolonization of management education and the justice dimensions of climate pedagogy by presenting the business school as a potential location for epistemic humility and ecological consciousness.

Management “Education” Practices: Degradation of Brazilian Higher Education & Global South Parallels

MED
Brauer Brayan Koren (Universidade Positivo); Marcello Romani-Dias (Fundação Getulio Vargas); Gabriela Fracasso Moraes (Positivo University); Aline dos Santos Barbosa (Fundação Getulio Vargas); Joao Lins Pereira Filho; Fernando Eduardo Kerschbaumer; Danielle Denes Dos Santos (Pontifical Catholic University of Parana)
The objective of this study is to examine the role of privatisation, market concentration, and destructive competition in the degradation of education within Brazilian higher education institutions. Drawing on the experiences of university managers occupying high-level positions, the study analyses how management “education” practices have displaced educational purposes in favour of corporate and market-oriented logics. The research is based on interviews with 42 rectors and academic directors operating in Brazil. Data were analysed through thematic analysis, organised around three categories: privatisation, market concentration, and destructive competition. The findings reveal an educational decline associated with the expansion of low-cost programmes characterised by fragile infrastructure, limited academic oversight, and reduced faculty engagement. Privatisation has intensified the commodification of education, while market concentration has reinforced homogenised offerings and constrained institutional diversity. Destructive competition has encouraged cost-cutting strategies that prioritise scale and efficiency over pedagogical quality. These dynamics have produced impoverished educational trajectories marked by content standardisation, instrumental curricula, and the precarisation of academic work. The study argues that management increasingly governs education as a commodity rather than as a substantive educational practice. Situating the Brazilian case within broader Global South dynamics, the article also advances critical implications and recommendations for management education.

Confronting Ideological Containment: Adding Value Flow Analysis to Strategic Management Pedagogy

MED
Brent D. Beal (The University of Texas at Tyler)
Strategic management pedagogy trains students to identify competitive advantage, create value, and capture it—but offers no vocabulary for tracing where that value goes once captured, or from whom it was taken. This paper argues that the absence of allocative reasoning in strategy education produces a systematic blind spot: students learn to conflate profit with value creation, treating firm success as evidence of social contribution regardless of whether surplus was genuinely created or merely transferred from workers, consumers, or the public. The paper introduces value flow analysis as a conceptual and pedagogical framework for making these dynamics visible. Value flow analysis does not replace existing strategic tools; it reframes them, enabling students to distinguish between strategies that expand total surplus and those that redistribute it. The paper further argues that this curricular silence is not incidental but reflects a deeper logic of ideological containment—a pedagogical structure that normalizes advantage without interrogating its origins or consequences. Drawing on canonical frameworks from Porter, Barney, and stakeholder theory, the paper demonstrates how value flow analysis can be integrated into existing course structures, equipping students to see strategy not only as a competitive exercise but as an allocative act with systemic implications.

The Challenges Facing Management Education and Scholarship: A Lacanian Psychoanalytical Analysis

MED
Silvia Cinque (University of Liverpool); Kenneth Starkey (University of Nottingham)
In this paper we argue that the challenges facing management education and scholarship cannot be addressed through critique alone. We adopt a Lacanian psychoanalytic perspective to examine their discursive and libidinal nature, and claim that change much depends on how academics, as subjects, relate to power, knowledge and desire in the socio-symbolic structures. Once presenting Lacan’s main constructs, we particularly focus on the four discourses (master, university, hysteric and analyst), and employ them to discuss management pedagogy and scholarship. We suggest that in a world dominated by the self- reinforcing discourses of the master and the university, it is in the discourses and the hysteric and analyst that we may find alternative voices that liberate rather than bind our thinking and our imagination, while losing tights with imaginary identifications shaping the academic ego and establishing spaces for symbolic determination.

Democracy in the Classroom: Reimagining Management Learning Through Deliberative Mini-Publics

MED
AKM Mohsin (University of Applied Sciences Upper Austria); Markus Gerschberger (University of Applied Sciences Upper Austria); Zach G. Zacharia (Lehigh University); Michael Plasch (University of Applied Sciences Upper Austria)
Democratic engagement has been widely recognized as essential for preparing managers to address the complex and contested challenges that organizations and societies face. However, research on management learning has largely emphasized instructor led pedagogies, rather than how democratic capacities may be developed collectively, and has tended to treat learning outcomes as stable, rather than understanding how democratic engagement can be learned through practice. Against this background, we explore how management learning can be organized around deliberative processes. We conceptualize Deliberative Management Learning as a collective capacity through which learners engage in shared reasoning and judgment. Ongoing interaction among perspectives plays a central role in developing this capacity. To further theorize how Deliberative Management Learning may be cultivated, we draw on insights from deliberative democratic theory and learning theory. We detail the learning processes that connect individual reflection, group deliberation, and classroom practices, and outline design principles for learning interventions aimed at fostering deliberative learning. We contribute to the management learning literature by emphasizing the role of collective deliberation in developing democratic capabilities and by helping to explain how educational settings can support shared judgment and responsibility. We illustrate our framework using a graduate management course organized around deliberative classroom practices.
