Lending a Hand via AI: Generative AI-Facilitated Helping, Moral Impostorism, and Ethical Passivity

OB
Puchu Zhao (Xiamen University); Xiangxiang Li (Sun Yat-Sen University); Yan Lin (Xiamen University)
Generative AI (GenAI) is rapidly proliferating in the modern workplace. Beyond completing in-role work tasks, employees also increasingly use these tools to engage in extra-role helping behaviors toward others—a phenomenon we term GenAI-facilitated helping. Although this approach substantially reduces the personal costs of helping while promoting the benefits to recipients, we propose that it may carry unintended ethical costs for the helpers themselves. Drawing on the theory of the moralization of effort, we propose that GenAI-facilitated helping triggers moral impostorism—a psychological state in which employees believe they are not as moral as they appear to be—which in turn promotes their ethical passivity, manifesting as ethical silence and unethical tolerance. Furthermore, this effect is amplified among employees high in conscientiousness. Results from two preregistered experiments and one multi-wave longitudinal survey support our theoretical model. This research advances the integration of the AI and helping behavior literatures, extends impostor phenomenon research into the moral domain, and contributes to our understanding of the ethical consequences of workplace AI use and the dark side of conscientiousness.

Human-Human v.s. Human-AI Collaboration: The Double-Edged Sword Effect of Moral Disengagement

OB
Yi Zhang (Zayed University); Juan Du (Shanghai International Studies University); Xinyue Lin (Zhejiang Sci-Tech University); Jiani Yin (Shanghai International Studies University)
The widespread use of artificial intelligence (AI) is changing teamwork in organizations, shifting collaboration from human–human to human–AI arrangements. Although task failure is common in human–AI collaboration, its effects on employees’ psychological states and behaviors remain underexplored. Drawing on social cognitive theory, this study examines the double-edged effects of moral disengagement following task failure in human–AI collaboration. Across three scenario-based experiments and one field survey, we find that task failure in human–AI collaboration is more likely to trigger employees’ moral disengagement than failure in human–human collaboration. Moral disengagement reduces employees’ negative affect and enhances work-related well-being, with leaders’ emotional support strengthening this effect. However, moral disengagement also suppresses employees’ job reflective learning and lowers task performance, with leaders’ instrumental support weakening this effect. By focusing on task failure in human–AI collaboration as a critical context, this study reveals the double-edged nature of moral disengagement and offers insights into leadership interventions following failure.

Be Your Opportunity or Trap? A Study on the Causes and Consequences of Seeking Help from GAI

OB
Jinyu Liu (Southwest Jiaotong University); Yan Peng (Southwest Jiaotong University)
As generative AI (GAI) becomes increasingly embedded in organizational workflows, employees frequently turn to it for assistance. However, the motivational foundations and behavioral consequences of such help-seeking remain insufficiently understood. Drawing on self-determination theory, we distinguish between autonomous and dependent help-seeking from GAI. We examine how AI awareness shapes these two forms of help-seeking, as well as their downstream effects on workplace behavior. We tested our theoretical model using a multi-method approach, including a three-wave field survey of full-time employees (N = 360) and three scenario-based experiments (N = 570). Results across studies indicate that AI awareness increases autonomous help-seeking from GAI while reducing dependent help-seeking from GAI; notably, these effects are attenuated by employees’ learning goal orientation. Furthermore, autonomous help-seeking from GAI promotes proactive behavior via enhanced role breadth self-efficacy, whereas dependent help-seeking from GAI increases time theft through heightened GAI dependency. This research advances the help-seeking and AI-at-work literatures by distinguishing between distinct forms of help-seeking from GAI and revealing their divergent motivational pathways and behavioral outcomes.
