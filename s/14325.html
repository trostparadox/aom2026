Enacted Leadership Structures in Long-Term Teams: Taking it to 11

Session overview

Research on leadership in teams has increasingly emphasized shared and distributed forms of influence, yet much of this work continues to privilege single leadership sources or role-based models. As a result, leadership is often conceptualized as residing in particular individuals rather than as an emergent, interactional process. Drawing on relational leadership theory (Uhl-Bien, 2006) and the Direction–Alignment–Commitment (DAC) framework (Drath et al., 2008), this qualitative multi-case study examines how leadership is enacted and understood in long-term, self-managing teams. Five local rock bands were selected as naturally occurring teams characterized by high interdependence, minimal formal hierarchy, and sustained performance demands. Data were collected through interviews, observations, and social media analysis and analyzed using deductive content analysis focused on leadership beliefs, practices, and outcomes. Findings show that leadership is enacted through dynamic, multi-source structures that vary across domains and time horizons. Direction emerged through shifting leadership configurations, alignment developed primarily through implicit role filling and collective negotiation, and commitment was sustained through relational and intrinsic motivations rather than formal authority. In contrast, team members’ leadership beliefs were more individual-centered, with participants often describing leadership as residing in visible or hierarchical figures such as founders or front persons. Overall, the study demonstrates that leadership in long-term teams functions as patterned interaction rather than a stable role structure, and highlights a divergence between how leadership is conceptualized and how it is enacted in practice.
