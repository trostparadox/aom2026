Linguistic Inclusivity in MNCs: The Role of Translanguaging and Communication Accommodation (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Mai Al-Naemi (Qatar University); Hyun-Jung (HJ) Lee (The London School of Economics & Political Science); Carol Wasbauer Reade (San Jose State University)
While there has been extensive discussion on lingua franca policies, their benefits, and challenges, less attention has been given to how local conditions shape their practice. Adopting a practice lens, we examine how a firm’s language policies and practices affect linguistic inclusivity. Our research was conducted within a Gulf-based multinational corporation where various native languages are spoken, with English serving as the official language. In Study 1, through participant observation of naturally occurring conversations, we identified Gulf-specific translanguaging practices and developed a two-dimensional typology. In Study 2, through additional observations and interviews with employees from different language backgrounds and varying levels of English proficiency, we explored how employees strategically use translanguaging to adapt communication and foster inclusivity. Our findings challenge the reliance on a single standard language as a lingua franca in linguistically diverse workplaces and highlight the importance of practice-informed language strategies to improve communication and inclusiveness.

Host-Country Nationals at the Strategic Apex: A Microfoundational View on Internationalization

IM
Dorota Piaskowska (University College Dublin); Jonas F. Rudsinske (Georg August University Göttingen)
Despite their unique abilities and positional advantages, host-country nationals (HCNs) at the strategic apex are surprisingly understudied in international business research. To address this gap, we develop a microfoundational explanation of their role in firms’ internationalization in specific host countries. We argue that HCNs shape their firms’ host-country specific foreign sales through joint effects of ability and inclination. We also consider the effects of host-country nationality intersecting with gender, and how HCNs’ influence on their firms’ internationalization is shaped by institutional quality and gender-discriminating institutions in the host country. Using a sample of 29,126 observations capturing country-specific foreign sales and HCNs at the apices of 3,432 firms in 147 host countries, we find that HCNs positively affect firms’ host-country specific foreign sales, especially in low- quality institutional environments. However, female HCNs’ influence weakens in countries with gender-discriminating institutions.

Everyday Nationalism at Work: Identity Threats and Reconciliation Among Chinese Employees in MNCs

IM
Lin Chen (Aalto University)
Multinational corporations (MNCs) are increasingly shaped by rising nationalism, populism, and geopolitical tensions. Yet, international business research has paid limited attention to how these macro-level dynamics are experienced and negotiated at the micro level. Drawing on 43 in-depth interviews with Chinese employees working in Western MNCs, this study examines how individuals perceive and respond to identity threats arising from the changing geopolitical relationship between China and the West. We analyse how everyday work interactions with Western and local colleagues are experienced as personal, professional, and cultural identity threats, and how employees engage in identity work to cope with and respond to these challenges. Our findings reveal a range of identity work strategies that reflect forms of banal and cultural nationalism embedded in daily organisational life and behaviour. By illuminating how geopolitics infiltrates workplace interactions and shapes identity processes, this paper advances research on identity, nationalism, and micro-level resistance in MNCs and contributes to a more socio- and geopolitically grounded understanding of contemporary MNCs and international business.

Host-Country Nationals’ Perceptions of Global Employees: A Stereotype Content Perspective

IM
Luisa Ferreira Pinto (University of Porto); Vilmante Kumpikaite-Valiuniene (Kaunas University of Technology); Jurga Duobiene (Kaunas University of Technology); Vaiva Pašakinskiene (Kaunas University of Technology); Kestutis Duoba (Kaunas University of Technology); Maike Andresen (Otto-Friedrich University Bamberg)
As workforces become increasingly internationalised, host-country nationals’ perceptions of foreign colleagues play a central role in shaping workplace relations and inclusion. Drawing on the Stereotype Content Model (SCM), this study examines how host-country nationals perceive different groups of global employees in the workplace. Focusing on Lithuania – a post-Soviet society that became a European Union (EU) member state and has recently transitioned from emigration to immigration – the study situates workplace perceptions within a context of growing labour mobility and limited historical diversity. Using survey data from 532 Lithuanian employees across diverse organisational settings, respondents evaluated four salient groups of global employees: Europeans from the EU; Slavs from outside the EU; Central Asians; and Africans/South Asians. The findings support the core assumptions of SCM, identifying three stereotype clusters based on perceived warmth and competence, with EU nationals as the most positively evaluated reference group. Perceived status, rather than competition, predicted warmth and competence perceptions. Stereotype content was significantly associated with attitudes, with warmth showing a stronger relationship than competence. By extending SCM to global employees in a professional work context and a post-Soviet, emerging immigration setting, this study advances theory on intergroup relations at work and informs diversity and inclusion practices in internationally diverse organisations.
