A Multi-Level Analysis of the Digital Entrepreneurial Ecosystems in Saudi Arabia

TIM
Haifa Mansour Alsughayer (Al-Imam Mohammad Ibn Saud Islamic University)
Digital entrepreneurial ecosystems (DEEs) represent complex, multi-actor systems where digital technologies, institutional frameworks, and entrepreneurial practices co-evolve to shape startup success within specific regional and global contexts. While DEEs have gained increasing attention in entrepreneurship and innovation research, most studies have remained focused on regional structures, overlooking the interplay between firm-level practices and systemic support. Addressing this gap, this study applies the Multi-Level Perspective (MLP), a socio-technical transitions framework, to analyse the DEE in Saudi Arabia, a digitally transforming economy influenced by both national visions and global pressures. Drawing on twenty-one semi-structured interviews with digital entrepreneurs and DEE experts, the study explores how firm-level innovation and regional structures evolve under the influence of external disruptions such as AI adoption, global governance standards, and the COVID-19 pandemic. The findings identify six key dimensions: digital infrastructure, talent, cybersecurity, funding, scalability, and user engagement, as central to the ecosystem’s responsiveness and sustainability. The study develops the Multi-Level Adaptive DEE Model (MLA-DEE) to illustrate the interconnected dynamics across micro (firm), meso (regional), and macro (global) levels, revealing patterns of alignment and friction that influence ecosystem adaptability. Theoretically, the research extends DEE literature by embedding the MLP within the study of digital entrepreneurship, offering a multi-level analytical model for understanding how regional ecosystems interact with global and entrepreneurial pressures. Practically, the study highlights structural mismatches, such as limited early-stage support, talent-policy misalignment, and slow regulatory responsiveness, and offers guidance for policymakers to design a more adaptive, inclusive, and context-sensitive digital ecosystem.

AI for the People: Scaffolding for Developing High-Resource Ecosystems in Resource-Scarce Contexts

TIM
Ayodeji Daramola (Nova School of Business and Economics); Leid Zejnilovic (Nova School of Business and Economics)
How do organizations build and sustain high-tech ecosystems in contexts of general scarcity? While existing research explains how intermediaries fill "institutional voids" in existing markets, it offers less insight into how actors construct functional high-skill fields in resource-scarce contexts. Through a longitudinal inductive study of Data Science Nigeria (DSN), we develop a process model of deliberate ecosystem scaffolding. We show that DSN did not merely scale a training program but employed scaffolding as an ex-ante strategy to bridge the gap between local potential and global opportunity. Unlike prior theories of scaffolding that rely on concealment to fix social norms, DSN erected a "conversion scaffold" designed for hyper-legibility, using corporate-grade governance and elite culture to manufacture the institutional trust required by global markets. We further show how the success of this scaffold generates "generative competition," necessitating continuous organizational recompositing to survive. We contribute to research on capability building and ecosystem work by theorizing scaffolding not as a repair mechanism, but as the primary architectural strategy for creating value in the world's most constrained environments.

From Displacement to Expansion: Innovation and Employment Dynamics in the Low-Altitude Economy

TIM
Shunru Chen (Jinan University); Liming Xu (University of Cambridge); Feng Tao (Jinan University)
Purpose: How does innovation reshape employment in emerging industries where technologies, organizational forms, and value chains are evolving? While prior research focuses on mature industries using aggregate indicators, little is known about how patent-based innovation affects labor outcomes in newly forming sectors. We address this gap by examining innovation–employment dynamics at the industry–firm interface. Design/methodology/approach: Using China’s low-altitude economy as a representative case, we construct a firm-level dataset linking patenting to employment. We identify industry-specific innovation through text-based patent classification and analyze how innovation influences employment and organizational restructuring, with attention to supply-chain positioning. Findings: We uncover a robust J-curve between patent-based innovation and employment. Innovation initially reduces employment through automation, but sustained patent accumulation and industrial scaling generate employment growth. This growth does not stem from uniform internal expansion; instead, innovation reallocates employment along the value chain through organizational reconfiguration and the shifting of high-skill functions toward upstream or specialized external actors. Originality/value: This study introduces a new paradigm for innovation–employment dynamics in emerging industries. It develops a J-curve labor framework, offers one of the first LLM-based approaches to patent identification, and provides the first firm-level evidence on employment in the low-altitude economy.

Multi-Dimensional Industry AI Penetration and Firm Network Advantage

TIM
Zhi Yang; Ruichen Zhang (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); ZEYU CHEN (Huazhong University of Science and Technology)
The explosive proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies challenges the persistence of traditional network brokerage advantage. Current literature is characterized by a theoretical tension between views predicting technology-enabled decentralization and those arguing for assets complementarity and centralization. This study seeks to clarify the how industry AI penetration reshapes the relationship between brokerage and firm performance. We develop an integrative framework that deconstructs industry AI penetration into two distinct dimensions: industry AI patent penetration and industry AI employee penetration. Leveraging a comprehensive dataset of patents and job postings from Chinese listed firms (2011–2023), our empirical analysis demonstrates that the impact of AI industry penetration on brokerage advantages varies fundamentally based on the attributes of the factors involved. In particular, industry AI patent penetration signifies the standardized diffusion of explicit knowledge and significantly attenuates brokerage advantages. Conversely, industry AI employee penetration reflects a reliance on scarce tacit knowledge or skill and significantly augments these advantages. Additionally, we also identify key moderating roles of small-worldness: high firm local clustering effectively buffers the erosion of advantages due to industry AI patent penetration, whereas short network path lengths further magnify the gains from industry AI employee penetration. By distinguishing the patterns of industry AI penetration, this study reconciles prevailing theoretical paradoxes and provides strategic insights for firms navigating network competition during the AI-driven digital transformation.

Re-Examining the Role of Dominant Design in Digital Industry Emergence

TIM
Yining Luo (Zhejiang University); Shaker A. Zahra (University of Minnesota)
The extant literature assumes that dominant designs consolidate industries through shake-out effects, yet how these designs emerge and drive industry formation remains underexplored. Drawing on a longitudinal case study of China’s cloud computing industry, we identify a dynamic, two-layered process: technological design dominance emerges first, followed by business model dominance. These feedback loops between the demand and supply sides act as transitional mechanisms that enable technological diffusion and catalyze broader industry formation. Our findings highlight the role of dominant design as a mechanism of industry transition rather than a signal of consolidation, contributing to the literature on industry emergence by clarifying how stage transitions unfold. Furthermore, we extend dominant design theory in the digital age by uncovering its layered emergence dynamics and incorporating business model considerations.
