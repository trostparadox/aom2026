The Normative Core of Sustainable Action: A Dual-Pathway Model Integrating Transformational Leadersh

ONE
HerHer Tsai (Hsing Wu University); Kent Hsieh
Organizational sustainability primarily depends on employees' voluntary workplace green behavior (VWGB). Among these behaviors, dietary choices— particularly low-meat behavior—represent a critical yet underexplored dimension of workplace sustainability, given that food systems account for approximately one-third of global greenhouse gas emissions. However, a major gap persists in leadership theory regarding the mechanisms that convert abstract environmental values into sustained, self-regulated action, especially for behaviors requiring daily commitment such as dietary modifications. By integrating transformational leadership theory with the value–belief–norm (VBN) framework, we propose and test a dual-pathway model to clarify how leaders shape employees’ VWGB in the absence of formal compliance. This dual-pathway model comprises an affective route through passion and a value-driven normative route through the VBN framework. In this study, this model was comprehensively evaluated through a multilevel path analysis on multiwave survey data collected from employees and department managers across diverse organizations in Taiwan. This robust multiwave design was found to substantially mitigate common method bias and establish a strong basis for causal inferences. By integrating the VBN framework into transformational leadership theory, this study transcends instrumental explanations to reveal the internalized moral pathways that underpin sustained VWGB. Our findings provide key actionable guidance for senior leadership and practitioners of the Organizations and the Natural Environment division focusing on the systematic embedding of sustainability values into organizational practices, thereby fostering key behaviors that substantially enhance corporate social performance and reduce costs associated with organizational compliance.

Sustainability Blind Spots: Managers Misperceive Support for Sustainability and Policy Effectiveness

ONE
Jeffrey Lees (University of Groningen); Jordana Composto (Princeton University); Elke Weber (Princeton University)
Understanding the roots of organizational inaction on sustainability is necessary to achieve a more sustainable economy. Insufficient information and miscalibrated beliefs about what are desirable, feasible, and effective sustainability policies have been shown to contribute to inaction, yet research across organizational and behavioral science suggest different reasons for inaction. Organizational scholarship suggests a pessimism towards current sustainability efforts, where managers believe that sustainability is not profitable and that the organization lacks the capabilities and knowledge to take effective action. Conversely, the behavioral sciences suggest inaction is rooted in pluralistic ignorance biases, where people significantly underestimate others’ support for taking action. We reconcile these two competing predictions across four studies using a range of survey and experimental methods, including a test of three corrective interventions. We found overwhelming support for the behavioral sciences' account. Managers consistently displayed (1) a positive preference for existing organizational sustainability policies, (2) pluralistic ignorance regarding support for sustainability policies they themselves generated as their aspirations, and (3) very low levels of knowledge of objective policy effectiveness (KOE). We found that KOE and the status quo bias moderated the effectiveness of interventions correcting perceptions of social norms and policy effectiveness. Our results contribute to organizational scholarship on managerial biases around sustainability.

From Actors to Relations: How Responsible Leadership Emerges in Multi-Stakeholder Collaborations

ONE
Ermal Hetemi (Linnaeus University); Daniel Nyberg (The University of Queensland)
How does responsible leadership emerge (or not) in multi-stakeholder collaborations when no single actor is in charge? The study draws on a multi-stakeholder collaboration in Sweden, the High-Capacity Transport (HCT) (2012–2023), with the aim of increasing addressing climate change through innovation in transportation and forestry. Based on 65 interviews, observations, and extensive report materials, the findings show how responsible leadership is co-constructed among multiple actors, leading us to reconceptualize it as a dynamic, collective, and processual construct enacted in relations. We develop a three-stage process model, showing how stakeholders negotiated meaning, authority, and relationships through shared interpretation and influence, progressively deepening their commitments and embedding responsibility into the collaborative process and across the three stages. We specify responsible leadership in multi-stakeholder collaborations as an ongoing balancing collective act through a cycle of tensions and responses where leadership repertoires (distributing, embedding and integrating) translate multi-stakeholder engagement into a temporary settlement and alignment of diverse stakeholders with policy implications and outcomes. We also offer practical guidance for multi-stakeholder collaborations that balance inclusion and direction for tackling grand challenges.

Corporate Sustainability Tensions and Practical Judgement: Sustainability Leaders’ Lived Experience (WITHDRAWN)

ONE
Luiza Burns Barreto (University of Cambridge); Elena P. Antonacopoulou (American University of Beirut Mediterraneo)
Building on paradox theory and practical judgement frameworks, this paper presents empirical findings from lived experiences of 22 sustainability leaders to explicate the tension sources, which manifest as an interconnected web across four levels: individual, organizational, external and systemic. The findings also reveal that to navigate this complexity, leaders exercise practical judgement through a dynamic and iterative process of multi-level awareness, deliberation and judgement, and action. They do so through an active inner-outer world interaction, employing contextual and systemic awareness alongside self-awareness, forming their judgement through reflexive and consultative practices, and enacting it through narrative and political navigation capabilities. Resilience emerges as a supporting mechanism providing emotional and psychological fortitude to navigate challenges. These insights unveil dimensions of practical judgement in sustainability leadership that enable leaders to deal with the complex and interconnected system of tensions they face as organizations attempt to balance their economic, social, and environmental responsibilities.
