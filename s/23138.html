Examining Trait and State Impostor Phenomenon Using Whole Trait Theory

MOC
Yan Yi Lance Du (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Impostor phenomenon (IP)—the subjective experience of feeling like a fake despite successes in life—has been traditionally conceptualized as a stable dispositional trait. Recent theorizing, however, proposes that impostor feelings may operate more as a dynamic state-like construct that fluctuates across time and situations. Drawing on Whole Trait Theory (WTT; Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015), this experience sampling method (ESM) study examined within-person variability in state IP (i.e., daily impostor feelings), and its situational and individual antecedents. A total of 167 undergraduate students completed a baseline trait questionnaire, followed by a 10-day ESM protocol that assessed state IP, situational eight DIAMONDS domains, and other state-level constructs (e.g., state self-esteem, HEXACO personality states, and state perfectionism). Consistent with WTT, state IP displayed (a) high convergent validity with trait IP; (b) adequate divergent validity with non-IP trait measures (i.e., trait self-esteem, trait core self-evaluations, and trait emotionality); (c) high temporal stability; and (d) substantial within-person variability. Daily impostor feelings were also meaningfully related to certain DIAMONDS situation dimensions (e.g., Duty, Intellect, Negativity, and Deception) and state-level constructs. This study reconciles the trait and state conceptualizations of IP, offering practical insights into how and when impostor feelings may arise in everyday life. Findings may inform interventions targeted at reducing impostor experiences and enhancing psychological well-being.

How a Norm Against Extended Work Hours Intensifies Employees’ Work and Nonwork Activities

MOC
Ruilin Huang (Stockholm School of Economics); Ioana Lupu (ESSEC Business School); Frida Pemer (Stockholm School of Economics)
Extended work hours have long been recognized as harmful to employees’ work-life balance and limiting their capacity to meet nonwork responsibilities. This study, however, uncovers the unintended consequences of a norm against extended work hours. Through a longitudinal interview study with 38 individuals in Finland, we examined their transition from intensive master’s studies, where coursework and part-time jobs were combined and occurred extensively at any time without clear work–nonwork boundaries, to full-time knowledge work characterized by a norm against extended work hours. Our findings show that while this norm helps protect employees’ work–nonwork boundaries, it simultaneously intensifies both work and nonwork activities. We identify both resisting and conforming practices from employees, with the former becoming marginalized, allowing the norm to perpetuate. We contribute to the work-life literature by illuminating the nuanced interplay between work intensification and work extensification. Whereas prior studies often treat them as coexisting or mutually reinforcing processes, we show that avoiding work extensification outside normal hours may instead foster work intensification. Further, departing from the common understanding that extended work hours limit employees’ abilities to engage in nonwork activities, we reveal that an intensification of nonwork activities can stem from the cascading effects of work intensification within regular work hours—effects that amplify employees’ need for work detachment after-hours, constrain work–nonwork flexibility, and foster the internalization of a productivity-oriented, fast-paced mindset.

Loneliness by Belief: How Lay Theories of Social Relationships Buffer Individuals from Loneliness

MOC
Shelly Qi (INSEAD); Hongzhi Chen (Fudan University); Ko Kuwabara (University of Kentucky)
Workplace loneliness is on the rise. Despite extensive research on loneliness, a key puzzle remains: why are some employees more vulnerable to loneliness than others, even when they appear similarly connected at work? To address this question, we examine individuals’ lay theories of social relationships as a mindset that shapes how they experience loneliness. We theorize that endorsing a growth lay theory of relationships—believing that relationships are malleable and can be strengthened through effort—buffers employees against loneliness, especially in strained networks marked by few friendship ties and many difficult ties. Evidence from an archival analysis, an online survey, and a longitudinal field study supports our theory. This research advances cognitive discrepancy theory of loneliness by clarifying how loneliness is shaped by subjective interpretations of workplace relationships, beyond objective connections. It also bridges workplace relationship research on relational schemas with social network perspectives on tie content and structure. Finally, it extends network agency and network dynamics research by showing that relational agency is grounded not only in network actions, but also in employees’ interpretations and expectations about relationship trajectories. Practically, our findings highlight the potential of shifting relational beliefs as a cost-effective approach to reducing workplace loneliness in the face of difficult relationships.

An Intraindividual Theory of Workplace Impostor Thoughts and Creativity

MOC
Basima Tewfik (Massachusetts Institute of Technology); Daniel Kim (University of Iowa)
Existing research on the impostor phenomenon—or ‘workplace impostor thoughts’—typically portrays such thoughts as undermining creativity: Individuals who believe that others overestimate their capabilities are assumed to be less creative. Initial theorizing, however, hints at a more complex reality: Workplace impostor thoughts sometimes precede creative highs, and, at other times, creative lows. Despite these allusions, prior work has not elaborated these possibilities into a coherent theory or identified the mechanisms that explain them, leaving open the critical question of how and when workplace impostor thoughts enable and inhibit creativity. Accordingly, we build a theory of workplace impostor thoughts and creativity that looks within a given individual. We theorize that workplace impostor thoughts have opposing effects. When construed positively—as signals to rise to the occasion—they foster task-focused attention that enhances creativity. When construed negatively—as reminders of not measuring up—they fuel rumination-based distraction that impairs creativity. We test this model in two experiments and two experience sampling studies (one in the supplement) across diverse samples of comedians, executive MBAs, and full-time employees (n = 3748 observations from 1062 individuals). We conclude with contributions to theory on workplace impostor thoughts and creativity.

Affective Variability Over Time and Its Effects on Employee Motivation and Well-Being

MOC
Sasha Pustovit (Towson University); Patrick Downes (University of Kansas); Miguel Caldas (The University of Texas at Tyler)
Moving beyond same-day approaches to studying affect at work, this study integrates within-person affective dynamics with between-person differences in day-to-day affective variability to understand employee well-being and motivation. We examine the consequences of affective variability using temporal delays, capturing processes of sensemaking and meaning construction that unfold over time. Drawing on experience-sampling data collected over ten workdays from full-time employees, we investigate how average levels and day-to-day variability in positive and negative affect predict motivation and well-being. Results show that both average positive affect and positive affect variability are positively associated with subsequent motivation and well-being. In contrast, average negative affect is negatively associated with these outcomes, whereas the effects of negative affect variability differ across outcomes, reflecting the informational value conveyed by affective fluctuations. Overall, these findings extend Affect-as-Information theory by demonstrating that affect conveys distinct informational value depending on whether it is experienced consistently or variably over time, and that the implications of affective variability depend on the outcome domain under consideration.
