Inspiration or Inhibition? Awe Elicitor Social Status and Divergent Outcomes of Interpersonal Awe

Monday, Aug 3 2026 · 10:00AM–11:00AM
Philadelphia Marriott Downtown — Poster Wall 11
OB

People

Authors: Xiao Chen Michigan State University; Jiajin Sophie Tong Peking University; Russell Eric Johnson Michigan State University

Session overview

Interpersonal awe, elicited by extraordinary, high-achieving individuals, is prevalent in organizations. However, interpersonal awe may not be uniformly beneficial when social status is brought to the foreground. We develop a status-contingent dual-path model of interpersonal awe, proposing that the awe elicitor’s relative social status can lead to both behavioral approach and behavioral inhibition, with downstream motivational and behavioral implications. Critically, we theorize that these effects depend on individuals’ status trajectories across time. Specifically, past status mobility (retrospective status transition since childhood) and status growth mindset (belief that one’s future status can improve) jointly influence how employees respond to status-laden interpersonal awe experiences. We test this model with two multi-wave experiments, manipulating the elicitor’s relative social status. In Study 1, participants read a fictional coworker scenario (higher vs lower social status), rated behavioral approach and inhibition, and one week later reported learning new skills and self-handicapping. Path analyses supported the proposed moderated effects where elicitor status predicted behavioral approach for people higher in past status mobility and predicted behavioral inhibition among people with higher status growth mindset. Behavioral approach was positively associated with learning, whereas behavioral inhibition predicted self-handicapping. Study 2 replicates and extends these findings using a real-world scenario, larger organizational sample, and a longer time lag. All hypotheses were supported, and outcomes expanded to include networking and compliance. Together, the findings show that interpersonal awe in hierarchical contexts can yield divergent motivational pathways and behavioral consequences depending on elicitor status and individuals’ past and anticipated status mobility.
