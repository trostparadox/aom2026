Accelerating Impact: How Startup Programs Build Competence, Strategy, and Social Value

ENT
Tatiana Iakovleva (University of Stavanger); Torill Thuestad Stokka (University of Stavanger); Maria Halsne Dahl
This study examines how accelerator programs influence (a) individual entrepreneurial competencies, (b) entrepreneurial firm strategy, and (c) ventures' perceived social impact. While prior research underscores the role of social value creation in new venture formation, the mechanisms by which accelerators —widely recognized as crucial entrepreneurial support structures—foster capabilities and strategic orientations that drive social impact remain underexplored. Drawing on a mixed-method design, this study combines survey data from 106 entrepreneurs who completed a three-month accelerator program with exploratory interviews with participants and accelerator managers. We differentiate between social learning program characteristics (e.g., mentorship, peer engagement) and formal organizational learning characteristics (e.g., structured training, tools) and investigate how these dimensions relate to competencies, firm strategy, and perceived social impact outcomes. Our findings indicate that social learning characteristics significantly enhance entrepreneurial competencies by fostering confidence, providing feedback, and supporting observational learning. These competencies, in turn, positively predict entrepreneurial firm strategy. Additionally, formal organizational learning characteristics are directly associated with entrepreneurial firm strategy, with qualitative data reinforcing how structured learning encourages strategic reflection and more risk-aware decision-making. While we find no significant indirect effect of program characteristics on financial performance, consistent with the early-stage nature of many ventures, both formal and social program characteristics exhibit a positive indirect effect on perceived social impact. This effect is mediated sequentially through entrepreneurial competencies and entrepreneurial firm strategy. These results highlight accelerator programs as structured and relational learning environments that promote social value creation, even in the absence of immediate financial outcomes.

Constraint or Catalyst? How Crises Shape Innovation and Exporting in Purpose Driven Firms

ENT
Ines Alvarez-Boulton; Ute Stephan (King's College London); Saul Estrin; Anna Rebmann (King's College London)
This paper explores how different crises influence the relationship between firms’ social goals and innovation and exporting – which are both drivers of firm performance and important to sustain social impact. Firms with social goals are often argued to be more resilient and have been found to innovate more than firms purely seeing financial returns. However, it is unclear whether this is always the case during crises. Drawing from the strategic adaptation literature we investigate how crises shape innovation and exporting depending on the firm’s emphasis on social goals. We test our predictions using a longitudinal representative sample of 428 UK small- and medium-sized firms exposed to two crises, Brexit and the Covid pandemic. While we found no moderating effect of Brexit, firms with stronger emphasis on social goals innovated and exported less when the Covid pandemic negatively impacted these firms. Our results and theorization of crisis responses contribute to the entrepreneurship literature researching strategic adaptation and nuancing arguments around the ‘local resourcefulness’ of purpose driven firms. Our findings also have direct implications for crisis response policy to support the innovation and exporting in firms with social goals as critical for post-crisis recovery.

Guilt: The Gift That Keeps on Giving. How Entrepreneurial Guilt Influences Pro-Social Actions

ENT
Nataliia Yakushko (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Melissa S. Cardon (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); David Gras (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Trenton A. Williams (Brigham Young University)
Social entrepreneurs are motivated to create social value, often driven by a sense of commitment to a cause, empathy, and compassion. While such positive motivations are commendable, the existing literature assumes that social entrepreneurs are primarily driven by pro-social motivation and views negative emotions, such as guilt, as barriers rather than potential motivators for entrepreneurial action. Thus, it overlooks the role that negative emotions can play in driving both immediate responses to social problems and long-term engagement in social ventures, leading to an incomplete understanding of what catalyzes and sustains social entrepreneurial action, especially during challenging times. We develop a theoretical model in which guilt acts as a driver for short-, medium-, and long-term, pro-social behavior, as entrepreneurs try to alleviate that guilt.

Heuristics and Bracketing: Navigating Uncertain Social Impacts in Prosocial Tech Interventions

ENT
Alessia Argiolas (Technical University of Munich); Hans Nikolas Rawhouser (University of Nevada Las Vegas)
Entrepreneurs acting prosocially face social impact uncertainty, or uncertainty about the connection between prosocial actions and social impact outcomes. We investigate how entrepreneurs and stakeholders identify social impact outcomes related to prosocial technology diffusion projects involving the placement of solar mini-grids to electrify remote villages in India and Kenya. Using verbal and visual qualitative evidence, we employ a grounded theory approach to develop a theoretical model in which heuristics (visual and cognitive) and bracketing (rejection of some outcomes) are used to deal with social impact uncertainty. The heuristics used depend on the perspectives of prosocial actors and target beneficiaries and their perception of social impact uncertainty, with increased experience often resulting in greater perception of uncertainty. We also advance understanding of the epistemology of social impact, showing that visual methods, temporal aspects and normative concerns are inherent in navigating social impact uncertainty.

Hybridization in Syndicated Social Impact Venture Capital Deals

ENT
Stefano Romito (University of Milan); Anita Quas; Clodia Vurro (University of Milan)
Social impact venture capitalists (SIVCs) pursue social and environmental goals alongside financial returns, unlike traditional Venture Capitalists (TVCs). Yet SIVCs and TVCs often syndicate. Drawing on institutional logics theory, we aim to examine whether SIVCs’ impact orientation is threatened, preserved, or transmitted to TVCs in mixed syndicates. Analyzing 9,866 European investments (2015–2023), we find that SIVC–TVC partnerships maintain a focus on impact-oriented ventures and invest in teams with stronger founders’ technical backgrounds to manage hybridization risks. However, prior co-investment ties attenuate the focus on the ventures’ impact orientation and technical human capital, evidence of hybridization as familiarity grows.
