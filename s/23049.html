Genius or Scenius? Innovation, Networks, and Citationality

CMS
Joeri M. Mol (University of Melbourne); Graham Edward Sewell (University of Melbourne)
We introduce the concept of scenius and contrast it with common notions of individual genius to capture the dynamics of a network of innovation, cast as a scene. We use Actor Network Theory (ANT) to understand the antecedents of a scene and to show how its various actors proceed to engage with each other through relations of power. In a reflexive manner, ANT acknowledges that the creation of the scene itself is perhaps its own greatest innovation. We demonstrate this through an extended case study of Jamaican music scene that, despite being part of a post-colonial economy, early 1960s grew from virtually nothing in the early 1960s to become an influential part of the international music industry within the space of two decades. We then introduce the concept of citationality and operationalize it to show how the network of actors in the Jamaican music scene innovated using familiarity to a manufacture a desire for its novel creations, first within the island and then on an international scale. We conclude by considering how our approach can be extended to other areas of innovation studies.

Entrepreneurship Under Constraint: Review of Sub-Saharan African Immigrant Entrepreneurship

CMS
Masud Aminu Bature (Anadolu University); Ozan Aglargoz (Anadolu University); Ridvan Kocaman (Anadolu University)
Entrepreneurship among Sub-Saharan African immigrants is frequently enacted under conditions of structural constraint rather than as an opportunity-driven choice. This study presents a systematic, theory-informed review of research on Sub-Saharan African immigrant entrepreneurship, examining how entrepreneurial activity is shaped by racialized institutional contexts, labor-market exclusion, and constrained access to formal economic resources in host countries. Drawing on 33 peer-reviewed articles indexed in Scopus and Web of Science, the review employs reflexive thematic analysis to synthesize empirical and conceptual insights across diverse geographical settings. The analysis identifies five interrelated themes: institutional and legal barriers, reliance on social and ethnic networks, transnational and diasporic practices, survival-oriented entrepreneurial strategies, and the influence of cultural and religious values. Across these themes, entrepreneurship emerges as a structurally induced response to exclusion and precarity rather than a voluntary pathway to growth. By critically interrogating dominant assumptions in immigrant entrepreneurship research, this review reframes Sub-Saharan African entrepreneurship as organizing under constraint and advances a research agenda foregrounding power, inequality, and institutional context.

Posting to Protest: Affective Practices of Emancipatory Entrepreneurship in Iran’s 2022 Uprising

CMS
Marilyn Poon (Paris Lodron University of Salzburg); Daniela Kranzlmueller
This empirical study centers on the Instagram activities of one female Iranian entrepreneur during the 2022 Women Life Freedom uprising as representative of many entrepreneurs-turned-activists who sought to resist the sociopolitical constraints imposed upon them. The concept of emancipatory entrepreneurship (Rindova et al., 2009) posits that entrepreneurs operate not only to pursue market-based goals but also seek pathways to free themselves from the power of another. Leveraging Entrepreneurship-as-practice theorizing, this study seeks to reveal the affective dynamics embedded in the emancipatory entrepreneurial practices of women in oppressive contexts. Applying a practice-based ethnographic approach, we trace affect-in-practice in the analysis of a series of Instagram account posts to reveal a series of interconnected practices that trespasses the enterprising domain into the politicizing domain. These nested practices we identified—encompassing the sharing of protest content to emotional expressions of solidarity—relationally constitute a form of affective organizing in resistance. By showing how new configurations of collective action in the digital can emerge through authorship and self-representation when individuals take action towards emancipation, we provide an empirical contribution to the concept of emancipatory entrepreneurship in a seldomly studied context of oppression. Our theoretical contribution to emancipatory entrepreneurship literature is based on practice-based theorizing, which complements the hitherto emphasis on the subjective-cognitive level of entrepreneurship. Adding to the conversation of what shapes and sustains a social movement from the perspective of organization studies—where organizations are not just entitative being but are inherently dynamic and ephemeral— we emphasize the entanglement of affect, technology, and social action with a feminist perspective on power. In our discussion, we first examine the subjective–affective level, focusing on how the entrepreneurial self is creatively authored through affective expressions and declarations. Second, we address the practice level, where emancipation emerges as a practical accomplishment enacted through nested, affect-infused entrepreneurial practices. Third, we turn to the structural–relational level, analysing how these practices define modes of engagement that confront and negotiate patriarchal power in a context of surveillance and repression. Together, these levels illuminate how emancipatory entrepreneurship unfolds not as a linear process but as an affective, practical, and political achievement.

Technology as Quasi-Object: Serresian Perspective to Material Agency in Organizing

CMS
Ville Kivivirta (University of Eastern Finland); Nina Lunkka (University of Eastern Finland); Anniina Rantakari (University of Oulu); Ville Pietiläinen (University of Lapland)
This paper examines material agency in organizing healthcare by drawing from Michel Serres’s distinctive and underutilized work on philosophy of technology. Prior organization research on material agency has advanced relational and processual understandings of technology by showing how agency emerges through ongoing interaction, intra-action, and performative enactment. However, less attention has been paid to how interaction becomes momentarily ordered as events unfold. Drawing on the notion of the quasi-object, this paper examines technology as circulating through interaction in ways that temporarily organize collective orientation. Based on a fine-grained analysis of naturally occurring interactions in video-recorded strategy workshops conducted during a major organizational restructuring in the healthcare sector, the findings show how circulation, translation, and passage points enable chatbot and remote services to introduce asymmetries that (re)configure collective as organizing unfolds. The paper contributes to a dynamic understanding of technology as a quasi-object that participates in the ongoing organizing in complex institutional settings such as healthcare.
