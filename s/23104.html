Edged Sword: How Peer Influences and Owner Age Impact Export Exit in Immigrant-Owned SMEs

HR
Yu Wei Ye (Toronto Metropolitan University); Sui Sui (Toronto Metropolitan University); Liang (Arthur) Li (Toronto Metropolitan University); Yuanqing Li (Montclair State University); Vikkram Singh
Amid a global aging workforce, many small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are led by older owners whose talents and experience are invaluable. However, leveraging these older entrepreneurs’ talent in dynamic contexts (like exporting) requires understanding how age influences their learning and strategic decisions. Using longitudinal panel data on immigrant entrepreneurs in Canada, we construct a multicultural sample comprising 20,050 firm-year observations and 5,525 firms. The results challenge a one-dimensional view of aging by demonstrating both the diminished learning and enhanced resilience of older owners. Aging owners, as experienced talent, learn and respond differently to their peer environment – They are less swayed by peer competition (a potential strength in stability), but also gain less from peer learning (a potential area needs development). The findings extend talent management theory to the domain of SMEs internationalization. The understanding of these nuances helps organizations retain older entrepreneurs in the active workforce. It also carries implications for policymakers to support older entrepreneurs via age-tailored promotion, and keep their businesses internationally engaged.

Intensifying HPWS in Response to Foreign Competition

HR
Chanhyuk Shin (Korea University); Ji-Won Song (The University of Edinburgh)
Human resource management (HRM) systems are increasingly shaped by competitive pressures that originate beyond organizational and national boundaries. While prior HRM research has emphasized how institutional environments sustain legitimacy-oriented employment practices, we know less about how organizations actively strengthen performance-oriented HR systems when exposed to foreign competition. Drawing on institutional agency perspectives and the convergence-divergence debate in international HRM, this study examines how industry-level inward foreign direct investment (FDI) is associated with the intensification of high-performance work systems (HPWS), and how organization-level agency conditions this relationship. Using panel data on 2,948 local organizations across 66 industries in Korea from 2006 to 2020, we find that higher inward FDI intensity within an industry is associated with greater intensification of HPWS among local organizations. Moreover, this association is stronger for organizations with low internationalization action orientation, highlighting how organizational motivation and capacity shape responses to external competitive pressures. By showing how HPWS are selectively intensified under conditions of institutional complexity, this study contributes to HRM research by clarifying when and why organizations prioritize performance-enhancing HRM systems in response to global competitive forces.

Responsible Leadership In the Age of Artificial Intelligence and its Effect on Belonging

HR
Parul Manocha (University of Alabama at Birmingham)
Responsible leadership matters – perhaps more so now than ever before. It matters for how organizations act, for how leaders make decisions, and for how people feel about the places, products, and processes that constitute the source of their livelihood. It also matters for the extent to which individuals feel a connection to something larger than themselves. Management research has long propounded the extent to which a sense of belonging is critical to individual and organizational well-being, and the numerous ways in which it is a core dimension of responsible leadership. Yet, belonging has become a murky notion as it is increasingly conflated with the concept of inclusion – a situation that has grown more pronounced and more consequential with the emergence and rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI). The distinction is important: inclusion grants access; belonging ensures identification, connection and palpable commitment. Responsible leaders have the capacity to promote both inclusion and belonging through one or both of two orientations: instrumental and integrative. The challenge, however, is these two orientations have themselves become conflated as AI becomes the default solution to structuring communication, evaluation, and coordination. In light of this, we assert that the substance, nature and boundaries of leadership are shifting, and that the notion of authentic belonging is getting lost in the shuffle. To bring much-needed clarity to these important distinctions, we develop a typology of leadership orientations, linking three AI modalities—assistive, augmentative, and autonomous—to the manner in which responsible leadership enacts and influences inclusion and belonging. Our approach offers insights to scholars and managers in demonstrating that as organizational life evolves alongside intelligent technologies, responsible leadership must also evolve to ensure that belonging does not become an afterthought.

Linking AI Knowledge Sharing and Creativity: The Role of Change Self-Efficacy and Support

HR
Elissar Abdul Khalek (Claremont Graduate University); Noura Yassine (Beirut Arab University); Abdul-Nasser El-Kassar (Lebanese American University); Mira Thoumy (Lebanese American University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is impacting organizations and changing the workplace by creating new expectations for employee adaptability and creativity. Prior research has extensively examined factors promoting and influencing employee creativity; however, more attention is now being paid to examining how employees apply AI-enabled disruption to boost and sustain their creativity and innovation. In this study, a conceptual model is developed and empirically tested. The model links AI-related knowledge sharing with employee creativity through employees’ change self-efficacy and examines the role organizational support for creativity plays in strengthening employees’ creativity. Survey data were collected from a sample of 399 employees working in AI-integrated environments across diverse industries in the United States. The proposed relationships of the conceptual model are tested via PLS-SEM analysis. Results indicate that AI-related knowledge sharing has a positive and significant effect on change self-efficacy, which, in turn, enhances employees’ creativity. Furthermore, the results show that perceived organizational support for creativity positively strengthens the influence of change self-efficacy, which, in turn, enhances employees’ creativity. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of fostering AI-related knowledge sharing and psychological readiness as strategic pathways for cultivating employee creativity and innovation in AI-augmented workplaces.

Reframing Responsible Restructuring: How Organizations Configure Layoff Practices Across Motives

HR
Nita Chhinzer (University of Guelph); Akierah Binns (University of Guelph)
Layoffs have become a widespread, dynamic workforce adjustment strategy, yet our understanding of how layoffs are executed in contemporary times remains conceptual and incomplete. Within this research discourse, responsible restructuring reflects approaches to layoffs that mitigate harm. Drawing on the dynamic capabilities perspective and bundling theory within strategic human resource management, we explore how organizations approach layoffs in practice, and how or if the concepts of responsible restructuring are adopted in current corporate layoffs. To do so, we analyze 566 employer-to-employee layoff announcements in Ontario, providing rare access to direct and largely unfiltered layoff announcements. We address four research questions: which layoff motives dominate contemporary narratives; how frequently specific layoff alternatives and supplements are used; whether the use of alternatives and supplements varies by motive, independently or as bundles; and how observed approaches align with responsible restructuring. The findings indicate that contemporary layoffs are predominantly framed as competitive-positioning motivated, followed by plant closings, structural change, and reorganization. We also develop a current and comprehensive inventory of layoff alternatives and supplements and build an empirically grounded understanding of layoff bundles in practice. Overall, the results challenge simplified, universalistic accounts of responsible restructuring by showing substantial heterogeneity in how harm-mitigation practices are combined across layoff motives. This reframes layoffs as strategic, configurable, and institutionally embedded change events, extending theory on dynamic capability enactment in restructuring.
