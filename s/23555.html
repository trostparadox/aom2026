Data Governance & Customer Engagement: Business Model Implications

TIM
Charles Baden-Fuller (City St George's - University of London); Stefan Haefliger (Stockholm School of Economics); Angelo Bello (Stockholm School of Economics)
Customer behavioural data have become critical strategic resources, yet conventional perspectives treat them as separable assets amenable to generic manipulation capabilities. This view overlooks fundamental complexities arising when data emerge from and influence ongoing customer-firm interactions. We develop a framework examining how temporal requirements (real-time versus batch processing), infrastructural dependencies (simple digital versus complex physical-digital arrangements), and control strategies across data commodification stages shape viable business models. Analysing these dimensions reveals four archetypal configurations, each requiring distinct organizational capabilities and governance approaches. Our framework demonstrates that behavioural customer data become firm-specific, non-replicable assets only when data governance choices align coherently with business model designs around customer engagement. We contribute to strategy by explicating mechanisms through which co-created data generate competitive advantage and by clarifying how solutions business models extend firm boundaries to encompass customers as active value co-creators rather than passive transactional partners.

Enabling Business Staff to Lead Digital Transformation: Unpacking the Attention Reallocation Process

TIM
Nan Zou (University of International Business and Economics); Chong Ma (University of International Business and Economics); Jiye Mao (ShanghaiTech University); Michael Myers (The University of Auckland)
The recent literature has shown that business staff need to deeply engage in digital transformation (DT) for DT to be successful in traditional firms. The foremost challenge to business staff’s engagement in DT is their attention reallocation. This is because what business staff do depends on what they attend to, and they must shift their attention to DT beyond their conventional work. Yet, the extant literature offers little understanding of business staff’s attention reallocation in DT. This case study focuses on the challenges and process of attention reallocation for business staff to lead DT. Our findings reveal four salient attention reallocation challenges encountered by business staff, including their initial disregard of DT as their focus, attention overload in balancing DT and routine work, dispersed foci and priorities without synergy, and diminishing engagement. We propose a process model that potentially enables business staff’s engagement in DT by unpacking the attention reallocation process. This model suggests that top management can take four measures, attention drawing, attention specializing, attention cohering, and attention sustaining.

Rates of Learning, Growth and Cost Reduction in Hardware Industries

TIM
Will Mosley (Melbourne Business School); David R. Keith (Melbourne Business School)
Successful venture outcomes for the commercialisation of radical clean energy innovation (cleantech) are ‘rare’, and cleantech investors form their expectations of risk, uncertainty and profit from limited information. This paucity of data may be contributing to episodic ‘boom-and-bust’ cleantech investing and comparably poor returns. Could cleantech venture returns improve with greater financial performance data? Hardware start-ups are particularly sensitive to assumptions of product growth and unit cost reduction, but, with the exception of the learning rate (a negative exponential relationship between cumulative production and unit cost), average industry benchmarks are unavailable within existing literature. We survey prior literature on diffusion and experience curves to benchmark rates of learning, growth and cost reduction for 100 hardware technologies (the largest dataset of its kind in the literature) and observe that technologies during their earliest phases of commercialization grew at 46% (7.8%-185%) p.a., reduced costs at 7.4% (-2.3% to 18%) p.a. with learning of 13.8% (-6.1% to 28%). Investors should expect hardware start-ups to present a painfully prolonged yet profitable path to 2.5% share of an initial market, rather than a quick path to profitability on a global scale. Stable year-on-year growth in one or few markets, supported by a sustained technology-specific demand-pull policy, is a likely indicator of future success. Efforts to ‘blow the doors off’ commercialization with a step change in growth will likely be ineffective. Technologies that are smaller, lower in investment cost, shorter in operating lifetime, more easily substitutable with incumbent solutions and with a greater range of market applications will likely experience higher and more consistent rates of learning, growth and cost reduction.

Digital Transformation as a Dynamic System: A Meta-Synthesis of Five Interdependent Dimensions

TIM
Fernando A R Serra (Ninth of July University); Isabelle Carlos Campos Rezende Cavalcante (Federal University of Paraíba); Vanessa Vasconcelos Scazziota (University of Evora)
This study develops an integrative framework that conceptualizes digital transformation (DT) as a dynamic system of interdependent dimensions. We conducted a qualitative meta-synthesis of 66 peer-reviewed case-study articles published between 2018 and 2024 (Web of Science and Scopus), covering 440 organizational cases across sectors and regions. Following established meta-synthesis procedures and grounded-theory coding (open–axial–selective) in Atlas.ti, we organized evidence using a 5W lens (Who–Why–Where–How–What). The synthesis identifies five recurring dimensions—actors, motivations, context, processes, and results—and reveals how they co-evolve through interaction mechanisms: motivations activate actor configurations; context mediates how motivations translate into transformation processes; processes combine adaptive governance with capability development to generate outcomes; and outcomes feed back to reshape motivations, producing self-reinforcing transformation cycles that distinguish DT from technology adoption. The framework integrates insights from dynamic capabilities, the resource-based view, and institutional theory. Managerially, it offers a diagnostic map for aligning stakeholders, tailoring governance to context, and sustaining transformation capability over time.
