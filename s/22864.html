A Look at How Host Nationals’ Stereotypes Predict Immigrant Newcomers’ Absenteeism Trajectories

OB
David Danner (University of Konstanz); Ann Sophie Lauterbach (Technische Universität Dresden); Florian Kunze (University of Konstanz); Benjamin Korman (Leibniz Institute for Educational Trajectories)
Although extensive work has explored how host country natives’ stereotypes shape their behavioral intentions toward immigrants, it is less understood how such stereotype perceptions shape immigrants’ behavior. This gap in knowledge is crucial given many societies’ increasing reliance on immigrant workers to fill labor-market shortages. Taking a dynamic social identity approach, we address this by exploring how natives’ stereotypes of immigrants predict immigrants’ withdrawal behavior, when entering a new organization. Matching absence records of 2,648 organizational newcomers from 63 nationalities with a second data source capturing natives’ stereotype content linked to these nationalities, we test whether natives’ stereotype content systematically shapes immigrant newcomers’ absenteeism trajectories over their first two years of employment. Our results reveal that relative to native newcomers, immigrant newcomers stereotyped as high in competence but low in warmth show increased absenteeism, whereas other stereotype groups show similar absenteeism development to natives. Further, groups stereotyped as low in competence exhibit lower overall absenteeism rates during their first two years of tenure. These findings suggest that some immigrants may be more motivated (or required) to prove themselves to their native colleagues than others, depending on their nationality. Our study thereby highlights immigrants’ differential absenteeism trajectories as a potential indication of social exclusion and a warning sign that organizations should take heed of.

Cultural Fit and Work–Family Dynamics Among H-1B Workers

OB
Neena Gopalan (University of Redlands); Murugan Pattusamy (University of Hyderabad); Jeffrey W. Alstete (Iona University)
This study explores how perceived cultural fit in the host country could potentially shape work–family experiences among highly skilled, H-1B visa holding employees in the United States. Focusing on job satisfaction, work–family interference, and work–family enrichment, we proposed a sequential mediation model in which cultural fit fosters enrichment by diminishing work-family interference and improving job satisfaction. Data analysis from 394 H-1B employees supported all proposed hypotheses. Cultural fit positively influenced work–family enrichment of H-1B employees, both directly and indirectly, by reducing their work–family interference and improving job satisfaction. By focusing on everyday work–family dynamics rather than exclusively career outcomes, this study highlights cultural fit as an essential resource for H-1B employees and provides actionable insights for organizations seeking to hire, support and retain H-1B talent.

From Guidance to Co-agency: Integrative Review of Highly Skilled Immigrant Workplace Integration

OB
Sijie Ren (Université Laval); Zhan SU
Despite widespread onboarding, mentoring, and inclusion efforts, highly skilled immigrants (HSIs) workplace integration remains uneven. Existing scholarship is largely organization-centric, leaving under-theorized the internal dynamics through which identical initiatives are appropriated, resisted, or rendered inert. We address this “black box” through an integrative review of 70 peer-reviewed studies published between 1990 and 2025. Grounded in Social Cognitive Theory, Self-Efficacy Theory, and Self-Determination Theory, we develop a conceptual model that positions HSIs as active integrators and specifies how motivational, cognitive, and behavioral agency unfold through four mechanisms (“I want,” “I can,” “I act,” and “I learn”) forming a self-reinforcing integration loop. We further theorize integration as co-produced: organizational initiatives operate as opportunity structures whose effects depend on how HSIs mobilize these mechanisms. A typology of four interaction logics (activation, amplification, misalignment/inhibition, and learning-based reconfiguration) explains why similar initiatives generate divergent trajectories. The review reconceptualizes workplace integration as a mechanism-based, co-constructed process, shifting the scholarly narrative from integration as organizational compliance to integration as agentic co-construction. By pinpointing where and why integration dynamics break down and illuminating how HSIs transform organizational opportunities into self-directed integration, the article advances a measurement-oriented and design-relevant agenda for cumulative theory building across organizational socialization and integration research.

Hometown Embeddedness and Adaptive Performance: The Moderating Role of Prior Host City Embeddedness

OB
Yuxin Fang (Nanjing University); Ruijia Geng (Nanjing University)
Return migration is often viewed as a way to bring back skills, experience, and external networks to noncore cities. Yet many return-to-hometown employees struggle to adapt, which limits the value that organizations and local governments expect from talent return. Drawing on job embeddedness theory, we develop a dual spatial embeddedness model to explain when hometown embeddedness supports adaptive performance after return and when this benefit is weakened by lingering ties to the prior host city. We test our arguments using a three-wave survey design with 321 Chinese hometown returnees who had prior out-of-town work experience. Results show that hometown embeddedness is positively related to adaptive performance. This positive association is negatively moderated by prior host city embeddedness. Further analyses indicate that the moderating effect is mainly driven by host city career embeddedness rather than host city community embeddedness. These findings extend embeddedness research beyond its traditional focus on retention by showing how locally rooted ties and familiarity can be mobilized as adaptation resources, while persistent host city career ties may operate as a constraint that reduces the performance returns of re-embeddedness. Our study offers a process-oriented account of returnees’ adjustment under competing geographic pulls and provides actionable implications for onboarding, career framing, and performance management in return migration contexts.
