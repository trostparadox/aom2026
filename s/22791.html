Sustainable Corporate Governance: Environmental Impact or Symbolic Management?

SIM
Jette Steen Knudsen (Tufts University); Steen Thomsen (Copenhagen Business School); Juyeong Jeong (Copenhagen Business School)
Companies increasingly rely on specialized governance mechanisms, such as sustainability committees, to manage their sustainability activities. However, it is unclear whether these mechanisms are symbolic or lead to improvements in sustainability outcomes since sustainability is an opaque field with a risk of decoupling between organizational practices and outcomes. We test for decoupling in a sample of the largest listed European companies 2002-2020, examining the association between four sustainable corporate governance mechanisms - ESG ratings, sustainability committees, sustainability incentives, and sustainability reporting - and corporate environmental outcomes reported in the Trucost database, which is considered one of the most accurate environmental sustainability datasets worldwide. We find no correlation between these sustainable corporate governance mechanisms and environmental outcomes. Fixed effects regressions show that they have insignificant effects on measurable environmental outcomes like greenhouse gas emissions, air pollution, water consumption, waste, or natural resource depletion. However, we find that companies with a large negative environmental impact reduce their impact proportionally more, a phenomenon we tentatively name “impact reversion.” Our findings suggest that symbolic governance and impact reversion coexist rather than contradict one another but they are governed by different logics: symbolic governance is about legitimacy while impact reversion is about materiality.

Corporate Social (Ir)Responsibility as a Driver of Chief Sustainability Officer Appointment

SIM
Beatrisa Pucalev (University of Mississippi); Jisun Kim (State University of New York Polytechnic Institute); Hyun-Soo Woo
Firms across industries have increasingly appointed Chief Sustainability Officers (CSOs) to lead and coordinate corporate social responsibility (CSR) activities and manage corporate irresponsibility (CSiR) risks. However, prior research offers inconsistent evidence on whether CSOs improve CSR. We believe one possibility may be reverse causality, whereby firms’ CSR and CSiR drive CSO appointments. Drawing on institutional theory, we argue that in the early stage of the CSO role emergence, firms with strong CSR appointed CSOs proactively to gain competitive advantage. In the late stage, as the CSO role became more common, CSiR emerged as the dominant driver, with firms appointing CSOs reactively to restore legitimacy following misconduct. Using data on S&P 1500 firms from 2004 to 2019, we find support for this temporal shift. Our findings provide a better understanding of the CSO–CSR relationship and hold important implications for future research and ethical practices within organizations.

Do Chief Sustainability Officers Make More Green Innovation? The Role of Stakeholders & Institutions

SIM
Shifang Zhao (Chongqing Technology and Business University); Carl Fey (BI Norwegian Business School); Tingyong Zhong (Chongqing Technology and Business University)
The emergence of chief sustainability officers (CSOs) is a new phenomenon in contemporary corporate governance, symbolizing firms’ commitment to sustainable development. This study integrates an attention-based view and stakeholder theory to investigate the direct impact and intermediate mechanisms between a firm having a CSO and corporate green innovation. We also explore how the institutional environment, including informal institutions such as Taoist culture, and the level of formal institutional development, affect the relationship between CSO presence and corporate green innovation. Using a secondary longitudinal dataset, which we compiled, of 5,268 publicly listed firms in China from 2006 to 2022, empirical results suggest that the presence of a CSO promotes corporate green innovation, with stakeholder green engagement— green investor participation and supply chain partner green attention —playing a partial, significant mediating role. Furthermore, the level of Taoist culture and institutional development enhances the impact of CSO presence on corporate green innovation. This study contributes to our scholarly and practical understanding of how and when firms’ CSO presence, stakeholders’ green engagement, and institutional environment are associated with corporate green innovation.

Recalibrating Openness at Scale - Access-Voice Dynamics in a Longitudinal Study

SIM
Hussein Fadlallah (Carnegie Mellon University); Robert A. Phillips (University of Virginia)
Digital platforms have been lauded for open access and for the opportunities created by low barriers to entry. Yet as platforms scale, critics have argued that openness can reproduce power asymmetries that marginalize underrepresented communities. We analytically separate openness of access from inclusiveness of voice and examine how they interact over time in a semi-decentralized digital context. Using a six-year qualitative study of a Reddit community, we analyze rule changes, archives of removed comments, and thousands of user reactions. We trace four governance pivots that form a pattern we denote as graduated enclosure: relational trust calibration, topic curation, active policing, and credential gating. Each pivot moves access and voice onto separate tracks and incrementally separates low barriers to entry from the ability to shape discourse. We contribute a conception of access – voice dynamics as a core design problem for digital communities and show how semi decentralized stewards utilize the polycentric architecture to manage voice distribution as growth raises visibility pressures.

Bytes of Caution: Negative Impact of TMT Digital Background on CSR Disclosure Quality

SIM
Jun Jin (Zhejiang University); Tailun Chen (Zhejiang University); Niels G. Noorderhaven (Tilburg University); Ximing Yin (Beijing Institute of Technology)
The fields of organization and management call for deeper exploration of digital transformation’s impact on corporate social responsibility (CSR). However, current research continues to exhibit significant divergences in viewpoints. This study therefore seeks to interpret the relationship between digital transformation and corporate CSR activities from a strategic decision-making perspective rather than a technological lens, focusing specifically on how top management teams’ digital background influences the quality of corporate CSR disclosures. Integrating the upper echelons theory and imprinting theory, our empirical findings based on data from Chinese listed companies from 2015 to 2020 indicate that TMT digital background exerts a negative effect on the quality of corporate CSR disclosure. We attribute this effect primarily to the “digital imprint” formed by the digital background on executives, leading them to favor “selective revealing” when dealing with strategic data disclosure issues. This foster reporting conservatism in CSR disclosures. By revealing the moderating role of online media coverage and ESG rating divergence on this relationship, we further validate the existence of this mechanism. The findings of this study provide new directions for understanding the impact of organizational digital transformation on non-market strategies.
