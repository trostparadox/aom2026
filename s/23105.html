Masculinity Contest Culture and Servant Leadership: Implications on Incivility & Turnover Intentions

HR
Jannifer David (University of Minnesota Duluth); Lin Xiu (University of Minnesota Duluth)
This research investigates the influence of Masculinity Contest Culture (MCC) on workplace incivility and turnover intentions and explores the moderating role of servant leadership—a leadership style that prioritizes support, inclusivity, and employee well-being. Utilizing the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model as a theoretical framework, this research examines how perceived MCC influences career sustainability and workplace dynamics. We conducted two studies to explore these relationships. In Study 1, a survey of 120 employees at a U.S. university revealed that perceived MCC is associated with workplace incivility and turnover intentions. Servant leadership emerges as a protective resource, mitigating the negative effects of perceived MCC, particularly in reducing incivility. In Study 2, using a broader sample of 300 participants across various industries, we found further evidence for the association between perceived MCC and both incivility and turnover intentions but does not find evidence of moderation. Instead, exploratory analysis suggests that workplace incivility may act as a potential mechanism through which MCC influences turnover intentions, warranting further investigation. Together, these findings expand the applicability of the MCC theory and illustrate how dominance-driven cultures can foster incivility and increase withdrawal intentions, potentially disrupting career trajectories for those less inclined to conform to such norms.

Effects of Work to Health Conflicts for Engagement & Performance at Work: Role of Servant Leadership

HR
Niharika Garud (University of Melbourne); Rahul Chandra Sheel (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Rakesh Kumar Pati (Deakin University)
Balancing work and health behaviors is a known challenge and a frequently occurring phenomenon across the globe. Prior research shows that conflicting demands of choosing between work and health can play an active role in shaping employees’ well-being and outcomes at work. We advance research on this phenomenon by drawing from work-life theory and conservation of resources perspective to develop theory on mechanisms through which experiencing conflict between work and health behaviors influence key work outcomes of anxiety, engagement, and performance. Furthermore, we build theory on how servant leaders help manage these conflicts in effective ways by examining the degree to which servant leadership moderates the proposed relationship. We gathered data from 411 employees and their supervisors over a five-month period to test our hypotheses and find that high work-to-health conflicts are strongly associated with high levels of anxiety, low engagement and job performance at work. We find that servant leadership weakened the link between followers’ anxiety caused by the work-health conflicts and engagement at work, especially for employees high in work-health conflicts. At low level of perceived servant leadership, followers experienced a stronger negative connection between anxiety and engagement, which then led to lower job performance. These results suggest that leaders play a role in shaping their employees’ ability to manage work-health conflicts and when leaders exhibit high levels of servant leadership, some of the negative outcomes experienced from dealing with such conflicts can be reduced to help improve engagement and performance at work.

Expert Leadership and Organizational Performance: A Theory and Integrative Review

HR
Jaason M. Geerts (The Canadian College of Health Leaders (CCHL))
Despite sustained investment in leadership development, many organizations continue to experience persistent leadership underperformance. This article addresses a central question for leadership and human resource management research: which leaders are most likely to generate consistently superior outcomes? Drawing on an integrative review of empirical evidence across sectors, leadership levels, and national contexts, the article advances the Theory of Expert Leadership (TEL). The TEL proposes that leaders who combine outstanding core business expertise with leadership capability and management experience are more likely to produce superior results than leaders who lack one or more of these elements. The review demonstrates robust, though probabilistic, associations between expert leadership and performance across professionalized and knowledge-intensive settings. The article clarifies key mechanisms, boundary conditions, and common misconceptions surrounding expert leadership, and examines why expert leaders remain systematically underrepresented in formal leadership roles. Implications are discussed for leadership theory, talent systems, and future research.

Developing Servant Leadership Through Socially Responsible Human Resource Management

HR
Zhi Liu (Renmin University of China); Zeyu Sun (Renmin University of China); Yu Zhou (Renmin University of China)
While the positive value of servant leadership is well established, the organizational mechanisms that proactively foster its development remain insufficiently understood. Integrating on social identity and social exchange theories, we propose and test a moderated dual-mediation model in which Socially Responsible Human Resource Management (SRHRM) promotes servant leadership by shaping leaders’ altruistic identity and altruistic reward expectancy. We further theorize that Machiavellianism serves as a boundary condition that dictates the dominance of these pathways. Using a three-wave, multi-source sample of 195 leaders and 770 subordinates, we find that SRHRM positively predicts both altruistic identity and altruistic reward expectancy, which in turn independently promote servant leadership. Moreover, Machiavellianism weakens the identity-based pathway by negatively moderating the relationship between SRHRM and altruistic identity, while strengthening the exchange-based pathway by positively moderating the relationship between SRHRM and altruistic reward expectancy. As a result, when Machiavellianism is low, altruistic identity is the dominant mediator, whereas when Machiavellianism is high, altruistic reward expectancy dominates. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings.
