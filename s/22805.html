Early-Life Traumatic Experiences and CEO Civic Engagement

SIM
Marie Charlotte Kionka (University of Bonn); David Antons (University of Bonn)
Chief executive officers’ (CEO) life experiences influence their values and cognitive frames and, in turn, firm outcomes, as stated by Upper Echelons theory. CEOs’ experiences are, however, not only a driver of firm outcomes but also of their personal contribution to society. Whereas the link between CEO background and Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) has been well studied, the antecedents of CEOs’ personal contribution to society, namely civic engagement, remain largely overlooked despite the increasing attention placed on CEO behavior. We seek to understand the drivers behind CEOs’ civic engagement. Drawing on post-traumatic growth and imprinting theory, we examine the influence of traumatic experiences in childhood as exemplified by exposure to natural disasters. Using a cross-sectional quantitative design, we analyze a sample of 826 US-born S&P1500 CEOs and their traumatic experience in their childhood county. We test whether traumatic experiences are associated with higher levels of civic engagement through imprinting and post-traumatic growth processes. We find that early-life traumatic experience is positively associated with CEO civic engagement and that this effect is moderately strengthened by traumatic experiences involving human casualties. Moreover, our results suggest that repeated exposure to traumatic experiences does not further amplify civic engagement.

Hype or Substance? Newly Appointed CEOs and the Catering Behavior in Digitalization Disclosure

SIM
Linbo Chen (Shanghai International Studies University); Feifei Liu (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
While digitalization disclosure gains increasing prominence as a corporate strategic initiative, the role of CEOs, as key decision-makers, in shaping firms’ disclosure catering behavior remains underexplored. Drawing on managerial entrenchment theory, we investigate how newly appointed CEOs influence such catering behavior and the contextual contingencies amid CEO succession dynamics. Using data on Chinese listed firms from 2011 to 2023, we confirm that firms' newly appointed CEOs show lower levels of digitalization disclosure catering behavior, driven by their motivation to mitigate positional vulnerability. The negative effect is strengthened by pre-succession high performance expectation gaps, external CEO appointments, or post-succession strong external attention. Moreover, this negative relationship is more pronounced for newly appointed CEOs with IT-related backgrounds. These findings deepen the understanding of catering behavior in digitalization disclosure from the perspective of CEO heterogeneity, extend the literature on the consequences of newly appointed CEOs, and provide valuable insights for digitalization disclosure practices.

From Classroom to Boardroom: Legitimizing Myths Amongst Mid-Career Managers

SIM
Venkatesha Murthy (Indian Institute of Management Tiruchirappalli); Amit Anand Tiwari (Indian Institute of Management Sirmaur); Sonali Narbariya; Samrat Gupta
This study advances research on understanding discrimination arising from caste systems within organizational contexts. Caste-based discrimination affects 330 million people in around the world. In this light, we examine mid-career business executive management students’ autobiographical living instances of discrimination rooted in the caste system in their circles that may point at aggregated individual discrimination and behavioral asymmetry. Building on the lenses of Social Dominance Theory (SDT), we analyzed the data using a structural model of narrative analysis leading to the formation of three constructs: (1) “hygiene” and spatial segregation; (2) friendship, marriage, and love; and (3) education and occupational mobility. We found that the traditional hierarchical groups develop a new set of disguised legitimizing myths under the notions of “personal choices,” replacing the older myths to defend their position and anchor for their inner group’s interest. The findings offer the top management with insights to consider deep-rooted caste-related issues in managing organizational processes such as hiring, training, and employee socialization.

CEO Nonlocality and Corporate Misbehavior: A Social Identity Perspective

SIM
Xiaoyang Deng (Wuhan University); Tao Wang (Wuhan University); Yongqing Ye (Wuhan University)
CEO locality—geographic origins of CEOs—shapes corporate conduct, but its mechanisms remain underexplored. Drawing on social identity theory, we propose outsider self-restraint as a novel mechanism, arguing that firms led by nonlocal CEOs are less prone to misbehavior compared to local CEOs. This self-restraint is particularly pronounced among CEOs with limited power—those who do not hold the dual role of CEO and board chair, and those with limited reputational capital—CEOs who have not received professional awards. The effect is most salient for CEOs who possess neither board duality nor reputational accolades, especially in institutional contexts characterized by tight cultural norms and advanced legal development. Using a longitudinal dataset of publicly listed Chinese firms, we employ a three-way analytical approach and find empirical support for most of our hypotheses. This study contributes to the literature on CEO nonlocality and corporate misbehavior by identifying self-restraint as a key behavioral mechanism underlying ethical firm leadership.

Revisiting the Link between Executive Early-Life Adverse Experience and Firm Prosocial Behavior

SIM
Halpitim Habibulla (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Fuji Xie (Antai College of Economics and Management, Shanghai Jiao Tong U.)
Despite an extensive work on executives’ early-life adverse experience (AE) and firm prosocial behavior, findings in the existing literature are mixed and coincidentally concentrated on a linear relationship between the two. Drawing on upper echelons theory, this study reinvestigates the relationship between executives’ early-life AEs and firm prosocial behavior by analyzing how CEOs with an environmental pollution experience (EPE) early in their lives influence firm green innovation, as a context. Using a panel dataset of Chinese publicly traded manufacturing firms, we find a U-shaped association between CEO early-life EPE and both green product and process innovation within firms. We explain this U-shaped relationship through CEOs’ two preferences of “acquiescence” and “redemption” after experiencing environmental pollution early in their lives. Additionally, we interestingly find the asymmetric moderation effects of regulatory pressure and normative pressure on such a U-shaped relationship. Specifically, the regulatory pressure attenuates the U-shaped relationship between CEO early-life EPE and green process innovation but does not significantly exert an impact on the U-shaped relationship between CEO early-life EPE and green product innovation. By contrast, the normative pressure enhances the U-shaped relationship between CEO early-life EPE and green product innovation but lacks a significant impact on the U-shaped relationship between CEO early-life EPE and green process innovation. Our study not only advances strategic leadership literature on executives’ prior experiences and firm outcomes but also adds to the literature on the influence of institutional pressures on firm behavior.
