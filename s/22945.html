Unpacking the Behavioral Mechanisms of Change Communication and Change Outcomes

ODC
Michel Zaitouni; Mohammed Laid Ouakouak; Elsa Abboud (Lebanese American University); Noufou Ouedraogo (Grant MacEwan University)
Purpose: The purpose of this study is to examine the role of Communication of Change in influencing both Employee Performance and Change Outcomes. Drawing on existing literature, a mediation model was developed that incorporates three mediating variables: Expected Change Outcomes for Employees, Expected Change Outcomes for the Organization, and Employee Support for Change. Design/methodology/Approach: A quantitative study involving 240 employees was conducted, and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) techniques were used to test the proposed hypotheses. Findings: The findings revealed that Communication of Change has a positive effect on both Expected Change Outcomes for Employees and Expected Change Outcomes for the Organization. The results also demonstrated that Expected Change Outcomes for Employees mediate the relationship between Communication of Change and Employee Support for Change. Furthermore, Change in Employee Performance emerged as a full mediator of the relationship between Employee Support for Change and Change Outcomes. Originality/Value: Overall, this study makes significant theoretical and practical contributions to the field of change management by clarifying the mechanisms through which Communication of Change enhances Employee Performance and promotes successful Change Outcomes.

The Agility of System-Wide Planned Change Processes: A Reflective Review

ODC
A.B. Rami Shani; Bruce Greenbaum (California Polytechnic State University-San Luis Obispo)
System-wide planned change efforts serve as tools for firms facing threats to their foundational survival and success, particularly given the myriad challenges companies face in ever-changing competitive environments. Companies often look to system-wide planned change initiatives to reconfigure and reconstruct the organization in the face of these threats. One of the often-desired outcomes of organizational change is the ability for the organization to be more agile. As a result of a planned change initiative, organizational agility can better position a firm to recognize and react to changes in their competitive environments. However, the planned change process itself needs to be examined as a critical component of that search for organizational agility. This article reflects on the process agility of five system-wide planned change approaches. This study proposes agility dimensions - tempo and pacing; decision speed and closure; routine flexibility; improvisation and learning capacity; and emotional and cultural enablers – that are used to differentiate the levels of agility of the planned change approaches and to guide further research, action and additional reflection. The value of planned change process agility to both researchers and practitioners is explored and venues for further study are offered.

Outside Pressure, Inside Change: A Multilevel Theory of Movement-Driven Transformation

ODC
Paul A. Raddatz (The University of Memphis)
How do social movements transform the organizations they target? This paper develops a multilevel process model explaining how contemporary movements drive organizational change through recursive, self-reinforcing dynamics. Drawing on social movement theory and organizational scholarship, I identify two complementary tactical pathways: (1) disruptive outsider tactics that create legitimacy shocks and (2) insider activism that translates movement demands into implementable practices. I theorize four cross-level mechanisms through which movement pressures cascade across organizations: top-down (executive responses creating accountability structures), bottom-up (employee innovations in proto-institutional spaces), outside-in (movement metrics reshaping competitive dynamics), and inside-out (reformed organizations becoming change advocates). These mechanisms interact recursively, transforming initial defensive responses into comprehensive organizational realignment. The framework reveals how sustained movement pressure combined with internal advocacy can overcome organizational inertia, ultimately redefining corporate purpose beyond shareholder primacy. Using examples of recent social movements, I demonstrate how movements exploit organizational vulnerabilities while partnering with internal reformers to embed new values and practices, reshaping the role of business in addressing societal challenges.

The Power of the Pause: Harnessing Liminal Intervention Points for High-Impact Change Practice

ODC
Steven H. Cady (Bowling Green State University); Shawn M. Otto (Bowling Green State University)
Organizational change is often treated as a quick succession of actions. However, empirical studies and practical observation tell a different story. High-impact change practice occurs in between actions, during moments of pause. These pauses, as described by liminal space theory, are opportunities for sense-making, emotion regulation, alignment, and innovation. As such, the power of the pause is an essential but underutilized capability in leading change. We present a framework that describes seven liminal intervention points embedded within the three phases of change: preliminal, liminal, and postliminal. Each intervention point highlights how leaders and practitioners can intentionally leverage pause moments to interrupt automatic patterns, surface insight, strengthen psychological safety, and support more adaptive action. Enterprise-level transformation examples illustrate how strategic pauses can accelerate progress and deepen the quality of collaboration. This framework offers scholar–practitioners a practical, human-centered approach to leading change with clarity and intentionality.
