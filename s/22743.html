The Dual Role of Algorithmic Management in the Newcomer Organizational Socialization Process

OB
Wanliang Lin; Mingyu Zhang (Beijing Jiaotong University); Wenjia Zhang (Beijing Jiaotong University)
Algorithms are increasingly automating or supporting managerial functions within organizations, reshaping traditional organizational environments. Can the widespread adoption of algorithmic management accelerate the organizational socialization of human newcomers? This is a practical challenge that all traditional organizations must confront in the new era. To address this challenge, drawing on the agency and communion framework, this study examines the double-edged-sword effect of algorithmic management on newcomer socialization, with achievement goal orientation and relationship orientation as key moderating roles. Data from 339 newcomers at a large Chinese platform enterprise show that algorithmic management promotes task information seeking, which enhances task mastery and, ultimately, increases intention to stay. Conversely, it undermines relationship building, which impedes social adjustment and, in turn, reduces intention to stay. Moreover, newcomers’ relational orientation intensified the communion-side costs of algorithmic management, whereas achievement goal orientation did not significantly moderate algorithmic management’s task-side effect. These findings deepen our understanding of how algorithmic management shapes newcomer organizational socialization and offer practical insights for facilitating newcomer socialization in the workplace.

Employee Perceptions of Job Insecurity from Algorithms and Alternative Cheap Labor Sources

OB
Mehnaz Rafi (University of Calgary); Justin Weinhardt (University of Calgary)
In today’s workplace, employees contend with multiple sources of job displacement, with algorithms representing just one of many evolving threats. Alongside technological threats, employees also face increasing competition from alternative cheaper labour sources, such as outsourcing, offshoring, gig work, and internships. However, despite these overlapping threats of job insecurity, research has not directly compared how employees perceive these different sources of job displacement or their impact on relative performance judgments and algorithm-aid adoption. This study (n = 465) shows that participants experience higher levels of job insecurity and feel more threatened by outsourcing and internships (i.e., cheap labor) than by algorithms. In turn, perceptions of job insecurity influence how they evaluate their relative performance and their decisions to use or rely on algorithm aids. By simultaneously comparing multiple job threats, our research offers an updated view on employee perceptions of sources of job insecurity and challenges in the workplace.

Algorithmic Control and App Workers’ Work-Related Flow: A Regulatory Focus Perspective

OB
qian li (Dalian University of Technology); Jianing Guo (Dalian University of Technology); Lei Lu (Peking University); haitong ? (Dalian University of Technology)
Algorithmic control fundamentally reshapes the workplace of employees in the app-based gig economy. Despite app workers' freelance status, this digital configuration exerts tight control to elicit their optimal effort, and concerns have grown regarding its impact on workers’ optimal state, such as work-related flow. Drawing on regulatory focus theory, this study examines the dual effects of algorithmic control on app workers’ work-related flow. We conduct a two-wave questionnaire survey (N=373) to test the hypothesis. The results show that algorithmic control enhances workers’ work-related flow through promotion regulatory focus but undermines it through prevention regulatory focus. Furthermore, algorithmic errors could strengthen the negative indirect effects of algorithmic control on work-related flow via prevention focus, while weakening the positive indirect effects of algorithmic control on work-related flow via promotion focus. This study contributes new insights into understanding how algorithmic technology shapes app workers’ regulatory focus and subsequent work states.

The Impact of Algorithmic Versus Human Decision-Making on Perceptions of Distributive Justice

OB
Emma Qu (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Yaping Gong (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology)
We investigate the influence of decision-agent type (algorithmic or human) on individuals’ perceptions of distributive justice in decision-making. Our proposed model suggests that interpersonal consistency and evaluative comprehensiveness act as mediating factors in this relationship, but in opposite directions. Drawing on motivated cognition and equity theory, we further posit that social comparison motivation, activated by relative versus absolute outcomes, moderates the mediation pathways. Results from three experiments supported our hypotheses, showing that under absolute outcome scenarios, i.e., when individuals’ outcomes do not include explicit comparisons to referent others, the relationship between decision-agent type (algorithmic or human) and distributive justice perception is more substantially mediated by the negative pathway through perceived evaluative comprehensiveness, while the positive pathway via perceived interpersonal consistency is less influential. However, when outcomes are presented in relative terms, i.e., when individuals’ outcomes include explicit comparisons to referent others, the relationship is primarily mediated through the positive pathway of perceived interpersonal consistency, and the negative mediating role of evaluative comprehensiveness is diminished.
