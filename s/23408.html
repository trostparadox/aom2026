What Is a Business School For? Values, Knowledge Boundaries, and Digital Futures

Session overview

Business schools are caught between competing visions of their purpose as knowledge producers, value shapers, innovation engines, and digital accelerators. This session examines the institutional soul of management education: how business education shapes or distorts student values over time, where the field's knowledge boundaries lie relative to education science, how digitalization is transforming the cognitive demands of management itself, and how global demographic shifts are forcing a rethink of what international business curricula must become. An essential session for anyone asking what business schools must evolve into.

Papers (5)

Becoming Good Managers or Losing Values? A Longitudinal Study of Business vs Non-Business Students

MED
Nosheen Sarwat (Carnegie Mellon University); Muhammad Abbas (National University of Computer and Emerging Sciences)
Business schools shape the values and behavioral orientations of future managers, yet critics argue that management education may unintentionally normalize socially undesirable values. Prior work associates business education with materialism, self-interest, and ethical leniency, but evidence is often cross-sectional, Western-centric, and limited in separating educational socialization from self-selection. We address these gaps with a longitudinal, three-wave study conducted in Pakistan, a collectivistic, Islamic context. Data were collected from undergraduates at major universities at program entry (T1; N = 2,264), mid-program (T2; N = 622), and near graduation (T3; N = 115). Business majors were compared with non-business majors to benchmark selection effects. Independent-samples t tests and one-way ANOVA were used to assess group differences and mean shifts across waves, complemented by longitudinal analyses that adjust for baseline levels. We track attitudes toward cheating, Machiavellianism, narcissism, materialism, greed, corruption tendencies, self-enhancement, selflessness, and life satisfaction as key outcomes during undergraduate training. Across time, prolonged exposure to business education is associated with systematic increases in tolerance for cheating, Machiavellianism, narcissism, materialism, greed, corruption tendencies, and self-enhancement, alongside declines in selflessness and life satisfaction. These patterns persist after accounting for initial differences, consistent with educational socialization rather than selection alone. By extending debates on management education beyond Western settings using rare multi-wave undergraduate data, the study advances theory on value formation in professional education and documents unintended moral and well-being consequences of business curricula. Implications include redesigning curricular content and pedagogy to foreground ethical reasoning, prosocial purpose, and student well-being throughout the degree.

Rewilding Business Education

MED
Lisa M. Hammond (University of South Florida)
This analysis argues that conventional business education remains misaligned with the complex socio-ecological contexts graduates are increasingly expected to navigate. Drawing on mixed-methods evidence from student surveys, practitioner interviews, and classroom action research, it introduces rewilding business education as an integrative pedagogical framework that combines biomimicry, contemplative pedagogy, and ethically grounded engagement with Indigenous knowledge. The paper demonstrates how rewilded pedagogy can cultivate systems literacy, ethical discernment, and relational leadership capacities without sacrificing rigor or accreditation alignment. Illustrative classroom examples are provided, alongside discussion of institutional constraints and ethical safeguards. The paper concludes with implications for curriculum design, faculty practice, and future research in business and management education.

Boundary Work in Management Education: Citation Networks and Cross-Field Exchange

MED
Jeffrey W. Alstete (Iona University); John P. Meyer (Iona University)
Scholars have long argued that management education should draw more systematically on the education sciences while also contributing back to foundational debates in higher education and learning research. Yet cumulative evidence on the extent of this two-way exchange remains limited. This study uses bibliometric techniques to map and quantify citation flows between Academy of Management Learning & Education (AMLE) and five leading education journals from 2002 to 2024. Using Lens.org data and VOSviewer, we conduct a cross-domain direct-citation network analysis of 1,464 AMLE articles (2002–2024) to map intellectual clusters and to quantify directional knowledge imports from, and exports to, five leading education journals. We then operationalize citation imports as references from AMLE to education journals and citation exports as citations from education journals back to AMLE, enabling an assessment of cross-field “trade balances.” The findings reveal a persistent pedagogical trade deficit in which AMLE imports substantially more from the education sciences than it exports, although this imbalance narrows modestly over time. Network visualizations show that AMLE draws heavily on foundational work in experiential learning, critical management education, and organizational behavior, while outbound influence to education journals remains concentrated in a limited set of clusters. We discuss implications for theories of disciplinary exchange, editorial strategies in management education, and authors seeking to write work that travels across fields.

Managerial Development in the Digital Context: A Conceptualization and Research Framework

MED
Xaver Neumeyer (The University of New Mexico)
Digitalization has transformed the foundations of management, altering how opportunities are recognized, evaluated, and pursued. Managers no longer act only with digital tools but through digital infrastructures of data, code, and algorithms that shape perception, motivation, and behavior. Yet research has rarely explored how these transformations affect the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions of managerial action. This paper argues that digitalization not only expands managerial reach but also redefines the mental and affective demands of management. Algorithmic feedback, constant connectivity, and information abundance require new forms of reasoning, emotional regulation, and adaptive behavior. Drawing on research in management, digital literacy, and information systems, we examine mechanisms such as abductive reasoning, digital competence, and technostress to illustrate how digital environments reshape managerial cognition, affect, and behavior. The resulting framework positions digitalization as both an enabler and constraint that continuously molds the technological mindset. In doing so, it provides conceptual grounding for future studies and practical guidance for educators and policymakers seeking to foster adaptive managerial capabilities in a technologically mediated economy.

Demography, Education, and IB Capabilities

MED
Artin Backtash-Rad (University of Surrey)
Profound demographic transformations are reshaping the international business environment, as population aging and workforce contraction in many advanced economies coexist with youthful and rapidly expanding labor forces in parts of the Global South. Although international business research has increasingly acknowledged the strategic implications of these demographic shifts, their consequences for management education systems-the primary institutional mechanism for developing managerial capabilities-remain under-theorized. This paper addresses this gap by examining how and to what extent national-level demographic variation affects management education across countries. Using a thematic analysis of scholarly literature spanning international business, management, education, and labor economics, the study develops an integrative theoretical framework that links national demographic profiles to management education system design. The analysis identifies six core themes that explain how demographic conditions shape the purpose of management education, pedagogical orientation, skill priorities, access and inequality, institutional configuration of business schools, and the global production and consumption of management knowledge. The paper makes a theoretical contribution by positioning management education as a demographically contingent institutional mediator between macro-population dynamics and managerial capability formation. In doing so, it bridges international business and management education literatures and challenges convergence-oriented assumptions about global education models. The framework provides a foundation for future empirical research and offers practical insights for business schools, policymakers, and accreditation bodies responding to accelerating global demographic divergence.
