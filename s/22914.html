Place Identity: A Case of Reclaiming Place (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Rama Krishna Reddy Kummitha
There has been a recent surge in resistance movements against urban rejuvenation projects due to their failure to accurately represent local realities. Citizens seek novel ways to fight against such projects, as they negate what is distinctive locally and subject citizens to a sense of placelessness. By examining the Barcelona theoretical case, where residents resisted austerity-infused urbanism, we investigate the role of place identity in reclaiming the place. We develop an inductive grounded process model of reclaiming place that emphasises three crucial phases – place identity distinctiveness, place identity guardrails and place identity salience – that help reclaim place.

Maintaining Places of Entrepreneurship: The Case of Asan Bazar

OMT
Arahat Sundar Tuladhar (UNSW Sydney); Melodie Cartel; Salih Zeki Ozdemir (UNSW Sydney)
Certain places like Silicon Valley are synonymous with entrepreneurship, famous for being home to thriving ventures and dense funding networks. We refer to these places as ‘places of entrepreneurship’, that is, geographically anchored settings where physical form, local culture, and historically embedded meanings converge to create conditions that systematically enable and amplify entrepreneurial success. Because places of entrepreneurship are vulnerable to disruption we ask: How can places of entrepreneurship be maintained? To answer this question, we investigate the case of the Asan Bazar in Kathmandu, an entrepreneurial hub founded in the 4th century by Newar merchants and renowned in South Asia for its vibrant entrepreneurial activity. Our preliminary findings unveil three mechanisms at play in the maintenance of the Asan Bazar: enforcing boundaries, reclaiming place, and caring for the place. By developing these insights, we aim to advance the emerging literature on place maintenance and deepen understanding of how entrepreneurial geographies can be preserved.

Third Places and Neighborhood Entrepreneurship: Evidence from Starbucks Cafés

OMT
Jinkyong Choi (Columbia Business School); Jorge Guzman (Columbia Business School); Mario Luis Small (Columbia University)
Sociologists have shown that “third places” such as neighborhood cafés help people maintain and use their network ties. Do they help local entrepreneurs, for whom networks are important? We examine whether the introduction of Starbucks cafés into U.S. neighborhoods with no coffee shops increased entrepreneurship. When compared to census tracts that were scheduled to receive a Starbucks but did not get one, tracts that received a Starbucks saw an increase in the number of startups of 9.1% to 22.5% (or 2.9 to 6.4 firms) per year, over the subsequent 7 years. A partnership between Starbucks and Magic Johnson focused on underprivileged neighborhoods produced larger effects. Analyses suggest a networks mechanism is at play.

When Does Business Advice Impact Entrepreneurial Performance? The Role of Place-Based Social Capital

OMT
Inara Tareque (Columbia Business School); Dan Jun Wang (Columbia University in the City of New York)
Advice from institutional channels– such as entrepreneurial support organizations– are vital resources for the growth of small and early-stage ventures. Yet, although prior work shows that advice can help nascent entrepreneurs in several ways, not every founder benefits from such advice. We show that macro-level place-based social factors, such as the level of social capital embedded in one’s community, shapes this relationship. To do so, we analyze data from the New York State Small Business Development Center, examining over 100,000 small businesses that received business advice through formal consultations between 2005 and 2022. Our findings reveal that while more business advice boosts the rate of venture creation and investments into the venture, this benefit is conditional on the concentration of social capital within the nascent entrepreneur’s local community: entrepreneurs embedded in communities with richer networks derive greater benefit from institutional business advice. By bridging research on regional social capital, trust, and entrepreneurial support through business advice, this study demonstrates how place-based social capital shapes the utility of formal advice. We contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how knowledge and social capital interact to drive outcomes for small businesses, particularly those outside the high-growth venture ecosystem.

A Relational Perspective of Neighborhood Status

OMT
Hesu Yoon (Center for Research in Economics and Statistics); Megan Evans
While urban stratification research has long recognized the importance of symbolic processes in shaping neighborhood status, quantitative studies still heavily rely on aggregated demographic and material measures, such as racial composition, median household income, and home values, to describe and explain neighborhood hierarchy. Bridging insights from the status literature in interpersonal and organizational contexts, we propose a network-based approach to measure neighborhood status, which is fundamentally a relational construct. We use the Airbnb data to construct a neighborhood name-dropping network and analyze symbolic status processes among neighborhoods in Chicago, Manhattan, and San Francisco. The findings show that neighborhood prestige is shaped by both underlying neighborhood qualities as well as racialized and classed spatial imageries proxied by neighborhood racial composition and socioeconomic status. Tourist attractions consistently predict network popularity across cities, highlighting the importance of recreational and cultural assets in shaping symbolic neighborhood status. The role of race and class vary depending on city-specific history and culture. While socioeconomic status is a primary differentiator among Chicago and Manhattan neighborhoods, whiteness serves as the key determinant of neighborhood prestige in San Francisco. These findings advance our understanding of how neighborhood status hierarchies are reproduced and constructed through everyday platform practices.
