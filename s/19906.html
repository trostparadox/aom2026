Session overview

Patriarchy is embedded in our unconscious assumptions of what organization and organizing is and it has affected Organization Theory’s (OT) very foundations. It is the cultural sea we swim in. Proceduralized knowledge production perpetuates a disciplined masculine rationality in theorizing (Ann Cunliffe, 2022), and from the standpoint of becoming-theorists, our awareness is sharpening of how patriarchy sediments the theories we draw on, including feminist ones, and the theories we create. Masculinized, we have been taught to put questions about “phallocentrism or sexism”, racism or colonialism “into brackets” (Sara Ahmed, 2017, p. 8). The invitational quest of this symposium is to uncover ways of bringing traumatizing patriarchal assumptions in theorizing into consciousness, to develop healing practices in OT and, by extension, across Management and Organization Studies (MOS) and AoM communities. Patriarchy is a central concept developed and extensively studied in feminist research. In the literal sense, “patriarchy” means “the rule of the father”, the authoritative role of men in heterosexual family structure. Second wave feminist scholars developed patriarchy into a theoretical concept to explain and analyze the nature of women's oppression broadly. This approach was criticized in the late 1980s /1990s for generalizing Western white women’s oppression, excluding black, indigenous, and non-Western women’s experiences. Feminists, including in MOS, travelled towards the term gender, despite risking losing the political sharpness of the concept of patriarchy (Joan Acker, 1989; Nicole Ferry, 2025). Addressing these critiques, intersections of gender relations with “citizenship, culture, ethnicity, identity, location, migration, movement, nationality, place, racialization, religion, and space” have been brought in (Jeff Hearn, 2015, p. 15– 16). Work by Emma Bell et. al., (2019, 2020), Marta Calás and Linda Smircich (2023), Cunliffe (2022), Ferry (2024), Marianna Fotaki & Alison Pullen (2024), Nancy Harding, Jackie Ford & Fotaki (2013), Silvia Gherardi (2018), Lauren McCarthy and Scott Taylor (2024) have re-examined patriarchy within the knowledge production that constitutes MOS, showing how our discipline has been blinded and deafened as a consequence (Fiona Wilson, 1996, in Fotaki & Pullen, 2024). Time is up! (Bell et al., 2019, 2020). Though how do we go about unblinding and undeafening OT without fighting against, but inviting masculinized gatekeepers to journey with us? Entering conversation is a way (Leanne Cutcher et al. 2020), constructive pluralism another (Silviya Svejenova, 2019), and feminist new materialisms offer relational ontologies beyond human (Diana Coole & Samantha Frost, 2010). Trauma is healed with relational ontologies, and we take guidance in Gloria Anzaldúa’s example of bringing together inner work and public acts in change (2015). We are realizing that developing awareness of how patriarchy is in our own heritage of masculinized norms of theory and theorizing, in continued agential cuts, is a task for the becoming-theorist, feminist and otherwise. The symposium considers three ideas. First, the idea that the very way we conceptualize and perform theorizing in OT, even feminist theorizing, is constrained by patriarchal assumptions. Second, that in the feminist theorists we turn to (for example Anzaldúa, Karen Barad, Hélène Cixous and Mary Parker Follett in our case), patriarchy remains a ghost haunting their texts. This unconscious influence of patriarchy is, we believe, at some level traumatizing. Here we are inspired by the work of Kate Lockwood Harris (2025). Harris defines trauma “as an event, context, or a process that disrupts a capacity for relating” (p. 5, emphasis in original). Trauma “may be physical and it can also be verbal abuse, emotional abuse, resource denial, and social alienation" (Thema Bryant-Davis & Carolota Ocampo, 2005 in Harris, 2025, p.5). With compassion, we understand that both those who enact and benefit from these acts, as well as those who endure the brutalities of it, experience trauma (Frantz Fanon, 1952/1967, in Harris, 2025). Traumas can be slow, cumulative processes building up over a career, a lifetime and carried intergenerationally (Bert Hellinger & Gabriele Ten Hövel, 1999). Schooled by PhD studies, journal editors, journal reviewers, conference conveyors we risk becoming blind to our traumas. As an act of contributing to keeping open and advancing the space for “[s]ubjectivist, intersubjective and non-westernized/non-malestream ontologies, epistemologies and ways of theorizing … [in] more situated, contextualized, personal and fluid forms of knowing” (Cunliffe, 2022, p. 9), our attention then moves, third, to imagining how to heal from and transform the traumas embedded in OT. This is an exploratory process bringing out insights relevant for organization theorists in MOS desiring to become aware of patriarchy’s dead hand on OT and how they might consciously see, feel and deal with it. A method of transformation is, as Harris suggests, acknowledging the exteriority-within: “we must know how to work with the existing system in order to change it. Because it’s in us. So, relational work is required, including shifting an entire culture’s internal relationships to ourselves” (2025, p. 229). The format will enact an invitation into the opening of reparative theorizing in MOS, set in motion by panelist presentations responded to in reflective writing and small group discussions, contemplating: Where do we see meaningful openings into relational and reparative theorizing? How can we individually and collectively un/learn and not unconsciously replicate patriarchal theorizing? How do we keep the wound visible as a site of generative transformation without being marginalized? Towards the end there will be a short plenary followed by relational responses from the panelists.
