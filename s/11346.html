Session overview

Discussion skills are typically conceptualized as individual-level communication competencies or observable interactional techniques. However, such skill-based perspectives insufficiently explain why individuals remain silent or disengaged in organizational contexts characterized by hierarchical sensitivity or limited psychological safety. This study reconceptualizes discussion skills as adaptive agency—a dynamic capacity that enables individuals to engage strategically in discussion under constraint. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory (Hobfoll, 1989), we conceptualize discussion skills as a form of resource regulation through which individuals conserve, mobilize, and deploy communicative resources in interactionally demanding environments. From this perspective, discussion is not merely a display of competence, but an adaptive response to perceived social and relational risk. While psychological safety represents a shared contextual condition that facilitates interpersonal risk-taking (Edmondson, 1999), it does not fully account for individual variation in participation under comparable conditions. Adaptive agency captures this variation by explaining how individuals selectively engage, regulate participation, and manage discussion- related risk. Empirically, the study draws on survey data collected from Japanese organizational contexts. Preliminary analysis focuses on patterns linking hierarchical position, organizational tenure, and perceived psychological safety to discussion-related adaptive agency. The findings suggest that discussion practices function as context- sensitive design choices rather than as transferable techniques. Overall, this study contributes to organizational behavior research by extending COR theory into communication processes and by offering a design-oriented reconceptualization of discussion skills as adaptive agency.
