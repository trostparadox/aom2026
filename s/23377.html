Why Do Men Leave? Feminist Protests’ Impact on Gender-Specific Employee Turnover Rate

STR
Xiaowen Tan (The University of Texas at Dallas); Cuili Qian (The University of Texas at Dallas); Riki Takeuchi (The University of Texas at Dallas)
This study examines how feminist protests influence employee turnover in organizations. Based on an integration of stakeholder theory and social information processing theory, we argue that large-scale feminist protests send social information that values gender equality. This raises employees’ (male in particular) expectations about firms’ gender practices, and the awareness of inequality in the workplace, and employees choose to leave their firm, leading to a higher level of the firm’s turnover rate. Using the context of Women’s March protests from 2017 to 2022 and employee turnover records from online professional profiles, we show that the scale of feminist protests increases firms’ male employee turnover rates. We further show that the influence of feminist protests on male employees’ turnover rate is weakened for firms that are internally aligned with the protest movements (i.e., firms with a higher female board ratio and with diver-sity and quality policy) but is strengthened when firms are less aligned with the protest goals (i.e., firms with negative media coverage and workplace safety violations). Our findings contribute to research on social movements by uncovering a new mechanism through which social activism can reshape organizations through employee turnover, moving beyond the external institutional pressure that literature has mainly focused on. Our study also joins the conversation with turnover literature by identifying a critical yet neglected external force—social activism.

Understanding Firms’ Timing of Cybersecurity Breach Disclosure Under the Stakeholder Theory

STR
Marcelo J. Alvarado-Vargas (University of Toledo); Maria Lapeira (Suffolk University); Sumit Kumar Kundu (Florida International University)
This paper analyses the conditions surrounding firms that take longer to disclose cybersecurity breaches. We draw upon stakeholder theory and consider conditions related to the power, legitimacy, and urgency of various stakeholders affected by cybersecurity incidents to understand the time it takes for firms to disclose a cyberattack in the aftermath. Analyzing data from U.S. firms that have experienced and disclosed cybersecurity breaches and using Fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (FsQCA), we find four configurations related to the delayed disclosure of cybersecurity breaches. We argue that the presence of a severe cybersecurity breach makes firms more likely to delay the reporting of cybersecurity incidents. The results lend support to the stakeholder explanation for firm decision-making involving disclosure and offer insights into how differences in power, legitimacy, and urgency of various stakeholders could be among the reasons for the differences in the timing of cybersecurity breach disclosures.

When Values Invite Vulnerability:Organizational and CEO Political Ideology as Signals in Cyberattack

STR
Andy Yangxiaoxue Mei (University of Houston); Steve Sauerwald (University of Houston)
Why do some firms attract more cyberattacks than others? Drawing on stakeholder and screening theories, we conceptualize hackers as a powerful but illegitimate stakeholder group that selects targets based on organizational and CEO political ideology as signals when information is limited. We argue that organizational and CEO political ideologies influence hacker behavior in a staggered manner. In the first stage, hackers use broad, visible signals, such as an organization’s political ideology, to choose targets. Liberal-leaning organizations, which signal openness and responsiveness to stakeholders, are attacked more often than conservative ones. In the second stage, following the initial cyberattack, hackers gain richer information. Hackers then use more detailed signals, such as the CEO’s political ideology, to decide how intensely to attack the firm. Firms with liberal CEOs suffer more severe attacks than those with conservative CEOs. We also argue that strong social performance and media controversies amplify these effects by increasing firms’ visibility and reputational salience. Using a two-stage hurdle model on 12,073 firm-year observations from S&P 1500 companies (2014–2022), we find supportive evidence for these effects across multiple model specifications and alternative cyberattack measures.

Weaponizing Activism: Why Do Firms Collaborate with Social Movement Organizations?

STR
Kate Niu
Research on private politics theorizes a firm's motivation to collaborate with social activists as a defensive logic: the desire to reduce stakeholder hostility and shape a more favorable nonmarket environment for themselves. Our study, in contrast, argue that collaboration with social activists can also be driven by an offensive logic: We posit that firms can leverage these collaborations as a competitive tool to undermine their rivals in their market environment. Drawing on competitive dynamics literature and social movement theories, we theorize that firms can gain a competitive advantage by directing social pressure toward competitors and they can achieve this by collaborating with social activists, a tie through which the firm gains access to redirect the activists’ focus toward the firm’ rivals. Using sigwatch data, we find that a firm's prior-year collaboration with a specific social movement organization (SMO) is positively associated with the likelihood that the SMO will subsequently target that firm’s competitors. This effect is stronger under conditions where firms have more to gain by damaging their rivals’ reputations, such as when competitors are highly similar and performance pressure is intense.

When Stakeholder Engagement Isn’t Enough: Home-Country Reputation and Infrastructure Projects

STR
Andy Buschmann (University of Michigan); Jake B. Grandy (University of Arkansas)
Why do some foreign infrastructure projects provoke intense social opposition despite extensive stakeholder engagement, while others proceed with relatively little resistance? We examine when and how firms’ home-country reputation shapes host-community approval of large-scale infrastructure projects, and whether firm-level stakeholder engagement can overcome reputational disadvantages. Integrating insights from nonmarket strategy, corporate social responsibility, and international business, we argue that stakeholder engagement is not uniformly effective: when a firm’s home country is poorly regarded, engagement may yield diminishing returns or even backfire by triggering suspicion and attribution of manipulation rather than responsiveness. We test these arguments in the context of foreign infrastructure investment in Myanmar during its democratic opening (2010-2021), a period marked by heightened civil society mobilization and geopolitical contestation. Leveraging access to the full population of approximately 1,500 large-scale foreign investment proposals submitted to the Myanmar Investment Commission, we use machine-learning and natural language processing techniques to extract detailed project characteristics and de jure stakeholder engagement practices from project proposals and contract documents. We spatially and temporally match these infrastructure projects to georeferenced protest events to analyze social mobilization against them. Across three complementary studies combining observational and survey data, we show that projects led by firms from negatively perceived home countries face systematically higher protest risk than comparable projects from better-regarded countries, even after accounting for stakeholder engagement practices. Our findings reveal fundamental limits to firm-level stakeholder engagement as a nonmarket strategy and demonstrate how country reputation conditions the political risks of global infrastructure investment.
