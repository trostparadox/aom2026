When Mandates Meet Meaning: How Behavioral Meaningfulness Differentially Moderates the Impact of In

ONE
Guo-hua Lin (Chaoyang University of Technology); Angela Shin-yih Chen (National Taipei University); Trung Kim Nguyen (Phenikaa University)
This study examines how behavioral meaningfulness moderates the impact of employee green behaviors (EGBs) on well-being and work attitudes. Drawing on Social Identity Theory and meaningful work literature, we distinguish between in-role (mandated) and extra-role (voluntary) green behaviors. Using a 10 consecutive workdays daily diary design (N=173), multilevel analysis reveals a critical boundary condition: in-role EGBs enhance well-being, engagement, and job satisfaction only when employees perceive the behaviors as personally meaningful. Conversely, extra-role EGBs consistently yield positive outcomes regardless of meaningfulness levels. By integrating social identity with individual sensemaking, this research provides a nuanced explanation of the micro-foundations of CSR. Practically, the findings suggest that organizations must imbue mandated sustainability tasks with purpose while fostering discretionary green initiatives to cultivate a thriving and committed workforce.

Nourishing or Backfiring? The Effects of Biophilic Work Design on Employee Green Service Innovation

ONE
Zhiyuan Ma (Nanjing University of Science and Technology); Xia Liu (Wuhan University); Zheng Jiayi (Nanjing University of Science and Technology)
With the growing demand for green service innovation in the service industry, exploring the advantages of integrating natural elements into workspace design has become increasingly critical. Drawing on attention restoration theory and attachment theory, we propose that exposure to biophilic work design can enhance green service innovation behaviors by fostering employees’ green emotional attachment and green mindfulness, conditional on green performance appraisal. Findings from an experimental study and a field survey reveal that biophilic work design positively influences employees’ green emotional attachment, which in turn enhances green mindfulness and ultimately promotes green service innovation behaviors. However, organizational green performance appraisals negatively moderate the relationship between biophilic work design and employees’ green emotional attachment. This research not only deepens the understanding of the complex mechanisms underlying employees’ green service innovation behaviors but also cautions organizational practitioners against excessive green performance appraisals, which may inadvertently limit employees’ behavioral agency.

How and When Green Visionary Leadership Promotes Employee Voluntary Green Behavior

ONE
Wei Li (East China Normal University); Zhonghuai Cao (East China Normal University); Zhenyuan Wang (East China Normal University)
Amid escalating pressures to address climate change, organizations increasingly rely on employees’ voluntary green behavior. Drawing on cognitive-affective processing system (CAPS) theory, this study examines how green visionary leadership promotes employees’ voluntary green behavior. We propose a moderated dual-mediation model in which green visionary leadership enhances voluntary green behavior through a cognitive pathway (green self-efficacy) and an affective pathway (green passion), and these effects are contingent on perceived leader behavioral integrity. Using a multi-wave, multi-source field study design, we collected matched data from 390 employee–supervisor dyads across multiple industries in China. Results reveal that green visionary leadership positively predicts employees’ voluntary green behavior, both directly and indirectly via green self-efficacy and green passion. Moreover, leader behavioral integrity strengthens the effects of green visionary leadership on both mediators and amplifies the corresponding indirect effects. Our current research advances theoretical understanding in the fields of behavioral ethics and green visionary leadership while delivering managerial implications for enhancing voluntary workplace green behaviors.

From Training to Moral Responsibility: How Eco-Education Shapes Green Behaviour at Work and Beyond

ONE
Dagmara Lewicka (AGH University of Kraków); Helena Starowicz-Rajca (AGH University of Kraków); Pawel Zajac
As global environmental challenges intensify, organizations are increasingly expected to act not only as economic actors but also as moral agents in addressing environmental responsibility. Eco-education has emerged as a prominent organizational tool for fostering employee green behaviour (EGB). While prior research has largely focused on its effects within the workplace, considerably less is known about how eco-education translates into pro-environmental behaviour beyond organizational boundaries, particularly in the home setting. This study examines how eco-education influences EGB at work and in private life, with particular attention to the mediating roles of perceived organizational support for the environment (POS-E) and green autonomy. Building on an ethically informed extension of the Ability–Motivation–Opportunity (AMO) framework, the study emphasizes the role of organizational legitimation and discretion in enabling environmentally responsible behaviour. Survey data were collected from 412 employees of a sustainability-oriented automotive R&D centre in Poland and analysed using structural equation modelling (CB-SEM) and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA). The results show that eco-education does not directly predict EGB in the workplace; instead, its effect is fully mediated by POS-E. In contrast, eco-education has a direct positive effect on green behaviour in the home setting. Green autonomy emerges as a significant predictor of workplace green behaviour but is not directly shaped by eco-education, indicating that organizational conditions play a critical enabling role. Strong spillover effects from workplace green behaviour to private environmental practices are also confirmed. FsQCA results further reveal distinct causal patterns across domains: while workplace behaviour is driven by equifinal pathways involving either POS-E or green autonomy, home-based behaviour rely on specific interactions between eco-education and workplace spillover. Overall, the study contributes to business ethics and sustainability research by demonstrating that eco-education is effective not as a standalone intervention but when embedded in supportive organizational contexts. The findings highlight how organizations shape environmentally responsible behaviour both within and beyond the workplace, raising important questions about the scope and boundaries of organizational influence.
