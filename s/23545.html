Session overview

This session focuses on the evaluative processes through which gender shapes status, credibility, and career opportunity. The papers examine youth cues, communal expectations, visibility of penalties, overwork, and the signaling value of prior promotions. Together, they show how gendered interpretations of competence and commitment continue to influence rewards, promotion, and workplace standing.

Gendered Youth Spotlighting: How Youth is Communicated as a Low-Status Cue in Organizations

DEI
Sonya Mishra (Tuck School of Business)
Although society encourages youthful appearances in women, in organizational contexts, where experience and competence are valued, young women report experiencing their youth as a liability. This contrasts with recent evidence showing that evaluators judge young women more favorably than young men, raising the question of why young women nonetheless report more negative youth-related experiences at work. Across three studies (N = 5,728) spanning field, qualitative, and experimental methods, this research argues that the tension arises from gendered youth spotlighting, defined as the tendency for young women (versus young men) to have their perceived youth, and the lower status it implies, highlighted at work more often. Study 1 showed that under 40 women in a large U.S. government sample were nearly twice as likely as men to report experiencing age-based discrimination, which in turn predicted stronger turnover intentions. Study 2 found that women MBA students reported more frequent youth spotlighting than men and, in qualitative accounts, interpreted these remarks as questioning their competence. Study 3 experimentally manipulated youth spotlighting and found that it elicited greater competence-based social identity threat among young women relative to young men, which in turn increased discrimination attributions. Together, these findings reveal how a diffuse status cue—youth—can be interpersonally activated more often for women, shaping how they interpret workplace outcomes. This research advances theories of status and identity threat by showing how seemingly positive interpersonal feedback can activate low-status cues, trigger cross-identity threat spillover, and contribute to early-career perceptions of discrimination and turnover.

Communal Expectations as a Source of Ingratiation, Burnout, and Turnover Among Women

DEI
Emily R. Ye (New York University); Lisa Michelle Leslie (New York University); Aastha Chadha; Colleen Flaherty Manchester (University of Minnesota)
Gender stereotypes dictate that women are and are expected to be communal (warm, compassionate, friendly), but not agentic (competent, ambitious, assertive). Prior work documents that low agency stereotypes have negative consequences for women’s careers, while paying relatively less attention to high communality stereotypes, and specifically the possibility that women’s react to communality stereotypes in ways that are costly for their careers. We propose that gender stereotypes lead women to believe that others expect them to display high levels of communality. Others’ expectation that women should be communal, in turn, motivate women to engage in higher levels of ingratiation—an impression management strategy intended to convey warmth and likeability—relative to men. We furthermore propose that women’s use of ingratiation as a strategy to conform to others’ stereotypic expectations is positively related to burnout and turnover. Our theorizing collectively suggests communality stereotypes are costly for women’s careers, in that they contribute to higher turnover rates among women than among men. We provide support for these ideas in a multi-source field study of employees of a large agrobusiness organization and a nationally representative study of employees from different organizations in the U.S. Our work advances understanding of how gender stereotypes constrain women’s work outcomes and provides new insight into the strategies women use to manage gender stereotypes and their associated career consequences.

When Women Are Ideal Workers: The Gendered Impact of Overwork on Wages and Promotion

DEI
Yeaseul Hur (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Despite women’s progress in human capital, gender inequalities in wages and promotion persist. Recent studies have attributed the disparities to women’s failure to meet firms’ expectations for overwork, assuming women would receive equal returns if they worked as many hours as men. We question and test this assumption by taking an innovative approach—conceptualizing overwork as exceeding workplace-specific time norms rather than surpassing absolute hours (e.g., 50 or more hours per week). Analyzing linked employer-employee data from 47,160 workers across 650 Korean firms between 2006 and 2022, we find that overworking women achieve higher wages and faster promotions compared to other women but still lag behind men who overwork to the same extent. We also find that when women deviate from time norms, they experience lower wages and slower promotions than men who deviate. We argue that these patterns reflect the gendered evaluation of work hours: due to bias about women’s low commitment, women face a higher bar, or double standards, in demonstrating commitment, while penalties intensify when their behavior appears to confirm this stereotype. Our findings highlight the role of organizational processes in sustaining gender disparities, moving beyond supply-side explanations such as time investment differences between men and women.

Bias-Amplified Signals: How Past Promotions Help Women’s Advancement in Engineering

DEI
Yuval Spiegler; Lakshmi Ramarajan (Harvard Business School)
Women’s advancement in male-dominated settings is rare and often stymied by gender bias in evaluations. We investigate how women’s past career achievements influence their advancement prospects over time in male-dominated settings. Drawing on signaling theory, we propose that in male-dominated settings, women’s prior accomplishments can serve as “bias-amplified signals,” whereby a positive achievement (such as past promotion) becomes more potent because it is viewed as being costlier for women to obtain. Using a unique longitudinal archival dataset from the Eclipse open-source software ecosystem, and a sample of 592 engineers who had already gained a promotion in this setting, we find that prior promotions significantly increase women’s chances of future advancement more than men’s. We supplement this data with an experiment in which evaluators assessed fictitious candidates whose gender and promotion histories varied, and who were either in a male-dominated or gender balanced context. Results reveal that the positive effect of past promotions on perceived promotability is contingent on those achievements occurring in male-dominated contexts. Our findings suggest women’s career accomplishments are perceived as more valuable precisely because they are seen as harder won in these environments. This study expands conventional accounts of gender bias in evaluations by introducing bias-amplified signals as a mechanism that can paradoxically facilitate women’s advancement in organizations and demonstrates how context and career history jointly shape evaluation outcomes.
