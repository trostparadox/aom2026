An Integration of Job Embeddedness and Proximal Withdrawal States Theories (WITHDRAWN)

OB
Vesa Peltokorpi (Hiroshima University); David G. Allen (Texas Christian University)
Two important theoretical advancements in understanding voluntary turnover suggest that employees become embedded in their organizations through a web of on- and off-the-job forces, and experience varying degrees of desire and perceived control over staying and leaving their organizations in terms of proximal withdrawal states. Although having complementary aspects, previous research has largely examined job embeddedness and proximal withdrawal theories in isolation. In this paper, we integrate these theories to examine how proximal withdrawal states mediate the relationships between job embeddedness and two outcomes: (1) job applications and (2) voluntary turnover. We also examine the indirect moderating effects of job alternatives on job applications and turnover through proximal withdrawal states. Findings from 1441 employees in a range of organizations provide general support for our hypotheses. This paper contributes to the literature by demonstrating that not all embedded employees stay in their organizations and that job embeddedness might also have negative consequences by “trapping” employees in their organizations.

Revisiting the Social Exchange Contract: How Employee Compensation Levels Shape Operational Efficien

OB
Xingxin Zhao (Anhui University of Finance and Economics); Xinru Ma (Sichuan University); Jinbin He (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
Firms increasingly recognize employee compensation as a strategic lever for operational excellence, yet evidence on whether higher wages enhance operational efficiency remains limited. This study revisits the social exchange contract, proposing that wages serve not only as economic incentives but also as relational signals that foster trust, cooperation, and process discipline. Drawing on Efficiency Wage Theory and Social Exchange Theory, it models the relationship between employee compensation and operational efficiency and tests it using a firm-level panel dataset and an instrumental variable approach based on an income tax policy change. The results confirm that higher relative wages significantly improve operational efficiency, particularly in smaller, younger firms, those rich in organizational capital, and under competitive market conditions. Furthermore, a transparent and trustworthy business environment amplifies these efficiency gains. Overall, the findings demonstrate that fair and competitive wages act as both financial and social mechanisms, aligning employee behavior with organizational efficiency and reinforcing the cooperative foundations of high-performing firms.

From One Foot Out to One Step Up: When Online Development Inverts the Effects of Turnover Intentions

OB
G. James Lemoine (State University of New York at Buffalo); Hamed Ghahremani (University of New Orleans); Jarvis Smallfield (Central Michigan University); Danielle Tussing (University at Buffalo - SUNY); Jeremy D. Meuser
Organizations have increasingly offered online development platforms as a means to retain and promote employees. Whereas they may be especially beneficial for employees who are planning to leave the organization, it is unclear whether such employees will actually engage in online development. Drawing on human capital theory, we predict that employees who intend to leave, as reflected by high turnover intention, will only engage with online development offerings when they have high technological self-efficacy. We further integrate substitutes for leadership theory, positing that the presence of servant leadership may undermine online development, rendering these employees less likely to take advantage of it. We explore interactive effects of these moderators, as well as downstream consequences of turnover behavior and promotions one year later. We test our predictions in multi-wave field study with over 1,000 employees and objective mediators and outcomes. Our research contributes to the contingency perspective on the link between turnover intention and behavior, advances nascent scholarly work on online development, and answers calls to expand substitutes for leadership research. We conclude by discussing practical implications for managers seeking to address retention challenges.

When Suffering Inspires: The Impact of Experiential Sharing on Newcomer Inspiration in Organizations

OB
Jingni Dong (Renmin University of China); Yueheng Wang (Renmin University of China); Yeun Joon Kim (University of Cambridge); Yuman Hong (Renmin University of China); Long Zhang (Hunan University)
Experience sharing is a common organizational practice for newcomer socialization. Prior research has predominantly focused on the motivational effects of sharing expertise with newcomers, while overlooking the potential benefits of sharing experiences of suffering. Drawing on social comparison theory, we propose that incorporating elements of suffering into experience sharing can enhance newcomers’ upward assimilative emotion (i.e., inspiration), which in turn promotes their job performance, proactivity and creativity. Furthermore, we identify the hierarchical level of the sharer as a critical boundary condition: suffering narratives shared by lower-level employees are more likely to evoke inspiration among newcomers and subsequently lead to greater improvements in their work outcomes. We tested our hypotheses through a 2 × 2 field experiment conducted in a real organizational setting, using dyadic data from 112 newcomer–coworker pairs collected at two time points. Path analysis results provided robust support for our theoretical model. The implications for newcomer socialization, social comparison theory and suffering literature are discussed.

A Latent Profile Analysis of Boundary Spanners’ Dual Organizational Identification and Behaviors

OB
Yan Qiao (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); PAN FAN (Harbin Institute of Technology); Fuli Li (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Haoyu Wang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Boundary spanners are traditionally theorized as bridges between their employer and the client organization, yet empirical observations reveal a puzzling behavioral spectrum—ranging from integrative behavior to unethical acts that unilaterally benefit their employer (UPE) or, more paradoxically, their client organization (UPC). To explain this behavioral divergence, we introduce a dual identification configurational approach to propose that boundary spanners can be categorized into distinct profiles that predict their divergent behaviors. Through one field study and two online surveys comprising 1,957 boundary spanners, our latent profile analyses reveal that these employees sort into six subpopulations based on their dual identification patterns: four quantitatively (integrated, moderate, diffuse, and low dual identification) and two qualitatively (dominant employer and dominant client identification) distinct profiles. We found that those with integrated dual identification engaged in more integrative behavior, whereas those in the dominant employer (client) identification profile enacted more unethical pro-employer (pro-client) behavior. By reframing boundary-spanning employees through the lens of dual-identification profiles, this research provides a novel framework that explains bridge-building and bridge-burning behaviors in cross-organizational roles.
