Do Place-Based R&D Subsidies Increase Skill Demand? Evidence from Online Job Postings (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Lisa Keding (RWTH Aachen University)
Innovation policy has increasingly shifted toward place-based approaches that aim to foster regional transformation by building local capabilities, particularly workforce skills. Despite this shift, existing evaluations of public R&D subsidies focus mainly on innovation outputs, employment levels, or firm growth, providing limited evidence on whether such policies induce capability formation inside firms. This paper examines whether and under which conditions public R&D funding supports capability building in the local workforce. We study the German WIR! program, a place-based policy targeting economically lagging regions that funds regional innovation consortia. Exploiting the program’s multi-stage selection process, we compare funded organizations with "near-winners" that just missed final funding to identify causal effects. Using a difference-in-differences design and data on online job postings from 2016–2022, we track organizations’ labor and skill demand as early indicators of capability formation. We find that funded organizations post significantly more vacancies after receiving support, with particularly strong increases in high-skilled and innovation-related occupations. These effects are driven by consortia pursuing path branching, while no comparable effects are observed for path modernization.

National Innovation Policy and the Governance of Technology: The Case of Space Technology

TIM
Mark E Keough (IE University); Petteri Leppänen (IE University)
Drawing on transaction cost economics (TCE), this paper proposes a framework that explains alternative pathways for the development of strategic technologies. We conceptualize technology policy as a governance problem in which nation choose among alternative strategies: technology sovereignty, market reliance, or hybrid arrangements. Strategic choice is based on two factors: economic impact of the technology, which determines dependency costs, and the nation’s ability to compete, which determines capability development costs. We apply the framework to the evolution of space policies among major space powers at three points in time (1966, 2023, and 2040). We observe a general movement towards technology sovereignty, but some nation-states are better served by hybrid strategies, such as alliances or niche. Our analysis reveals that despite Europe’s technological capability, under-spending relative to economic rivals risks a deteriorating competitive position, forcing Europe into alliances to access space independently. The paper makes two contributions. First, we advance work on technology sovereignty by proposing a TCE-based framework that operationalizes when sovereignty strategies are both justified and feasible. Second, we contribute to the literature on the business of space by illuminating the policies of major space powers over time, demonstrating the growing centrality of space technology in national innovation policy.

Targeted R&D Policy and Innovation Ecosystem Dynamics: Evidence from the National Quantum Initiative

TIM
Amit Das (University of Oregon); Lauren Lanahan (University of Oregon)
Targeted R&D subsidies aim to accelerate strategic technologies, yet their broader ecosystem consequences remain poorly understood. We argue that these policies create artificial markets that generate concentrated demand shocks for specialized, inelastic labor. This triggers a dual allocation mechanism where: (1) factor market competition reallocates scarce knowledge workers from adjacent domains to the targeted domain, and (2) political access stratifies which organizations capture policy-created gains. Using the U.S. National Quantum Initiative Act in 2018 as a quasi-natural experiment, we analyze USPTO patents and federal lobbying records from 2010 to 2024 to examine these claims. We report that quantum computing patents surged almost 16-fold post-policy, while adjacent technological domains declined in annual patent growth. These patterns resulted from reallocation of high-productivity inventors from adjacent domains. Moreover, the inventors produced almost half of quantum patents despite representing only one quarter of inventors, indicating adjacent domains lost their productive, boundary-spanning researchers. Altogether, we contribute to innovation scholarship by revealing how the political construction of markets and factor market competition jointly transform targeted innovation policy into redistributive reallocation, rather than net expansion of innovative capacity.

Racing to the bottom: the impact of technology and patent races on product quality

TIM
Rohin Vrajesh (Bocconi University); Aldona Kapacinskaite (INSEAD); Keyvan Vakili (London Business School)
Technology and patent races are widely viewed as mechanisms that spur competition, increase R&D investment, and facilitate follow-on innovation. Yet, little is known about their implications for product quality. We theorize that stronger incentives to accelerate invention and patenting can compromise product quality by encouraging firms to prioritize speed over thorough development. Using data linking patents to the products that embody them, we show that greater pressure to “rush to the patent office” leads to patents associated with lower-quality products. Exploiting the U.S. transition from a first-to-invent to a first-to-file patent system under the America Invents Act as an exogenous shock to filing incentives, we find that medical device firms experienced a significant increase in product recalls. By uncovering a trade-off between speed to patent and product quality, this study broadens the literature on technology races and intellectual property rights, highlighting an important downstream cost of accelerated innovation.
