Counted and Committed: Embedding Proportional Incentives in Democratic Organisations

OMT
Nuno Gil; Felipe Massa (University of Vermont); Maria Cristina Sousa (The University of Manchester)
Democratic organizations pursuing social change rely on voluntary participation and distributed authority to coordinate collective action, yet sustaining commitment is difficult when outcomes are uncertain and collective effort must be maintained over the long haul. Movement organizers and much of the existing literature on distributed governance have treated proportional incentives as incompatible with democratic organizing, emphasizing moral obligation and belonging as alternative bases of commitment that don’t corrode democratic values. We study Leste, a housing collective in São Paulo, Brazil, that combines democratic governance with a point-based system linking participation to priority in housing allocation. Drawing on interviews, archival materials, and participation data for approximately 13,000 families over more than a decade, we show how proportional incentives can be embedded within a democratic participation architecture. In Leste, points render effort visible and consequential without commodifying participation because differentiation is collectively governed, transparent, and open to revision. The findings demonstrate how democratic organizations can sustain commitment under prolonged uncertainty by governing not only participation, but the meaning of unequal outcomes themselves.

The Promise and Reality of Democratic Organizing: Vote Delegation

OMT
Ying-Ying Hsieh (Imperial Business School, Imperial London); Helge Klapper (Purdue University); Robert Janjic (University of Zurich)
Organizations that value direct member involvement and democratic principles often rely on formal voting procedures for decision-making. Given that participation in such processes is costly, some collectives adopt vote delegation regimes where voting rights can be transferred. While this is supposed to facilitate and sustain engagement, the effects on overall levels of participation are still unclear. To that end, we examine how the introduction of vote delegation in decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) affects members’ voting behavior. Drawing on data from more than 5,000 organizational decisions, results of the empirical analysis show that while some individuals use the newly available channel, overall participation goes down, suggesting that increases in delegation do not compensate for the reduction in direct voting. This illustrates the challenges that organizations face in terms of how to employ democratic elements in their decision-making processes in a sustainable manner.

From Decision to Default: Programmed Power and the Architecture of Digital Governance

OMT
Shuang Liu (Georgia State University)
Digital platforms increasingly govern social and economic life not through formal decision processes but through infrastructural presets that eliminate decisions altogether. This paper introduces the concept of programmed power to theorize how digitally mediated systems exercise control not through overt managerial authority but through silent defaults embedded in algorithms, interfaces, and technical architectures. Moving beyond existing accounts of algorithmic decision making, we argue that platforms produce order by filtering communication, suppressing reflexivity, and removing the conditions for contestation. Drawing on systems theory and platform studies, we show how this shift from programmes to presets transforms organizational governance by eliminating decision moments and replacing legitimation by procedure with legitimation by execution. We develop four interlocking mechanisms: encoding, filtering, suppression, and closure to explain how control is distributed across platform infrastructures. Theoretically, the paper advances a systems-theoretic critique of algorithmic governance by reconceptualizing how power, communication, and contingency are managed in digitally mediated environments. Practically, it offers a vocabulary for diagnosing the effects of platform design on autonomy, accountability, and systemic reflexivity.

Melting the Iron Cage: The Infrastructure of Distributed Control

OMT
Simon Altmejd (McGill University)
In contemporary market conditions, organizations often pursue decentralization through collaborative digital infrastructures that promise autonomy. Yet these infrastructures are frequently experienced as tighter monitoring. This paper reframes this paradox by studying decentralization through the lens of organizational control. Drawing on the STS concept of infrastructuring and Beniger’s notion of a “crisis of control”, I argue that decentralization is not the pursuit of autonomy per se, but a reconfiguration of control in response to complexity. I develop this perspective through a three-year ethnography of leadership succession at Trucker Inc, a large family business in the trucking industry. After a breakdown in the family-based clan control system that had prevailed for generations at Trucker Inc, the new leadership group launched a decentralization effort centered on a collaborative digital infrastructure to distribute decision-making. Rather than enabling local initiative, this effort produced standardization. I explain this outcome through a three-step theoretical model. First, the crisis of control was diffused from owners to workers and managers. Second, these actors sought to address this crisis by becoming invested in local networks of accountability. Third, participation in these networks created an imperative to cultivate “good” data, which performed standardization as emergent rather than from top-down planning. I call this phenomenon distributed control. This study contributes to conversations on decentralization and organizational control.

Finding the Right Stride: Navigating Autonomy and Control in Self-Organizing Communities

OMT
Elie Abi Saad (Umeå University); Sujith Nair (BI Norwegian Business School); Mayank Gupta
This paper examines a unique form of community-based organizing in which decentralized, volunteer-led groups operate autonomously while remaining embedded within a broader organizational structure. Drawing on an inductive case study of “parkrun”— a global initiative for promoting and sustaining local running communities—we analyze how individual communities balance the need for local autonomy and centralized control as they self-organize over time. Our findings reveal three self-organizing tensions: operational, volunteering, and membership, which local communities continually grappled with and negotiated during their evolution. We show that the co-existence of autonomy and control is not indicative of a static design choice but rather a dynamic, ongoing accomplishment that is actively enacted as communities develop. We integrate these insights into a process model that explains how self-organizing communities are sustained over time.
