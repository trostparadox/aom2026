FDI and Exploitation? Navigating Institutional Complexity in Countries With Weak Labor Rights

IM
Chris Michael Jones (Aston University); Ha-Phuong Luong (University of Reading)
This research investigates why some multinational enterprises invest in countries with weak labor rights protection. Firms that emphasize cost-leadership and intangible asset-based strategies are more likely to expand into these markets. Yet, companies based in countries with stronger labor market institutions show greater restraint. These findings suggest that business strategy and home-country norms jointly shape where firms invest. For managers, the results highlight the trade-offs between cost and resource-based advantages with reputational risk. Investing in environments with weak labor rights creates long-term legitimacy challenges, emphasizing the need for stronger internal governance and transparent labor practices to ensure responsible global expansion.

Mitigating Exposure to Cascading Social Hazards Along Global Value Chains

IM
Simon Peter Iskander (University of Toronto, Rotman School of Management); Anthony Goerzen (Queen's University); Christian Geisler Asmussen (Copenhagen Business School); Niron Hashai (IDC Herzliya)
Recent and upcoming pieces of legislation propose to hold multinational enterprises (MNEs) legally liable for social and environmental violations anywhere along their global value chains (GVCs), creating a significant managerial problem: MNEs are now exposed to both financial and reputational hazards from transactions beyond their organizational and geographical boundaries, which cascade throughout their GVCs and ultimately harm the lead firm. How, then, can MNEs mitigate their exposure to these ‘cascading’ social hazards (CSH) along their GVCs? While MNEs conventionally rely on cascading contracts to manage transactional hazards, we argue that CSH represent a distinct category of hazard that cannot be detected – let alone remedied – by conventional governance approaches. We develop a formal contracting model, identifying the hypothesized conditions under which internalization becomes the transaction-cost-economizing approach to mitigate exposure to CSH. To test our hypotheses, we adopt a novel methodological approach, linking 365,820 GVC-level activities to the internalization decisions of 9,029 lead parent firms and their 207,054 subsidiaries across 240 industries in 168 countries between 2018-2022. Consistent with our model, we observe that MNEs in reputationally-sensitive industries deviate from conventional internalization theory, instead preferring to ‘discontinuously’ internalize upstream activities in response to high CSH along their GVCs.

Stakeholder Identification and Inclusion in Ecologically Embedded Global Production Networks

IM
Christopher R Whynacht (University of Massachusetts Boston)
Environmental degradation is becoming increasingly disruptive to global business as we exceed critical ecological boundaries. One reason this excessively heavy human footprint is placed on ecosystems is that many perspectives of the global economy, and models of international business, frequently treat the environment as an externality. This encourages increased profit-taking at the expense of the natural environment. This conceptual paper provides support for internalizing ecological actors in global economic models. Global Production Networks (GPNs) are a powerful approach to studying relationships within systems of production. The descriptive and explanatory power of GPNs relies on the degree to which these networks extend beyond pure economic or industrial connections to include more actors who represent diverse stakeholder perspectives. Using that framework, an Actor-Network Theory (ANT) inspired ecologically inclusive approach is presented to also bring in non-human actors, i.e. the ecological production network. This paper provides methodological support on identifying and incorporating the actions and influence that ecological stakeholders have on the GPN. The ecologically inclusive approach demonstrates a way of making GPNs more environmentally grounded thereby extending the power and scope of the method.

When Does Foreign Investment Advance Human Development? A Local Capability Threshold Model

IM
Kristian Hoelscher (Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO)); Anne Jamison (Copenhagen Business School)
Foreign direct investment is often promoted as a pathway to better lives, yet communities experience foreign projects unevenly. We develop a local capability threshold perspective that explains when and where FDI improves human development and when it instead erodes it. We define local capability as a place’s preexisting bundle of human capital, public goods, and institutional capacity that enables residents and local organisations to recognise, assimilate, and exploit external resources brought by multinational enterprises. Using panel evidence from Africa that links project-level investment data to gridded human development measures, we estimate the effect of foreign entry on subsequent local human development changes after matching treated and untreated locations. Results show that, on average, foreign entry does not raise human development and often reduces it in low-capability regions, while effects become negligible or mildly positive in higher-capability regions. Quantile and interaction analyses converge on the same implication: foreign investment’s societal impact depends on whether communities cross a local capability threshold. Conceptually, we introduce a local capability threshold mechanism that explains heterogeneous FDI effects on human development and integrates embeddedness and absorptive capacity in a place-based theory of welfare change.
