Incentivized to React? When CEO Wealth Sensitivity Shapes Competitive Behavior

STR
Oleksandr (Alex) Tsaruk (University of Idaho)
A CEO’s motivation to counteract rivals’ aggression is vital in competitive dynamics, as an inadequate response can harm the firm’s performance. Agency theory suggests that compensation can incentivize CEOs to act in the firm’s best interests. We show that the CEO compensation structure affects how the firm responds to rivals’ aggressiveness. We introduce CEO wealth sensitivity to stock price (delta) and stock return volatility (vega) as differential characteristics of CEO compensation that influence competitive response to rivals’ aggressiveness. Using a panel of S&P 500 firms, we found that the relationship between rivals’ and the consequent focal firm’s competitive aggressiveness is contingent on the CEO’s wealth sensitivity. The study introduces the agency mechanisms to competitive dynamics and CEO wealth sensitivity as a contingency for a firm’s competitive behavior. Keywords: executive compensation; wealth sensitivity; agency theory; competitive dynamics

New CEOs’ Severance Pay

STR
Conor Callahan (University of Illinois at Chicago); Anthea (Yan) Zhang (Rice University); Anastasiya Zavyalova (Rice University)
Most CEOs at U.S. public companies receive severance pay agreements at the time of their appointments, but CEO severance pay remains among the most controversial forms of executive compensation as it can be viewed as a “reward for failure.” The debate surrounding CEO severance pay has been amplified due to mixed conclusions about how these agreements influence subsequent organizational outcomes. Drawing upon agency theory, we propose that since severance pay agreements intend to reduce newly appointed CEOs’ employment risk concerns, greater severance pay agreements will be associated with increased risk-taking. An unintended outcome of greater severance pay, however, is extreme firm performance. We further expect that these relationships will be amplified when newly appointed CEOs also receive greater stock options pay, as severance pay protects CEOs from downside risk while stock options provide upside incentives for CEOs to take risk. To test these predictions, we manually collected data on severance pay agreements for 1,036 CEOs appointed in 2008–2017. Using a two-stage least squares regression approach, we find support for our theoretical arguments. Our study reveals important insights into how severance agreements affect newly appointed CEOs’ strategic choices.

Board Adaptation to Passive Investment: Aligning CEO Pay with Passive Investor Preferences

STR
Joel Andrus (University of Arkansas); Felipe Calvano (University of Illinois at Chicago)
We develop theory about how exogenous increases in passive investment can lead managers to seek out directors who have experience with these investors. We then show how firms appoint these directors to the compensation committee, where they help align firm governance practices with the preferences of these powerful, albeit passive, investors. The result is that firms increase their use of pay-for-performance contracts for the CEO, which should increase shareholder value. However, this also increases the CEO's excess compensation. We contribute to the board adaptation literature by highlighting how firms seek out new board members who have prior experience with these passive investors. The selection of such directors provides a previously unknown, if indirect, pathway to the boardroom through which passive investors can influence corporate governance mechanisms.

Green Ripples Effect: The Impact of Internal Pay Dispersion on Green M&As

STR
Chunhui Cao (Shanghai International Studies University); Weizhe Huang (Shanghai International Studies University); zhao liming (Wenzhou University)
In recent years, the practice of corporate green mergers and acquisitions (M&As) has been in full swing, demonstrating unique advantages and playing a crucial role in the green transformation of enterprises. This study empirically examines the impact of internal pay dispersion between executives and employees on green M&A activities, using a sample of listed companies in China from 2010 to 2024. The findings reveal an inverted U-shaped relationship: as the internal pay dispersion increases, the likelihood of enterprises engaging in green M&A initially rises before declining. Additionally, external executive pay gap, employee mistreatment and organizational slack moderate this relationship. Specifically, when executives perceive their compensation to be lower than the industry average, employees feel a greater sense of unfair treatment, and organizational slack is higher, the inverted U-shaped relationship becomes significantly steeper. This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of internal pay dispersion and its implications for green M&A, offering valuable insights for both scholars and practitioners.
