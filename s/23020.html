Corruption Exposure and Corporate Social Performance: Evidence from Multinational Firms

IM
John Fan Zhang (Macau University of Science and Technology); Jacky Y. C. SO (Macau University of Science and Technology)
This paper investigates how multinationals’ exposure to foreign corruption cultures influences their social performance. Using a sample of U.S.-listed multinationals from 2004 to 2014. empirical results reveal a significantly negative relation between corruption exposure and social performance. This relation remains robust across 12 econometric methods — including instrumental variable estimation, Heckman selection model, propensity score matching, and difference-in-differences — which address endogeneity issues like unobserved firm traits driving location choices and CSR performance. Further analysis shows the negative effect is only significant in non-financial crisis periods, while the degree of internationalization (measured by the number of foreign countries with subsidiaries) exhibits no consistently significant association with social performance. These findings highlight that underlying national culture such as corruption, rather than internationalization itself, shapes multinationals’ social performance, resolving prior debates on the internationalization-CSR link and bridging macro (national corruption) and micro (firm CSR) research on corruption’s consequences.

When Global Experience Backfires: How International Leaders Contribute to CSI

IM
Xin Tan; Stacey Fitzsimmons (University of Victoria)
International professionals, such as immigrants and returnees, have been socialized in multiple institutional logics. When employed in leadership roles within emerging markets, they are often portrayed as carriers of legitimate practices that facilitate positive institutional change. However, despite this presumed positive role of global talent, the dark side of their exposure to alternative institutional logics manifests in heightened moral ambiguity at a micro-foundational level. Analyzing a longitudinal panel data set of Chinese publicly traded firms from 2007 to 2023, we find that firms with greater representation of international professionals within their top leadership teams exhibit more corporate social irresponsibility (CSI) incidents. The relationship is stronger when leaders’ international experience comes from (1) institutionally distant countries, which introduces more conflicting institutional logics; (2) countries with weaker institutions, which import logics that normalize lower compliance standards; and (3) a broader portfolio of countries among the top leadership team as a whole, which heightens heterogeneity of logics within the top leadership team. Our study advances micro-level foundations of CSI research, institutional logics, and upper echelons theory by revealing the paradoxical consequences of international professionals; while they bring the benefits of international perspectives to their emerging market firms, they may also introduce more social irresponsibility.

Corporate Social Irresponsibility and Sales Performance of Domestic vs. Foreign Firms

IM
Jing Gu (Xiamen University); Juan Bu (Indiana University Bloomington); Yuntao Bai (Xiamen University); Mingjun CAI (Indiana University)
This study investigates the differential impact of corporate social irresponsibility (CSI) incidents on the sales performance of domestic versus foreign firms. Drawing on Black Sheep Effect, we theorize that because customers perceive domestic firms as in-group members, a CSI incident is viewed as a profound betrayal, leading to a more intense customer reaction. This more severe response imposes harsher penalties on domestic firms, forcing them to divert resources from core operations to crisis management. Consequently, domestic firms experience a more significant negative impact on their sales performance than foreign firms. Moreover, we find a paradoxical effect in which sustainability at the country level amplifies the negative sales impact on domestic firms, while a firm's own sustainability performance can mitigate it. Using a comprehensive sample of 115,465 firm-year observations from global listed firms between 2013 and 2021, our empirical findings support these arguments. This study introduces Black Sheep Effect as a novel mechanism to explain the liability of localness in market reactions to CSI.

Black Sheep at Home: The Twin Pitfalls of Distinctive and Dissimilar CSI Across Borders

IM
Jihun Choi (Korea University); Young-Kyu Kim (Korea University); Taewoo Roh (Hanyang University)
Corporate social irresponsibility (CSI) is a critical risk for multinational enterprises (MNEs), yet whether overseas CSI meaningfully affects home-country outcomes remains unclear. We argue that what matters is not only CSI intensity, but cross-national CSI patterns that shape how domestic stakeholders interpret a firm’s conduct. Integrating attribution theory with the black sheep effect under U.S.–China rivalry, we predict stronger penalties when U.S. MNEs’ CSI is high in the U.S. but low in China, a pattern that can be interpreted as ingroup betrayal rather than context-driven deviation. We also predict that greater CSI dissimilarity across countries strengthens the negative association between domestic CSI and domestic earnings. Using RepRisk CSI data merged with CRSP/Compustat and MSCI, two-stage least squares models with a Heckman correction support both predictions: domestic CSI most strongly depresses subsequent domestic earnings when CSI is high in the U.S. but low in China, and CSI dissimilarity further exacerbates this penalty.

Internationalization Scope across Environmental Standards and Firm Carbon Performance

IM
Xiaoyu Yu (Shanghai University); Man Hu (Shanghai University)
While decades of research have linked internationalization scope to multiple forms of firm performance, little is known about its effects on carbon performance. Drawing on reverse knowledge transfer theory, this study examines how internationalization scope, distinguished by host-country environmental standards, affects firms’ carbon performance. We categorize internationalization scope into expansion toward countries with standards superior versus inferior to China’s. The results show that expansion into higher-standard countries enhances, whereas expansion into lower-standard countries weakens, parent-firm carbon performance. Moreover, green innovation strengthens the positive effect, while carbon disclosure mitigates the negative one. By linking internationalization scope with environmental heterogeneity, this study advances understanding of how multinational expansion shapes firms’ sustainability outcomes and extends RKT theory to the context of environmental sustainability.
