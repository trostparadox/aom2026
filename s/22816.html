Biogeddon: Double-Edged Connectivity Between Biodiversity and Resilience

SIM
Leyla Azizi (Dresden University of Technology); Rajat Panwar (Oregon State University)
Biodiversity loss is a core Earth-system boundary that amplifies pressures across other processes and undermines the ecological functions on which economies depend. It weakens societal and economic resilience by eroding the natural systems that sustain production and well-being. Firms can counter this trend by integrating biodiversity into strategy to strengthen socio-ecological resilience. Methodologically, this study conducts a systematic literature review that synthesizes natural- science research into an integrative agenda to help scholars and managers reorient research and practice. Direct and indirect drivers interact synergistically, while resilience arises from coupled biophysical and social-ecological mechanisms. Management can expand the viable operating space by reducing local stressors and by designing functional connectivity without triggering pressure thresholds. Therefore, resilience depends on governing connectivity: maintaining links that enable recovery, while disrupting pathways and interfaces that transmit cumulative harm and prevent threshold crossings.

Financing and Governance in the Clean Energy Transition:Evidence from SDG 7.a Scholarship

SIM
Anshul Verma (S. P. Jain Institute of Management and Research, INDIA); Amol Dhaigude; Soma Amol Dhaigude (National Institute of Technology Karnataka)
The global transition to clean energy is central to sustainable development and climate action, yet progress remains uneven across regions and institutional contexts. This study examines how financing mechanisms, technological innovation, and governance arrangements have shaped the scholarly discourse surrounding Sustainable Development Goal 7.a, which emphasizes international cooperation, clean energy research, and technology transfer. Drawing on a comprehensive corpus of peer-reviewed research within the business and management domain, the study systematically maps the evolution of global scholarship on SDG 7.a to identify dominant themes, influential actors, and emerging research frontiers. The analysis reveals a dual focus in the literature: one stream emphasizing technological advancement and innovation diffusion in clean energy systems, and another centered on economic, financial, and institutional mechanisms that enable or constrain energy transitions. Further, the findings highlight the central role of global collaboration networks— particularly involving China, the United States, and the United Kingdom—in shaping research agendas and knowledge flows. By integrating insights on finance, innovation, and governance, this study advances understanding of the social and organizational foundations of clean energy transitions and identifies critical gaps related to institutional capacity, intermediary actors, and equity in emerging and developing economies. The study offers a research agenda to inform future scholarship and policy interventions aimed at accelerating inclusive and sustainable clean energy transitions worldwide.

Resource Extraction and Depletion: Natural Resource Dependence Perspective

SIM
Niamh M. Brennan (University College Dublin); sean power (Montpellier Business School); Adrian Pablo Zicari (ESSEC Business School)
Organizational views of natural resources have shifted over time, from an exclusively consumptive approach towards long-term sustainable management perspectives. We illustrate this shift in perspective using a historical case. We mobilize natural resource dependence theory to analyze our case, developing a conceptual model for that purpose. The case company, the Forestal Land, Timber and Railways Company (La Forestal), a British company listed on the London Stock Exchange, made tanning extract from quebracho trees in Argentina, depleting 3-million acres of forests over a 63-year period (1905-1968). The destruction also threatened the Company’s viability, and it had to pivot to survive. We analyze how the Company’s perspectives on nature evolved over time. La Forestal escaped failure by reinventing its business model, switching to more sustainable wattle/mimosa plantations and forestry practices in Africa. In our chronological periodization, we examine how the Company framed its existential struggles in the Chairmen’s speeches. La Forestal handsomely rewarded its British shareholders in the Global North with dividends. However, the consequences of the Company’s operations in the Global South for the local environment and communities in Argentia were devastating, with stakeholders in the Global South disproportionately harmed. Our historical case represents a microcosm of present-day sustainability issues.

Linking Corporate Governance to Climate Finance

SIM
Tetsekela Anyiam-Osigwe; Sherwat Elwan Ibrahim (American University Cairo)
The corporate sector continues to grapple with securing a climate-resilient future. We introduce the concept of “climate-conscious” boards as a benchmark for assessing the readiness of corporate governance structures to manage climate-related risks and opportunities. A board is climate-conscious when it demonstrates a commitment to climate-specific governance, indicated by the presence of a climate advocate, an internal climate-related committee, membership in external corporate climate clubs, and a commitment to achieve net zero. Using cases of three major commercial banks in Africa and triangulating board-level data, official documentation, and semi-structured interviews between 2015 and 2024, we explore the implications of climate-conscious boards for firms’ access to external climate finance, including green bonds and multilateral climate-linked loans. While access to such finance depends on factors beyond the board’s control such as regulatory frameworks and creditor capacity, we find that board characteristics can create an enabling governance structure. Our findings are structured into two themes: internal board dynamics and external perceptions of the board. This analysis highlights the role of climate-conscious governance in pioneering banks and provides avenues for future research on corporate climate governance in and beyond Africa.

Managing Legitimacy: Sustainability Strategy in Choosing a Unified Certification Provider for Financ

SIM
Qing Hui; Xiaojie Wu
Typically, companies seek separate external certification for the credibility of financial and non-financial information to gain legitimacy. However, we observe that some companies choose to hire the same Certification provider for dual certification. Specifically, drawing on social exchange theory, we find that companies hiring the same Certification provider for both ESG and financial certification do so with the intent of managing legitimacy and are willing to incur higher legitimacy costs. This effect is more pronounced in firms with a higher probability of financial restatements, those with higher Certification provider reputation, and in cases involving the hiring of a new Certification provider. Our study is significant for sustainability as it provides a novel theoretical framework to explain how corporate decision-makers’ intention to merge financial and non-financial information certifiers for legitimacy management leads to higher costs. Keywords: Managing legitimacy, Sustainability, Social exchange theory, Legitimacy costs, Certification provider, Organizational reputation
