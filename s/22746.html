The Higher They Are, the Harder They Fall: A Process View of Changes to Nurses’ Commitment

OB
Frances Jorgensen (Royal Roads University); Adelle Bish (US Army War College); Mette Strange Noesgaard (Aalborg University)
In this paper, we present a longitudinal process study that illustrates changes in nurses’ commitment to various targets throughout the COVID-19 pandemic. Our qualitative study includes three waves of interviews (March 2020 to June 2024) with 35 registered nurses from Canadian hospitals. Our findings reveal that pandemic-related factors such as changing job demands, interpersonal relationships, and public sentiment influence both the level of commitment experienced by the nurses and their commitment to self-identified targets of commitment. These same factors appeared to be associated with intensified commitment to nursing in the early stages of the pandemic to re-prioritizing commitment targets and eventually leading to quondam commitment for some. This paper contributes by providing a process view of changes in multi-foci commitment, we provide new insights into commitment systems theory, illustrating some unexpected changes to targets and antecedents to quondam commitment and add to the scant literature on the dark side of commitment. Practical implications for how managers might mitigate the impact of a crisis on nurses’ commitment are included.

Reluctant Compliance: How Compliance and Contract Breach Shape Organizational Identification Change

OB
David R. Hekman (University of Colorado-Boulder)
Most research on organizational identification assumes it develops when employees cognitively resonate with favorable organizational attributes. We extend this view by showing that identification can also increase through behavioral compliance-based rationalization, particularly when employees hold negative evaluations of the organization. In a longitudinal study of 117 physicians adopting a mandated secure messaging technology, we examine whether employees increase identification after adopting new role behaviors despite experiencing psychological contract breach. Results show that new role adoption predicted increases in organizational identification specifically among physicians who perceived psychological contract breach. These findings extend and enrich the dominant perception-based view of identification and reveal a darker pathway through which identification can strengthen via rationalization of reluctant compliance, with implications for understanding identification under adverse organizational conditions.

Observed Justice Change: Cognitive Motives Underlying Observer Responses to Organisational Change

OB
Xiaomin Xu (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen); Changqin Lu (Peking University); Xizhi Liu (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen)
Organisational change is pervasive yet frequently unsuccessful due to employees’ resistance. Although organisational justice is an established predictor of change acceptance, prior research has largely adopted a recipient perspective, focusing on employees’ own treatment. This approach overlooks the fact that organisational change unfolds in a visible and uncertain environment in which employees actively observe how coworkers are treated and how such treatment changes over time. To address this gap, we develop a dual-motivation model of observed justice change – changes over time in how fairly coworkers are treated. Integrating the deontic justice model and fairness heuristic theory, we propose that observed justice change is cognitively interpreted through two concurrent pathways: a moral-concern perspective of person–organisation moral value fit and a self-concern perspective of organisational trust. We further examine how employees’ other-orientation and self-orientation shape the relative salience of these pathways. Two multi-wave field studies supported our model: observed justice change uniquely predicts counterproductive work behaviour and organisation-directed citizenship behaviour, above and beyond personally experienced justice and emotional reactions. This research extends justice literature by conceptualising observed justice as a dynamic, socially embedded signal that shapes employee responses via cognitive evaluations that simultaneously reflect moral and self-concerned considerations.

A Longitudinal Analysis of the Effects of Job Content Plateaus on Employee Personality Change

OB
Linh Bui; Thao Phan Hanh Nguyen (VinUniversity)
Job content plateaus—a form of work stagnation in which employees perceive few opportunities for challenge or growth—are increasingly common in contemporary organizations. Extending prior research that has primarily examined their short-term consequences for employee well-being, the present study investigates their long-term implications for personality development. Drawing on cognitive dissonance theory and the person–environment transaction perspective, we propose that increases in job content plateaus heighten mental health challenges over time, which subsequently shape personality changes by making employees more neurotic, less extraverted, less conscientious, and less agreeable. We further argue that these indirect effects are more pronounced among employees with a strong mastery orientation, who may experience greater dissonance when confronted with prolonged stagnation. Using latent difference score analyses on two five-wave longitudinal samples from the HILDA survey (Sample 1: 3,307 employees; Sample 2: 2,056 employees), we found support for our hypothesized model. Overall, our findings suggest that personality change serves as a long-term adaptive response that enables employees to cope with job content plateaus in organizational settings.
