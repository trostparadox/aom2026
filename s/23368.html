Beyond Influence: The Role of Corporate Political Activity in Reconfiguring Fossil Power from Within

ONE
Claire-Lise Speisser (CNAM)
Research on corporate political activity (CPA) has predominantly conceptualized it as an outward-facing strategy aimed at influencing public policy. We challenge this view by examining how CPA also operates as an internal political process that reshapes organizational dynamics in contested industries. Drawing on a longitudinal ethnographic study of a fossil-fuel multinational engaged in climate action and low-carbon initiatives, we develop a multi-level process model that explains how CPA contributes to the reconfiguration of fossil power from within. Our findings show how actors mobilize CPA to support emerging climate-oriented activities, triggering dynamics of organizational autonomization that are subsequently met by strategic containment from top management. In response, actors engage in hybrid political–business entrepreneurship that re-politicizes climate action at the field level, generating system-level effects that feed back into organizational dynamics. By theorizing CPA as a form of system-oriented political work that simultaneously enables and constrains climate transformation, this study advances research on CPA and the organizational politics of climate action. We contribute to management scholarship by showing that corporate political activity does not merely defend incumbent interests externally actively structures organizational and system-level pathways of change in fossil-dominated organizations.

Voluntary Sustainability When Political Conditions Support Regulation: The Case of Port Gree

ONE
Jette Steen Knudsen (Tufts University); Elizabeth DeSombre (Wellesley College); Molly Elder (Tufts University)
Under what conditions do organizations adopt greening measures? Economic and political factors like wealth, democracy, decentralization, and corruption can influence the shape of sub-state greening behavior. We examine the content, extent, and determinants of port decisions to take on a set of 22 greening measures in the busiest 209 seaports in the world over the period 2005-2020 using data we collected. Greening measure adoption varies widely. We find that most measures are adopted by fewer than half the ports examined and are most frequently undertaken without regulatory mandate or incentive. We generate and statistically evaluate a set of hypotheses about state-level factors that could contribute to the likelihood of port greening. Our findings suggest that voluntary adoption is most likely when the political conditions for mandatory regulation would otherwise be strong—specifically, decentralized democracies with locally affected populations. These conditions may create incentives for ports to act preemptively rather than face regulation.

When CEO Political Ideology Matters: Institutional Consonance and Facility Environmental Performance

ONE
Youn Seo Jo (University of Kansas); Chang Hoon Oh (University of Kansas)
This paper examines how CEO political ideology translates into facility-level environmental performance and how that relationship depends on subnational institutional context. While prior research largely emphasizes firm-level outcomes, we argue that realized environmental performance is produced through operational decisions at geographically dispersed facilities that face heterogeneous local pressures. We collect a longitudinal panel data of U.S. manufacturing facilities, and our final sample includes 13,278 facility-year observations covering 2,206 facilities owned by 188 publicly traded firms from 2011-2022. From our analysis, we find no robust direct relationship between CEO liberalism and facility emissions. However, consistent with an institutional contingency perspective, CEO liberalism is associated with lower facility emissions in more liberal states, suggesting that political alignment strengthens the translation of CEO preferences into operational outcomes. Disaster exposure is linked to lower emissions on average, but its interaction with CEO liberalism runs counter to our directional expectation, implying that shock-driven pressures may alter the marginal role of leadership ideology. Overall, the findings extend the upper echelons theory by identifying place-based institutional conditions that shape when CEO ideology matters at the operational level.

Political Ideology and Carbon Emissions Along Supply Chain

ONE
Jingru YU (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Haibin Yang (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
How do firms respond to regulatory pressure on carbon emissions? Firms may propagate sustainability practices to suppliers (i.e., decarbonization propagation) or transfer pollution upstream (i.e., carbon outsourcing). We identify a key driving force to reconcile these opposing perspectives by contending that liberal CEOs tend to propagate sustainable practices to suppliers, while conservative CEOs prioritize shareholder value by shifting carbon emissions. Utilizing California cap-and-trade program as the research context and analyses of U.S. public firms from 2007 to 2018 support this argument. We further investigate how CEO restricted stock value and ESG institutional ownership moderate the relationship between CEO liberalism and supplier firms’ carbon emissions. This study contributes to corporate social responsibility and political ideology literature by highlighting CEO political ideology as a key factor in firms’ responses to carbon regulation.
