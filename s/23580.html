Artificial Intelligence Use in the Workplace and Employee Innovative Work Behavior

TIM
Ruizhe Hou (University of Amsterdam); ZHONGXU LIU (Fudan University); Yanjing Li (University of Chicago); Jiexuan Zhang; Xuanhong You (Rutgers University-New Brunswick); CHENGKAI PAN (East China University of Science and Technology)
Driven by digital transformation, artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in workplace activities, exerting complex influences on employee behavior. While AI can enhance efficiency and decision quality, it may also generate uncertainty and psychological strain. This study examines how workplace AI use affects employee innovative work behavior by introducing approach-oriented and avoidance-oriented job crafting as dual mediating mechanisms, and task interdependence and power distance as contextual moderators. Using survey data from employees in manufacturing, service, internet, and financial industries, the results show that workplace AI use positively predicts employee innovative work behavior. This relationship is partially mediated by approach-oriented job crafting and negatively mediated by avoidance-oriented job crafting. Moreover, task interdependence and power distance significantly condition these indirect effects. Overall, this study advances research on AI and organizational behavior by clarifying the behavioral pathways through which AI shapes employee innovation and offers practical insights for managing AI-driven workplace transformation.

Beyond Helping or Hurting? Dual Appraisals of Workplace AI, Skill Readiness, and Employee Well-Being

TIM
Marsela Thanasi (American University of the Middle East); David Urbano (Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona)
As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes routine in workplace decision-making, employee outcomes diverge. This study explains when workplace AI enhances versus undermines employee well-being. We develop and test a dual-appraisal model in which employees experience workplace AI as decision-support value (a resource) and/or as threat (a stressor). Drawing on cognitive appraisal theory and conservation of resources, we argue that these appraisals shape AI-related work well-being partly through AI skill readiness and that training conditions their effects. Using the OECD AI Worker Survey (2022; N = 2,083) across seven high-income economies and two sectors (finance and insurance; manufacturing), we estimate a covariance-based structural equation model. Decision-support value is positively associated with well-being, and readiness strongly predicts well-being while partially mediating the value pathway. Threat appraisal is negatively associated with well-being yet modestly increases skill readiness, yielding a small positive indirect effect that does not offset direct harm. Training support is not uniformly protective: it strengthens the decision-support value–well-being association but intensifies the negative threat–well-being link. Together, the findings shift attention from AI adoption to an appraisal-and-capability framework of AI-enabled work well-being, showing that training can both enable value realization and amplify strain under threat.

The Double-Edged Sword Effect of Artificial Intelligence on Knowledge Workers’ Innovative Behaviours

TIM
Keyu Chen (Zhejiang University)
The rapid diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI) assistants into workplace processes has sparked a contentious debate regarding whether these technologies stimulate or suppress human innovation. Grounded in self-determination theory, this study investigates the double-edged effects of AI assistants on knowledge workers’ innovative behavior. Analysis of data collected through a situational experiment involving 400 employees across seven Chinese enterprises reveals the coexistence of opposing mechanisms. Specifically, AI-assistant intelligence enhances innovative performance both directly and indirectly by reducing perceived work intensity. However, highly intelligent AI assistants simultaneously trigger personal development concerns, thereby inhibiting innovative behavior. Crucially, organizational AI readiness functions as a critical boundary condition that amplifies both the positive pathway of reduced workload and the negative pathway of development concerns. By explicating how AI assistants can concurrently bolster and undermine innovation, this study contributes to the “AI-driven innovation” debate and offers insights for maximizing the benefits of AI while mitigating its drawbacks.

When Algorithms Do Not Standardize: Strategic Differentiation Among High-Skilled Professionals?

TIM
Xiaoyan Liang; Qijie Xiao (Monash University); Liang Wen (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Nadia K Kougiannou (Nottingham Trent University)
Management scholarship predominantly characterizes algorithmic management as a force of standardization that flattens organizational hierarchies and compels workers toward uniform compliance. Yet, this “convergence thesis” fails to explain why high-skilled professionals—endowed with distinct cultural capital—exhibit divergent, rather than homogenized, responses to digital control. To resolve this paradox, we adopt a Bourdieusian lens to examine the “digital field of care” within China’s internet hospitals. We reveal that the platform does not function as a level playing field but as a refraction of the traditional order, where strategic engagement is determined by professional habitus. We identify three strategic archetypes: Symbolic Maximisers, elite physicians who reverse-engineer the algorithm to convert digital metrics into offline domination; Economic Pragmatists, lower-tier physicians who suffer “algorithmic entrapment,” reproducing their subordinate status; and Bricoleurs, middle-tier physicians whose strategic indifference inoculates them against platform discipline. Our study theorizes the digital platform as a mechanism of social reproduction rather than disruption, challenging the “algo-economicus” perspective by demonstrating how the “old world” hierarchy is re-inscribed through new digital rules.
