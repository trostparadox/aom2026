Equal Leave, Equal Outcomes? Evidence from a Symmetric Parental Leave Reform

DEI
Isabel Villamor (IESE Business School); Aaron Martinez (IESE Business School); Marta M Elvira (IESE Business School); Halil Sabanci (Frankfurt School of Finance and Management)
Despite substantial recent progress, the gender pay gap persists across countries and widens at the transition to parenthood, when mothers’ earnings slow while fathers’ earnings remain stable. Earlier parental-leave reforms have produced some convergence in employment outcomes but often stop short of full parity in leave entitlements, leaving the underlying labor-market dynamics that produce gender inequality largely unchanged. This study examines whether policy symmetry (i.e., identical, fully paid, non-transferable, and partly mandatory for both parents) is associated with differences in employment outcomes, earning trajectories, and broader career patterns after childbirth. Drawing on nationally representative employer–employee data, we estimate the effects of Spain’s 2019–2021 symmetric parental leave reform, the first in a major economy to mandate fully equal conditions. We apply difference-in-differences and event-study models to identify reform-induced changes in post-birth earnings and employment continuity. After the reform, our results show that the gender gap in post-birth earnings narrowed by roughly half, driven primarily by mothers’ stronger employment continuity rather than declines in fathers’ earnings. These findings reveal that when parental interruptions are structured equivalently for both parents, gendered career outcomes can converge. More broadly, the study refines social-role and ideal-worker perspectives by showing that symmetric parental leave access removes gender as a signal of expected absence, reshaping both parental labor-supply behavior and organizational responses to parenthood, thus altering key labor-market processes that generate gender differences in careers.

All AI Are Not Created Equal: Artificial Intelligence, Gender Diversity, and Social Performance

DEI
Zhenzhong Ma (University of Windsor); YUFAN YANG (Harbin Institute of Technology); dongbo wang; Israr Ahmad (Harbin Institute of Technology)
The exponential growth of artificial intelligence (AI) has reshaped contemporary business landscape. Drawing on institutional theory and the attention-based view, this study develops a framework to examine the distinct roles of analytical AI and automatic AI in enhancing gender diversity and firm performance (market value, green innovation, and ESG performance). Using data from publicly listed firms, our analysis reveals that while both analytical AI and automatic AI enhance firm performance, only analytical AI acts as an augmenting tool in improving gender diversity. Automatic AI, in contrast, shows no significant effect on gender diversity. In addition, female representation in top management positively moderates the impact of analytical AI on gender diversity. This study advances understanding of differential roles of analytical AI and automatic AI in promoting gender diversity and achieving sustainable performance. The findings offer important insights for future research directions and practical implications in leveraging AI technologies to empower vulnerable employees.

Who Gains from Instructor-Led Generative AI Integration? Gender and Cross-National Context Impacts

DEI
Ying Gao (Troy University); Guilin Zhang (Troy University)
Generative AI is rapidly entering university classrooms, yet its benefits and burdens may be unevenly distributed across student groups. We develop and test an equity-relevant process model of instructor-led generative AI integration that links students’ pre-assignment attitudes to post-assignment attitudes, perceived learning effectiveness, and intentions to use AI in future coursework. Survey data from 215 undergraduate business students who completed a structured, instructor-guided ChatGPT-supported assignment across two campuses (United States and Vietnam) support a sequential pathway: initial attitudes shape post-assignment attitudes, which in turn predict perceived learning effectiveness and downstream intentions. Importantly, these relationships vary by social context. Attitude consistency is stronger among male students, suggesting that a single structured experience shifts women’s evaluations more than men’s. Further, the link between post-assignment attitudes and perceived learning effectiveness differs across campus contexts, consistent with students weighing ethical/pedagogical concerns versus instrumental supports, such as language and academic scaffolding, differently. By specifying when and for whom instructor-led AI integration produces stronger learning value and adoption intentions, this study advances DEI-relevant theorizing on sociotechnical inequities in AI-enabled learning and offers design guidance for culturally responsive and gender-attentive AI pedagogy.

Words That Exclude: Gendered Verbiage as a Mechanism of Entrepreneurial Inequality

DEI
Vishal K. Gupta (University of Alabama); Golshan Javadian (Morgan State University); Crystal Dobratz (Colorado School of Mines); Alka Gupta
Gender inequality persists in entrepreneurship, yet the role of language in shaping entrepreneurial evaluations remains poorly understood. Drawing on social role theory and social dominance theory, we examine whether gendered verbiage appears in entrepreneurship texts and how such language influences new venture assessments. Using mixed-method design, we analyze popular press articles, academic research publications, and teaching cases, and conduct an experiment assessing new venture evaluations. Across all text genres, we find that masculine wording significantly outweighs feminine wording, regardless of industry gender-typing. Experimental results show that ventures described using masculine language are evaluated more favorably, perceived to have greater long-term growth potential, and granted higher cognitive legitimacy than those described with feminine language. These effects hold irrespective of the entrepreneur’s gender. Together, our findings reveal a linguistic mechanism through which gender inequality is reproduced in entrepreneurship and highlight how seemingly neutral texts can systematically advantage ventures aligned with masculine norms. Keywords: gendered verbiage, entrepreneurship texts, new ventures, gender inequality

The Wakanda Factor: African Diaspora Studies as a Key to HBCU Graduation Rates and Management Education

DEI
Tyechia V Paul (Fayetteville State University)
This paper examines whether predominantly and historically black colleges and universities’ (PHBCU) graduation rates differ between those that require all students so complete at least one course in Africana Studies and those that don’t. It performs quantitative analysis of readily available data from the National Center for Education Statistics and university websites, to determine that there is a statistically significant difference in gradation rates. It extrapolates the psychosocial professional qualities that students gain from such courses, and how that benefits them and their employers. It also explores why this finding is important for universities, business schools and management programs. Keywords: Black history, Africana studies, workforce development, management, education
