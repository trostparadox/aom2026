Walking the Talk: How Interactions Condition Supplier Learning for Supply Chain Sustainability (WITHDRAWN)

OSCM
Marlene Zeitler (University Ulm)
Supplier development is a widely used approach aimed at disseminating sustainability expectations across multi-tier supply chains. Prior research has primarily examined the adoption and performance effects of supplier development practices, paying limited attention to the interactional processes through which suppliers interpret and enact sustainability requirements. This study adopts an interactional perspective to examine how buyer-supplier interactions shape learning and the dissemination of sustainability practices. Using an abductive qualitative design, we analyze 26 semi-structured interviews with first-tier suppliers and original equipment manufacturers in the German automotive sector, complemented by supplier-generated artefacts from an in-person supplier development training. The findings identify four interactional mechanisms: (1) informal and dialogic interaction spaces foster deeper engagement than formalized formats alone; (2) framing logics shape how suppliers translate normative sustainability goals into operational action; (3) interactional continuity enables sustained learning, while (4) limited relational depth constrains boundary-spanning behavior. These findings advance theory by foregrounding interactional mechanisms, inform practice by guiding the design of supplier development programs, and contribute societally by clarifying conditions under which sustainability efforts generate meaningful impact beyond compliance.

Green Innovators in the Supply Chain: Unlocking Financial Gains for Suppliers

OSCM
Xin Deng (The University of Manchester); Hesham Ali (University of Nottingham); Aarti Krishnan
Firms are increasingly evaluated not only on their own environmental practices but also on those of their supply chain partners. While prior research shows that major customers can shape supplier operations and compliance, less is known about whether and how customer practices influence financial market perceptions of suppliers. Drawing on signaling theory, we argue that customer green innovation, captured through green patenting, serves as a credible, observable, and costly signal that reduces investor uncertainty and lowers suppliers’ cost of financing. Using a comprehensive longitudinal dataset of 3,551 publicly listed U.S. supplier firms and their major customers between 1994 and 2020, we show that suppliers associated with green-innovating customers experience significantly lower financing costs. This effect is amplified when suppliers themselves face greater environmental risk, and when customers possess stronger reputation, consistent with the theoretical premise that uncertainty and the credibility of signals amplify investor responses. Our findings contribute to the supply chain management literature by theorizing and demonstrating a cross-party signaling mechanism in which customers’ green innovation spills over to financial markets, shaping investor beliefs about suppliers and their access to capital.

Same Misconduct, Different Consequences: Sustainability-Related Framing in Supply Chains

OSCM
Ruth Schültken (University of Mannheim); Christoph Bode (University of Mannheim)
When supply chain misconduct becomes public, its communication shapes stakeholder responses. This research introduces sustainability-related framing as an emphasis framing mechanism that makes one sustainability dimension more salient in the communication of supply chain misconduct. Drawing on a scenario-based experiment involving 260 buyers, both professional and consumers, it investigates whether this framing mechanism causes differences in buyers’ sanctioning behaviors in response to the misconduct. It reveals four key insights: (1) emphasizing social consequences increases sanctioning behaviors compared to emphasizing environmental consequences; (2) this framing effect is weaker among professional buyers than among consumers; (3) the effect is independent of whether the decision is made based on affect or cognition, decision strategy constitutes a distinct driver of sanctioning behaviors; and (4) economic sanctioning (purchase probability) and social sanctioning (engaging in negative word-of-mouth) are both affected by framing but operate beyond based on distinct mechanisms. These insights underscore the critical role of the emphasized sustainability dimension in supply chain governance and contribute to the literature on framing in the context of sustainability, sensemaking, and moral circle theory. Further, this research deepens the understanding of buyers’ sanctioning behaviors in supply chain management.

Fragile Reputations: Credibility-Gated CSR and Buyer Responses to Supplier Misconduct

OSCM
Christoph Bode (University of Mannheim); Ruth Schültken (University of Mannheim)
We develop a model of how corporate sustainability (CSR) reputation structure shapes stakeholder responses to corporate social irresponsibility (CSI). Moving beyond unidimensional conceptions of reputation, we propose that stakeholder beliefs of CSR reputation are characterized by two analytically separable dimensions: expected integrity (what stakeholders expect) and assessment certainty (how confident they are in that expectation). This two-dimensional framework explains when positive CSR reputation provides insurance-like protection against sanctions following CSI and when it backfires, amplifying negative reactions. The key insight is that high integrity combined with low assessment certainty creates fragile positive reputations that are vulnerable to collapse when tested by negative evidence. We derive formal conditions for insurance and backfire effects, establish boundary conditions related to institutional informativeness, and test them in a multi-stage empirical research design that manipulates the theoretical dimensions.
