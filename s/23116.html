Training Intensity and Quiet Quitting: A Curvilinear Approach

HR
Subbulakshmi Somu (St. Josephs Institute of Management); Md Asadul Islam (King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals)
In knowledge-intensive workplaces like academia, where performance pressures are high, training and development are typically framed as an important job resource within Job Demand Resources (JDR) theory. But through the perspective of a too much of a good thing (TMGT) effect, this paper states that beyond the point of optimal levels, training can have unintended negative effects. Specifically, excessive training can transform from a supportive resource into an additional job demand, increasing perceived workload and causing subtle withdrawal behaviours such as quiet quitting. This study investigates the nonlinear association between training intensity and the behavioural and emotional quiet quitting among the members of the faculty in the private higher educational institutions. Drawing on JDR and TMGT theories, this study hypothesises and tests a moderated mediation model where perceived workload mediates the curvilinear relationships between training intensity and quiet quitting, with the perceived organisational support moderating these relationships by remodelling the demand resource appraisals of employees. Based on survey data gathered from 510 faculty members and analysed using PLS-SEM, the results show that training intensity has a U-shaped relationship with quiet quitting. Moderate levels of training reduce disengagement, whereas low and excessive training foster behavioural and emotional withdrawal. These adverse effects are mitigated by perceived organisational support. This study contributes to the JD-R and TMGT scholarship through reconceptualizing training intensity as a possible job demand and provides practical implications of the scholar in developing faculty development practices that improve performance without leading to silent disengagement.

Turnover on a Timer: How Macro Events Influence Workforce Disruptions Over Time

HR
T.J. Raineri (Rutgers University); Michael Sturman (Rutgers University); Haein Won (Rutgers University-New Brunswick)
This study extends Event Systems Theory and group-level theories of collective turnover to examine how macro-level events shape the timing and trajectory of voluntary collective turnover. Using U.S. presidential elections as exogenous macro events, I analyze how event effects are conditioned by work-unit context—specifically proximity to the event, racial diversity, and organizational commitment—across 134 U.S. civil service agencies and departments. Drawing on longitudinal federal workforce data and multilevel discontinuous growth models, I find that macro events generate significant increases in collective turnover, with group-level characteristics influencing both the immediacy and accumulation of turnover following the event. Racial diversity and organizational commitment play dual roles, accelerating the onset of turnover while buffering longer-term cumulative losses. Together, these findings advance understanding of how macro-level disruptions cascade through organizations and identify the contextual conditions under which collective turnover unfolds over time.

Control and Cohesion: The Effects of a Remote Management Training on Job Satisfaction

HR
Beth Ann Livingston (University of Iowa); Jake T. Harrison (University of Iowa); Drew Jauron (University of Iowa); Amy E. Colbert (University of Iowa); Stephen Hyrum Courtright (Texas A&M University); Diane Rohlman (University of Iowa)
A new era of remote and hybrid work has caused concerns about employees being able to work well together when they are not always co-located. At the same time, managers in hybrid workplaces may feel they must use more controlling behaviors to manage employees who are working in hybrid/remote modalities. In this paper, we design a training intervention focused on trust and relationship building across different work modalities directed at managers in two organizations. Our pilot study established that the training was well-accepted and that learning occurred. Our focal study deploys an experimental design with a delayed intervention control group in which we trained managers asynchronously and synchronously and assessed the effects of the intervention on employee outcomes over time. We find that a training intervention focused on behaviors that managers can enact to support hybrid teams has significant effects on employee ratings of managers’ controlling behaviors and on overall team cohesion, both of which are related to employee job satisfaction. Our research has implications for the training of managers in organizations and for future research on micromanagement and remote work.
