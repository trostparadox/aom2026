How and When AI-Augmented Versus AI-Automated Feedback Sources Influence Employee Feedback Seeking

HR
Maona Mu (Renmin University of China); Wenhao Luo (North China University of Technology)
Two distinct types of AI-involved feedback sources have emerged in practice: AI-automated and AI-augmented feedback sources. AI-automated feedback sources refer to AI systems that independently monitor, evaluate, and provide feedback on employees’ job performance without human managerial involvement. In contrast, AI-augmented feedback sources refer to human managers who integrate AI analysis with their professional expertise to monitor, evaluate, and deliver final feedback on employees’ job performance. Drawing on the stereotype content model, we propose that, depending on feedback source type and feedback valence, employees develop differentiated warmth and competence perceptions of AI-involved feedback sources, which in turn shape their feedback-seeking behavior. Results from a scenario-based experiment and a two-wave field survey show that when delivering negative (vs. positive) feedback, AI-augmented (vs. AI-automated) feedback sources elicit higher warmth perception, thereby promoting employees’ feedback-seeking behavior. Conversely, when delivering positive (vs. negative) feedback, AI-augmented (vs. AI-automated) feedback sources reduce competence perception, leading to lower feedback-seeking behavior. This research advances understanding of the effects of different AI-involved feedback sources and offers guidance on how and when organizations can effectively leverage AI in performance feedback.

Reconstructing Negative Feedback: Differentiating Promotion- and Prevention-Framed Negative Feedback Through Discrete Emotional Processes

HR
Tong Boreas Wu (The University of Auckland); Lu Lucy Xing (The University of Auckland); Lixin Jiang (The University of Auckland)
Negative feedback is a critical yet double-edged managerial tool, often failing to improve performance. Extant research predominantly attributes this variability to recipient characteristics, overlooking how the very framing of feedback by providers shapes employee reactions. Drawing on regulatory focus theory and achievement emotion theory, this study distinguishes negative feedback into prevention- framed negative feedback and promotion-framed negative feedback. We propose a conceptual model in which these two forms of negative feedback influence the two task outcomes through distinct prospective emotions: anxiety emerges when individuals anticipate potential shortfalls in future performance, whereas hope arises when performance are perceived as potentially improvable. We expect prevention-framed negative feedback to induce anxiety, which can positively impact employees’ motivation to improve and affect task performance in a non- linear, inverted U-shaped manner: moderate anxiety may mobilize effort and temporarily support performance, while high anxiety may overwhelm emotional resources and impair performance. Conversely, promotion- framed negative feedback is expected to to enhance employees’ motivation to improve and task performance by eliciting hope. Our study advanced the one-sided understanding of negative feedback, and provided theoretical and practical implications.

How Does Prior Team Performance Achievement Influence Leader Psychological Strain?

HR
Qi Zhao (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Jingyu Dong (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Fuli Li (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Ziqiong Qiao (Xi'an Jiaotong University); PAN FAN (Harbin Institute of Technology)
Although leader well-being has gained increasing research attention in recent years, our understanding of how team performance achievement affects leader well-being is still limited. Drawn on attribution theory and the job demands-resources model, we hypothesize that prior team performance achievement is negatively linked to leader psychological strain via leader-perceived team effort. Furthermore, we hypothesize that team proactive personality can intensify the positive relationship between prior team performance achievement and leader-perceived team effort. Compared to leaders with a less proactive team, those with a highly proactive team are more inclined to attribute team performance achievement to effort, ultimately affecting their psychological strain. Our findings based on data from a large diary product sales company in China (1562 members from 91 business units and their leaders) support our hypotheses. These results challenge the widely-held belief that excellent team performance is universally good for leader well-being. Instead, we emphasize the effort attribution in leaders’ performance judgment of team performance and historical information of team characteristics (i.e., team proactive personality) in affecting leader attribution. Theoretical and practical implications were discussed.

Navigating Employee Resilience in the Workplace: A Dual Pathway (WITHDRAWN)

HR
Haina Zhang (Sichuan University); Wei Sun (Qilu University of Technology)
As organizations grapple with unprecedented changes and uncertainties relating to digital transformation, fostering employee resilience has become a critical priority for organizational success and sustainability. Drawing upon conservation of resources (COR) theory, this study advances our understanding of employee resilience-building process through examining the impact of inclusive leadership—as a crucial work resource—on resilience via a dual-pathway, i.e., attitudinal pathway (affective commitment) and behavioral pathway (job crafting). Additionally, we explore the moderating role of self-efficacy as a personal resource in these mediating relationships. We tested our model through two empirical studies, including an experimental study with 300 participants (study 1) and a field survey study with three waves of data from 348 employees (study 2). The empirical findings support our dual-pathway model and demonstrate the contrasting moderating roles of self-efficacy in different pathways such as ‘resource substitution effect’ on the attitudinal pathway and a ‘resource enhancement effect’ on the behavioral pathway. Our research provides novel insights to employee resilience by identifying resources such as inclusive leadership and self-efficacy through a dual pathway model and offers managerial implications for how employee resilience in the workplace could be alleviated through managers’ leadership practice.

A Daily Dynamic Perspective on Leader Feedback and Employee Performance: Felt Trust as the Mediator

HR
Qin Su (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Yolanda N. Li (Lingnan University); Julie Zhu (Fuzhou University); Vickie (Weiqi Hou, University of Macau)
The leader feedback literature has paid considerable attention to the distinct and static effects of positive and negative feedback on employee outcomes, while overlooking the facts that leader positive and negative feedback toward the same employee may coexist and change from day to day. To address this oversight, we, in this research, draw on the control theory and the prospect theory to propose that daily changes of leader positive and negative feedback can jointly shape employees’ perception of the leader-employee relationship and performance. Specifically, we propose that daily increased leader positive feedback together with daily decreased leader negative feedback will have positive effects on employees’ felt trust by the leader, which in turn leads to higher employee work engagement and task performance that day. Results from two experience sampling studies (Study 1 with 512 daily responses from 66 employees; Study 2 with 1,300 daily responses from 138 employees) support our hypotheses. This research contributes to leader feedback research by proposing an interactive, dynamic perspective that simultaneously investigates the effects of changes in leader positive and negative feedback on employees.
