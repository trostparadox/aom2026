A Creature In - But Not Of - the System: Collective Memory in the Soviet Aerospace Industry

MH
Vladimir Korovkin (SKOLKOVO Moscow School of Management); Christopher Andrew Hartwell (Zurich University of Applied Sciences)
Memory is not history, but the concept of collective memory posits that group representations of the past can have more sway in influencing the present than actual historical events. The extensive literature on the concept of collective memory understands such remembering with groups or at the national level as a tool of reinforcement, a way to make sense of history and support group identity and/or dynamics. However, it is possible that collective memory arises not only to solidify a group identity but as a way to push back against external forces, being more reactive to the present than proactive in building the past. This paper explores this possibility by examining the vicissitudes of collective memory within the aerospace industry of the Soviet Union in the 1960s and 1970s, focusing in particular on the use of collective memory within the industry as a source of resistance. Using interviews, case studies, and secondary Russian-language material on the industry, we find that aerospace engineers within the industry, across geography and firms, instrumentalized collective memory as a tool against an oppressive system. More than just a study of a clash of guilds, our analysis shows how the shaping of a collective memory was a central weapon for technical staff against an ossified Communist system, a deliberate case of temporal work. In a supreme irony, this collective memory was permitted by precisely the system it was oriented against because it also advanced their need for “heroes” emerging from Communism.

Welcome to the W: Stakeholder Mnemonic Communities in Times of Growth

MH
Matthew CB Lyle (State University of New York at Binghamton); Risa F (Isard) Isley (The University of Connecticut); Ajhanai Channel Inez Keaton (University of Massachusetts Amherst)
Despite our understanding of stakeholder mnemonic communities (i.e., groups invested in their collective memories of organizations), or SMCs, we have yet to theorize how they respond to the influx of new organizational stakeholders who neither formed nor maintained such collective memories. We thus developed a case study of Superfans.com, a message board of longtime fans of the Women’s National Basketball Association (WNBA), amidst its influx of new fans surrounding Caitlin Clark’s rookie season. Our analyses suggest SMCs navigate these periods by acting as mnemonic havens, delimiting their boundaries to enliven a history they now view as marginalized by new stakeholders and discuss the organization’s future with a newly developed, relatively shared internal appreciation for the story it tells. We highlight the specificity of an SMC’s memories alongside key (un)resolved tensions, offering a view of mnemonic communities as adherents to a story of the past who decry the ahistorical perspectives of outsiders alongside the alternative stories that emerge within their boundaries.

Intergenerational Counter-Memory Mobilization: Nurturing Memories of Vila Socó Tragedy

MH
Rodolfo Ferreira Maritan (FGV EAESP); Amon Barros (FGV EAESP); Caio César Coelho (University of São Paulo)
This article examines how organizational memory can be a site of political struggle. Under an authoritarian regime in the Global South, where histories of state repression and corporate irresponsibility are deeply intertwined, we draw on a historical study of the Vila Socó disaster, an industrial catastrophe managed by Petrobras, Brazil’s state-owned oil company. We trace how corporate, and state actors engaged in forgetting work to erase mnemonic traces and suppress accountability through silence, bureaucratic absorption, and control of memory infrastructures. In response, community members enacted what we call Intergenerational Counter Memory Mobilization, a concept we develop to capture a grassroots, temporal extended form of resistance that reconstructs silenced pasts across generations. Our findings reveal how organizational memory can be a site of dispute between institutionalized, legal and operational mechanisms and community memory work that is dispersed and constrained by organization and repression. We contribute to organizational memory studies theorizing memory as political action. Our case shows how the local community actively remembers its past and resists institutional forgetting, showcasing the entanglement of mnemonic practices, corporate irresponsibility, and political power.

Constructing the Underdog Institution: From Bricolage to Symbolic Identity

MH
Ajay Shah (Emory University)
Recent work at the intersection of materiality and organizational studies has considered the ways in which buildings and other material artifacts reflect an organization's identity and history. While such work has considered how actors interpret and imbue meanings into these artifacts, this line of work has paid less attention to the process in which certain artifacts are constructed and how actors assign meanings to the process of material construction. Such a focus becomes particularly germane when considering material artifacts produced through the process of bricolage: creatively repurposing resources under constraint. Drawing upon extensive archival work and secondary sources, I examine a key historical event occurring at what is now known as Georgia State University (GSU). An under-resourced institution at the time, catering primarily to working students who took evening courses, the institution engaged in a process of bricolage to convert a six-story parking garage into an academic building to accommodate an influx of veterans returning home from World War II. Considering work on underdog narratives and attribution theory, I examine how the narrative of the garage conversion became an enduring symbolic legacy for the institution. Examining contemporaneous records and later retellings of the event, I illustrate how an achievement involving a network of state actors coalesced into a singular heroic narrative centered on the college’s long-time director. Such narrative reconstruction, I argue, attributed the achievement to a popular leader, thereby creating institutional lore that celebrated an underdog spirit and success “against all odds.”
