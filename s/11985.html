Failing Global Talent Management: Microfoundational Frictions in Japanese MNEs in Emerging Markets

Authors: Akiko Sato Kyushu University; Kunio Ikari Kyushu University

Session overview

Global talent management (GTM) is promoted as a strategic system through which multinational enterprises (MNEs) identify high potentials, develop leadership pipelines, and mobilize talent internationally. Yet GTM often fails to “travel” to foreign subsidiaries, particularly in emerging markets where labor institutions and career norms diverge from those of headquarters (HQ). Drawing on an interview-based multiple case study of 16 Japanese manufacturing subsidiaries in Vietnam and Thailand, with two HQ contrast cases, we examine how GTM is enacted locally and why outcomes vary across subsidiaries. We identify three recurring microfoundational frictions that constrain the portability of GTM. Comparability friction arises when subsidiaries doubt the commensurability of global “potential” standards, undermining legitimate global talent pooling. Mobility/rotation friction emerges when HQ-intended developmental rotations conflict with specialization norms, family constraints, and occupational licensing regimes, disrupting pipeline continuity. Coupling/legitimacy friction occurs when global frameworks remain cognitively distant and relationally filtered through expatriate channels, producing ceremonial adoption. These frictions form a sequential bottleneck chain, amplified by emerging-market scarcity: weak comparability legitimacy blocks pooling, infeasible mobility fractures development, and coupling deficits delay institutionalization. We theorize GTM portability as a microfoundational enactment process under institutional duality, contributing mechanism-level boundary conditions for when global pipelines become operational in diverse host contexts.
