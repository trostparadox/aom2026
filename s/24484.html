From Industrial Titans to Platform Founders: Reputational Governance Through Elite Giving

OMT
Kathleen Park (Boston University); Gavin M. Schwarz (UNSW Sydney); Dave Bouckenooghe (Brock University); Faisal AlReshaid (American University of Kuwait)
Elite philanthropic giving by technology-oriented entrepreneurs is often celebrated as evidence of elevated leadership and social responsibility. Yet such giving can also operate as a strategic response to ethical contestation rather than as a mechanism of accountability. We examine founder philanthropy under a dual ledger of reputational susceptibility—arising from corporate harms and biographical factors influencing perceptions of moral credibility. Using a historical-comparative interpretive analysis of iconic technology-driven leaders, we develop a framework showing how elite giving enables reputational reengineering while limiting moral reckoning. Our paper contributes to management theory through the empirical surfacing and conceptual elaboration of notions of moral decoupling and ethical omission. We theoretically deepen moral decoupling as a founder-level strategy through which leaders separate public personas from ethically contested organizational practices or potentially sensitive personal background factors, and we introduce ethical omission to explain a deliberately patterned non-engagement with more justice-oriented causes that might implicate the sources and consequences of wealth creation or highlight background vulnerability. Across cases and eras, we find a consistent preference for high-visibility, widely laudable types of philanthropy while avoiding potentially sensitive domains such as labor rights, racial equity, and reparative justice—especially where these intersect with corporate controversy or founder biography. We fundamentally argue that entrepreneurial philanthropy operates as reputational governance: a mode of legitimacy management that neutralizes critique, stabilizes elite influence, and distances accountability. We conclude by delineating implications for research, policy, and practice concerned with transparency, the many layered meanings of justice, and the ethics of elite power.
