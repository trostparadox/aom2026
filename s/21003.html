Embodiment in Virtual Reality: Implications for Public Speaking and Bias

Monday, Aug 3 2026 · 4:00PM–5:00PM
Pennsylvania Convention Center — Poster Wall 4
MED

People

Authors: Anely Bekbergenova; Nicolas Andreas Tamm Garetto Technical University of Munich; Regina Hagl Technical University of Munich; Claudia Peus Technical University of Munich

Session overview

Immersive virtual reality (VR) enables individuals to embody avatars that differ from their physical characteristics, such as gender or professional status, thereby offering a unique opportunity to study how identity cues shape behavior. Drawing on the Proteus effect, which suggests that individuals adapt their behavior to the expectations associated with their virtual self-representation, this research investigates how virtual embodiment influences public speaking performance and implicit bias (N = 322). Specifically, we examine whether embodying a male or female avatar portrayed as an experienced professional or as lacking experience affects leadership-related behaviors. Using a 2 (avatar gender: male vs. female) × 2 (experience: high vs. low) between-subjects design, we assess participants’ verbal charisma, nonverbal expressiveness, vocal cues, self-evaluated performance, and perceived authenticity. In addition, we explore whether embodying a female avatar reduces implicit gender bias. Results indicate that embodying an experienced (i.e., professionally successful) virtual human enhances participants’ use of substantive charismatic speech elements Moreover, embodying an experienced professional female avatar was associated with less biased responses. Together, these findings highlight the potential of VR-based embodiment as a scalable and engaging intervention for improving leadership-relevant communication skills while simultaneously reducing biases. This research contributes to the literature on the uses of immersive virtual reality, embodiment, and leadership development by demonstrating how identity manipulation in immersive environments can foster behavioral change.
