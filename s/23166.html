Data-Driven Business Models in an Imperfect World

CTO
Abayomi Baiyere (Copenhagen Business School); Chee-wee Tan (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Eric.T.K. Lim; Peter Johan Lambaerts (JAMK University of Applied Sciences)
Although there has been advances in the literature on business models, it is striking that many viewpoints on business models – particularly data-driven business models – are formulated under the assumption of uncheckered data access – what we refer to as the “perfect world” view. While this view has value for theoretical purposes, the reality in many organizations highlights a struggle to establish data-driven business models in an imperfect world where access to the required data is far from given, even when available. Our study is positioned to juxtapose the process of establishing business models alongside checkered access to data. With this aim, we join the pockets of emerging literature that strives to reorient the dominant assumptions in a digital era in which data is a pivotal ingredient of business models. Drawing on an in-depth longitudinal study of an IIoT company’s efforts to structure a business model around their IIoT innovations, we reveal why contemporary assumptions of business models from extant literature may require a rethink in the context of IIoT. By empirically exploring how the notions of value creation and value capture unfold in the case company, we derive plausible business model pathways contingent on data access. We specifically show how three inflection points of data access affect the value creation and value capture logic in a data-driven business model.

Tough Coupling: Development of Digital Tools When Developer and User Practices Differ

CTO
Ekaterina Mavrina (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Katharina Cepa (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Digital tools can transform domains rooted in analog artifacts by supporting more time- and cost-efficient decisions. To deliver value to users, digital tool development iterates between developers’ adjustments in their digital environment and users’ testing in their physical environment. In our qualitative study of digital tool development in the research consortium on dementia diagnosis, we found that such iterations proved infeasible. To explain this unexpected outcome, we adopted a practice lens that connects organizing with the material and temporal elements of practices. We found stark differences in the domain-specific artifacts, rhythms, and schedules associated with the use of those artifacts between practices of bioinformaticians - developers - who manipulated digital artifacts to generate protein predictions, and practices of neurochemists - users - who manipulated analog artifacts to test selected proteins in lab experiments. Our findings revealed that the mutual dependence of developers and users on external artifacts created persistent handover challenges. Developers, despite their ability to quickly adjust digital artifacts in a time-independent manner, could not tolerate long delays in user testing. For users, an uncertain time of developer input was problematic because they needed to timely schedule adjustments of analog artifacts. We found that developers and users engaged in asymmetric coordination practices: continuous data scaffolding and punctuated information patchworking, respectively. Those practices were tailored to their rhythms and schedules. Our findings have theoretical implications for digital tool development and cross-domain coordination.

Understanding Resistance to Inter-Organizational Digital Transformation Over Time

CTO
Jas Kalra (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester); Kell Jones; Grant Mills (University College London); Kieran Conboy
Digital transformation (DT) promises increased coordination, information flows, and performance gains; however, many programs stall before delivering system-level value. We examine how resistance emerges and becomes consequential across organizational boundaries over time. Drawing on a longitudinal qualitative case of a multi-site control-room initiative in UK capital projects, we analyze interviews, observations, and organizational artefacts using abductive, process-oriented theorizing. We identify four generative mechanisms linking locally rational accommodations to network-level failure: (1) vision– resource gaps that legitimize temporary exceptions; (2) symbolic adoption that re-badges legacy artefacts without altering underlying data flows; (3) operational decoupling that institutionalizes dual baselines and parallel regimes; and (4) business-case evaporation as reconciliation work overwhelms visible benefits, culminating in quiet rollback. Temporal asymmetry across sites amplifies these dynamics: while no single organization appears non-compliant, the network fails to transform. We contribute a mechanism-rich process model that reframes resistance to DT as a network-level phenomenon, showing how governance of data rights, information quality, and decision rights, which are issues between organizations rather than within them, co-produce resistance over time. For practice, we highlight diagnostic signs (e.g., dual baselines, parallel regimes) and pacing/sequencing levers for orchestrators to convert symbolic compliance into operational coupling.

Sequencing Digital Transformation: Grounding the Dynamic Capability Orchestration Framework

CTO
Mario Neururer (University of Innsbruck); Maximilian Schreieck (Universität Innsbruck)
Research on digital transformation agrees that organizations rely on dynamic capabilities to adapt to digital change, yet existing frameworks rarely explain how these capabilities organize over time. Most studies enumerate capability domains but leave unclear how communication, learning, coordination, and integration connect sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring in practice. As a result, digital transformation often appears static rather than an ongoing organizing process. This study develops the Dynamic Capability Orchestration framework, which conceptualizes digital transformation as a sequenced, networked, and recursive process. Drawing on a systematic review of eighty-four peer-reviewed studies published between 2011 and 2023, we analyze 2,051 capability instances using qualitative coding, network analysis, and sequence mapping. The findings identify three ordinary meta-capabilities that establish structural foundations and nine dynamic meta-capabilities that drive adaptation. Network and sequence analyses reveal recurring orchestration patterns and transformation loops, advancing a process-theoretical account of capability-based organizational change.

How Manufacturing Creates Value with AI: A Configurational Framework of Human, Data, and Scenarios

CTO
Haijian Liu; Shuyi Du (Nanjing University)
With the widespread adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in manufacturing, many firms have encountered the Solow Paradox, whereby substantial technological investment yields limited value creation. How can traditional manufacturing firms overcome the persistent challenge of transforming AI capabilities into measurable value? We investigate this question by conducting a longitudinal case study grounded in configuration theory, which allows us to systematically examine the process mechanisms of strategic alignment and element configuration through which AI becomes embedded in value creation. We identify the dynamic configuration of strategy and the “Human-Data-Scenario” elements as the core mechanism enabling AI-driven value. We further delineate this process into three stages: Configuration Triggering, Configuration Focusing, and Configuration Synergizing. This progression follows a sequential logic of Strategic Alignment, Element Configuration and Value Creation. Crucially, at each stage, we trace how the evolving relationships and co-evolution among the human, data, and scenario elements interact with AI to shape the path of value realization. Through this staged framework, the research elucidates the underlying logic through which traditional manufacturing firms translate AI applications into sustained value creation.
