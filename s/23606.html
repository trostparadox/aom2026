Inclusion Under Pressure: DEI Backlash, Retrenchment, and Resistance

Moderator: Patricia G. Martinez Loyola Marymount University

Session overview

This session examines what happens when organizational commitments to diversity, equity, and inclusion come under political, legal, and institutional pressure. The papers focus on the burdens of DEI professionals, rhetorical silence, employee sensemaking during retrenchment, the theory-practice gap, and the evolution of DEI strategy over time. Together, they provide a timely picture of how organizations sustain, dilute, or retreat from inclusion efforts in contested environments.

Papers (4)

The Personal Costs of Carrying the (DEI) Flag for Diversity, Equity & Inclusion Professionals

DEI
Patricia G. Martinez (Loyola Marymount University); Monica C. Gavino (San Jose State University); Marla L. White (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Danielle Beu Ammeter (University of Mississippi); Darryl B. Rice (Miami University); Jennifer Robin Bishop (Case Western Reserve University)
This study examines how the growing backlash against diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives in the United States impacts DEI professionals in higher education. Drawing on social information processing theory, we explore how state-level political climates, institutional characteristics (e.g., mission, public/private status), and leadership directives shape these professionals’ workplace experiences. As part of a larger project to assess the state of Diversity, Equity and Inclusion (DEI) practices in business schools, we began by reviewing 40 AACSB-accredited business schools’ websites for DEI-related content. Using the collected data about DEI practices at the institutional (university) and college levels, we created a set of interview questions focused on DEI policies and practices in order to conduct semi-structured interviews with 19 DEI professionals across AACSB-accredited business schools. While these issues were not the original focus of the study, thematic analysis revealed pervasive personal-level challenges, including inequitable work conditions, discriminatory behaviors, lack of leadership support, and burnout, all exacerbated by anti-DEI legislation and shifting institutional priorities. Our findings extend the literature on DEI backlash and political ideology by highlighting macro-level influences on workplace environments and offer practical implications for sustaining inclusive practices amid sociopolitical resistance.

Where Silence Prevails: A Longitudinal Exploration of DEI Statements in Business Schools

DEI
O. Jerome Stewart (University of San Francisco); Allegra Chen-Carrel (University of San Francisco); Richard Cadiz (University of San Francisco); Prentice Townsend (University of San Francisco); Alex Aguilar (University of San Francisco)
The past decade has seen dramatic changes in rhetoric around diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI), with both increased emphasis after widespread Black Lives Matter protests, and more recent political backlash. Here, a longitudinal approach is utilized to analyze DEI statements on public-facing business school webpages over time to better understand how business schools respond to the pressures to address racism within their institutions and ensuing backlash. We find the George Floyd Uprising, while an important inflection point for the creation of DEI statements more broadly, was largely met with silence in U.S. business schools. For the business schools that did create statements, a mix of strategies were utilized in response to current backlash, ranging from removing or adjusting statements, to remaining steadfast in their rhetorical commitments. We illustrate how business schools deployed various rhetorical strategies in their DEI statements to reproduce colorblind ideology, aligning with interests to maintain the status quo.

Bridging the Chasm Between the Theory & Practice of Diversity, Equity, & Inclusion in Organizations

DEI
Carla D Jones (Sam Houston State University); Bina Ajay (Sam Houston State University); Huda Masood (Sam Houston State University)
Events in the past five years have encouraged organizations to re-evaluate their diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) efforts. However, despite these increased efforts, the employee outcomes have had limited effectiveness. These outcomes could have been either due to the empty commitments from organizations that they did not intend to keep or genuine obstacles that prohibited the DEI efforts from coming to fruition. We specifically discuss these obstacles and propose that the difficulty in navigating these obstacles is further complicated by a widening chasm between DEI theory and practice. To further examine this chasm, we organized a symposium at the 2023 Academy of Management (AOM) annual conference hosting a panel of expert DEI scholars and practitioners to overview (a) common organizational challenges in implementing DEI; (b) the role of the chasm between DEI theory and practice in exacerbating these challenges; and (c) how to narrow the gap. Based on the outcomes of the symposium, we identified three broad themes and reviewed scholarly literature to further examine them. The first two themes overviewed the challenges pertaining to the chasm between theory and practice, and the final theme proposed recommended DEI practices at various stages of implementation. Finally, we propose research areas to minimize the chasm.

Diversity as Evolution: A Multilevel Evolutionary Model of DEI Strategy

DEI
Quentin T Baldwin; Adrian Bell (The University of Utah); A.K. Ward (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) have become central to organizational legitimacy and identity, yet organizational DEI trajectories remain deeply uneven. While some organizations sustain DEI as a substantive and durable commitment, others adopt DEI symbolically or reverse prior investments altogether, even under similar institutional conditions. Existing DEI research emphasizes moral commitments, business-case logics, leadership advocacy, or specific practices, but offers limited explanation for why DEI strategies persist, drift, or unravel over time. We address this gap by theorizing DEI strategy as an evolutionary, multilevel process shaped by the interaction of external evaluation, internal coordination, and field-level differentiation. Drawing on institutional theory, organizational evolution, and cultural evolution, we develop a formal model showing how inter-organizational selection pressures, intra-organizational coordination capacity, and variance in DEI practices jointly produce four stable DEI outcomes: substantive persistence, symbolic compliance, reversal or neglect, and values-driven persistence. This framework reconceptualizes symbolic DEI not as managerial failure or moral deficiency, but as a predictable equilibrium arising from misalignment between external demands and internal capacities. By introducing field-level variance as a core mechanism shaping the visibility and evaluability of DEI, the paper advances DEI scholarship by explaining persistent heterogeneity in organizational commitments and offers a foundation for future research on inequality, legitimacy, and organizational change.
