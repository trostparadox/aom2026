U.S. Trends in Job Stability by Education Group in the 21st Century

CAR
Michael Lachanski (Georgetown University)
This article uses formal measurement methods adapted from demography to examine trends in private sector job stability by education group in the 21st century. I investigate time trends in summary measures capturing different notions of job stability. Data is obtained from Current Population Survey’s Job Tenure Supplement and the U.S. Census Bureau’s Quarterly Workforce Indicators from 1998 to 2024. Using one summary measure, expected tenure at hiring, analogous to the life expectancy measures in demography and public health research, workers with a BA and those without a BA have experienced diverging trajectories in job stability, with BA positions accumulating nearly all of the gains in job stability in the 21st century. Using another measure, the expected tenure for employment relationships that have already survived for five years, the population-as-a-whole has experienced a decline in job stability. The decline is similar in magnitude for both education groups. One’s conclusion about whether job instability and educational polarization are increasing hinges on whether one studies shorter-term or longer-term employment relationships. These results are not driven by sex-specific trends in job stability.

Mapping the Future of Work: Conceptual Foundations and Bibliometric Insights

CAR
Tatia Alpenidze (University of Padua); Martina Gianecchini
Research on the future of work has expanded rapidly in response to technological change, yet the field remains conceptually fragmented and methodologically heterogeneous. To bring greater coherence to this growing literature, this study combines a narrative review of conceptual definitions with a PRISMA-ScR guided scoping review with bibliometric analysis. The dataset comprises 1,803 peer-reviewed journal articles published between 2021 and 2026 and indexed in Scopus. Bibliometric techniques, including keyword frequency analysis and co-word clustering, are employed to examine publication trends, thematic emphases, and the overall intellectual structure of the field. The results indicate a highly interdisciplinary and dispersed research landscape, with contributions spanning multiple disciplines and publication outlets. Research on the future of work is predominantly shaped by technological perspectives, reflecting sustained attention to digitalization, automation, and artificial intelligence. At the same time, a substantial body of work focuses on education, skills, and employability, highlighting the importance of the human side in integrating technology into labor market. Other strands of research address transformations in work organization, employment relations, and worker well-being, often in connection with recent global disruptions. Overall, the findings suggest that literature has grown rapidly and covers a broad range of topics, remaining fragmented, with limited integration across conceptual and disciplinary boundaries. This review provides a structured overview of the field and lays the groundwork for more cumulative, interdisciplinary research on the future of work.

“Algorithmic Career” in the Age of Career Ecosystem Transformation

CAR
Liad Bareket-Bojmel (Peres Academic Center); Yehuda Baruch (University of Southampton)
We introduce and develop the concept of the “Algorithmic Career”, a term proposed here to capture the growing role of Artificial Intelligence (AI) as a systemic actor within the career ecosystem. Building on Career Ecosystem Theory and extending the notion of algorithmic management, we conceptualize AI as a mega-actor that actively reconfigures careers at the individual, organizational, state, and global market levels. We developed a comprehensive, multi-level framework comprising of seven operating mechanisms: career channeling, algorithmic gatekeeping, prediction, autonomous agents, profiling, nudging, and scoring. Through these mechanisms, AI shapes access to opportunities, mobility, and employability across ecosystem levels. Yet, it can also reduce the worth of human capital and undermine the value of humans’ work. Thus, these mechanisms may generate both benefits and risks. In addition, we illustrate how traditional career practices can be transformed through these algorithmic mechanisms, demonstrating the practical ways in which AI may influence career development processes. Positioned within contemporary career theories: protean career, boundaryless career, intelligent career, kaleidoscope career model, sustainable career and career shocks and chance events, the Algorithmic Career marks a shift from viewing AI as a managerial tool to recognizing it as a co-constructive force that participates in the design, direction, and evolution of careers within their broader context, such as economic, social, technological and environmental. The paper concludes by discussing ethical, trust, and accountability challenges and outlines future empirical directions for examining Algorithmic Career mechanisms across evolving career systems.

Examining the Structure of Digital Career Adaptability: The Critical Dimension and a Five-Factor Mod

CAR
mengqin jiang (Wuhan University); YANGPING LI (Wuhan University)
Digital transformation has intensified algorithmically mediated information and decision environments at work, raising demands for career adaptation that extend beyond planning, self-regulation, exploration, and efficacy beliefs. This study develops and validates a Digital Career Adaptability (DCA) scale that captures psychosocial resources for career adjustment in digitally mediated contexts, with particular attention to evaluative judgment under information abundance and technological risk. Following a multi-stage scale development procedure, we first collected qualitative data from employed workers and platform workers (N = 35) using open-ended interviews and written responses. Grounded theory analyses supported a five-dimension framework—Concern, Control, Curiosity, Confidence, and Critical—and informed item generation. We then administered two-wave online surveys among workers in digital career contexts (final N = 252) and conducted item analyses, scale purification, exploratory factor analysis, and confirmatory factor analysis. Results supported a 30-item, five-factor structure and a higher-order DCA construct reflected by the five dimensions. The scale demonstrated good model fit, strong internal consistency, and adequate convergent and discriminant validity. Evidence for criterion-related validity emerged from associations with work attitudes and career outcomes, and the Critical dimension showed incremental validity for performance-relevant outcomes such as work engagement and income. Overall, the DCA scale offers a psychometrically sound instrument for theory testing on digital career adaptation processes and provides a practical basis for talent development and assessment in organizations implementing digital and AI-enabled work systems.

Preparing Job Posting Data for Management Research: A Reproducible Pipeline for Job Analytics

CAR
Shuo (Luna) Yang (The Pennsylvania State University); Ricardo Reyna (Texas State University); C. Michael Smith (Pennsylvania State U); Darren Greway (Pennsylvania State U); Victoria Martinez Garcia (The Pennsylvania State University-Penn State Brandywine); Haniya Raheel (Pennsylvania State U); Aditi Joshi (The Pennsylvania State University); Hetvi Kalpeshkumar (The Pennsylvania State University-Penn State Great Valley); Satish Mahadevan Srinivasan (Pennsylvania State U)
Job postings have become a foundational data source for human resources (HR) research, enabling scholars to examine skill demands, job design practices, and workforce requirements at unprecedented scale. However, the heterogeneous nature of job posting text presents substantial methodological challenges that threaten the validity of HR analytics and theory development. Postings vary in structure, contain extensive boilerplate language unrelated to job content, include duplicates across platforms, and embed sensitive information alongside substantive requirements. This methodology paper addresses these challenges by providing a systematic, auditable, and reproducible process for preparing job posting data for rigorous job content analysis. We formalize an end-to-end pipeline that preserves data provenance, standardizes metadata fields, removes duplicates and non-substantive content while retaining occupationally meaningful information, segments descriptions into analytically interpretable components, implements privacy and ethical safeguards, and validates cleaning decisions through transparent quality checks. The contribution is a set of methodological recommendations and reporting standards designed to improve replicability, reduce measurement error, and support credible theory-building and evidence-based HR practice using job posting data.
