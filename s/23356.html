Liability of Patient Capital: Dedicated Owners and Performance Feedback at Varying Thresholds

STR
Nauman Asghar (University of Liverpool); Philipp Meyer-Doyle (INSEAD)
Dedicated institutional owners are often regarded as stabilizing governance actors who promote innovation and long-term strategies. Yet we argue that their “patience” can be a double-edged sword when firms face performance decline, creating what we call the liability of patient capital. Drawing on performance feedback theory, we develop a performance-contingent view of institutional ownership. We argue that dedicated owners dampen responsiveness during moderate performance shortfalls and postpone pressing for changes until performance deteriorates to survival-threatening levels. In contrast, transient (short-term oriented) owners exert pressure to react quickly to modest shortfalls but tend to disengage and sell as distress deepens. Using a panel of publicly listed firms in the U.S. and Canada (2000–2018), we find strong support for our predictions. We further show that reduced responsiveness to moderate performance shortfalls under the influence of dedicated owners negatively affects a firm’s future performance.

When Slack Fuels Innovation: Ownership Concentration as a Governance Catalyst for R&D activities

STR
Saehwa Hong (Kutztown University of Pennsylvania); Jinhwan Jo (Wright State University)
This study examines how ownership concentration moderates the relationship between unabsorbed financial slack and R&D activities through the lens of agency theory. Agency theory highlights the challenges of misaligned incentives between managers and shareholders, particularly in dispersed ownership structures, which often result in suboptimal resource allocation. We argue that unabsorbed slack has a stronger positive impact on R&D investments in firms with concentrated ownership, as such ownership concentration mitigates agency problems by enhancing managerial accountability and promoting long-term R&D investments. Using a comprehensive dataset of South Korean manufacturing firms from 2011 to 2023, we find robust empirical support for our hypotheses. Our findings underscore the importance of distinguishing ownership concentration levels to expand our understanding of how unabsorbed financial slack fosters R&D activities. This study contributes to agency theory and strategic management literature by elucidating the interplay between corporate governance, resource allocation, and innovation.

Designing Investment Organizations For Social Impact

STR
Leo Denis (Ecole polytechnique); Nicolas MOTTIS (Ecole polytechnique)
Sustainable investors seek to align capital markets with sustainability goals, but are often criticized for their limited social impact. Understanding this paradox requires shifting the focus from investors’ interactions with firms to the organizational design of institutional investors—the intermediaries that manage financial capital. We examine how asset owners and asset managers jointly design these investment organizations to pursue financial and social objectives. Based on an 18-month ethnography in a private equity fund, we trace how these actors negotiate and stabilize the fund’s organizational architecture (its objectives, resources, and control structures) before it becomes active on the market. We identify two interrelated mechanisms. First, locking in the organizational architecture early reduces agency issues and shapes subsequent investment behavior. Second, integrating financial and social logics coherently throughout this architecture creates a hybrid organization capable of pursuing dual objectives. These design choices generate a framing effect that directs attention and decision-making toward the pre-locked goals. By theorizing these mechanisms, we offer an organizational perspective on financial intermediation, showing how early design choices influence investment behavior. We finally conceptualize sustainable finance as an organizational design problem in which social impact potential is built through the design of investment organizations.

Institutional Investors: From Expressive Commitments to Demonstrations of Ownership

STR
Alina Georgiana Andrei (Texas A&M University); Mallory Elise Flowers (University of Rhode Island)
This study examines how institutional investors’ expressive commitments to active ownership -such as pledging to initiatives like the UN Principles for Responsible Investment - shape their demonstrations of ownership commitment, specifically their willingness to maintain or increase ownership stakes in portfolio firms. Drawing on psychological ownership and corporate governance theories, we argue that expressive commitments generate a relational dimension of ownership that extends beyond purely financial motivations. Building on organizational commitment research, we identify values alignment, external scrutiny, and vested interest as moderators of the relationship between expressive commitments and ownership demonstrations. Using a global dataset of 109,713 investor–firm dyad-years over 17 years, we find consistent evidence for these mechanisms. Our findings contribute to behavioral corporate governance, psychological ownership, and responsible investment research.

Common Ownership, Firm Hiring and the Allocation of Human Capital

STR
Zeyang Xue (Boston University); Mario Leccese (Boston University)
We study how common ownership shapes the allocation of human capital across firms. We construct a novel dataset combining LinkedIn-based employment histories with data on venture capital (VC) investments in U.S. startups. Exploiting the staggered adoption of liability waivers that allow investors to hold stakes in competing business opportunities as a plausibly exogenous shock to common ownership, we show that ventures with shared investors employ more workers, pay higher wages, and experience greater turnover than independent ones. At the same time, common ownership increases job-to-job mobility within investor portfolios, suggesting that shared investors facilitate the reallocation of workers across portfolio firms. Aggregating to the local market level, we find that common ownership significantly increases worker mobility without depressing overall employment or wages, though these positive effects weaken in more concentrated labor markets. Taken together, the results are consistent with coordination synergies that relax matching frictions outweighing potential monopsony effects.
