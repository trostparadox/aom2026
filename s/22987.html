Who Loses from Foreign Market Exit? International R&D Collaborations and Inventor Performance

IM
Alessandra Perri (Luiss university); Elisa Sabbadin (Luiss university); Ke Wu (LUISS Guido Carli University)
While international business research has recently examined the drivers of foreign divestment, its implications for knowledge creation inside the multinational enterprise (MNE) remain unexplored. Building on the knowledge-based view of the MNE and literature on international knowledge sourcing, we take a microfoundations approach to study the relationship between foreign divestment and MNE inventors’ innovative performance. We suggest that foreign divestment may narrow the scope of the firm’s international knowledge network, thus negatively affecting inventors who previously collaborated with peers in the divested unit, as they lose privileged access to localized, non-redundant technology that is key to knowledge recombination. Drawing on the case of General Motors’ divestment of its European operations, we examine the inventive performance of 1,366 firm inventors over 19,124 inventor–year observations spanning the period 2010–2023. Our results reveal that inventors with prior knowledge-based collaborations with the divested subsidiary are associated to a decline in innovation performance following the foreign divestment, consistent with their loss of preferential access to European technological knowledge. However, inventors with direct channels to alternative international knowledge sources, such as those established via prior intrafirm cross-border mobility or collaborations with peers in other foreign subsidiaries, are protected from this negative effect.

Does Easing Short-Term Visits Improve Cross-Border Acquisition Outcomes?

IM
Jongik Chang (Korea University)
We examine whether reducing barriers to short-term international mobility improves cross-border acquisition (CBA) outcomes. Leveraging the staggered implementations of the U.S. Visa Waiver Program (VWP) which allows eligible foreign nationals to enter the United States for up to 90 days without a visa, we analyse 25,774 announced CBAs involving U.S. firms from 1978 to 2024 in a staggered difference-in-differences design. We find that VWP adoption increases the likelihood of deal completion and strengthens deal terms (larger deal size and higher acquired equity stake), while reducing time to completion and attenuating announcement-period stock price shock. These effects are more pronounced when acquirers and targets are separated by larger institutional, cultural, or knowledge distances. Overall, our findings suggest that policy-enabled short-term visits mitigate information asymmetry by easing soft-information frictions and facilitating trust-building in international deal-making, thereby improving CBA outcomes.

The Liability of Loyalty: How Political Hedging Shapes Firm Survival After Democratic Breakdown

IM
Andy Buschmann (University of Michigan)
Firm survival depends not only on market factors but also on firms’ ability to navigate the nonmarket environment. Prior research shows that political hedging, defined as diversifying corporate political connections across rival political camps, can protect firms from political turnover and enhance performance. However, this evidence comes from democratic or democratizing contexts where institutional constraints limit the incentive and ability of political officeholders to engage in repression and expropriation. I examine whether political hedging retains its value when these constraints collapse during democratic breakdown. Using Myanmar’s 2021 military coup as an empirical case, I link the universe of formally registered firms to all national and regional politicians during the country’s decade-long semi-democratic period and exploit the coup as an exogenous shock to corporate political ties. Analyzing firm survival following the coup, I find that most hedged connection profiles substantially increase survival relative to unconnected firms and those tied exclusively to pro-democracy actors. Unexpectedly, hedged firms also outperform firms connected solely to the military victors. To explain this advantage, I identify a novel mechanism operating beyond state-firm relations: activist targeting. Pro-democracy activists strategically concentrated boycotts and international pressure on firms with exclusive military ties, while hedged firms were less likely to become focal targets. My findings extend political hedging theory by showing that, after democratic breakdown, diversified political connections enhance survival not necessarily through continued access to political power but by mitigating punishment from non-governmental stakeholders.

Strategic Divergence and Localized Clustering: MNE Innovation Restructuring Under Political Tension

IM
Yuqing Tang (Renmin University of China)
Drawing on Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) and the institutional theory, this study examines how bilateral political tension between home and host countries reshapes the innovation structure of multinational enterprises (MNEs). Analyzing 637,690 granted Chinese invention patents filed by MNEs with Chinese subsidiaries from 2012 to 2022, we find that political tension triggers a profound structural realignment in patent strategies. First, tension significantly decreases the likelihood of patent filing through local subsidiaries, reflecting a centralization of intellectual property ownership toward global ultimate owners (GUO) to establish a “legal firewall.” Second, political tension reduces geographic decoupling between applicants and inventors, driving a shift toward localized innovation clusters that minimize cross-border transaction costs. Third, we identify a strategic divergence: while headquarters reduce reliance on Chinese inventors (defensive retreat following TCE logic), Chinese subsidiaries paradoxically increase their employment of local inventors (reversal effect following institutional legitimacy logic). This divergence reveals that different organizational units within the same MNE navigate geopolitical tensions through fundamentally different strategic logics—efficiency-driven risk mitigation for headquarters versus legitimacy-driven commitment signaling for subsidiaries. Our findings contribute to understanding how organizations restructure their innovation structures to navigate geopolitical fragmentation, highlighting the micro foundations of MNE adaptation to institutional uncertainty.
