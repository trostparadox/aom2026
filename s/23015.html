Leadership Behaviors in Global Virtual Teams: A Qualitative Comparison of High and Low Performers

IM
Fatih Çetin (Baskent University); Faruk SAHIN (Mugla Sitki Koçman University); Ernesto Tavoletti (University of Macerata); Vas Taras (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
This study investigates leadership phenomenon in virtual teams, specifically examining effective leadership behaviours by comparing high- and low-performing teams. Using a qualitative research approach and team members' perspectives, it derives leadership behaviours that contribute to virtual team performance. Based on in-depth data obtained in the form of online free-text comments from members of 20 high-performing and 18 low-performing Global Virtual Teams within the X-Culture project, we identified four leadership dimensions that contribute to team performance, introduced as the ‘4Cs Virtual Leadership Model’: ‘Clarity', 'Communication', 'Catalyst', and ‘Caring'. Accordingly, this study makes a valuable contribution to the exploration of leadership behaviours that drive virtual team performance, an area that has not been extensively explored in previous research. The results could inform future research into the combined impact of various leadership behaviours on the effectiveness of virtual teams, with a focus on the core elements of functional leadership and several adaptive behaviours.

Feeling the Distance: A Mixed-Methods Study of Affective Retooling in Virtual Collaboration

IM
Fernando Trochez (Georgia State University); Leigh Anne Liu (Georgia State University); Allan W. Bird (Goa Institute of Management); Mourad Dakhli (Georgia State University); Edward Yihuai Wang (Georgia State University)
The rapid expansion of virtual collaboration has redefined global work, yet the affect-based competencies that enable effective cross-cultural interaction to remain insufficiently understood. Addressing this gap, we introduce the concept of affective retooling, describing the iterative emotional adjustment through which individuals reinterpret cultural cues and internalize new interaction norms in virtual collaboration. Grounded in Allport’s Intergroup Contact Theory and Molinsky’s Cultural Retooling Framework, we conceptualize Global Virtual Teams (GVTs) as developmental interventions for the enlargement of affect-based competencies and performance. Using a mixed-method design, we analyzed survey data and qualitative reflections from individuals engaged in international virtual collaboration. Quantitative analyses demonstrated significant improvements in intercultural sensitivity, specifically in interaction confidence, engagement, and attentiveness, as well as higher individual performance among GVT participants compared to a control group. Thematic analysis further revealed a cyclical progression in which participants progressed from initial enthusiasm through frustration and adjustment to eventual growth and intercultural understanding. By theorizing intercultural sensitivity as an affect-based developmental competency shaped through affective retooling our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of intercultural competence acquisition. Practically, the study positions global virtual teams as strategic platforms for cultivating global readiness, enabling organizations to transform cultural diversity into a source of collaboration, innovation, and performance.

A Dual-Level Cultural Perspective on Joint Problem Solving and Knowledge Transfer

IM
Chansoo Park (Memorial University of Newfoundland); Youngrae Kim; Magdalyn Adel Knopp
We aim to disentangle the cultural complexities of knowledge transfer by adopting a dual-level approach, examining how organizational and national cultural differences influence tacit and explicit knowledge transfer in international joint ventures (IJVs). Our hypotheses are tested using a structural equation model (SEM) based on survey data from 334 South Korean IJVs. We find that joint problem solving mediates both relationships; however, contrary to our expectations, the mediating effect is stronger for explicit knowledge than tacit knowledge, National cultural differences amplify joint problem solving’s role in tacit—but not explicit—knowledge transfer, supporting the identity-based logic of social identity theory. Using Social Identity Theory, this study uncovers how cultural differences shape knowledge transfer in IJVs. We identify a distinctive pattern: joint problem solving mediates organizational culture differences more strongly for explicit, formal knowledge, while national cultural differences enhance tacit, relationship-based knowledge transfer. These findings refine theory by revealing when and how cultural identity boundaries can both hinder and enhance knowledge sharing across organizations.

Between Cultures and Screens: Exploring the Nexus of Biculturalism in Global Virtual Teams

IM
Taha Kamat (Georg August University Göttingen)
Global demographic change and migration are reshaping international business and global work. As global virtual teams (GVTs) become a dominant form of collaboration, cultural diversity is increasingly embodied at the individual level. Responding to calls to move beyond static, nation-based views of culture, this study examines bicultural individuals as key actors in global virtual work. Focusing on second-generation migrants in Germany, a context marked by significant demographic and generational shifts, the study explores how biculturals experience and navigate cultural dynamics in GVTs. Based on qualitative in-depth interviews with bicultural professionals working in GVTs, the study investigates lived experiences of cultural interplay, highlighting cultural awareness, cultural frame switching, cultural connection, and cultural brokerage. The findings show that biculturals actively leverage their dual cultural socialization to interpret cultural cues, adapt communication, and bridge cultural divides in digitally mediated teams.

Strategic Alignment in the MNE: Corporate Terminology Management as a Coordination Mechanism

IM
Sergi Casals (Universidad EAFIT); Juan Carlos Diaz-Vásquez (Universidad EAFIT)
Multinational enterprises (MNEs) struggle with strategic alignment across borders because the adoption of a common language does not guarantee shared meaning. This disconnect leads to conceptual misalignment, where identical terms are interpreted differently across diverse organizational contexts, undermining global operations. This study addresses this managerial challenge by conceptualizing corporate terminology management as a strategic coordination mechanism. Drawing on an interdisciplinary framework and a critical realist, multi-location case study of an international joint venture, we investigate how the systematic management of corporate terminology facilitates alignment across hierarchical and functional boundaries. Our analysis identifies three generative mechanisms (Adaptive Decision-Making Flow, Knowledge Integration, and Knowledge Organization) that constitute a coordination system for the firm. We find that effective alignment is achieved not through rigid linguistic homogenization, but through organized variation, which maintains a stable conceptual core while enabling local flexibility. These findings substantively affect practice by repositioning terminology management from a peripheral administrative task to a strategic imperative. For global managers, this provides a novel framework to resolve the integration-responsiveness tension, ensuring that strategic intent is accurately translated into local action without sacrificing global coherence.
