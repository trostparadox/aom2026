Dissonance in Signaling: The Consequences of Parent-CVC Character Incongruence

STR
Michael S. McLeod (Wichita State University); Muhammad A. Muhammad (Texas Tech University); Joshua Sears (University of North Carolina Wilmington)
This study examines how misalignment between the character signals of corporate venture capital (CVC) units and their parent firms influences trust and investment relationship formation. Drawing on signaling theory, we investigate how ethos—reflecting overall character and conveyed by both CVCs and their parent firms—functions as a trust-building cue in contexts of intellectual property risk. Analyzing 110 CVCs and 1,301 ventures (2014–2018), we find that CVC ethos signals increase investment likelihood, while signal incongruence from parents and CVCs diminishes trust and weakens these effects. The findings identify hierarchical signal misalignment as a distinct barrier to CVC effectiveness.

When the Machine and Human Disagree: Evaluating the Novelty in Entrepreneurial Funding

STR
Lun Li (Beijing Normal University); Shiyu Wu (Beijing Normal University); Jianghua Zhou (Beijing Normal University); Kaige Xu (Beijing Normal University); Xiaohua Li (Northwest University)
Emerging evidence suggests that algorithmic performance in entrepreneurial resources allocation significantly diverges from that of humans. However, research investigating the underlying mechanisms driving these discrepancies remains scarce, particularly regarding the evaluation of venture novelty. To bridge this gap, this study examines how human and AI evaluators differ in assessing startup novelty within a high-stakes entrepreneurial context. Drawing on data from a real-world venture fund competition, we find a fundamental divergence: human evaluators assess startup novelty positively, whereas AI evaluators exhibit a systematic negative inclination. We propose that human evaluators employ a theory-based causal logic, while AI evaluators utilize a data-based predictive logic. Furthermore, we reveal that these tendencies are contingent on their information processing capability. High information load attenuates the human preference for novelty, while high terminological density softens the AI’s skepticism toward it. By uncovering these distinct decision logics, this study offers critical insights into the evolving landscape of strategic decision-making in entrepreneurial funding.

When Scope Pays Off: Digital Scope as a Latent Demand-Side Capability

STR
Biswapriya Saha (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Research on strategic scope remains ambiguous about whether broader activity portfolios advantage firms or impose costly complexity, particularly in resource-constrained entrepreneurial settings. We address this ambiguity by reconceptualizing digital scope as a latent, demand-side capability whose value is revealed, rather than continuously realized, when institutional disruptions reshape demand and evaluative criteria. Using India’s 2016 demonetization as a plausibly exogenous demand shock to digital financial services, we examine how pre-shock digital scope and access to co-located resources shape startup funding outcomes. Analyzing a longitudinal panel of 3924 Indian fintech startups using difference-in-differences method, we show that the shock does not increase early-stage funding on average. Instead, institutional disruption functions as a selection environment, reallocating capital toward startups whose pre-shock configurations align with newly salient demands. Startups with broader demand-side digital scope experience a substantial post-shock increase in the likelihood of securing Series A funding, but only when embedded in financial and technological ecosystems that support rapid commercialization. No corresponding scope premium emerges in regulatory ecosystems. By showing that scope advantages are episodic and ecosystem-contingent, this study reframes strategic scope as a demand-side capability whose value is revealed under disruption and advances understanding of how institutional shocks selectively shape entrepreneurial outcomes.

Missed Acquisitions and Subsequent Acquisition Behavior: The Role of Stock Market Reactions

STR
Julian Nickel (Justus Liebig University Giessen); Björn Hofmann (Justus Liebig University Giessen); Petrit Ademi (University of St. Gallen); Monika Schuhmacher
Competitive bidding situations in corporate acquisitions naturally involve winners and losers. The latter, bidders that fail to acquire a coveted target, experience a missed acquisition. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm and related research on behavioral decision-making, we conceptualize missed acquisitions as a salient qualitative distinct failure experience and examine how they shape firms’ subsequent acquisition behavior. Using a global sample of 446 acquisition announcements involving multiple public bidders, we show that firms that miss out on competitive bids subsequently engage in higher acquisition spending than firms that complete comparable deals. Moreover, stock market reactions to abandonment and completion announcements systematically moderate this relationship. Specifically, the positive differential effect of a missed acquisition relative to a completed one becomes more pronounced as market reactions turn more favorable. These findings contribute to acquisition research and behavioral theory by demonstrating that firms’ post-acquisition investment behavior depends not only on whether an acquisition attempt is completed or abandoned, but also on how that outcome is externally evaluated. In doing so, we identify an important boundary condition for behavioral responses following completed versus missed acquisitions in competitive strategic settings.

Reputations Over Returns: How Founder Status Overpowers Operating Performance in Private Equity

STR
Ozan Kirtac (London School of Economics and Political Science)
This study investigates a puzzle in private equity investment decisions: why does founder status often outweigh operating performance in investment evaluations? Study 1 analyzes a field survey of 381 investment professionals evaluating active deals across screening and due diligence stages. Logistic models reveal a suppression pattern in investment decision-making: the marginal effect of operating performance diminishes as founder status increases and becomes negligible beyond a high-status threshold. Suppression weakens in due diligence and among more experienced evaluators. Study 2, a preregistered 2×2 field experiment with 80 practicing investors, manipulates founder status and operating performance, providing causal evidence that operating performance increases advancement only when founder status is low. The findings introduce suppression as a distinct signal-portfolio outcome and clarify how bounded attention and reputational defensibility shape investment decision-making in private equity evaluations. Practical implications follow for investors, founders, and intermediaries involved in designing and interpreting early-stage deal materials.
