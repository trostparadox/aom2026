How Early Parental Divorce Leads to Reduced Entrepreneurial Activity Among Family Firm Successors

ENT
Zhenduo ZHU (Guangdong University of Foreign Studies); Qiao Liu (Guangdong University of Foreign Studies)
Parental divorce leads to family breakdown, which has a profound impact on children, especially in family businesses where founder parents are responsible not only for raising their children but also for grooming successors. Based on the life course theory, we explores the impact mechanism of parental divorce on the entrepreneurial activities of family business successors. Based on an empirical analysis of Chinese listed family firms in the manufacturing sector from 2007 to 2023, we find that parental divorce significantly inhibits offspring's entrepreneurial activity. However, increased parental accompaniment after divorce mitigates this inhibitory effect. Furthermore, this negative impact is also reduced when there are more siblings among the heirs or when the proportion of brothers is higher. The findings provide theoretical and practical guidance for family businesses on how to maintain entrepreneurial spirit in the face of family changes and expand the application of life course theory in family business research.

Mortality Awareness in Family Businesses: Exploring the Dutch Context

ENT
Sharda Nandram (Nyenrode Business University); Raysa Geaquinto Rocha (University of Essex); Henk van Beek (Nyenrode Business University)
Succession remains the Achilles' heel of family business long-term sustainability. Mortality exerts a profound influence on succession dynamics, yet operates largely beneath conscious awareness. Succession resistance represents a psychological defense mechanism against mortality awareness, suggesting that effective succession requires confronting existential realities that leaders systematically avoid. The absence of empirical research on mortality awareness in family business succession represents a valuable research avenue. Accordingly, using IBM SPSS and PROCESS, we analyze a quantitative survey of 200 family business leaders in the Netherlands to examine the relationships among mortality awareness, intentions for harmonious succession processes, and attitudes toward succession planning in Dutch family businesses. Our findings provide robust support for the conceptual model. The partial mediation pattern reveals that mortality awareness operates through dual pathways: a conscious, intention-mediated route accounting for nearly half the total effect, and a residual direct pathway suggesting parallel implicit processes. This study advances family business succession theory by providing a pioneering empirical evidence of mortality awareness as a dual-pathway mechanism influencing succession planning. These findings question the assumption that succession resistance stems solely from rational considerations, revealing implicit psychological processes that demand fundamentally different interventions to ensure transgenerational continuity.

Next Generation Family Business Leaders: Antecedents & Behavioral Mechanisms of Succession Intention

ENT
Anita Zehrer (MCI Management Center Innsbruck); Kevin S. Groves (Pepperdine University)
Building upon the theory of planned behavior and the family business literature, we examine the role of an entrepreneurial family background (family business exposure and family business self-efficacy) as an intergenerational influence on the succession intention of next generation family business leaders, and the mechanisms underlying that intention within the family. The mediating effects of attitude towards business succession and perceived family support are tested using a sample of 276 next generation family business leaders from Austria, USA, as well as other countries. Consistent with the theory and family business research, our results provide strong support for the direct and indirect effects of family business exposure and family business self-efficacy on succession intention through the mediation variables of attitude towards family business succession and perceived family support. Our findings contribute to the emerging literature on the entrepreneurial succession intention of next generation leaders by advancing theory on the explanatory mechanisms of intention to assume leadership roles in the family business. We conclude with a discussion of future research directions for family business scholarship and the practical implications for family business firms.

Prince or King? Bottom Up Leadership Construction During Succession Processes in Family Firms

ENT
Domenico Appio (Politecnico di Milano); Chiara Pantalena (Politecnico di Milano)
Succession is a pivotal moment in the lifecycle of family firms. While existing research predominantly conceptualizes succession as an incumbent-driven, top-down process, the agency of successors and the mechanisms through which they can proactively reshape the transition has been overlooked. This study examines how successors can drive succession in family firms. Drawing on an in-depth, revelatory case of Acetaia Giusti, a 400-year-old family business renowned for its artisanal balsamic vinegar, we develop an inductively derived model of a bottom-up model of leadership construction in intra-family succession. Our analysis traces how the seventeenth-generation successor strategically navigated and reshaped the transition while honoring long-standing family traditions. We identify two core phases, legitimation and identification. Successors move between these phases by managing organizational goal that shape both their evolving self-concept and the firm’s strategic direction. This dynamic interplay generates two critical outcomes (goal elicitation and a goal-centric self-concept) that ultimately empower the successor to redefine their role, renew the organization’s trajectory, and assume leadership. By illuminating the mechanisms through which successors actively drive the succession process, this study advances theory on family business leadership transitions and highlights how bottom-up agency can foster renewal in long-standing family firms.

Succession Intention in Family Firms: A Transformational-Transactional Leadership Perspective

ENT
Özgür Atilgan (Yeditepe University); R. Gabrielle Swab (Georgia Southern University); Franz Kellermanns (University of North Carolina at Charlotte); Esra Memili (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
We investigate how the leadership styles of predecessors (the CEO and the founder of the family firm) influence the next-generation family members’ succession intention (NFMSI) in family firms. Drawing on the Transactional–Transformational Leadership Paradigm (TTLP), Social Exchange Theory (SET), and the family business succession literature, we examine the impact of transactional and transformational leadership styles on the NFMSI. We also explore the impact of professionalization on the relationships between these two leadership styles and the NFMSI. The analyses of data from 582 respondents across 291 family firms support our predictions that both the transactional and transformational leadership styles will be positively associated with NFMSI. Interestingly, the results also show that professionalization mitigates the positive impact of transformational leadership on NFMSI. Our findings advance leadership, social exchange, and succession literatures by demonstrating that the predecessors’ transactional and transformational leadership styles can be important antecedents of the NFMSI.
