Generative AI Scoring of Constructed Responses: Psychometric Evidence for Research Applications

RM
Thomas Cavanagh (Dominican University of California); Christa Kiersch (University of Wisconsin-La Crosse); Alexander O'Brien (University of Wisconsin-La Crosse)
Constructed response assessments provide rich evidence of transfer and deep learning but require resource-intensive human scoring that creates practical barriers for management researchers. This study examines the reliability and validity of generative AI scoring as an alternative to traditional human scoring for research applications. Using an Evidence-Centered Design framework, we evaluated three commercial GenAI systems (GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Flash) scoring constructed responses assessing transfer of learning in motivation and leadership content domains (N = 243). Results demonstrated that GenAI achieved interrater reliability comparable to trained human raters, excellent test-retest reliability (ICC > .90) across all items, strong convergent validity with both research assistant and subject matter expert scores, and equivalent construct validity in predicting retention performance. Item-level analyses revealed that scoring challenges stemmed from item characteristics rather than rater type. These findings provide substantial psychometric evidence supporting GenAI as a reliable, valid, and accessible alternative to human scoring for constructed response assessments in management research contexts.

Too-Much-of-a-Good-Thing? A Review of (Inverted) U-Shaped Effects in Leadership Research

RM
Shilaan Alzahawi (Texas A&M University)
Leadership research has increasingly embraced "too-much-of-a-good-thing" theorizing, hypothesizing that many antecedents of leadership effectiveness exhibit (inverted) U-shaped rather than monotonic relationships. To test these hypotheses, researchers almost exclusively rely on quadratic regressions, treating a statistically significant quadratic term as evidence of a U-shape. In this paper, I illuminate why this approach is fundamentally flawed. I run an exhaustive search across 28 academic journals, and apply several analytical and simulation methods, to systematically review the trustworthiness of U-shaped effects in the leadership literature. The results strongly suggest that U-shaped hypothesis tests in the leadership literature suffer, concurrently, from a lack of statistical power and an excess of false positive results. Rather than calling for a paradigmatic shift away from U-shaped hypotheses, I conclude with a call for cautious interpretation and valid testing of U-shaped effects in leadership research.

On Substance and Shadow: Separating Substantive and Perceived Covariance in Personality Structures

RM
Andrew Perossa; Brian S. Connelly (University of Toronto); Michael P. Wilmot (University of Arkansas); Ray Fang
Organizational research routinely derives personality trait structures from single-rater measures, such as self-reports in selection research, supervisor ratings in performance studies, and peer ratings in team research. These structures inform scale development, construct validation, and applied recommendations. Yet single-rater structures blend two distinct sources of covariance: substantive covariance (genuine trait co-occurrence in targets) and perceived covariance (systematic rater biases). We use multilevel factor analysis to separate these sources in two large multirater samples: management students rated by acquaintances (Ntargets = 669; Ninformants = 2,059) and managers rated by colleagues (Ntargets = 6,026; Ninformants = 70,363). Between-target covariance (reflecting informant consensus) estimates substantive structure; within-target covariance (reflecting individual rater deviations from consensus) estimates perceived structure. Results reveal that substantive and perceived structures are similar but not parallel: roughly half of the factors differed meaningfully between levels in both samples. Critically, single-rater factor structures paralleled perceived—not substantive—covariance, suggesting that conventional trait structures partly reflect shared rater expectations rather than genuine trait architecture. We discuss implications for construct validation, scale development, and the interpretation of individual difference structures in organizational research.

Range Restriction Modeling of Adverse Impact, Job Performance, and Diversity Outcomes of Attraction

RM
Matt Cho (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Daniel Newman (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Chen Tang (American University); Leo Alexander (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Using Monte-Carlo simulations and a range restriction model of a job hiring process where applicants are attracted simultaneously based on minority/majority status (e.g., diversity recruiting) and/or on applicant qualifications (e.g., qualifications-based recruiting), prior to being screened on a selection predictor composite, we formalize the patterns of attraction effects on adverse impact, diversity, and job performance. Results reveal a paradox: no single attraction scenario improves all three outcomes (adverse impact, diversity, and job performance), although there exist applicant attraction conditions that can improve any two of the three. The pattern of attraction scenario results and the paradox persisted across a wide range of predictor composites. Counterintuitive implications for practice are discussed. Keywords: adverse impact, personnel selection, simulation

Does Gender-Neutral Language Influence Employee Surveys? Evidence from Hebrew and Arabic Samples

RM
Nir Milstein (Bar-Ilan University); Ronit Kark (Bar-Ilan University); Asmahan Masry-Herzallah
Surveys are central to management research, yet subtle design features can bias responses. One overlooked factor is survey language in gendered languages, such as Hebrew, Arabic, many Latin languages, and others, where the masculine form is typically the default. To examine whether alternative framings influence responses, we conducted three studies (N = 1,141) comparing masculine, feminine, and gender-neutral language in Hebrew and Arabic. Results showed that job satisfaction was consistently lower in the gender-neutral condition, whereas effects for other variables varied across contexts. The non-conventional feminine and neutral forms were associated with higher levels of careless responding, despite stable reliability and measurement invariance. These findings identify language framing as a subtle but systematic source of measurement bias, with implications for data validity and cross-cultural comparability. We recommend treating survey language as a deliberate design choice, monitoring careless responding when non-default options are used, and employing matched language to maintain inclusiveness without introducing systematic bias.
