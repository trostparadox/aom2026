Session overview

Nepotism—the appointment or preferential treatment of family members in positions of power—is a widespread practice across politics and business. Rather than being merely poorly understood, it is more accurately described as a socially contested and often negatively perceived practice, particularly due to concerns about merit, fairness, and transparency (Vinton, 1998). In government settings, for instance, countries such as the Philippines, India, and Brazil have implemented strict anti-nepotism regulations to curb corruption and promote competency, yet such policies sometimes lead to unexpected outcomes, including the rise of informal patronage networks (White, 2000). Empirical evidence shows strong persistence of political dynasties—in the Philippines, incumbents are five times more likely to have family members succeed them after elections compared to non-incumbents who narrowly lose, demonstrating the enduring advantages of family ties in public office (Querubin, 2016). In business contexts, family relationships remain influential in wealth accumulation and succession, with nepotism enduring even in large corporations (Gilding, 2005). Family businesses face strategic challenges in integrating next-generation members while maintaining legitimacy and performance (Barach et al., 1988). Research on nonprofits suggests that family relationships among board members may actually improve organizational outcomes, including better financial management and reporting quality (Boland et al., 2021). Research on nonprofit governance reveals that family relationships among board members may not be detrimental and can actually improve organizational outcomes. Boland et al. (2021) found that nonprofits with family relationships among board members demonstrated less management spending, lower excess cash levels, better reporting quality, and higher contributions compared to organizations without such relationships. This challenges conventional assumptions about family ties in governance. Brown (2005) emphasized that interpersonal dimensions of board performance uniquely explain organizational effectiveness judgments. Herman & Renz (2004) noted that finding the right fit among practices matters more than following prescribed "best practices." While Jegers (2009) discussed general governance frameworks for nonprofits, research on family firms provides additional context. Cascino et al. (2010) found family firms conveyed higher quality financial information, and Luo & Chung (2005) demonstrated that family ties can improve performance by providing informal norms that strengthen organizational intermediation, particularly during institutional transitions. Despite a small but growing body of research on nepotism in organizations, some questions still remind. Specifically, what is it that people actually view as nepotism? If so, do they interpret perceived nepotism through a justice lens, evaluating it according to key dimensions of fairness such as consistency, bias-freeness, and transparency?
