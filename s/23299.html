Casuistic Entrepreneurship: A Case Based Theory of Opportunities, Judgment, and Legitimation

ENT
Antonio Paco Giuliani (University of Bologna)
Entrepreneurship research has been organized around debates about whether opportunities are discovered or created. These approaches have generated powerful insights, yet they leave under-theorized how entrepreneurs and their audiences move from equivocal situations to plausible commitments when neither the opportunity nor its evaluation criteria are settled. I develop casuistry as an entrepreneurship theory. Casuistry treats entrepreneurial action as case-based practical reasoning: actors mobilize exemplary cases, map similarities and differences, argue by precedent, and progressively specify what counts as an opportunity, for whom, and with what moral and market consequences. Casuistry builds on opportunity discovery by taking seriously exogenous change and real constraints, and on opportunity creation by centering enactment and contestation under uncertainty. Its distinctive contribution is to theorize the reasoning infrastructure that links cognition, narrative, categorization, endorsement, and legitimation into an unfolding process that shapes both opportunity beliefs and opportunity realities. The theory yields propositions about entrepreneurial judgment, audience adjudication, category work, and path creation, and it opens an agenda for studying entrepreneurship as distributed, precedent-seeking, and performative.

Novel Framework of Entrepreneurial Judgment and Action: Evidence from High-Growth Entrepreneurs

ENT
Artur Tavares Vilas Boas Ribeiro (University of São Paulo); Inna Kozlinska (University of Groningen); Ana Paula Paes Leme Barbosa (University of Sao Paulo)
Despite growing interest in entrepreneurial judgment, substantial debate remains regarding how judgment unfolds into action under uncertainty. Existing theoretical work largely treats judgment and action as linear or abstract constructs, offering limited insight into the micro-processes through which beliefs are actualized into action. Through an inductive qualitative study of 34 venture-backed high-growth entrepreneurs, we conceptualize entrepreneurial judgment as part of a belief system in which entrepreneurs iteratively assess resources, market conditions, and opportunity costs until action thresholds are crossed. Our findings reveal cyclical transitions between distal judgments - self-evaluations of values and aspirations that generate entrepreneurial intentions - and proximal judgments that assess specific market conditions and resource availability to determine when to act. We advance entrepreneurial judgment literature in two ways: first, by theorizing how judgments and intentions co-evolve through a non-linear enactment dynamic rather than following sequential stages; second, by shifting analytical attention from uncertainty reduction toward belief formation as the central driver of judgment-to-action dynamics. Our framework integrates judgment more coherently with entrepreneurial action literature, offering a unified account of how subjective beliefs and opportunity cost evaluations drive resource commitment under uncertainty.

Opportunity and Uncertainty: A Systematic Review of How Literature Incorporates Both Constructs

ENT
Victor Silva Corrêa (Paulista University); Maria Arruda; Wilquer Silvano de Souza Ferreira
Purpose – This systematic review explores how entrepreneurship literature integrates the constructs of opportunity and uncertainty, addressing the theoretical tension between opportunity-based approaches and Knightian uncertainty. Design/methodology/approach – A systematic review of 78 peer-reviewed articles (2012–2024) from Scopus and Web of Science was conducted. Articles were inductively categorized to examine how opportunity and uncertainty are conceptualized and interrelated. Findings – Opportunity is predominantly treated as a theoretical construct, while uncertainty is framed as contextual. Discovery and creation theories dominate, with Shane, Venkataraman, and Kirzner representing discovery, and Alvarez, Barney, and Sarasvathy representing creation. Knightian uncertainty is most cited, followed by Milliken’s typology. The review introduces a novel quadrant-based framework, revealing a previously unexplored theory–context dynamic and proposing a fourth quadrant that conceptualizes uncertainty as theory and opportunity as context. Originality – This study makes four contributions: (1) it uncovers a theory–context pattern in how opportunity and uncertainty are integrated; (2) it maps dominant and emerging schools of thought and key authors; (3) it introduces a 3A–3B–3AB classification system for contextual uncertainty; and (4) it proposes a quadrant-based analytical framework that identifies conceptual intersections and a new research agenda. This framework challenges prevailing assumptions and opens avenues for theoretical-empirical exploration of entrepreneurial phenomena through the lens of uncertainty.

Why Finding a Problem Is Insufficient: A Case for Interest–Market Fit and Entrepreneurial Well-Being

ENT
Tomé Guerreiro D O Salgueiro (University of Delaware); Johann Ducharme (University of Delaware); Vincent L. DiFelice (University of Delaware)
Entrepreneurship research and pedagogy have emphasized early problem identification and rapid progression toward product–market fit. While this orientation has generated valuable insights and promises to reduce market risk, it rests on a largely unexamined axiom: that entrepreneurs already know what genuinely interest them before engaging in entrepreneurial action. In this paper, we argue that this assumption represents an overlooked weakness in prevailing models of entrepreneurial emergence. Drawing on Aristotelian understandings of eudaimonic well-being (EWB), we introduce the concept of interest–market fit as an antecedent condition in the entrepreneurial process. Interest–market fit refers to the developmental alignment between an entrepreneur’s interests and a domain of economic activity, prior to the identification of a specific problem or venture idea. We suggest that bypassing this formative stage may short-circuit pathways to meaningful, sustainable, and fulfilling entrepreneurial, personal, and career outcomes. By theorizing interest– market fit, this paper contributes a human-developmental foundation to entrepreneurship theory and offers implications for research, education, and practice.
