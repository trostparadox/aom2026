Freeze or Fight: Innovation Response Under Asymmetric and Symmetric Contexts

STR
Weixi Sha (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Yanan Xu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Chengcheng Xia (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
The ambiguous viewpoints on competition-innovation relationship may stem from neglecting competition’s perceptual dimension, specifically failing to distinguish asymmetric competition (i.e., non-mutual recognition) from symmetric (i.e., mutual recognition) competition. And the greatest danger for firms may not be intense, but unrecognized competitions. We theorize that symmetric competition fosters a “fight” response, enhancing vigilance and perceived control, thus stimulating inbound open, diversified, and explorative innovations. Conversely, asymmetric competition triggers a “freeze” response via threat rigidity, suppressing innovation. The data of Chinese enterprises from 2011 to 2023 provides empirical support for our hypotheses. Our study offers a new perspective on the competition-innovation paradox by highlighting the heterogeneous effects of different competitive perceptions.

What Strategic Management Can Learn from Poker: Game Theory and Beyond

STR
Juha Uotila (University of Warwick)
In this paper, we examine the links between business and the game of poker, and explore how an understanding of poker theory can inform strategic management. We first consider the application of game theory in strategy research and discuss ways in which concepts and mechanisms identified in the poker world can be useful to shed light on strategy phenomena and open up new lines of inquiry. We use four game theoretical concepts – credible commitments, mixed strategies, metagame, and exploitative strategies – to illustrate the parallels between poker and business, and suggest avenues for future research. We further discuss the applicability of poker knowledge to the world of strategic management more broadly, including its potential to contribute to our understanding of behavioral strategy and decision making under uncertainty.

From Mutual Forbearance to Rivalry: The Role of Non-Overlapping Markets and Industry Growth

STR
Takhaui Kamzabek (SDU University); Dan Lovallo (The University of Sydney)
Multi-market contact (MMC) is often seen as a source of mutual forbearance, where firms competing across multiple markets restrain aggressive behavior to avoid costly retaliation. However, recent evidence suggests this logic does not always hold, especially for investment decisions. This study examines when MMC gives rise to multifrontal competition by identifying two key boundary conditions: (1) profitable non-overlapping markets, which provide resources to finance aggressive moves in contested markets, and (2) growth in markets important to rivals, which draws competitive attention despite retaliation risks. Using a panel of S&P multi-segment firms and comprehensive MMC measures (sales-at-risk, market importance to MMC rivals, and rivals’ market dominance), we find that firms are motivated to invest in shared markets when they earn profits from non-overlapping markets and when rival-important markets are growing. These findings suggest that mutual forbearance is contingent and breaks down when resource availability and growth incentives are strong.

Setting the Stage for the Battle: Multi-Country Overlap and Technological Entry

STR
Gabriel Franceschini (University of Bologna); Marco Corsino; Claudio Giachetti (University of Bologna); Paola Giuri (University of Bologna)
Technological competition increasingly unfolds in the technological arena, where firms position themselves through patenting rather than direct market confrontation. In this context, technological entry serves as a central mechanism of exploratory search. Building on research that extends multimarket competition to knowledge-based rivalry, we conceptualize multi-country overlap in knowledge activities—defined as the extent to which a focal firm and its rivals patent in the same geographical jurisdictions—as a form of multipoint contact in technological competition and examine how it shapes firms’ technological entry decisions. We distinguish between entry into technological domains that are new to the focal firm but already established by rivals and entry into unexplored domains that are new to both the focal firm and its rivals. Drawing on the logic underlying the mutual forbearance hypothesis, we examine whether repeated contact with the same rivals across national patent jurisdictions restrains technological entry when competition unfolds through anticipatory knowledge positioning rather than direct attacks. Using an original panel of 1,619 firm-year observations from the woodworking machinery industry, we find that greater multi-country overlap is associated with higher entry intensity along both dimensions, with a stronger effect for entry into unexplored domains. These findings show that, in technological competition, multipoint contact can stimulate both technological convergence and differentiation rather than restrain exploratory search.

What a Rival Believes: How Implicit Theories Drive Competitive Interpretation and Responses

STR
Michael C. Withers (University of Notre Dame); Mia Simmons-Yi (University of Notre Dame); Susan Grace Oldroyd (Texas A&M University)
Research on competitive dynamics emphasizes that competitive behavior is influenced not only by firm actions but also by how managers interpret rival moves. However, the role of managers’ underlying beliefs about the nature of competition in influencing competitive interpretation and responses is undertheorized. Building on this insight, we integrate competitive dynamics research with the implicit theories literature to develop a theory of how managers’ zero- and positive-sum beliefs about competition shape competitive interpretation, perceived tension, and responses. We theorize that implicit theories of competition function as meaning systems that influence attention, attribution of rival intent, and perceived competitive tension, and that asymmetries and misperceptions of rivals’ implicit theories amplify competitive tension, escalation, and aggressive responses.
