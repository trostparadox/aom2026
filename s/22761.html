A Status Account of Managerial Responses to Subordinate Wrongdoing

OB
Ruo Mo (Montpellier Business School)
Despite the serious consequences of managerial cover-ups of subordinate wrongdoing, the current knowledge of its psychological drivers is limited, which primarily assumes that managers engage in cover-ups out of a desire to protect others. We complement this view and suggest that managerial cover-up behavior is also motivated by concerns about managers’ own social standing and self-worth. Drawing on self-evaluation maintenance theory, we build a model to offer a status-based account of managerial cover-up behavior. Specifically, we propose that when managers perceive themselves as having lower status, they are more likely to experience self-esteem threats, which in turn motivates them to cover up their subordinates’ wrongdoing. The indirect effect of perceived low status on cover-up behavior is stronger when managers have high leader-member exchange with their superiors. Evidence from two multi-wave studies supported the theoretical model. This research not only advances the theory building of cover-up behavior but also contributes to the broader literature on social closeness and responses to wrongdoing.

The Punishment Penalty

OB
Lauren Eskreis-Winkler; Kaitlin Woolley; Victoria Liu (Northwestern University); Rachel Jensen (Northwestern University)
In a just world, people judge wrongdoers based on what they did wrong—that is, their wrongdoing. Yet across 10 studies (N = 3,930), observers were more likely to judge and discriminate against wrongdoers when they learned the punishment the wrongdoer received versus the act of wrongdoing committed. This punishment penalty occurred both within and beyond the criminal justice system: for criminals convicted of crimes (Study 1), students suspended from school (Study 2), undergraduates placed on probation (Study 2), and employees penalized at work (Study 2). Why is punishment more stigmatizing than wrongdoing itself? We find that the standard punishments society metes out imply that the wrongdoer cannot change (Studies 3-4). Replacing standard punishments with reform-minded ones (Study 4) or highlighting the reform-minded features of standard punishments (Study 5) leads people to believe the offender can change, causing the punishment penalty to attenuate. Long-standing philosophies of justice suggest punishment is a tool for reform. Yet we find this statement is not true, psychologically. Society’s most common punishments led observers to believe the wrongdoer was less likely to change, and engendered discrimination.

Trust Repair Following Organizational Misconduct: A Systematic Review and Integrative Framework

OB
Changfeng Wang (Zhejiang Normal University); Yanglei Cao (Zhejiang Normal University); Xianrui Bao (Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine); Qiuling Luo (Zhejiang Normal University)
Organizational misconduct not only disrupts operations but fundamentally severs the ethical bond between firms and stakeholders. While research on firm trust repair (FTR) has proliferated, the literature remains fragmented, often isolating specific repair strategies (e.g., apologies) from the temporal context of the crisis. To address the disconnect, we systematically review 222 articles published between 1996 and 2025. By synthesizing these findings through the dual lenses of attribution theory and crisis lifecycle theory, we construct an integrative process framework. The framework initially classifies organizational misconduct into no violation (external), competence (unstable/controllable), and integrity (stable/controllable) violations. Subsequently, we establish a dynamic three-stage process model: (1) the acute stage, requiring affective repair strategies (e.g., apologies, denial) to soothe immediate negative emotions; (2) the chronic stage, necessitating informational repair strategies (e.g., explanation, transparency) to facilitate cognitive reconstruction; and (3) the terminal stage, employing functional repair strategies (e.g., contract, compensation, organizational change and trust transfer) to address root causes and prevent recurrence. Our analysis reveals that the efficacy of FTR is not intrinsic to specific strategies but is contingent upon their alignment with both the violation archetype and the crisis lifecycle stage.

Moral Self-Regulatory Perspective Toward the Effect of Punishment for Employee UPB

OB
Dayoung Kim (Auburn University); Lei Huang (Auburn University); Jiayu Song (Auburn University)
Unethical pro-organizational behavior (UPB)—acts that are morally questionable but aim to benefit an organization—has often been described as behavior that organizations tolerate or even reward due to its pro-organizational nature. Challenging this assumption, the present paper investigates how punishment for UPB affects punished individuals’ psychological and behavioral responses. Integrating moral self-regulation theory and social persuasion theory, we expect that punishment for UPB can lead to both self-focused regulation, represented by the internalization of the moral aspect of one’s role (i.e., role ethicality), and other-focused regulation, represented by one’s confidence in organizational integrity (i.e., organizational trustworthiness) among punished UPB actors. We further expect that punishment framing—specifically, how the punishment motive is communicated (i.e., ethical versus business-frames)— will moderate such effects. Across two vignette-based experiments, we find some support for our hypotheses. Punishment for UPB was positively associated with punished individuals’ role ethicality. Moreover, it was positively associated with organizational trustworthiness only when the punishment was ethically framed, which in turn increases their intentions for ethical voice. Punishment framing did not moderate the relationship between punishment and role ethicality. Theoretical contributions and implications for managerial practices and future research are discussed.
