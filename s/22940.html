How Digital Transformation Enhances ESG Performance: A TOE Framework Perspective

ODC
Hubin Li (School of Management, Northwestern Polytechnical University); Xue yan Dong (School of Management, Northwestern Polytechnical University); Jun Zhang
Digital transformation is reshaping how firms create, govern, and disclose value; yet, its implications for environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance in emerging markets remain underexplored. Drawing on signalling theory and the Technology–Organization–Environment (TOE) framework, this study examines how digital transformation affects corporate ESG performance and how this relationship depends on the surrounding institutional context. Using panel data for Chinese A-share listed firms from 2011 to 2023, we construct a firm-level digital transformation index and estimate panel regression models with extensive robustness checks. The empirical results indicate that digital transformation has a significant positive impact on ESG performance. Moreover, this positive effect is amplified in firms operating in stronger institutional environments, under more intensive media supervision, in regions with deeper Confucian cultural traditions, and along high-speed railway corridors that facilitate information flows and resource mobility. These findings highlight that technological adoption alone is insufficient; governance mechanisms, cultural norms, and infrastructural conditions jointly shape whether digital investments translate into sustainability outcomes. The study contributes to the growing literature on digital transformation and ESG by integrating TOE and signalling perspectives in an emerging-market setting and by identifying context-specific conditions under which digital tools are most effective in enhancing ESG performance. It also offers actionable implications for managers, regulators, and investors seeking to leverage digital transformation to support sustainable corporate development.

The Future We Fear: How Undesirable Futures Drive Anticipatory Adaptation in Digital Sustainability

ODC
Angela Greco (Delft University of Technology)
Organizations are increasingly adopting digital technologies to advance their sustainability goals. Yet, the asynchronous pace of digitalization and sustainability transitions creates a critical risk: lock-ins that limit adaptability, entrench narrow optimization logics, and generate unintended, undesirable environmental and social consequences. Existing research offers little insight into how organizations can address these new forms of rigidity or unintended consequences in the present, before they become irreversible in the future. Building on the literature of future-making, we examine this phenomenon in a three-year action research study of a Dutch social housing association that utilizes predictive digital twin technology to achieve carbon neutrality. We observed how managers and stakeholders engaged in an iterative three-practice process that we refer to as anticipatory adaptation. Anticipatory adaptation evolved from (a) extending temporal attention beyond the project lifecycle, to (b) redistributing communicative agency across organizational and community levels, and then (c) integrating user concerns into experimental design adjustments. We identified three mechanisms—attuning to emerging concerns, recasting futures, and redistributing agency—which combined in different ways throughout the process to enable anticipatory adaptation. Our data challenges the boundaries of ‘desirable’ future imaginaries, suggesting that undesirable, even dystopian, futures serve as potent catalysts for organizational responsiveness in contexts in which organizations interact with marginalized communities. We offer a conceptual model for how organizations can balance desirable and undesirable futures when using digital tools for sustainability.

The Role of Risk-Taking Pioneers in Technological Diffusion: Case of Datafication in Retailing

ODC
Lauri Juhani Paavola (University of Eastern Finland); Richard Cuthbertson (University of Oxford)
Theories looking at technological diffusion tend to explain datafication through the combined effects of less expensive data storage and analytics alongside more advanced data applications. When the benefits exceed the costs, datafication is seen to progress. However, companies looking to gain first-mover advantage often react before the benefits are obvious (i.e., before benefits exceed costs), and may therefore have an unknown, and understudied, influence on the overall diffusion process. Our study analyzes how such risk-taking industry pioneers influence technological diffusion processes. We discover three distinct mechanisms: (1) Risk-taking pioneer(s) search for more value from the data and so advance the understanding of data use, (2) Risk-taking pioneer(s) monetize such discovered value with external partners and hence alter industry dynamics, and (3) External partners and competitors combine efforts to create competition for the risk-taking pioneer(s). We describe these mechanisms and develop a process model of how they interrelate. Furthermore, our analysis suggests that the intent to disperse costs propels the diffusion process forward. Our longitudinal analysis spans a period starting from the risk-taking pioneers’ implementation (1995) to the point at which the benefits of data are considered to exceed its collection and utilization costs (2015).
