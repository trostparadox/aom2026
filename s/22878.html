The Tech Overload Trap: How Blurred Work Boundaries Drive Quiet Quitting

OB
Sushmita Bordoloi (Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee); Rajib Lochan Dhar (Indian Institute of Technology, Roorkee)
The expectation of being constantly connected has become a defining feature of contemporary work life. As digital tools increasingly blur temporal and spatial boundaries, employees face growing technology-enabled job demands that may quietly erode engagement rather than provoke overt withdrawal. Building on this concern, the present study examines how Technology Overload (TOL) relates to Quiet Quitting (QQ) by drawing on the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) framework. Specifically, we propose that Work-Family Conflict (WFC) acts as a key mechanism through which technology-induced demands spill over into the nonwork domain and subsequently shape withdrawal-oriented work behaviors. At the same time, this process may not operate uniformly across employees. Technological Self-Efficacy (TSE) is therefore introduced as a boundary condition that can attenuate the adverse consequences of technology overload. Using survey data from 241 employees working in technology-driven industries in India and analyzed through Structural Equation Modeling, the findings support the proposed moderated mediation model. TOL is indirectly associated with higher levels of QQ through increased WFC, while higher TSE weakens this indirect effect. These results suggest that Quiet Quitting in digitally saturated contexts may reflect an adaptive response to sustained demands rather than a simple lack of motivation. By extending the JD-R framework to the emerging phenomenon of quiet quitting, this study contributes to the growing literature on digital stress and employee withdrawal. Practically, the findings point to the need for organizations to calibrate technology-related job demands, strengthen employees’ technological capabilities, and adopt boundary-supportive practices to sustain engagement in always-on work environments.

Job Complexity and Employee Wellbeing: The Moderating Role of Trait Plasticity

OB
Peter Joseph O'Connor (Queensland University of Technology); Andrew Spark (Queensland University of Technology); Nerina Jimmieson (Queensland University of Technology)
Job complexity has become an increasingly prominent feature of contemporary work, yet its implications for employee wellbeing remain unclear. Although complex jobs can provide opportunities for engagement and growth, they are also associated with greater cognitive demands and strain. As a result, existing research offers mixed evidence regarding whether job complexity enhances or undermines wellbeing. We argue that these inconsistencies reflect unexamined individual differences in adaptability to complex work environments. Focusing on trait plasticity, a higher-order personality dimension capturing openness to experience and extraversion, we propose that job complexity has divergent wellbeing consequences depending on employees’ dispositional capacity to engage with novelty and challenge. Using data from 8,081 employed adults drawn from the Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia survey, we examine the relationship between job complexity, job satisfaction, and job stress, and test trait plasticity as a moderator. Multilevel analyses accounting for occupational clustering reveal that job complexity is associated with higher job stress on average, but its effects on both job satisfaction and stress depend on trait plasticity. For individuals high in plasticity, greater job complexity is associated with relatively higher job satisfaction and lower stress, whereas for individuals low in plasticity, greater complexity is associated with poorer wellbeing. These findings suggest that job complexity is neither uniformly beneficial nor detrimental and highlight the importance of considering personality-based differences in adaptability when evaluating the wellbeing consequences of complex work.

Interrupted but Activated: How Digital Work Interruptions Drive Innovation and Well-Being

OB
Xin Ai (Tsinghua University); Xiaoming Zheng (Tsinghua University); Yating Wang (Renmin University of China); Peiqi Yang (Renmin University of China); Sheng Zhang (East China Normal University)
Traditional research has widely viewed interruptions as intrusive and depleting. However, with the proliferation of instant messaging and collaboration platforms as the primary means of real-time interaction in the workplace, the core context of interruptions has shifted to digital environments. Most existing studies treat digital technology merely as a channel for delivering interruptions, overlooking how features of the digital work ecology, such as the “always-on” culture, unpredictability in triggering, responsiveness to orchestrated information flow, and micro-task nature, may fundamentally alter the nature and mechanisms of interruptions. To address this gap, this study draws on proactive motivation theory to propose that digital work interruptions can be transformed into contextual stimuli that promote employee innovation and well-being by stimulating state curiosity (energized to), enhancing the sense of control (can do), and strengthening perceived obligation (reason to). Employing both laboratory experiments and experience sampling methodology, the research validates the unique psychological effects of digital work interruptions and their dynamic mechanisms in real-world work settings. By shifting beyond the traditional negative perspective on interruptions, this study reveals their transformation from “passive burden” to “active energizer” in digital contexts. It enriches proactive motivation theory by being the first to demonstrate that transient, exogenous digital work interruptions can simultaneously trigger the proactive motivation pathways. Furthermore, it offers a new theoretical lens for understanding job design in the digital age.

Meditation App Use for Employee Stress Management: A Multi-Wave Field Study on the Role of Frequency (WITHDRAWN)

OB
Rebekka Tavakoli (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Maria Tims; Susanne Beijer (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Psychological stress is widespread among today’s workforce, threatening employee health and wellbeing while imposing significant costs on organisations and society. Meditation apps have gained popularity as stress management tools among Western workers, yet the impact of meditation frequency on reducing stress over time is underexplored as well as the mechanism through which meditation frequency unfolds its effect on stress. We conducted a multi-source, multi-wave field study with N = 440 existing users of a commercially available meditation app in the Netherlands. To examine the interplay between stress levels and meditation frequency over time as well as the moderating role of user motivation, participants were free to use the app at their own discretion. We furthermore examined the role of psychological capital as a mediating mechanism. Results showed that participants on average meditated 5.8 times per week. However, based on cross-lagged panel modelling, no support was found for our hypotheses that the level of stress positively influences meditation frequency (inverted U-shape) and that meditation frequency, in turn, reduces stress (U-shape) over time, through PsyCap. We highlight areas for future research into this stress-management strategy as the dose-response relationship between meditation app use and stress remains a puzzle. Keywords: meditation app, stress, dose-response, psychological capital, CLPM

Exhausted by Exploitation: Daily Resource Loss, Sleep, and Employee Engagement

OB
Yasir Mansoor Kundi; Subhan Shahid; Mohammed Aboramadan; Naseeb Dar (IESE Business School)
Drawing on conservation of resources theory, this study examines the daily effects of exploitative leadership on employee work engagement. Using a 12-day diary design with 874 observations nested within 80 employees, we show that daily exploitative leadership depletes employees’ physical and emotional resources and undermines sleep quality. However, only physical resource depletion indirectly reduces next-day work engagement via poorer sleep. These findings highlight the short-term resource-based and physiological mechanisms through which exploitative leadership erodes engagement and contribute to daily leadership and employee well-being research.
