From Failure to Pivot: Considering Psychological Capital Dynamism and Funding Performance Feedback

ENT
Shutao Zhou (Shenzhen University); Yifu Li; Peng Luo (Shenzhen University); Shuijing Jie (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
Increasing studies focus on the antecedents of entrepreneurial pivoting after venture failure. This study adds to this research stream by examining how the dynamism of entrepreneurs' psychological capital – captured as psychological capital discrepancy – affects pivoting in crowdfunding. We propose a research model considering the moderating effects of funding performance feedback grounded in social cognitive theory. To test our hypotheses, we constructed a unique dataset of 445 matched pairs of reward-based crowdfunding projects with prior failure experience from Kickstarter. Our hierarchical regression results reveal an inverted U-shaped relationship between psychological capital discrepancy and the likelihood of project category pivoting. Furthermore, our moderating analysis results show that the funding ratio strengthens this relationship, while funding speed weakens it. Robustness checks affirmed the reliability of our findings. This research advances theory by clarifying how dynamic psychological capital influences post-failure pivoting behavior in crowdfunding. We also provide several practical implications.

From Failure to Restoration: Shame as a Self-Regulatory Force in Entrepreneurship

ENT
Gabriella Cacciotti (Baylor University); Stephen Xu Zhang (Baylor University); Siran Zhan (UNSW Sydney)
Entrepreneurial failure often elicits shame, an emotion typically viewed as inhibiting re-entry and recovery. Challenging this assumption, we develop and test a theory of shame as a self-regulatory and relational signal that can motivate restoration. Across two studies, we show that shame following entrepreneurial failure can fuel re-entry by activating restorative (rather than protective) motivation and, among re-entering entrepreneurs, is associated with superior subsequent venture performance. Drawing on control theory and sociological theories of emotion, we further demonstrate that this performance effect is stronger when entrepreneurs internally attribute responsibility for prior failure and weaker in industries dominated by large incumbent firms. Together, our findings reconceptualize shame as a dynamic force shaping post-failure engagement and performance.

Guilty as Charged? How Framing Prior Entrepreneurial Failure Shapes Equity Investors’ Evaluations

ENT
Maja Schmitz (University of Passau); Suleika Bort (University of Passau)
A critical concern for failed founders re-entering the entrepreneurial landscape with a new venture is to convince investors that prior failure is not a flaw but an opportunity. Drawing on organizational stigma and framing literatures, this study examines how different frames potentially shift investors’ judgements of prior entrepreneurial failure along a continuum from stigmatization to elevation. Two experiments in the equity crowdfunding context highlight that investors’ judgements related to prior entrepreneurial failure correspond rather to indifference than stigmatization. Investors’ comments that overemphasize values such as security and responsibility at the expense of courage and resilience can draw other investors into a stigmatization process. Entrepreneurs’ narratives that focus on the actor instead of the event of prior failure have the potential to positively influence investor judgements and funding decisions related to the subsequently founded venture. Keywords: entrepreneurial failure, organizational stigma, framing

Suppressed Entrepreneurial Action: Pre-Launch Failure as a Developmental Breakdown

ENT
Abhinav Chaturvedi (BML Munjal University)
Research on entrepreneurship failure is primarily centred on post-entry outcomes, implicitly treating venture creation as the act of entrepreneurship and ignoring failures that occur during venture development. This conceptual work addresses this gap in the literature by theorising pre-launch failure as a developmental breakdown in the venture emergence process prior to market entry. In this study, insights from the theory of entrepreneurial action, process perspectives on organising, fear-of-failure scholarship, and research on opportunity evaluation are considered to re-conceptualise non-entry not as the absence of entrepreneurial intention or motivation, but as suppressed entrepreneurial action amidst uncertain conditions. With this background, a process model of pre-launch failure is developed, specifying three mutually reinforcing mechanisms: anticipatory fear that adjusts the threshold for developmental action in the face of uncertainty, constrained opportunity evaluation, which prolongs ambiguity in the absence of validating feedback, and disrupted learning and sequencing, which cuts action-feedback loops required for progression towards venture creation. With the Global South as an analytically useful context, it is evident that high costs of failure, limited to no institutional support, and informality amplify these three universal mechanisms without resorting to contextual exceptionality. This paper extends the study of entrepreneurship failure upstream by redefining non-entry as a processual outcome and providing a developmental explanation for entrepreneurial heterogeneity based on early-stage variations in venture emergence.
