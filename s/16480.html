Session overview

Death is a ubiquitous human experience—yet has received scattered attention in organizational studies. Extant research considers a wide array of death-related phenomena, from how people respond to the loss of their enterprises to how compassion is sustained in the context of caring at the point of end-of-life rather than waiting for death. However, attention to death has been either proximal or metaphorical. While research examined how people cope with the prospect or aftermath of literal death—the loss of life of loved ones or colleagues—or manage their wealth and ownership, researchers often use death as a metaphor to describe the termination of organizational activities, such as when organizations permanently close, social movements dissolve, or strategic initiatives end. The diminishing awareness about death’s integral place in life has implications for how we conceptualize temporality, agency, and life in organizational scholarship in view of ‘distant’ mortality. We have yet to understand how organizing and life become shaped when death is more than a metaphor or a manageable outcome, but an integral and necessary part of life itself—one that reshapes temporality, moral responsibility, identity, and meaning. In this panel symposium, we bring together scholars whose work engages with different aspects of death across a range of contexts, interpretive lenses, and philosophical orientations. In doing so, we hope to seed new ideas about how we think about death and what this means for theoretical, phenomenological, and philosophical work in organizational theory, entrepreneurship, and organizational behavior.
