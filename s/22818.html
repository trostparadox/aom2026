The Role of Expectancy Violation in Attributing the Blame to an Organization

SIM
Gul Beril KIMILLI (Université catholique de Louvain); Flore Bridoux (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Corentin Hericher (Université catholique de Louvain); Nicolas Raineri (ICN Business School)
This research studies the extent to which corporate social responsibility (CSR) may backfire by increasing blame following organizational harm. Integrating expectancy violation theory, we hypothesize that CSR elevates employees’ positive expectancies about how the organization will treat stakeholders. When a harmful event contradicts these expectancies, employees experience stronger negative expectancy violation, which increases blame attributions. Two studies test this model. In Study 1 (five-day vignette experiment), participants were exposed to CSR versus business-as-usual practices and then to a harmful versus business-as-usual event; CSR exposure increased positive expectancies, and higher expectancies amplified the effect of harm on negative expectancy violation, which indirectly increased blame. Study 2 (two-wave situated experiment) replicates the moderated mediation pattern in a workplace-anchored context: following a harmful event, employees with higher positive expectancies reported stronger negative expectancy violation and greater blame. Overall, the findings suggest that CSR can create a moral baseline that heightens perceived violations when harm occurs, making reputational benefits conditional and potentially costly when organizations fall short of the standards they signal.

Firms’ Response Speed to Media Threats Under Stakeholder Expectation Violations

SIM
Rui Jiang (The University of Texas at Dallas); Zhiang “John” Lin (The University of Texas at Dallas)
While research on firms’ responses to media threats has explored what firms do and say (substantive actions and response content), less is known about why response speed varies, which has strategic ramifications. Extending threat rigidity theory with expectancy violations theory, we focus on the temporal dimension and posit that firms’ response speed is driven by available routines and perceived urgency of differing natures of media threats. Specifically, ethics-related media threats may induce slower responses than operation-related ones due to the lack of existing routines for governance complexities. Further, when media coverage contradicts stakeholder expectations—whether socially or financially based—the heightened urgency may prompt firms to respond more swiftly. Using a sample of public Chinese firms between 2016 and 2022, we find empirical support for our theoretical framework. By showing how different media threats and stakeholder expectations jointly shape firms’ response speed strategies, this study contributes to a nuanced understanding of how firms perceive and navigate media threats.

Wind of Change: How Individuals Tackle Wicked Problems Through Enclaving

SIM
Madeleine Meurer (Rotterdam School of Management); Christian Fieseler (BI Norwegian Business School); Simona Irina Goia (Bucharest University of Economic Studies); Vanessa Vargas
Wicked problems are commonly theorized as requiring collective action because their interdependence, causal ambiguity, and evaluative pluralism exceed the capacity of isolated actors. Yet in many settings, collective action remains absent or episodic for extended periods, while individuals still initiate and sustain locally grounded entrepreneurial initiatives that improve everyday life. How do individuals persist in tackling wicked problems when collective infrastructures do not consolidate? We address this question in disability inclusion in Romania, where formal rights coexist with persistent implementation gaps, low visibility, and fragmented accountability. Drawing on 53 life-story and semi-structured interviews, we trace engagement across four phases and combine phase-specific topic modeling, and inductive qualitative process theorizing, triangulated with social media artifacts, field observations, and archival sources. We theorize enclaving as a generative mechanism through which individuals construct bounded arenas that make responsibility man-ageable and progress perceptible in the absence of collective action. Enclaving unfolds as individuals first manufacture tractable visibility – turning private exclusion into publicly contestable claims, dignity frames, and attention events – and then convert that visibility into entrepreneurial activities by building bounded infrastructures (artifacts, services, and organizational forms) that make inclusion possible despite unreliable institutions; over time, these infrastructures create coordination anchors that enable multiple actors to trans-late, normalize, and align dispersed efforts into emerging collective capacity. Enclaving shifts how we theorize wicked problems by treating collective action not as a prerequisite for meaningful engagement but as a downstream accomplishment that can emerge from sustained individual initiatives.

Speaking Louder Under Pressure: Negative Performance Feedback and Strategic Communication on CSR

SIM
William C Zhou (Sacred Heart University)
How does a firm respond to its corporate social responsibility (CSR) performance shortfall? Although CSR performance has been recognized as a critical component of overall assessment, we are not clear of the impact of negative CSR performance discrepancy on the subsequent CSR-related decision making. In this paper, by integrating impression management literature into the behavior theory of the firm (BTOF) framework, we argue that when experiencing negative CSR performance feedback, a firm will use impression management tactics and strengthen its strategic communication on CSR. We also find that the language comprehensibility and concreteness of communication weaken the relationship between negative CSR performance feedback and strengthening CSR communication in the following. Both theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

The Impact OF Human–AI Collaboration on Pro-Job Unethical Behavior: A Moderated Mediation Model

SIM
SUN meiquan (Zhejiang University); Shenjiang Mo (Zhejiang University)
Human-AI collaboration has been adopted for promoting work efficiency in the workplace. However, little is known about how and when different modes of human-AI collaboration result in pro-job unethical behavior. Drawing on social cognitive theory, we conduct two experiments to explore the underlying mechanisms that transmit the effects of two human-AI collaboration modes on employees’ pro-job unethical behavior. Results showed that human-AI augmented collaboration is negatively related to pro-job unethical behavior through sense of agency, and human-AI arrested collaboration is positively related to pro-job unethical behavior through the same mechanism. Furthermore, time pressure strengthens the indirect relationship between human-AI augmented collaboration and pro-job unethical behavior, while weakening that between human-AI arrested collaboration and pro-job unethical behavior. Overall, this research contributes to research on human-AI collaboration and time pressure, and also has practical implications for managing unethical consequence in human-AI collaboration.
