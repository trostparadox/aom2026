Holding Life Together: Women’s Entrepreneurship as Everyday Resistance During War

ENT
Sanita Rugina (Maastricht University); Trang Dinh; Kim Klyver (University of Southern Denmark)
Research on wartime entrepreneurship predominantly emphasizes structural conditions and overarching narratives, often overlooking the lived, everyday realities of agency and resistance. This gap obscures our understanding of how entrepreneurs navigate the profound disruptions of conflict. We address this using an inductive, qualitative study of Ukrainian women entrepreneurs in wartime Ukraine. We develop a framework of entrepreneurship as everyday resistance, identifying how entrepreneurs use present-oriented acts to guard everyday life and future-oriented acts to rebuild life and normalcy. We find that variations in sociopsychological resources (social support and agency) enable entrepreneurs to expand their present-oriented acts with future-oriented acts, thereby extending adaptive resistance with transformative resistance. This study contributes to entrepreneurship as world-making literature by theorizing how entrepreneurship operates as an everyday, spatiotemporal process of resistance and by offering a novel spectrum of resistance, showing how everyday acts create alternative structures of life amid chaos.

Liminal Anchoring and Identity Work of Foreign Microentrepreneurs in China

ENT
Jon K. Newton (NYU Shanghai); Daniel W. Lund (Independent University, Bangladesh); Ying Guo (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Jaideep Prabhu (University of Cambridge)
This study examines the identity work of foreign microentrepreneurs in China who live and operate their businesses within the ambiguities of liminality. The study seeks to understand how the microentrepreneurs navigate and leverage their identities within liminality. This qualitative research utilizes an interview study of 15 microentrepreneurs from western countries who have been living and operating their businesses in China for more than five years. Through open, axial, and selective data coding, themes emerge that are interpreted and reported through an interactionist approach. The study finds that rather than transitioning through the typical identity-transitioning stages of separation, liminality, and aggregation, the foreign microentrepreneurs socio-personally realign, detach from their home country cultures and ties, and anchor within the ‘neither here nor there’ phase of liminality. By anchoring within liminality, the foreign microentrepreneurs identify with the ambiguities of liminality from which they derive their entrepreneurial motivations, ideas, and drive. The study informs microentrepreneurship and identity work research, practitioners working with foreign microentrepreneurs, and microentrepreneurs working in China.

Striking Out On My Own: How Freelancers Manage Isolated Work Environments

ENT
Stella Seyb (Texas Christian University)
Entrepreneurship can be a lonely journey. Previous research suggests that work-related isolation can be best managed through reduction-based strategies. However, for a growing proportion of the workforce substantially reducing isolation isn’t feasible because their work is inherently isolating. Indeed, the nature of work has changed such that more individuals are engaged as freelancers, going from gig to gig. Given they cannot derive the benefits others obtain from working under a strong organizational culture or within a founding team, freelancers can struggle to manage the challenges of their inherently isolated working environments. We inductively examine how freelancers manage isolation through a qualitative study of 51 freelancers in the US, UK, and Australia. We find freelancers can leverage isolation by taking an anti-growth stance, adopting an anti-hustle mindset, and saying “no” more often. Additionally, we identify mechanisms through which freelancers can increase their connections to others. The emergent theory explains how taking this push-pull approach to managing isolation helps freelancers learn about their craft, business, and themselves. By building new theory in the freelancing context, we advance understanding of how a growing group of entrepreneurs can mitigate a central downside of recent transformations in work and shape the isolated aspects of their entrepreneurial action in ways that benefit them.

When Work No Longer Works: Repairing Everyday Work and Life via Entrepreneurial Practice

ENT
Jane Yan Jiang (Nanjing University)
Across highly urbanized societies, growing numbers of young professionals report disengagement, burnout, and a perceived absence of meaning in everyday work. While prior research has examined entrepreneurial motivation in terms of opportunity pursuit, autonomy, identity, or lifestyle preferences, less is known about how entrepreneurship becomes a response to perceived erosion in the experiential conditions of work and life. Drawing on an inductive multiple-case qualitative study of young, highly educated craft entrepreneurs who relocated from metropolitan careers to Jingdezhen, China, this study examines how experiential concerns associated with standardized urban work are translated into entrepreneurial practice. Based on in-depth interviews, participant observation, social media materials, and archival data, we identify a restoration-oriented motivational pattern through which entrepreneurs engage in entrepreneurship to repair and recraft dimensions of everyday life experienced as absent or eroded, including embodied creative engagement, workable temporal rhythms, and relational depth. We develop a process model showing how this restoration-oriented motivation reorganizes entrepreneurial activity across five interrelated domains: offering selection, temporal structuring, resource mobilization, relational work, and place evaluation. Rather than treating motivation as a static antecedent or entrepreneurship as a linear growth-oriented process, our findings demonstrate how entrepreneurial practice functions as an ongoing means of reconstructing livable forms of work and life. This study contributes to entrepreneurship research by theorizing repairing-and-recrafting as a distinct motivational orientation, refining understanding of heterogeneity within lifestyle-oriented entrepreneurship, and conceptualizing place as an experiential infrastructure continuously evaluated through everyday practice. The findings also offer practical insights for policymakers and organizations seeking to support sustainable, place-based entrepreneurship beyond growth-centric models.

Who Gains from Meaningful Work? A COR Perspective on Meaningful Work, Work–Life Balance & Well-Being

ENT
Andrea Zorn (Auckland University of Technology); Jarrod Haar (Massey University); Theodore E Zorn (Massey University)
There is recognition that meaningful work is an important workplace factor, and similarly so work-life balance. These represent the ethical importance of work that is both meaningful and in balance with work and non-work roles. The present study explores meaningful work as an antecedent and work-life balance as a mediator towards happiness and job depression. Importantly, we fill a major gap in the meaningful work literature by comparing relationships across three occupational categories: (a) employees, (b) managers, and (c) entrepreneurs. We use a large dataset of over 1500 New Zealand participants and find that meaningful work is beneficial to all factors, with work-life balance playing a consistent partial mediating effect. However, there are differences across the positions especially for entrepreneurs. Entrepreneurs report lower work-life balance when meaningful work is low but equally high when it is high. Interestingly, entrepreneurs are happier than employees and managers when work has limited meaning but similar happiness when it is meaningful. Entrepreneurs also report higher job depression when meaningful work is low, but the lowest when high. Finally, moderated mediation shows effects only for entrepreneurs, with them reporting significant indirect effects from meaningful work to happiness (positive) and job depression (negative). We discuss the implications for ethical work contexts and the interesting effects found for entrepreneurs.
