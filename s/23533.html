Capitalizing on Vulnerability in Digital Platforms: Actor Orchestration and Polyphony Management

TIM
Matheus dos Graciani dos Santos; Leonardo Gomes; Andre Ferri Conejo; Samara De Carvalho Pedro (ESPM); Lucas Emmanuel Nascimento Silva (University of São Paulo)
The actions of digital platforms to orchestrate their complementors have become a critical subject. While existing research acknowledges non-traditional work and supplier relationships as a defining feature of platforms, it has overlooked how orchestrators strategically design these arrangements to capitalize on complementor vulnerability. A critical and underexplored side of this discussion is how digital platforms manage the polyphony resulting from their actions - the dissonant voices and discourses that arise because of platform work and lead to societal scrutiny. In this research we investigated how digital platforms manage complementor vulnerability and with what consequences. Drawing on an in-depth single case study of a transactional platform in the food delivery sector in Brazil, we identify specific processes through which digital platforms deliberately amplify physical, organizational, cognitive, and financial vulnerability which improve their performance but result in increased polyphony, which they then regulate through performative actions aimed at reducing vulnerability perceptions. This study contributes to research on the dark side of platform orchestration and polyphony management by developing a novel theory that frames orchestration not only as the materialization of a value proposition but also as the paradoxical management of non-employee and supplier contracts to capitalize on vulnerability.

Pathways To Collapse: A Process Theory Of Open Innovation Platform Failure

TIM
Basudeo Dubey (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Vikrant Sihag (Eindhoven University of Technology); Saurav Snehvrat
Open innovation (OI) platforms promise to revolutionize value creation, yet their failure rate remains stubbornly high. While prior research has identified various factors contributing to failure, it has largely offered static, fragmented explanations. We lack a coherent process theory that explains how platforms fail over time. To address this, we conduct a qualitative meta-analysis of 42 peer-reviewed studies, aggregating data from 45 distinct cases of platform failure. Our analysis reveals four distinct process archetypes of failure: The Architect (failure by flawed design), The Gatekeeper (failure by internal resistance), The Merchant (failure by betrayal of trust), and The Orchestrator (failure by complexity choke). We integrate these archetypes into a novel theoretical framework that conceptualizes failure as a breakdown in either a platform’s internal Architectural Integrity or its external Ecosystem Cohesion. By moving beyond static factor lists to map the dynamic trajectories of collapse, we contribute a more sophisticated, multi-level, and process-based theory of platform mortality.

Platform Flexibility and Complement Quality

TIM
Michail Batikas (Nova School of Business and Economics); Tim Meyer (University of St. Gallen); Jörg Claussen
Platforms can react more flexibly to high demand than traditional organizations because increasing demand on platforms attracts new complementors, which can match this demand by providing additional supply. In this paper, we study the potentially negative long-term consequences of such platform flexibility. Drawing on Airbnb as our context, we find that additional complementors entering in high-demand periods provide less attractive complements and exert lower effort. Importantly, lower-quality complements entering in high-demand periods appear to remain active beyond these high-demand periods. This can be harmful for the platform in the longer run, as it downgrades overall platform quality and drives away customers who transact with such complementors (even after demand has faded). Thus, short-term benefits from platform flexibility come at the expense of long-term downsides from lower complementor quality.

The End of Platform Power? AI Agents Composability, and the Future of Digital Markets

TIM
Maximilian Stallkamp (East Carolina University); Joseph Simpson (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Much of the research on digital platforms assumes that intermediaries orchestrate multi-sided markets to optimize ecosystem-wide effectiveness, efficiency, and profitability. However, the development of personal algorithmic agents (PAAs) fundamentally alters the calculus of this configuration by introducing user-aligned intermediaries that can search, negotiate, and transact across platforms in autonomous fashion, thereby posing an explicit challenge to existing modes of platform ownership and governance. PAAs, in short, represent user preferences, constraints, and goals, not those of platform owners and complementors. Management and organizational scholars have only begun to fathom the implications of these changes for market coordination, particularly those involving six dimensions that will reshape platforms: discovery, data, API access, complementor substitution, trust, and regulation. In functioning across these key dimensions, PAAs use composability – the capacity to integrate heterogeneous digital inputs into coherent actions – in attempting to reconfigure intermediation once controlled by platforms. Drawing on transaction cost economics and ecosystem governance, we identify and explicate the mechanisms through which composability redistributes power between platforms, complementors, and agents, thereby yielding three contingent equilibria: agent-centric disintermediation, hybrid co-optation, and platform entrenchment. Our novel approach situates user-aligned intermediation as the soon-to-be dominant organizing logic of digital platforms. By specifying the manner in which composability unbundles discovery and infrastructure, we reveal the conditions under which autonomous pursuit replaces centralized orchestration, thereby upending platform power dynamics in a manner that impacts existing management theory and practice.

When to Enforce? The Impact of IP Enforcement Timing on Original Products Performance

TIM
Guoqiao Liu (Zhejiang University); Yao Fu (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
Imitation is pervasive in platform markets, yet little is known about when innovators should enforce intellectual property (IP) rights and how the timing of enforcement shapes performance outcomes. We argue that IP enforcement timing is strategy-relevant because it determines how long copycats remain active and thus governs the accumulation of both discovery and substitution effects. Drawing on a temporal perspective, we theorize that early copycat exposure can enhance original product performance through discovery effect, whereas prolonged exposure intensifies substitution effect, yielding an inverted U-shaped relationship between enforcement timing and performance of original products. We further propose that this relationship is moderated by market positioning difference between copycats and originals and by copycat visibility. We test our hypotheses using detailed product-level data from Alibaba’s Original Design Protection Platform from 2018 to 2023, which enables fine-grained matching between originals, verified copycats, and enforcement actions. Our results support the predicted inverted U-shaped relationship and show that positioning difference flattens the curve, while copycat visibility does not significantly steepen it. This study contributes to IP strategy, platform strategy, and imitation research by highlighting timing as a critical but overlooked strategic dimension of IP enforcement in platform-based markets.
