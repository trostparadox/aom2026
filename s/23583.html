Beyond Multihoming: Platform Recombination and Complementor Competitive Advantage

TIM
Tommy Pan Fang (Rice University); David R. Clough (The University of British Columbia)
Digital platforms often provide resources that enable complementors to develop diverse offerings for platform users. This phenomenon presents a fundamental puzzle for the resource-based view: how can complementors sustain competitive advantage when the resources they integrate, such as public APIs and standardized platform data, are transparent and accessible to rivals? We introduce the concept of generative recombination, whereby a complementor integrates resources from multiple, non-substitute platforms concurrently. We develop a typology of inter-platform strategies that distinguishes platform recombination from established strategies such as multihoming and platform bundling. Drawing on the distinction between characteristic and linkage ambiguity, we theorize that platform recombination generates sustainable competitive advantage by constructing linkage ambiguity: while the components remain transparent, complementors shift the locus of inimitability from the possession of scarce resources to the orchestration of their connections. We develop a set of propositions that specify how architectural complexity, semi-modular interfaces, and data synthesis create this ambiguity, and how orchestration capabilities and user-base overlap condition these effects. Our framework reveals that sustainable competitive advantage in platform ecosystems can not only emerge from accessing scarce resources, but by connecting multiple platforms and combining their functionality into inimitable configurations.

Faster Than Peers? Optimal Relative Iteration Speed and Digital Product Performance

TIM
Jiamin Dong (Central South University); Shihao Mao (Central South University); Yingzhu Fu (Central South University)
In highly dynamic and competitive digital platforms, a large number of digital products continuously iterate to improve their performance. However, existing research still shows significant divergence regarding the relationship between iteration speed and product performance. To reconcile this contradiction, this study distinguishes between two iteration modes and examines relative iteration speed within each mode. Drawing on optimal distinctiveness theory, we propose that digital products need to manage their iteration speed relative to peers to balance legitimacy and competitive advantage. Using application data from the iOS platform, our empirical analysis shows that relative main iteration speed exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with performance, while relative minor iteration speed has a positive relationship with performance. Furthermore, launch product distinctiveness moderates both relationships by attenuating the effects of the two iteration modes on performance. This study not only extends optimal distinctiveness theory from the spatial dimension to the temporal dimension but also provides practical guidance for iteration strategy in digital products.

From Social Mediators to Digital Enablers: Intermediary Brokerage in Rural Platform Adoption

TIM
Tianqi Yu (University of Sussex)
This paper examines how human intermediaries facilitate the adoption of digital platform services among marginalised beneficiaries in rural areas. Drawing upon brokerage literature, this study analyses 49 interviews conducted with 13 Digital Platform-Powered Social Enterprises (DPSEs) in the agricultural sector. Our findings reveal three types of brokerage practices, characterised by different intermediary profiles, stages of involvement, and relational positions within the local context. The analysis identifies key enabling factors, such as trust requirements, digital infrastructure needs, and local resource utilisation, that drive intermediaries’ operational effectiveness. This paper extends digital platform literature by demonstrating how each type of brokerage employs distinct processes and achieves effective platform implementation outcomes.

Platform-Within-Platform: Data Network Effects and the Catch-up Strategy

TIM
Huiyi Litan (Renmin University of China); Di Zhou (Tongji University); Ke Rong (Tsinghua University)
Digital platforms increasingly compete not just on installed user bases but on data-driven service quality. This shift has given rise to a ``Platform-within-Platform'' (PwP) structure, where an entrant platform operates through a dominant intermediary to access traffic while retaining proprietary data for algorithmic learning. We develop a two-period game-theoretic model to examine how external data network effects---learning from volume generated on the incumbent's platform---reshape competitive strategies. We identify a fundamental trade-off: the entrant must balance sacrificing short-term margins (via commission fees and introductory pricing) against the long-term strategic value of data accumulation. Our analysis yields several interesting results. First, stronger external data network effects not only increase the entrant's incentive to join but also induce a ``price-to-learn'' strategy, where the entrant aggressively discounts to fuel a ``data escalator.'' Second, internal data capability acts as a two-edged sword: while it typically supports premium pricing, in high-learning regimes, it amplifies the incentive to cut prices to leverage learning efficiency. Finally, the PwP structure enables a data-weak entrant to leverage the incumbent's traffic to close the initial quality gap and potentially surpass the incumbent in service quality, provided the external learning effect exceeds a critical threshold. These findings contribute to the platform envelopment literature by formalizing how data flows through nested intermediation to alter competitive hierarchies, and reveal a private-social misalignment where the strategic pursuit of data learning may erode consumer surplus despite quality gains.
