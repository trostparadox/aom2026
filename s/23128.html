Refugee Workers’ Responses to Dehumanization Through Self-Enhancement and Self-Protection

MOC
Patricia Tabarani; Constantin Lagios (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Teresa Cardador; Simon Lloyd D. Restubog (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Jasmin Diab
This study examines how refugees in the Global South respond to their widespread dehumanization at work and the consequences of these response strategies for work-related outcomes. We draw on the self-enhancement and self-protection framework to argue that refugees engage in two distinct yet interconnected responses: volunteering in refugee-support organizations to restore self-concept and agency, and avoidance of host nationals to shield against further dehumanization. We propose that participation in the shadow job market—characterized by informal and unregulated work—intensifies both responses by heightening refugees’ exposure to exploitation and precarity. Using a mixed-method design that combines three-wave survey data and qualitative interviews with refugees residing in Lebanese camps, our findings reveal that volunteering enhances refugees’ well-being and social entrepreneurial intentions, while avoidance undermines their sense of inclusion and belonging. This research advances theory on workplace dehumanization and underscores the importance of culturally grounded interventions that recognize refugees’ agency and mobilize community-based resources to promote dignity and inclusion in precarious work environments.

You’ve Got a Friend in Me: Managing Identity and Image in Parasocial Encounters

MOC
Ben Fagan (University of Cincinnati); Rebecca Arwine (University of Cincinnati); Elaine Hollensbe (University of Cincinnati)
In this study, we explore how individuals in highly visible, public-facing roles iteratively manage their identity and image under conditions of constant visibility, particularly in response to parasocial encounters (one-sided relationships between a publicly visible professional and an audience). Drawing on interviews with professionals across four highly visible occupations, we used an inductive qualitative approach, informed by grounded theory, to uncover how they manage their identity and image under conditions of constant visibility. The findings revealed an interdependent process through which professionals navigated the tensions between who they are and how they believe they are seen, balancing authenticity with public expectation. Professionals described cycles of identity and image protection that helped them maintain alignment between a coherent sense of self and a credible public image when parasocial encounters strained that balance. These cycles were shaped by how individuals interpreted parasocial encounters—as affirming, intrusive, or both—and by the subsequent identity and image management strategies they used. Our emergent theoretical model representing these cycles integrates parasocial theory with management research, showing that parasocial dynamics influence not only audiences but also the professionals subject to them. Additionally, this study extends identity work research by highlighting visibility as both a condition of and a challenge to a coherent sense of self in modern professional life.

Selling Out? How Platform Workers Enact Meaningful Work Under Audience Evaluation

MOC
Dan O'Sullivan (UCL School of Management)
Meaningful work often stems from pursuing passion and self-actualization. Yet modern work increasingly unfolds under conditions of external audience evaluation, raising questions about how workers pursue meaningful work under pressure to satisfy audiences. Existing meaning research largely conceptualizes others as providers or withholders of support and affirmation, overlooking the role of evaluation. I conducted a qualitative study of individuals offering creative services on online labour platforms, where tensions between self-actualization and audience demands are especially pronounced. I found that these tensions confronted workers with difficult choices between nurturing work meaning and securing instrumental success. They responded with either capitulatory routines – adapting their self-presentation and tasks to please audiences – or protective routines – distancing themselves from audiences to preserve meaningfulness. Surprisingly, capitulatory routines were associated with drastic changes in work meanings – from self-expression, to problem-solving, to system optimization. More starkly, workers described these changes positively, continuing to experience meaningfulness. Challenging assumptions that the pursuit of meaning is intentional and agentic, these findings highlight a process of incidental meaning enactment as workers capitulate to audience demands. They also shed light on how external audiences complicate meaningful work, and how platform control may reshape work meanings.

A Clown Car of One’s Own: How Marginalized Creatives Cultivate Space for Creative Work

MOC
Martina Pizzinato (UCL School of Management)
Creative work is often deeply personal, serving as a powerful medium for self-expression. Yet the environments in which creatives work can be hostile to such expression, particularly for those creatives with marginalized identities. Understanding how marginalized creatives experience this tension is essential to advancing our understanding of creative work as a social process—one shaped not only by collaboration and relationships but also by identity and inequality. Drawing on an inductive, qualitative study of women in the stand-up comedy industry, I identify the marginalized creativity tension—a tension women face between the marginalized creative threats embedded in their creative environments and their own marginalized creative values. In response to this tension, women engage in generative reclamation, a process of embracing and amplifying marginalized aspects of themselves to construct an alternative system of creative values, work, and community. Through this process, women come to view their gender not as a limitation but as a generative force that enriches and transforms their creative work. This study contributes to creativity and creative work literature by showing how marginalized creatives’ identities and experiences of marginalization not only shape how they engage in creative work but also how they define its meaning and purpose.

Humanizing Self, Relationships, and Career: How Loss Affects the Work Domain in the Long Term

MOC
Lidiia Pletneva (The London School of Economics & Political Science)
Through an inductive study of employees who experienced a significant personal loss due to death, this paper examines how such personal events affect the work domain in the long term. Drawing on 53 in-depth interviews and supplementary archival data, I develop a conceptual model that shifts the focus from the predominant in the literature short-term costs of personal grief for organizations to the potential long-term benefits of having employees with grieving experience. Specifically, the model shows that personal loss can humanize professional self, relationships at work, and career as people make sense of the loss, the feelings it made them experience, and the reaction to sharing the loss with colleagues. Consequently, the long-term impact of personal loss results in reallocating work-life balance resources, becoming a more self-compassionate and compassionate employee, engaging in job crafting behavior, and altering career path. Such behavioral changes create a new, resilient to further changes, status quo that addresses the needs that existed prior to the loss. This paper advances theorizing on grief in organizations, the work-life interface, posttraumatic growth, and humanizing organizations.

Revisiting Superstitious Learning: Epistemic and Pragmatic Pressures in Dysfunctional Learning (WITHDRAWN)

MOC
Xena Welch Guerra (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
The concept of superstitious learning describes how experience-based learning under ambiguity can give rise to invalid beliefs and ineffective practices. Despite increasing interest in ambiguity and its implications for learning processes, the question of when ambiguity gives rise to superstitious learning remains poorly understood. Advancing our understanding of these dynamics, this paper develops a contingency model that explains how epistemic and pragmatic pressures shape learning under ambiguity. We argue that these pressures increase actors’ propensity to impose ambiguity closure, particularly when ambiguity is perceived to have a low likelihood of being resolved through further experience. By specifying the conditions under which ambiguity gives rise to superstitious learning, our model advances research on organizational learning and adaptation and contributes to broader debates about how actors form and sustain causal understandings under conditions of indeterminacy.

Blurred Lines: Distinguishing Between Identity, Image, and the Self-Concept Towards a Process Theory

MOC
Tamika Money
Research across fields has long suggested a close relationship between identity and image yet the conceptual boundary between the constructs—and their relationship to the self-concept—remains blurred. In particular, identity is often used interchangeably with self-concept, and existing models primarily theorize identity-image dynamics in moments of disruption or instability. To address this, I advance a Self-Conceptualization Process Theory (SCPT) call upon disparate literatures to refine current definitions for identity, image, and the self-concept, which theorizes identity and image as interrelated components nested within an overarching self-concept. SCPT further theorizes three key mechanisms—establish, enact, and evaluate—to explain how internal self-understanding is structured, externally expressed and socially evaluated. By shifting attention from episodic change to routine self-concept maintenance, SCPT clarifies boundaries among identity, image, and the self-concept while preserving their interdependence. This framework further contributes to identity and image research by introducing a process model that is longitudinal, recursive, and empirically observable, thereby enabling future research to directly examine how identity and image are established, enacted, and evaluated in action across levels and contexts.

Before The Improvisation Begins: An Inductive Study of Work Under Persistent Uncertainty

MOC
Carisa Lam; Anthony Klotz (UCL School of Management); Colin M. Fisher (University College London)
In many forms of contemporary work, individuals must “make their own luck,” remaining engaged with uncertainty long before it is clear whether action will be possible or worthwhile. Research on organizational improvisation explains how actors respond once situations call for action, but offers less insight into how individuals stay engaged with uncertainty before improvisation can begin. Drawing on an inductive qualitative study of 48 professional wildlife photographers, an extreme case of work defined by limited control and fleeting moments, we examine how consequential moments come to be realized. We show that opportunities do not pre-exist as identifiable objects but emerge through action as individuals sustain engagement with unfolding situations. Photographers organize engagement through selective control, strategic positioning, and an improvisational stance marked by embodied attention and generative waiting. We theorize this process as structured serendipity, extending theories of improvisation by shifting attention from how people act once opportunities are clear to how they stay engaged with uncertainty until action becomes possible
