Unfinished Care and Burnout in Nursing Homes: The Moderating Role of Managerial Support

HCM
Yannai DeJonghe (Ghent University); Jeroen Trybou (Ghent University); Els Clays (Ghent University); Herlinde Wynendaele
Aim: Nursing homes face increasing workforce pressures due to population ageing, rising resident acuity, and persistent staffing shortages, heightening workloads and time pressure for care workers. These conditions increase unfinished care and may elevate burnout risk, yet evidence in nursing home settings and across professions remains limited. Methods: A cross-sectional, multicenter survey was conducted in 56 Belgian nursing homes (May– December 2021). Data from 1,054 care workers (registered nurses, nursing assistants, and allied health professionals) were analyzed. Unfinished care, burnout dimensions, managerial support, and sociodemographic variables were assessed. Two-level linear mixed models accounted for clustering and tested moderation effects in line with the Job Demands–Resources framework. Results: All domains of unfinished care were positively associated with emotional exhaustion and depersonalization and negatively associated with personal accomplishment. Favorable managerial support attenuated associations with depersonalization, whereas unfavorable support amplified these effects. Conclusion: Unfinished care is consistently associated with higher burnout risk among nursing home care workers. Managerial support acts as a key buffering resource, underscoring the importance of supportive leadership and balanced workload distribution in nursing homes. Key words: Nursing Homes; Unfinished Care; Burnout, Professional; Nurses; Nursing Assistants; Allied Health Personnel; Quality of Health Care; Cross-Sectional Studies

A Time-Lagged Study of Ethical Climate, Burnout, Incivility, and Irritation in Healthcare Management

HCM
GOVIND GOPI VERMA (Amrita School of Business); Aditya Simha (University of Wisconsin-Whitewater); Balakrishnan Unni (Amrita School of Business); Amrita Raj Kumar (Amrita School of Business); Jayanthan Raghupath (Amrita School of Business)
Escalating burnout among healthcare professionals threatens care quality, patient safety, and workforce stability. Hospital leaders frequently invest in improving ethical climate under the assumption that it directly lowers burnout. However, little is known about the mechanisms through which ethical climate influences burnout, resulting in interventions that may overlook critical drivers of emotional strain. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we propose and test a time-lagged sequential mediation model that explains a previously overlooked pathway linking ethical climate to burnout through workplace incivility and employee irritation. Using two-wave survey data from 229 hospital employees, we find that ethical climate does not directly reduce burnout; instead, its effect operates indirectly by reducing incivility, which subsequently lowers irritation and ultimately decreases burnout. These findings highlight relational and emotional mechanisms that healthcare administrators can target to more effectively prevent burnout by strengthening interpersonal norms and supporting irritation-management resources. Together, this study offers evidence-based guidance for building healthier and more sustainable healthcare work environments.

When Fear Fuels Purpose: Risk, Support, and Burnout Among Healthcare Workers in COVID-19

HCM
Steven William Day (North Carolina Central University); Shingirai Christopher Kwaramba; Quinton Nottingham (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Mathew Loos
The COVID-19 pandemic exposed a critical tension in safety-critical organizations: those tasked with saving lives often faced the highest personal risk. This study explores how healthcare professionals’ perceptions of risk shaped their emotional and attitudinal outcomes during the crisis. Grounded in the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) framework and the tripartite model of risk perception, we examine whether deliberative, affective, and experiential dimensions of perceived risk function as job stressors influencing job satisfaction, emotional exhaustion, and depersonalization. Drawing on survey data from 731 frontline healthcare workers across the United States, structural equation modeling was employed to test the proposed relationships and moderating effects of perceived adequacy of personal protective equipment (PPE). Results partially supported the tripartite framework, revealing a more parsimonious bi-dimensional structure wherein affective and deliberative risk perceptions exerted the greatest influence on emotional outcomes. Notably, affective risk was associated with both higher job satisfaction and increased emotional exhaustion—suggesting that fear can simultaneously inspire purpose and deplete psychological resources. Perceived PPE adequacy buffered these negative effects, emphasizing the symbolic and practical power of organizational support in high-risk environments. The study extends the JD-R model by incorporating risk perception as a central job demand and identifying perceived safety as a critical organizational resource. By reframing safety as both a technical and emotional construct, this research highlights how managing risk perception is essential to sustaining workforce resilience and organizational continuity in times of crisis.

Crafting Social Resources Under Intensified Job Demands: Evidence from Nurses

HCM
Guiyu Cai (HEC Montréal); Sari Mansour (TÉLUQ university); Denis Chenevert
Understanding how nurses manage intensified job demands during times of crisis offers valuable insights into how organizations can better support nurses and promote their overall well being. Our present study addresses nurses’ relational job crafting behaviors (i.e., social expansion) as a tool to foster positive psychological states when faced with intensified job demands during the COVID crisis. Using a role-resource view of expansion oriented relational crafting, we propose that on the basis of the level of job autonomy, nurses respond to work intensification via crafting social expansion that further enhance their psychological well-being. We tested our hypotheses using a two-wave sample of 344 registered nurses to examine how intensified demands are related to within-person changes in job crafting, well-being, and turnover intention. Findings largely supported our hypotheses, suggesting that under the condition of high job autonomy, intensified demands at Time 1 predicts significant increase in crafting social resources six months later, these changes are also positively related to increases in well-being and decreases in turnover intention.

Effects of Flexible Work Arrangements on Health and Work-Family Conflict among Hospital Nurses

HCM
Theresa Maurer (University of Hamburg); Eva Maria Wild (University of Siegen); Vera Winter (University of Wuppertal)
Nurses’ working conditions often pose a threat to their health and an impediment to work-life balance, exacerbating nursing staff shortage problems and threatening quality patient care. One possible organizational strategy to increase the attractiveness of the job is the use of flexible work arrangements, which give nurses more leeway in determining their working hours, hence potentially decreasing work-family conflicts and being beneficial to their health. As robust evidence on the effects of flexible working time models on work- and health-related variables of nurses is still lacking, the aim of this study is to investigate the effects of an innovative flexible working intervention on occupational health and work-family conflict of nurses in a large German university hospital in the period from 2022 to 2025. The intervention includes a bundle of different flexible working arrangements such as 4-hour short shifts, extended or long day or night shifts, and telework. The study follows the design of a prospective cluster-controlled intervention study with an open cohort stepped wedge design and continuous recruitment. 131 units of the university hospital are assigned to clusters and introduced to the intervention in stages. The data basis consists of primary and secondary data: (1) survey data of employees at four points in time, (2) personnel statistics (e.g. data on staff turnover, absenteeism), and (3) utilization statistics. Panel data are available on the unit level. Analyses are performed at cluster level (unit) as within-cluster and between-cluster analyses. Statistical analyses are performed using (Generalized) Linear Mixed Models ((G)LMM) and Two-way Fixed Effects Models. Three waves of survey data are used in this study. The average response rate is around 16%. The survey sample is representative of the study population. Preliminary results indicate a positive significant association between the use of flexible working time models and health-related outcomes (p < 0.1). However, preliminary findings further indicate an increase in work-family conflict associated with the use of flexible working time models (p < 0.1). Tentative evidence suggests that this effect is driven by individuals without children under 18. Our study adds to the existing literature on flexible work arrangements, work-related variables, and health outcomes of nurses. Our results provide evidence-based support for hospital management to decide whether similar interventions should be implemented.
