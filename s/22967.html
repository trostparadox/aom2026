Saying One Thing, Signaling Another: Decoupled Categorization Under Multi-Audience Evaluation

OMT
Anni Chen (Southeast University)
How do producers strategically organize multiple category signals when different audiences apply partially incompatible evaluative standards? Integrating research on market categorization with institutional complexity, we develop a category decoupling perspective in which producers manage cross-audience tension by differentiating the salience of multiple categories rather than simply spanning or repositioning categories. We argue that higher state involvement in a product amplifies the tension between ideological legitimacy and market appeal, increasing the likelihood that firms select a low state-associated category as the dominant label while retaining higher state-associated categories as peripheral signals. We further theorize that this tendency is weaker for higher-status producers but stronger under greater policy uncertainty. The arguments are tested and supported in the Chinese film industry (2012–2019) by combining film-genre classifications with industry policy texts. Overall, the study advances category theory by theorizing category decoupling as a distinct category strategy and by clarifying how institutional complexity becomes observable in categorical choices and how powerful evaluators can reshape the meaning and valuation of categories.

Recoupling and Relapse: Peer Transitions as Field-Level Cues for Decoupling Exit

OMT
Jaekyung Lee (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology); Yeonjae Lee; Yunsung Lee (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology); Sangchan Park (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology)
Institutional research has long portrayed decoupling as persistent, sustained by organizations’ pursuit of legitimacy through symbolic adoption while buffering their technical core. Yet we know less about when organizations exit decoupling to engage in substantive implementation, especially when internal efficiency logics do not clearly mandate change. We develop a field-level account of decoupling exit by highlighting that organizations revise assessments of feasibility and legitimacy based on peers’ transitions into substantive implementation (peer recoupling) and back into decoupling (peer relapse). Using Korea’s environmental technology certification program as an empirical setting, we find that peer recoupling increases a focal organization’s likelihood of exiting decoupling, whereas peer relapse attenuates the positive effect of peer recoupling. This study contributes to the literature on institutional decoupling by theorizing peer transitions as field-level social-validation cues that explain when and why organizations abandon decoupling for substantive implementation.

Devouring Symbols: Institutionalization from Below

OMT
Maria Fernanda Rios Cavalcanti (FGV EAESP); Yuan Li (Saint Mary's College of California)
Although symbols are central to institutionalization, existing perspectives assume mutual intelligibility among actors and understate how power asymmetries shape encounters with imposed symbols. As a result, they leave unexplained how marginalized actors engage authoritative symbols when recognition fails and sanction risks are high. In such settings, symbols are binding yet not fully intelligible, forcing actors to improvise how they can be enacted and stabilized in practice while remaining outwardly compliant. Drawing on Deleuze’s account of sense emerging under constraint, we develop symbolic anthropophagy as the mechanism through which marginalized actors generate new meanings in these encounters and embed them in seemingly compliant practices. Our model specifies three interconnected mechanisms - selective capture, repetition-with-difference, and double-coded crystallization - through which imposed foreign symbols are locally reworked while preserving institutional legibility. Using cases from prior research on Brazilian workers, we illustrate this process and distinguish it from decoupling, bricolage, and semantic fit. We show how meanings created from below stabilize into institutionalized practices that are incidental, cumulative, and often opaque rather than negotiated or co-constructed.

The Uncomfortable Truth of Conformity: A Systematic Review and Unified Model of Decoupling

OMT
Ankita Mishra (Management Development Institute Gurgaon); Madhushree Nanda Agarwal (Management Development Institute Gurgaon); Arun Kumar Tripathy (Management Development Institute Gurgaon)
Global businesses increasingly commit to social and environmental responsibilities, yet often fail to achieve intended societal outcomes. Institutional theory explains this puzzle through two distinct concepts: Policy–Practice Decoupling (PPD), where organizations symbolically adopt policies without implementing them (a failure of implementation), and Means–Ends Decoupling (MED), where organizations substantively implement practices that fail to achieve their goals (a failure of impact). Despite these conceptual connections, research on decoupling remains fragmented. Crucially, no review synthesizes PPD and MED as a continuous path-dependent trajectory, obscuring why institutionally prescribed programs so often fail their core purpose. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic literature review of 81 studies using an Antecedents- Decisions-Outcomes (ADO) framework. We propose a Unified Process Model of Institutional Failure that identifies three causal mechanisms, i.e., the Forced Compliance Loop, Enforcement Paradox, and Cycle of Endemic Reform, which drive organizations from an initial state of evasion (the "Legitimacy Façade") into a state of ineffective compliance (the "Technocratic Trap"). Ultimately, both trajectories culminate in a shared societal failure: what we term the ‘Ethical Value Gap’. We conclude that without addressing these structural barriers, forcing organizations to "walk the talk" simply shifts them from idle evasion to active, costly waste.
