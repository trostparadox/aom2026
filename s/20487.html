Session overview

Workforce reintegration after incarceration represents a persistent challenge at the intersection of inequality, work, and organizational practice. Justice-impacted women in the United States face durable barriers to employment and advancement, including stigma attached to criminal records, constrained access to professional networks, housing instability, and organizational practices that implicitly exclude non-normative career histories. These barriers are not solely individual obstacles but are reproduced through institutional and organizational arrangements that shape who is seen as employable, legitimate, and adaptable at work. This panel symposium reframes workforce reintegration as a process of social repair, integrating Social Issues in Management perspectives on stigma, dignity, and institutional responsibility with Organizational Behavior research on identity reconstruction, emotional regulation, resilience, and adaptation to change, and Human Resources scholarship on workforce development, mentoring, and equitable employment systems. Rather than positioning resilience as an individual trait, the panel advances a multilevel view in which adaptive capacity is cultivated through stabilizing infrastructure, mentoring relationships, and human-centered program design that supports sustained workforce participation. Anchored by curated excerpts from the documentary The Church of Ms. Burton: The Story of the SAFE Housing Network, the session integrates theory, practitioner insight, and lived experience to examine how organizations and ecosystems can either reproduce or mitigate structural harm during stigmatized work transitions. Panelists explore implications for management theory, workforce program design, and HR practices related to hiring, onboarding, supervision, and retention of justice-impacted workers, offering actionable insights for scholars and practitioners across SIM, OB, and HR.
