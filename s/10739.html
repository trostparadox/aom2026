Submission Number: 10739 Survey Design and Data-Integrity Issues in Primary Sample Data Collection: Detecting Technology-Based Deviant Workplace Behaviors Division: Research Methods (RM) | Submission Type: Poster Abstract Online self-report surveys are increasingly used to study sensitive workplace phenomena, yet they are vulnerable to insufficient effort responding, satisficing, and fraudulent participation—threats that can undermine measurement quality and substantive inference. These risks are particularly salient in primary data collection, where researchers design, field, and manage their own surveys rather than relying on third-party respondent panels or proprietary screening tools. This poster documents a replicable survey-design and data-integrity workflow implemented during primary sample data collection for research on technology-based deviant workplace behaviors (TbDWB). Drawing on best practices from the IER literature, the workflow integrates layered front-end controls (e.g., CAPTCHA, multiple attention/authentication checks with termination logic, randomization, and pilot-informed completion-time thresholds) with transparent post-collection screening procedures conducted using standard statistical software. Across recruitment efforts, 1,747 responses were initially collected, yielding 1,228 valid cases after integrity controls were applied; the cleaned dataset supported a split-sample design (Sample 1 n=643; Sample 2 n=585) used for measurement development and validation. Substantive results are reported elsewhere; the contribution here is methodological: a practical, auditable blueprint for managing data-integrity risks in primary online surveys addressing sensitive behaviors. Extended Abstract Research using self-report surveys to measure deviant workplace behaviors (DWB) faces persistent data-quality challenges, particularly when data are collected online and address sensitive or stigmatized behaviors. Inattentive responding, satisficing, and fraudulent participation can undermine reliability, validity, and substantive inference. Although expanding research methods literature provides guidance for identifying insufficient effort responding (IER) and careless responses, less attention has been devoted to how these recommendations can be systematically operationalized in primary data collection, where researchers design, field, and manage their own surveys rather than relying exclusively on third-party respondent panels or vendors. This extended abstract documents a replicable, end-to-end survey-design and data-integrity workflow implemented during primary sample data collection for studies examining technology-based deviant workplace behaviors (TbDWB). The purpose is methodological rather than substantive: to provide practical, traceable guidance for researchers collecting their own online survey data on sensitive workplace phenomena. The workflow integrates front-end survey design decisions, embedded authentication and attention checks, and post-collection screening procedures to enhance data integrity while preserving respondent anonymity and transparency. Methodological Context and Motivation Classic DWB research relied heavily on in-person or supervised data collection, which imposed natural constraints on inattentive or fraudulent responding (Robinson & Bennett, 1995; Bennett & Robinson, 2000). Contemporary research increasingly examines digitally mediated forms of deviance— such as misuse of organizational systems or inappropriate electronic communication—using online surveys. While online administration expands reach and feasibility, it also introduces heightened methodological risks, as anonymous and remotely accessed surveys may increase opportunities for satisficing, misrepresentation of eligibility, or automated response behavior. These risks are particularly salient in primary researcher-managed data collection, where investigators retain control over survey design, screening logic, and incentive administration. In contrast to third-party panel providers—who may apply proprietary or opaque data-quality controls—primary data collection places responsibility for data-integrity governance squarely on the researcher. Transparent documentation of integrity safeguards is therefore essential for reproducibility, reviewer evaluation, and cumulative methods learning. Primary Data Collection Design Data were collected using a self-administered online survey hosted in Qualtrics. Recruitment relied on primary sampling strategies rather than paid third-party respondent panels, including professional networking platforms, social media groups, academic networks, and snowball sampling. This approach provided flexibility and control over survey implementation while requiring deliberate attention to data-quality risks. To preserve respondent anonymity while incentivizing participation, a two-survey design was used. Survey 1 collected all substantive responses. Survey 2, administered separately, collected contact information solely for incentive drawings. No identifying information was linked to substantive survey responses. Front-End Integrity Controls (Prevention and Termination) Multiple preventive and termination-based controls were embedded directly into the Qualtrics survey to reduce low-quality responses at the point of data entry: • CAPTCHA verification was implemented at survey entry to reduce automated access. • Multiple attention/authentication checks were distributed throughout the survey, drawing on best practices for detecting inattentive responding. • Termination logic was applied such that respondents failing two or more of three attention checks were automatically terminated and excluded from further participation. • Randomization of items and blocks was used to reduce patterned responding and limit the effectiveness of scripted or automated completion. • Completion-time monitoring was informed by pilot testing (average completion ˜ 17 minutes), with completions under a conservative minimum threshold (7 minutes) flagged as likely low-quality. Together, these controls reflect a layered design principle: early prevention and termination reduce downstream contamination and limit reliance on post hoc data screening alone. Post-Collection Screening and Data Cleaning Following data export, additional screening procedures were implemented in SPSS to ensure consistency and auditability: • Removal of auto-terminated cases and respondents failing attention checks. • Exclusion of extremely fast completions below the minimum-time threshold. • Identification of straight-line responding using zero-variance (SD = 0) checks across multi-item scales. • Duplicate-case review using built-in SPSS tools. Embedded metadata fields (e.g., browser, operating system, language) were reviewed descriptively to flag anomalies; however, no cases were excluded solely on the basis of metadata in the absence of other indicators. Sample Yield and Replication Strategy Across recruitment efforts, 1,747 responses were initially collected. After applying the integrity controls and screening procedures described above, the final analytic dataset consisted of 1,228 valid cases, illustrating the substantial impact of layered data-integrity safeguards in primary data collection. To support replication and reduce overfitting in scale development and validation, the cleaned dataset was randomly split into two independent samples (Sample 1 n = 643; Sample 2 n = 585), consistent with established measurement development guidelines. Illustrative Measurement Outcomes The screened dataset supported reliable measurement of TbDWB dimensions, with internal consistency estimates consistent with expectations for multi-item behavioral scales. These outcomes are presented illustratively to demonstrate the downstream implications of data-integrity procedures rather than as primary substantive findings. Full theoretical models and hypothesis tests based on these data are reported elsewhere. Contributions to Research Methods Practice This extended abstract contributes to research methods scholarship by: • Demonstrating how recommendations from the IER and careless responding literature can be operationalized concretely in primary online data collection. • Highlighting the distinct design and governance responsibilities faced by researchers who collect their own samples, rather than outsourcing data-quality controls to third-party vendors. • Providing a transparent, auditable workflow that can be adapted to other sensitive research domains, including ethics, misconduct, and cybersecurity behaviors. • Emphasizing the importance of documenting decision rules and attrition to support reviewer evaluation and reproducibility. Future Considerations An important direction for future methods research is the systematic comparison of data quality obtained through primary researcher-managed sampling versus paid third-party respondent panels, particularly for sensitive workplace constructs. Comparative evidence on these tradeoffs would help researchers balance cost, control, transparency, and validity risk when designing online studies. In the interim, researchers conducting primary data collection can strengthen credibility by implementing layered controls, pre-specifying screening rules, and reporting attrition transparently.
