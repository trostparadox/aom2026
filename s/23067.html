Health Research and Organizational Adaptation to Policy Change: Evidence from Hospitals

HCM
Anand Bhatia (IE University); Antonio Garcia Romero; Josep A. Tribo (Stevens Institute of Technology); Anand Goel (Stevens Institute of Technology)
Organizations differ in their ability to adapt to policy-induced changes in incentives. We examine how strategic investments in health research and health information technology shape organizational adaptation to policy change. We use the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP) as an empirical setting, applying it to a panel of 2,328 U.S. public hospitals from 2011 to 2019. We find that hospitals with greater research activity experience lower performance penalties following policy implementation. We have quantified that a 1-standard-deviation improvement in hospitals’ research output can generate aggregate savings of roughly $3.7 billion per year. Moreover, digital infrastructure amplifies the performance benefits of research, whereas digital investments alone do not improve outcomes without research capability. Additional analyses show that research-active hospitals exhibit smoother performance trajectories after penalties, consistent with greater capacity to adjust operations over time. These findings contribute to research on organizational adaptation by showing how investments in knowledge generation and information infrastructure jointly enable effective responses to policy change.

Health IT Diffusion and Physician Density

HCM
Jianjing Lin (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Lucy Xiaolu Wang; Jason Huh (Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute)
This paper examines how the diffusion of advanced health information technology (HIT) affects the density of hospital-based (HB) physicians. Leveraging sharp county-level increases in HIT adoption driven by federal incentives, we compare physician density per 100k population in counties with rapid diffusion (treatment group) to those with slower or no uptake during our sample period (control group). Using an event-study framework, we find that HIT diffusion led to a 11.1\% increase in the HB physician rate in treated counties relative to control counties, and medical and surgical specialties account for most of the increase. Moreover, the growth in HB physician density is evident, absent major consolidation activities, and contributes to overall growth in total physicians. This growth is concentrated among early-career physicians and in physician shortage areas. Mechanism tests suggest that physicians benefit financially from practicing in treated counties, with higher Medicare reimbursement and hospital profits. Various robustness checks support our results. Our findings suggest that strategic HIT investments can attract physicians and reduce geographic disparities in health care access.

Stocking Under the Influence: Spillovers from Commercial Drug Coverage to Medicare Utilization

HCM
Josh Feng; Luca Maini (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill); Emma Dean (Dartmouth College)
We document a novel channel through which commercial drug coverage affects Medicare Part B utilization. Exploiting plausibly exogenous differences in state-level commercial coverage of physician-administered drugs, we show that a 10pp increase in exclusion rates among commercial formularies leads to a 1.7pp reduction in market share among Part B beneficiaries. Prescribing behavior of physicians operating across multiple facilities suggests that facilities drive variation in product utilization, likely through the preferred stocking of products with better commercial coverage. Our results suggest that commercial market inefficiencies can affect government spending and support the idea that facilities can directly influence the quality of care.

Beyond Capital: How Funding Sources Drive Innovation Performance in Digital Healthcare Ventures

HCM
Ali Mchiri (Sam Houston State University); Gregory N. Orewa (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Kristin Burton (Purdue University Northwest); Thomas Nguyen
Research on digital healthcare innovation often focuses on addressing the need for delivering high-end user solutions. However, little is known about how different types of funding sources influence the innovative performance of digital healthcare ventures, as measured by patent activities. Our research addresses a critical gap in understanding how external funding dependencies shape innovation outcomes in healthcare ventures, guiding hospital leaders in evaluating potential technology partnerships. Using a comprehensive dataset, PitchBook, we analyzed 409 digital healthcare ventures through negative binomial regression to examine relationships between five funding source categories (venture capital, corporate-backed funding, angel investments, private equity, and accelerator/incubator funding) and patent outputs. Our findings indicate that healthcare ventures backed by venture capital showed the strongest association with innovation output. Contrary to expectations, accelerator/incubator-backed firms demonstrated the lowest patent output while angel-backed firms, traditionally considered innovation catalysts, showed only marginal significance. Drawing on the Resource Dependence Theory (RDT), our study provides the first comprehensive examination of multiple funding sources in digital healthcare ventures. Our study offers actionable insights for healthcare leaders navigating technology sourcing decisions and advances understanding of funding-innovation relationships in capital-intensive, highly regulated industries.

Boundary Spanning in Entrepreneurial Founding Teams: Navigating Dual Complexities

HCM
Ahmed Zahlan (University College London); Ouafaa Hmaddi (The City College of New York of the City University of New York); Ravi Prakash Ranjan (Mohammed VI Polytechnic University); David Hayes
This paper examines how nascent AI healthcare ventures navigate the dual complexities of advanced technology and highly regulated healthcare systems. While interdisciplinary founding teams are recognized as essential, prior research has not detailed how such teams coordinate under the combined pressures of high epistemic distance (e.g., divergent vocabularies and error tolerances) and institutional density (e.g., privacy, ethics, and regulatory constraints). Drawing on boundary-spanning theory, we conduct a qualitative multi-case study of 53 founders from U.S.-based AI healthcare startups. Our analysis inductively identifies three interdependent mechanisms of boundary spanning: epistemic translation, which aligns clinical requirements with implementable AI models and evidence standards; institutional negotiation, which interprets and sequences compliance, regulatory pathways, and legitimacy-building with external stakeholders; and resource orchestration, which mobilizes talent, data, partnerships, and capital to enable the other mechanisms at the right time. We find that heterogeneity in founding teams (clinical, technical, and managerial expertise) constitutes an enabling condition, but venture advancement depends critically on the repeated enactment and strategic sequencing of these mechanisms. The study contributes to entrepreneurship and innovation research by developing a mechanism-based process model of boundary spanning in AI healthcare startups and by demonstrating how founders convert epistemic and institutional frictions into coordinated action. It further informs practice by highlighting the importance of sequencing legitimacy-building activities prior to major growth milestones.
