The Diversity Paradox: How Pursuing Observable Diversity in Teams Can Undermine Cognitive Diversity

OMT
Megan Elisa Gabruk (University of Michigan); Maxim Sytch (University of Michigan); Pedro Aceves (Johns Hopkins University); Scott Page (University of Michigan)
This study identifies and examines the diversity paradox, wherein efforts to create observably diverse teams may paradoxically lead to greater cognitive homogeneity. Individuals often form diverse teams using easily observable, surface-level markers like race or gender as selection criteria. However, research shows that people frequently feel apprehensive about working with colleagues who appear dissimilar. Our theory proposes that individuals manage this discomfort through compensatory behavior: selecting visibly diverse teammates who are, at the same time, more cognitively similar. As a result, teams that appear demographically diverse may actually exhibit less cognitive diversity than possible, or even less than comparable demographically homogeneous teams. To test this idea, we analyzed data from over 4 million collaborative teams, comprising 3 million unique patent inventors on U.S. patents issued between 1977 and 2019. We use gender as a salient indicator of surface-level diversity and employ machine learning techniques to assess collaborators’ knowledge diversity based on the nature of their prior inventions. We find evidence for the diversity paradox. This study provides insights for research and practice on team composition, collaborative practices, and organizational diversity interventions.

Attending to the Hierarchy: Job Seeker Responses to Diversity Signals and the Perpetuation of Labor

OMT
Yuval Spiegler
Existing research shows that job seekers interpret organizational diversity as a signal of potential bias, but focuses on broad cues (e.g., executive photos, aggregate representation) available at the application stage. Yet candidates can encounter more granular information – such as through contacts or site visits – about their potential supervisor, peers, and subordinate demographics. I term these Hierarchical Demographic Configurations and ask how this information will inform candidate choices. A conjoint experiment with Black and White respondents reveals that preferences are structure- and identity-contingent: Black participants favored racially similar managers and peers, while White participants preferred mixed peer groups and exhibited gendered preferences for leadership diversity. Both groups resisted managing racially homogeneous subordinate teams that differed from their own identity. I then ask whether such preferences can help explain persistent interorganizational differences in diversity. Embedding these empirically calibrated preferences in an agent-based labor market simulation reveals that even modest, individual-level preferences generate enduring organizational segregation, even absent bias in hiring. The findings suggest that candidates respond not only to aggregate diversity, but to its hierarchical structure, and that structural inequalities in organizational composition may endure not only because of biased gatekeeping, but also because job seekers interpret and attend to the demographic legacies of past exclusion when making employment decisions.

Shared Identities, Unequal Returns: The Paradox of Homophily for Dominant and Underrepresented Group

OMT
Yiftach Yarmar (INSEAD)
Scholars have identified a "paradox of connection" in professional services, where clients select providers based on social similarity rather than human capital, often yielding suboptimal economic returns. This study investigates whether this trade-off operates differently across dominant and underrepresented social groups. Using a comprehensive dataset of 1.2 million Florida real estate listings, I examine how seller–agent racial homophily shapes both partner selection and transaction performance. I argue that for underrepresented sellers, shared ascribed status serves as a critical signal of trustworthiness and safety, leading them to substitute agent human capital for homophily during selection to mitigate perceived discrimination. However, during the transaction stage, the mechanism shifts. While shared identity fosters prosocial motivation and discretionary effort, these benefits only materialize when coupled with professional expertise. Consequently, homophily transforms from a substitute to a complement to human capital regarding performance outcomes. Results confirm that underrepresented sellers are more likely to select same-race agents with lower experience, resulting in lower average sales probabilities. Yet, when these sellers match with high-experience in-group agents, the performance gap narrows significantly, as expertise enables agents to effectively channel identity-based motivation. This research advances theories of inequality by demonstrating that homophily’s value is status-contingent: it functions as a relational mechanism that can reproduce structural disadvantage through network segregation or, when combined with high human capital, help overcome market frictions.

From Wallets to Wages: Consumer Income, Job Design, and Pay Disparities

OMT
Soohyun Roh (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
Pay differences between organizations are a key source of wage inequality. I propose a novel perspective on these differences by starting from the consumers that these businesses serve. Firms that serve high-income consumers specialize jobs into higher-paying and higher-skilled positions focused on quality, while those that serve lower-income consumers emphasize cost minimization by requiring workers to perform a wider range of general tasks. Matching consumer foot traffic data and establishment-level wage records, I find that establishments serving higher-income consumers pay their workers more. This effect holds comparing among establishments in the same neighborhoods and industries. Longitudinally, establishments increase wages when they shift toward higher-income customers. Analysis of online job postings further reveals that jobs at higher-income-serving firms involve a narrower set of tasks that command higher market value. These findings show how consumer markets shape firms' internal job design and contribute to pay inequality across organizations.

Who Gets Connected? Network Drivers of Racial Inequality in Tie Formation

OMT
Sanaz Mobasseri (UCL School of Management); Mabel Abraham (Columbia Business School); Elizabeth Linos
Intraorganizational networks are critical for careers, but Black employees derive fewer benefits from these networks than their White counterparts, primarily due to a lack of connections to White coworkers. Drawing on organizational theories of networks and inequality, we argue that shared contacts—employees who facilitate ties between mutual contacts—produce racial disparities in tie formation when facilitating connections that enable access to career-advancing tasks. Such high-stakes work can amplify shared contacts’ tendencies to make race-based inferences either about an employee’s competence or about others’ racial preferences and biases, making them more likely to facilitate connections for their White than Black coworkers. Using a rich longitudinal network dataset, including personnel and staffing records of 2,418 new hires in an elite professional services firm, we estimate the causal effect of shared contacts on subsequent tie formation. We find that shared contacts facilitate connections at a rate that is 72% lower for Black than for White new hires, although no such gap exists in the absence of a shared contact. This paper uncovers a novel triadic network mechanism that serves as the primary driver of racial disparities in intraorganizational networks, thereby limiting Black employees’ access to career-advancing work.
