Unanchored But Called: Freelancers’ Identity Challenges and Career Outcomes

CAR
Yuhee Jung (Waseda University); Norihiko Takeuchi (Waseda University)
Freelancers often face identity challenges—difficulty sustaining a coherent work identity without stable organizational roles—particularly in Japan, where occupational identity has traditionally been anchored in stable, firm-based employment. Yet little is known about how freelancers experiencing identity challenges achieve occupational identity and subsequent career outcomes. Drawing on career construction theory and conservation of resources theory, we propose that identity challenges undermine occupational identity achievement, thereby reducing subjective career success and perceived career growth, with calling functioning as a protective resource. Using three-wave longitudinal data from Japanese freelancers (n = 1,177), we find that identity challenges negatively predict occupational identity achievement, which in turn predicts lower career outcomes; this indirect effect emerges only among freelancers with low levels of calling. To complement these findings, we conducted a supplementary qualitative study (n = 208). Thematic analysis revealed four recurring challenges—viability concerns, organizational burden, relational isolation, and career-path ambiguity—arising from the nature of freelance work. Importantly, we also identified a Japan-specific challenge: social stigma toward freelancers. Together, the findings from Study 1 and Study 2 highlight that identity challenges—rooted in independent work and cultural legitimacy threats—undermine freelancers’ occupational identity achievement and career outcomes but are buffered by calling.

Embracing the In-Between: The Liminal Career of a Field Manager in Development Sector

CAR
Vipin Kumar Chathayam (Goa Institute of Management); Saikat Chakraborty (Indian Institute of Management Calcutta)
This paper explores the concept of a liminal career through the narrative of an Intergovernmental Organisation (IGO) Field Manager in rural India. Employing a bricolage methodology and a co-writing approach, we combine narrative vignettes with reflexive analysis to examine the manager’s lived experiences. Illuminating how a liminal career navigates between liminal spaces and drawing on the notion of lay morality, we highlight the significance of lay moral reasoning in decision-making and identity construction within these liminal spaces, contributing to a different understanding of the enactment of liminal careers amid complex and ambiguous institutional pressures. We argue for reconceptualizing liminal careers as fluid journeys characterized not only by the ongoing negotiation between conflicting demands and identities but also by the development of liminal competencies achieved through the integration of moral evaluations and responses shaping such career paths. This perspective calls for new approaches to career research, especially adopting innovative research methodologies of writing differently to capture the complexities of liminal careers. In so doing, this paper attempts to explore the relational, collective, and moral dimensions of liminal careers, contributing to the growing movement in career studies that recognises the need for more nuanced, context-sensitive approaches to understanding and supporting career development.

How Dual Identity Positioning Shapes Occupational Survival in Project Based Career Systems

CAR
Yuze Sui (Stanford University); Tianyu Du (Stanford University); Daniel A. McFarland (Stanford University)
In competitive project-based industries, career survival depends on dual identity positioning. Workers must position themselves in ways intelligible to external audiences by selecting into certain product genres and to internal collaborators by performing certain production roles. We develop this perspective by studying individual careers within the U.S. film industry. We ask how genre and role positioning jointly shape occupational survival, and how the returns to dual identity positioning depend on the performance histories of workers. Using six decades of film data concerning directors’, screenwriters’, producers’, and actors’ careers, we model the timing of their re-employment. We show that specialization in genres and roles improves the likelihood of securing sustained work, but in occupation-specific ways, and sometimes only in combination. We further show that the returns of external and internal identity positioning are moderated by distinct reputational concerns: the value of genre specialization is mostly altered by the individual’s reputation for making quality films, while the value of role specialization is mostly altered by the individual’s reputation for their filmmaking productivity. These results demonstrate that project-based careers are governed by two intertwined identity systems whose distinct reputational concerns—of quality and quantity— selectively relax or intensify the constraints of dual identity positioning.

Self and Structure: The Dual Dynamics of Career Inclusivity

CAR
Hang Tran (University of Danang); Hong T. M. Bui (VinUniversity)
Dominant career theories implicitly assume that effort, adaptability, and belonging can be converted into security, mobility, or stable inclusion. Yet in many labor markets characterized by chronic institutional weakness, this convertibility is structurally foreclosed. Drawing on 104 in-depth interviews across diverse occupational roles, this study examines how careers remain viable when advancement, formal membership, and protection cannot be reliably achieved. We show that occupational inclusion under such conditions is not a function of agency or structure alone, but of their ongoing compensation. We theorize career inclusivity, as a system-level relational condition through which individuals remain meaningfully engaged, socially embedded, and occupationally viable despite blocked mobility. Career inclusivity emerges from the interaction between self-inclusivity - ethical self-regulation, psychological endurance, and identity coherence - and structural-inclusivity, which conditions whether effort can be translated into protection or advancement. We further identify compensatory resilience as a non-normative, costly mechanism through which individuals mobilize moral, cultural, and relational resources to preserve participation when institutional convertibility is absent. Four empirically grounded career pathways demonstrate how inclusion persists without progression, membership, or security.

Working Class Experiences as a Source of Career Capital?

CAR
Andrew Kozhevnikov (University of Leeds); Emily Yarrow (Newcastle University)
Working-class men continue to be among the most marginalised groups of workers in British society. Yet, in this article, we ask whether and how can male working-classness be a source of career capital during austerity in the UK? We explore career capital within the intelligent career framework in the spatio-temporal context of austerity in the UK caused by Brexit and the Covid-19 pandemic. Drawing on qualitative interviews with 43 self-identifying men from working-class backgrounds in the UK, with a range of career experiences, we explore the mobilisation of working-classness as a contemporary and valuable source of career capital. We argue that there are distinct working-class career benefits as experienced and perceived by working-class men, which can be positively deployed and activated to counter the deeply entrenched existing inequalities in the workplace.
