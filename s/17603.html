Is Sharma More Creative Than Yang? An Examination of Creativity Stereotypes Associated With Asians

Saturday, Aug 1 2026 · 8:30AM–9:30AM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 2
DEI

Authors: Xinlang Zhou Drexel University; Snehal Hora

Session overview

In the US, Asians are referring as the “model minority,” a stereotype that emphasizes diligence and competence but implicitly casts Asians as less creative. This bias not only undervalues Asians’ creative contributions but also treats Asians as a homogeneous group obscuring meaningful differences across Asian subgroups in how creativity is expressed and evaluated. For instance, Lu (2022) demonstrates South Asians (SAs) are perceived as more leader-like than East Asians (EAs) due to their perceived creativity. However, the psychological mechanisms underlying the evaluative difference in the creativity of SAs versus EAs remains unclear. We thus propose a theoretical framework linking subgroup differences in creativity evaluation to self-regulatory focus and the componential model of creativity. Drawing on regulatory focus theory, we argue that EAs are more likely to enact a prevention-focused orientation emphasizing persistence and carefulness, whereas SAs are more likely to enact a promotion-focused orientation emphasizing exploration and risk-taking. These orientations do not reflect differences in creative capacity but shape the specific components of creativity–novelty and usefulness–that evaluators assess to form an overall perception of creativity. Although creativity is defined by both novelty and usefulness, organizational contexts, especially in the Western world, disproportionately privilege novelty over usefulness. Consequently, promotion-focused behaviors that cue expression of novelty align more closely with creativity prototypes, while prevention-focused contributions are less likely to be recognized as creative. By disaggregating Asian subgroups and identifying regulatory focus as a key mechanism, this research clarifies how cultural stereotypes and evaluation biases jointly shape creativity judgments at work.
