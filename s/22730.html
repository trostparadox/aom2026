Leveraging AI–Based Performance Evaluation for Politically Unskilled Employees

OB
Fan Luo (Jinan University); Meilan Nong
Artificial intelligence (AI)–based performance evaluation is increasingly replacing human–led appraisal processes in organizations, yet who benefits, who is harmed, and whether this shift truly empowers employees remain unclear. Drawing on deontic justice theory, we argue that employee political skill is a critical individual characteristic that shapes differential responses to AI–based evaluation. Specifically, we theorize that politically unskilled employees, who are typically disadvantaged by subjective and socially driven evaluations, will perceive AI evaluations as more legitimately justifiable, thereby enhancing their perceptions of overall justice and motivating greater work efforts. In contrast, politically skilled employees, who are accustomed to leveraging social influence, will regard AI evaluations as lacking legitimate justification, which may in turn trigger psychological contract breach and foster social loafing. Across an experiment and a multi-source, multi-wave field study, we find support for a moderated mediation model in which political skill shapes employees’ deontic reactions to AI versus human evaluations. Our research aims to contribute to the emerging work of leveraging AI to foster fairness in human resource management.

The Impact of Organizational AI Adoption on Employee Adaptive Service Performance

OB
Xiaojiao Yang (Southwest Jiaotong University); Chunyong Tang (Southwest Jiaotong University); Yan Peng (Southwest Jiaotong University)
Given that organizational artificial intelligence adoption fundamentally transforms employees’ work methods and job roles in the workplace, few studies have explored whether employees can adapt to this dynamic work environment. This study employs paradox theory to develop and test a conceptual model that explores the effect of organizational AI adoption on frontline employees’ adaptive service performance. Through an experimental design and a multi-wave time-lagged survey, the findings demonstrated that the impact of organizational AI adoption on paradox mindset and consequent adaptive service performance depends upon leader AI symbolization. Specifically, when leader AI symbolization is high, organizational AI adoption promotes adaptive service performance among frontline employees through a paradox mindset. This study highlights the importance of leadership in analyzing how organizational AI adoption affects employee adaptive outcomes, and also advancing our appreciation of the complicated impacts of AI adoption on adaptive service performance within dynamic environments.

Mechanism of Influence of Dependence on Intelligent Machines on Job Performance

OB
Yiwen Shi (Beijing Institute of Technology); Xinhang Zhao (Beijing Institute of Technology); Pingqing Liu (Beijing Institute of Technology)
As artificial intelligence becomes deeply embedded in work settings, employees’ growing dependence on intelligent systems underscores the phenomenon of “dependence on intelligent machines.” Based on social interaction theory, this paper develops a “human-machine-interpersonal” dual-path framework, introducing self-esteem threat and workplace friendship as mediators and examining the moderating role of AI’s warmth perceived. It systematically reveals the impact mechanism of dependence on intelligent machines on employee performance. Using empirical analysis of three-stage longitudinal data from 594 matched leader-employee questionnaires, the study finds that, in the human-machine relationship pathway, dependence on intelligent machines directly or indirectly triggers or intensifies employees’ self-esteem threats during daily tasks, ultimately leading to decreased task performance. In the interpersonal relationship pathway, dependence on intelligent machines weakens workplace friendships, which negatively affects relationship performance. Meanwhile, AI’s warmth perceived plays a significant moderating role in both pathways. When AI systems demonstrate higher warmth, they effectively alleviate the negative effects of dependence on intelligent machines on employees’ self-esteem threats and workplace friendships. In summary, this study verifies how dependence on intelligent machines affects employee performance through dual “human-machine-interpersonal” pathways, offering theoretical and practical insights for organizations to optimize human-machine collaboration and balance technological dependence with employee well-being.

Not All AI Attitudes Are Equal: How and When AI-Threat Awareness Affects Job Performance

OB
Yiting Dong (London School of Economics and Political Science); Jiaqing (Kathy) Sun (The London School of Economics & Political Science); Yuanmei (Elly) Qu (Rowan University); Yunxia Shi
As artificial intelligence (AI) increasingly transforms work, employees develop three distinct attitudes toward these technologies. Employees may perceive AI as threatening their job security and relevance (AI-threat awareness), as beneficial for enhancing their capabilities (AI-opportunity awareness), or as simultaneously presenting both threats and opportunities (AI ambivalence). We focus on AI-threat awareness because it may prevent employees from realizing AI's potential benefits, representing a critical challenge for organizations. Across a pilot study, an online survey (N = 184), an experimental vignette study (N = 156), and a time-lagged, multisource field study (N = 127), we examine how AI-threat awareness influences job performance through emotional mechanisms and identify when these effects are most pronounced. Results demonstrate that AI-threat awareness increases employee anxiety and reduces gratitude, which in turn impairs job performance. Moreover, regulatory focus moderates these emotional pathways: prevention-focused employees experience amplified anxiety in response to AI-threat awareness, whereas promotion-focused employees are buffered against reduced gratitude. By measuring all three AI attitude types across studies, we isolate the unique effects of AI-threat awareness and identify regulatory focus as a critical moderator determining for whom these effects are strongest, offering actionable insights for organizations managing AI adoption.
