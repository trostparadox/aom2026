Architectural Knowledge Discrepancy, Innovation Performance Feedback, and CVC Investment

TIM
Xiafei Chen (University of Science and Technology of China); Yi Yang (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Lingling Qin (Guangdong University of Technology)
This paper investigates the impact of architectural knowledge discrepancy on corporate venture capital (CVC) investments. Building on research on CVC and architectural knowledge, we theorize two countervailing mechanisms: legitimacy pressures that increase CVC investment and competitive pressures that reduces it. Using 8202 firm-year observations from 544 U.S. firms between 1990 and 2020, we find that architectural knowledge discrepancy has a U-shaped relationship with CVC investments and that both positive and negative innovation performance feedback attenuate this curvilinear pattern. These findings identify architectural knowledge discrepancy as a novel antecedent of CVC activity and highlight aspiration-based innovation performance feedback as an important boundary condition shaping firms’ external venturing.

From Technology to Product Markets: Product Market Clarity and Startup Patent Sales

TIM
Yoon Jung Kwon (Jenny (Yoon Jung) Kwon); Colleen Cunningham (The University of Utah)
Inventing startups often transact their inventions in the market for technology (MFT), yet buyers face substantial uncertainty when evaluating early-stage technologies whose commercial applications have not yet been established. This study examines how product market clarity—the extent to which a patent’s potential commercial applicability is concentrated in a clearly identifiable product market—shapes startup patent sales. We argue that patents with greater product market clarity present buyers with a more interpretable commercial identity, reducing search and evaluation costs and facilitating transactions, and that the value of such clarity depends on both the information environment and competitive conditions faced by buyers. Using a dataset of 7,667 U.S. medical device startup patents and probabilistic linkages between patents and potential product markets, we employ discrete-time event history analyses to examine the timing of patent sales. We find that patents with greater product market clarity are more likely to be sold, that this relationship is significantly stronger following patent publication and when innovative activity is high across relevant product markets. Together, these findings highlight the role of commercial applicability, information disclosure, and buyer evaluation environments in shaping technology transactions involving early-stage inventions and contribute to research on innovation strategy and MFT transactions.

Noise or Signal? Professional Backgrounds and Prediction of Startup Potential

TIM
Nathan Rietzler (Stockholm School of Economics); Yuqi Zhang (Harvard Business School)
Accelerators have emerged as critical intermediaries in the entrepreneurial ecosystem, yet little is known about how the professional composition of their evaluation panels affects the predictive validity of startup selection decisions. Does adding other professional backgrounds to evaluation panels that are typically only composed of investors introduce noise that obscures venture quality, or does it provide valid signals that enhance prediction? Drawing on 91,423 evaluations of 19,152 early-stage startups submitted to a large global accelerator between 2016 and 2024, we examine whether professionally diverse panels predict venture success more accurately than homogeneous panels. Leveraging stratified random assignment of evaluators to startups, we find that diverse panels significantly improve funding and survival prediction for high-scoring ventures. Investors assign systematically lower recommendation scores than entrepreneurs and executives, reflecting more conservative screening thresholds. Analysis of written feedback shows that all evaluators rely on shared baseline standards but diverge in their emphasis on market opportunity, team strength, competitive positioning, and execution, leading diverse panels to include a broader and more complementary set of evaluative perspectives. We further show that feedback diversity is a key mechanism linking panel composition to predictive accuracy, as panels that draw on more varied evaluative dimensions produce recommendation scores that more strongly predict long-term outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that professional diversity is signal, not noise, capturing complementary screening heuristics that enhance predictive accuracy in high-uncertainty selection contexts.

Software Startups’ Patenting and Venture Capital Responses During the Early Years of AI

TIM
Shinjinee Chattopadhyay; Florence E M Honore (University of Wisconsin-Madison); Jinting Zhou (University of Wisconsin-Madison)
This paper investigates how investors value patents in the early diffusion stages of a new general-purpose technology (GPT), artificial intelligence. We propose that appropriability theory and signaling theory yield divergent predictions about investor preferences, creating a core tension in the evaluation of upstream versus downstream startups. Drawing on a sample of 3,265 U.S. IT software startups founded between 2005 and 2017, the study examines the "patent-funding link". To address potential endogeneity, we use a two-stage least squares (2SLS) identification strategy, instrumenting patent approval with quasi-random examiner leniency. The findings reveal that a first patent grant increases the likelihood of securing external funding by approximately 16% for upstream startups. This contradicts traditional appropriability logic, which suggests that value capture shifts downstream toward application holders, and is instead aligned with the signaling and market for technology theories which predict that investors would fund upstream startups on the basis of their tangible signals and contractable assets such as patents. The effect increases to 27% when the patent specifically involves computational claims (e.g., core algorithms and architectures). In contrast, the patent-funding link for downstream startups is statistically insignificant. When upstream startups are on the direct path between granted patent and funding, they obtain their next round of funding at a faster rate than downstream startups. However, we do not observe difference in the rate of a positive exit (M&A or IPO). These results suggest that in the unsettled early stages of a GPT, patents function less as barriers to entry and more as high-fidelity signals of technical expertise and generative appropriability. This study contributes to the literature on startup financing and GPTs by demonstrating that upstream foundational innovation remains a primary driver of venture-capital interest and strategic exit potential.

Who Guides Innovation Finance Under Uncertainty? Experts, Crowds, and Open-Access Token Fundraising

TIM
Maria Anna Halbinger (Baruch College, CUNY); Gianna Figà-Talamanca (University of Perugia); Romi Kher (Baruch College, CUNY)
Innovation projects increasingly rely on open-access token fundraising (e.g., Initial Coin Offerings) to finance development. These mechanisms broaden access to capital but raise duty-of-care questions in opaque, high-uncertainty markets. We examine the conditions under which two archetypal opinion-leader channels—epistemic authorities and social proof—help an innovation project’s token sale reach its hardcap, i.e., the publicly stated total funding needed to execute planned development and commercialization milestones. Using a global sample of 916 ICOs, we operationalize epistemic authority as expert ratings and social proof as crowd attention. Our findings suggest that both channels are positively associated with reaching the hardcap. However, when perceived fraud risk is high, only expert ratings remain associated with success, whereas crowd attention does not. This pattern suggests an authority–risk mechanism: when deception risk is salient, human expertise, not social proof, helps innovators clear this diffusion-relevant financing threshold. Our findings imply that crowd attention alone is not a sufficient basis for discharging a duty of care; instead, platforms and issuers should complement disclosure with independent expert screening to better protect investors while preserving access to capital. These findings inform governance of adjacent forms of open-access token fundraising.
