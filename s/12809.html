Session overview

This symposium advances a dynamic, integrative perspective on organizational structure by foregrounding the individual-level psychological and behavioral processes through which structure operates. We adopt two complementary approaches. First, we examine how structural features shape individuals’ emotions, perceptions, and actions. Second, we investigate how individuals’ abilities, orientations, and preferences feedback to maintain, contest, or destabilize structural arrangements. Bringing together five papers spanning work groups, organizations, labor markets, startups, and leadership hierarchies, the symposium highlights multiple micro-level mechanisms—including emotion regulation, structural preferences, selection dynamics, perceptual sensitivity to status, and social dominance orientation—through which structure and individuals are mutually constitutive. Collectively, these papers offer a process-oriented understanding of structure as both shaping and shaped by individual-level organizational behavior. Building on these two overarching approaches, the five papers deepen our understanding of how organizational structures and individual (re)actions are linked. Addressing the first approach—how structural features shape individual responses—Jinyoung Sohn and Siyu Yu et al.’s paper examines how hierarchical group structures influence members’ emotional regulation, showing that heightened surface acting undermines information contribution. Ronnie Lee and Reuben Hurst’s paper focuses on early-stage startups and demonstrates signaling egalitarian, autonomy-promoting structures in recruitment can backfire by generating unfavorable perceptions among targeted candidates. Mike Lee’s paper examines preferences for decentralization, showing how variation in structural demands and preference heterogeneity in individual preferences produces systematic selection effects across organizational forms. The second approach—how individuals influence structural stability and change—is addressed by two papers. Siyu Yu et al.’s paper shows that followers’ status acuity can reduce cooperation and support when leaders’ status is unstable, thereby disrupting leadership and status dynamics. Chadé Mikara Darby et al.’s paper demonstrates that individuals’ social dominance orientation contributes to the maintenance of unequal hierarchies by shaping opposition to marginalized candidates in leadership selection. Together, these papers illustrate how individual differences play a central role in reinforcing or challenging organizational structures.
