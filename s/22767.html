Do Empathic Founders Have Stronger People Orientation? Empathy, Employee Turnover & Org Design

OB
Catharina Janz (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf)
Empathy has become a widely recognized leadership quality, yet its organizational consequences in entrepreneurial contexts remain poorly understood. Drawing on Upper Echelons Theory, we argue that founders’ trait empathy – particularly when carried out authentically – reduces employee turnover (inward people orientation) and manifests customer focus in the form of relatively larger marketing teams (outward people orientation). Using a unique longitudinal dataset of 407 U.S. high-tech ventures founded in 2010, we combine unobtrusive, text-based measures of founders’ trait empathy and authenticity derived from approximately 2.4 million words posted on X (formerly Twitter) with machine-learning-based classifications of nearly 30,000 firm-month observations from LinkedIn and further data sources. Our findings show that empathic founding teams experience significantly lower employee turnover and build proportionally larger marketing team. Moreover, authenticity amplifies these relationships, underscoring that empathy’s impact is boosted by its congruence with founders’ genuine values. Our study moves the entrepreneurial empathy research beyond situational empathy processes towards venture-level outcomes of empathic venture leadership. We thereby extend Upper Echelons Theory and illustrate how digital trace data can reveal the behavioral foundations of employee turnover and organizational design in entrepreneurial ventures.

A Privacy Regulation Perspective on the Bounded Effects of Receiving Supervisor Empathy

OB
Xiaoxiao Jiang; Gillian Yeo; March To (RMIT University)
Empathy is regarded as a hallmark of effective leadership, presumed to strengthen supervisor-subordinate relationships by enabling supervisors to understand and resonate with subordinates’ inner states. This view, however, rests on the assumption that subordinates invariably welcome and benefit from receiving supervisor empathy. Challenging this assumption, we draw on privacy regulation theory to examine when and why receiving supervisor empathy may be experienced as intrusive and how subordinates respond to such experience. We employed a mixed-methods design comprising a quantitative survey study (Study 1) and a qualitative critical incident study (Study 2) to examine our hypotheses. Study 1 supports that receiving supervisor empathy can elicit perceptions of intrusiveness—particularly among subordinates with strong boundary control preferences—which in turn trigger privacy regulation behaviors, including increased avoidance of and reduced affiliative engagement with supervisors. Study 2 corroborates these findings and provides richer insights into how personal boundary control and related factors function as core moderators that shape the outcomes of receiving supervisor empathy. By qualifying prevailing assumptions about the uniformly positive effects of empathy in leadership, this research highlights the contingent and boundary-sensitive effects of receiving empathy and extends privacy regulation theory to hierarchical workplace relationships.

What Do You Need To Hear? (Mis)Alignment in Sympathy Expressions

OB
Daniel J. Chiacchia (University of Toronto)
Grief is a prevalent, consequential, and underexamined experience in organizational life. Despite the ubiquity of grief at work, people often do not know what to say when someone is grieving. Integrating the Interpersonal Emotion Regulation Literature with Basic Psychological Needs Theory, the current research reveals a paradox. When sympathizers communicate with those who are grieving, they systematically prioritize changing the griever’s feelings by positively reframing their experiences over validating them, even when doing so undermines the very goal they are trying to accomplish (i.e., alleviating distress). Prioritizing a positive reframe backfires because it is misaligned with what grievers need to hear – a message that makes them feel understood. However, positive reframing does not always backfire. When loss events thwart one’s need for esteem (e.g., job loss), positive reframing instead becomes the more effective strategy. Across an archival analysis of over 600 sympathy cards, surveys of over 1,000 people consoling someone in their grief (including over 150 medical professionals), and three experiments with over 1,500 active grievers, I find support for this nuanced paradox. Theoretically, this research expands Basic Psychological Needs Theory by taking need satisfaction out from within individuals and into the rich interplay of interpersonal communication between them. Practically, this research has important implications for sympathy communication at work, as well as the role that training may play in facilitating need-message alignment.

Organizational Emotional Citizenship: Conceptualization and Development of a New Construct

OB
Dahyun Lee (Sungkyunkwan University)
Contemporary organizations depend increasingly on dense interaction, collaboration, and continuous interpersonal coordination. As work becomes more interdependent and socially embedded, employees are required not only to perform tasks but also to sustain the emotional conditions under which those tasks can be accomplished. Yet existing organizational research has largely treated emotional stability, psychological safety, and relational quality as ambient states or climates rather than as outcomes of ongoing work. As a result, the everyday labor through which emotional order is monitored, contained, governed, and restored remains theoretically underdeveloped. In this paper, we introduce Organizational Emotional Citizenship (OEC) to capture the normatively expected yet institutionally unassigned work through which employees assume responsibility for maintaining the emotional functioning of their organizational community. We conceptualize OEC as a multidimensional construct encompassing emotional attunement, emotional supportiveness, affective norm maintenance, and emotional repair and recovery. By reframing emotional maintenance as a form of citizenship and distributed affective labor, this paper advances emotion research beyond experience and climate, extends citizenship theory beyond prosocial contribution, and makes visible a neglected layer of organizing: the informal, unevenly distributed work that sustains emotional order. We develop the theoretical foundations of OEC, differentiate it from related constructs, articulate its nomological network, and outline its multilevel dynamics. In doing so, we offer a new lens for understanding how organizations hold together emotionally and open new avenues for research on emotional responsibility, governance, and inequality at work.

Positive Vibes Only! The Development and Validation of the Toxic Positivity at the Workplace Scale

OB
Raghid Al Hajj (Gulf University for Science and Technology); John Edward Fiset (Saint Mary's University); Dev Bhave (Singapore Management University); Charlotte Genge
Toxic positivity, while widely discussed in both popular and professional circles, has received limited attention in organizational research, both theoretically and empirically. It refers to the pervasive and imposed promotion of positive emotions, attitudes, or opinions while simultaneously invalidating negative ones. Drawing on the social functional theory of emotions, we position toxic positivity as a distinct workplace climate factor that shapes dynamics at both interpersonal and organizational levels. We begin by conceptualizing and defining how toxic positivity is perceived within organizations. Through four independent studies involving 1,073 participants, we developed and validated the Toxic Positivity at the Workplace Scale (TPaWS), which demonstrated strong psychometric properties. This represents the first psychometrically reliable instrument systematically measuring toxic positivity in organizational contexts. Our findings demonstrate the TPaWS’s value in advancing research on this underexplored construct while also equipping organizations with a clearer understanding of its impact on employees’ well-being and performance.
