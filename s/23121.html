Order of Information and Debiasing Tools is Integral Concerning Neurodivergent Candidate Attributes

CM
Carman W. Fowler (Duke University); Richard Paul Larrick; Kimberly A Wade-Benzoni (Duke University)
Multiple factors can bias an employer’s perceptions of a candidate’s fit for a job. Charisma or likeability can exert an inordinate effect, leading managers to weigh feel over actual skill competency. This offers a unique challenge for neurodiverse candidates, whose presentation of atypical social ability may lead to the formation of negative perceptions when they are otherwise strong analytical and technical fits. Our results show that the effect of these perceptions were robust, holding through diagnosis disclosure and various interventions. However, our research has found efficacy in leveraging the order in which candidate information is presented. Overwhelmingly, candidates whose social ability was viewed second, following their skill competencies, were affected by a nearly 20-point reduction on overall ratings. Following these insights, we found debiasing tactics suggested by competency-based hiring models worked most effectively when conducted following candidate information, but before final overall ratings were completed. This research outlines that not only does the order of information matter, but it matters when debiasing tools are implemented in the hiring process.

Moral Exclusion & Workplace Dynamics: How Perceived Dissimilarity Fuels Conflict, Incivility, Gossip

CM
Anjali Bansal (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Kubilay Gok (Winona State University); C Lakshman (Mount Marty University)
Research has consistently underscored the importance of dissimilarity between supervisors and subordinates in shaping employee attitudes and counterproductive work behaviors. Grounded in the moral exclusion theory, this study delves into the psychological and social mechanisms by which perceived deep-level dissimilarity fosters conflict, incivility, and gossip in workplace settings. Based on data from 144 supervisor-subordinate dyads, with subordinate data collected in two waves separated by a two-week interval, the findings reveal a moderated mediation model, where supervisors’ perceptions of deep-level dissimilarity with subordinates significantly contributes to task and relationship conflict, which in turn, escalate supervisor incivility and employees’ negative gossip tendencies. These effects are moderated by subordinate performance. We provide valuable insights for practitioners, emphasizing the importance of addressing perceived fit between supervisors and subordinates to mitigate conflict, reduce incivility, and ultimately curtail counterproductive behaviors in the workplace.

Examining the Variety of Multiple Team Membership: Roles of Faultlines and New Member Contingencies

CM
Yun Chung (University of Idaho); Youngkyun Park (University of Idaho)
Multiple team membership (MTM) configurations have received considerable scholarly attention since O'Leary, Mortensen, and Woolley's (2011) foundational work. However, research has predominantly focused on the quantity of external memberships, with limited examination of team-level MTM variety—the aggregated diversity of knowledge and experiences team members acquire through their external team involvements. Team-level MTM variety presents theoretical tension: it can enhance information processing capabilities while simultaneously increasing coordination costs. To reconcile these competing perspectives, we examine intra-team subgroup configurations (i.e., faultlines) as moderators and incorporate a temporal perspective for investigating newcomer proportions. Using monthly longitudinal data from 201 mutual fund management teams spanning 918 fund managers and 11,531 observations (January 2000- December 2017), we find that MTM knowledge variety has a positive relationship with team performance, above and beyond MTM number. This positive relationship is weakened when demographic faultlines are strong but amplified when informational faultlines are strong. Additionally, newcomer proportion moderates the MTM variety-performance relationship temporally: the relationship becomes positive between the fourth to sixth months and subsequently diminishes, suggesting that integration processes require time to translate MTM variety into performance benefits.

Navigating Opportunities and Challenges: Insider Activist Negotiations for Diversity Initiatives

CM
Cynthia S. Wang (Northwestern University); Moqiu Cheng (Northwestern University); Jennifer Ann Whitson (University of California Los Angeles); Brayden G. King (Northwestern University); Alexander Keogh (Brigham Young University)
We introduce a phase-based theoretical framework of insider activism centered upon negotiation as an undertheorized mechanism of organizational change. We propose that fluctuating balances of opportunities and challenges in the sociopolitical environment produce three recurring phases of insider activism: endorsement, active struggle, and repression. Each phase shapes insider activists’ negotiation goals and strategies. By integrating macro-level movement dynamics with micro-level negotiation processes, our framework advances understanding of how insider activist efforts translate into organizational change.

New Venture Team Conflict: A Review and Agenda for Future Research

CM
Bret Bradley (University of Oklahoma); Jacob Whitney (Kennesaw State University); Emily Neubert (Texas Christian University); Lowell Busenitz (University of Oklahoma)
Conflict is one of the most important dynamics within teams and research on conflict in new venture teams (NVTs) has grown dramatically over the last 30 years. Yet despite the increased attention in the entrepreneurship literature, this research remains scattered and disorganized, and it is not clear how best to move this conversation forward. We thus undertook a scoping review to understand the framing, functioning, and findings of NVT conflict in order to highlight avenues of future research to move this conversation forward. Our review of 51 articles revealed that while task and relationship conflict have provided a strong foundation for understanding NVT conflict, researchers should address at least three critical avenues of future theorizing and empirical work: (1) broaden research on uniquely entrepreneurial conflict types and outcomes; (2) study the microfoundations of NVT conflict such as dyadic origins, team member roles, and changes over time; and (3) investigate contingencies that can clarify when conflict supports, not stifles, NVTs and their success. We call on entrepreneurial team researchers to make conflict more central in future theorizing and research.
