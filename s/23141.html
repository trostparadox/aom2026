Mobility Ladder or Trap? The Heterogeneous Impact of Educational Homophily on Promotion

MOC
Wenming Deng (Tsinghua University); Jiatan Chen (Tsinghua University); Meiling Huang (Tsinghua University)
Educational homophily is widely documented to facilitate labor market entry and initial placement. Yet, we know little about how this tendency to bond with similar others shapes career mobility in everyday workplace interactions beyond the recruitment phase. Drawing on a resource quality perspective, we propose that educational homophily becomes a mobility trap when coupled with non-elite educational backgrounds. Specifically, we challenge the content-neutral assumption of traditional network theory by arguing that the utility of an “echo chamber” depends on the quality of resources being echoed. We posit that for non-elite employees, homophily leads to a “double disadvantage” of structural redundancy and inferior resource quality, insulating them from advancement; conversely, for elite employees, homophily acts as a mobility mechanism by circulating high-quality strategic resources that buffer the negative effects of closure. We test our theoretical model using large-scale archival data from a leading Chinese educational services firm. By analyzing digital communication logs and personnel records of 26,920 employees, we find that educational homophily significantly reduces the likelihood of promotion for non-elite employees while increasing it for their elite counterparts. Our findings reveal that homophily is not a neutral social fact but a resource-dependent mechanism that actively reproduces inequality through voluntary association.

Does Threat Backfire? How Activist Board Placements Constrain Firm Strategic Change

MOC
Eugene See (Coastal Carolina University); Albert Minkyu Ahn (Hong Kong Baptist University)
This study examines how threat associated with activist board placements impacts firm strategic change. Drawing on threat-rigidity theory, we argue that increased activist threat constrains boards’ strategic responsiveness rather than catalyzing adaptation. Using a sample of activist board appointments, we find that strategic change declines monotonically as threat levels increase. We further show that the commitment of activist-appointed directors shapes strategic change only under moderate threat, while their prior activist board experience becomes consequential only under extreme threat. These findings highlight the behavioral limits of board responsiveness under escalating activist pressure and advance a threat-based perspective on shareholder activism.

Ours Over Time: Collective Psychological Ownership Trajectory and New Venture Team Performance

MOC
Yohanes Adeatma Antonio (Nanyang Business School); Marilyn A. Uy (Nanyang Technological University); Gabriel Henry Jacob (Nanyang Technological University); Tony Antonio; Liestya Padmawidjaja (Ciputra University)
While existing research has demonstrated a strong link between collective psychological ownership (CPO) and team performance (Gray, 2020), it has primarily focused on static viewpoints. This paper argues that a dynamic perspective also influences performance and examines how the CPO trajectory affects the performance of new venture teams. We apply psychological ownership theory and action theory to hypothesize a positive relationship between them, and we examine three team processes—team cohesion, potency, and information elaboration—to evaluate their effects on both initial CPO and its trajectory. The study involves 65 new venture teams from a university incubator in Indonesia, with each team member surveyed four times over four months. The results reveal positive relationships between team potency and initial CPO, and between CPO slope and team performance.

From Progress to Backlash: A Cyclical Process Theory of Organizational Diversity Initiatives

MOC
Adrienne Mishel Kafka (Duke University); Samuel Allen (University of Pittsburgh); Morela Hernandez (University of Michigan)
Management scholarship has largely examined the design and effectiveness of organizational diversity initiatives under the implicit assumption that such initiatives reflect a forward-moving trajectory of societal progress. We challenge this assumption by theorizing diversity initiatives as politically generative governance arrangements whose legitimacy is continuously renegotiated. Drawing on policy feedback theory from political science, which examines how policies can reshape their own future iterations, we develop theory on how organizational initiatives reshape the cyclical centering (and decentering) of marginalized workers over time. Our theory homes in on the sociopolitical and inter-organizational forces that can activate the contestation of resource redistributions and beliefs regarding meritocracy and power structures. Specifically, we propose that supporters of diversity initiatives mobilize to defend and expand centering efforts through self-reinforcing feedback, while self-undermining feedback is generated as opponents mobilize to dismantle their progress. Through narrative analysis of historical episodes, we conceptualize this cyclical process as recurring phases of expansion, contestation, and retrenchment whereby tipping points convert diffuse sociopolitical reactions into legitimacy signals for organizational recalibration. In doing so, we recast backlash not as design failure, but as a systematic and endogenous outcome of initiative success and driver of future expansion.

Artificial Intelligence Orientation: Towards an Integrated Model

MOC
Dapeng Liang; Yiding Zhang (Harbin Institute of Technology)
This study develops and validates a theoretical model of artificial intelligence orientation (AIO) to explain how firms strategically integrate artificial intelligence (AI) into organizational transformation. Grounded in dynamic capability theory, we extend beyond human-centric decision-making logics to conceptualize AIO as a strategic response mechanism. Using a large-scale survey and multidimensional factor analysis, we validate a 17-item AIO scale, which is best represented by four dimensions: AI Learning, AI Dissemination, AI application, and AI ecosystem. Empirical results show that these dimensions influence firm performance in their unique ways, confirming the construct validity and strategic relevance of AIO. This study advances AI research by offering a quantifiable lens to examine AI-driven cognitive restructuring and behavioral transformation in organizations. It further provides actionable guidance for managers to build AI-enabled dynamic capabilities and for policymakers to design supportive AI governance systems.
