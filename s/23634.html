The Evolving Landscape of Work: An International Analysis

MC
Bianka Zakova (Prague University of Economics and Business)
This dissertation explores technostress in the banking sector through a comparative lens, focusing on employees in the Czech Republic and Germany—two countries with distinct economic roles. Rapid digitalisation and the integration of advanced technologies (AI and chatbots) have transformed banking operations, creating new demands that may strain employees while offering opportunities for efficiency. Guided by the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) theory, the study examines how technostress and eustress as job demands interact with job (technology, leadership support, coworker support) and personal resources (attitudes toward technology) to influence work engagement and exhaustion. A mixed-methods design combines qualitative insights from focus groups and semi-structured interviews with quantitative data collected via a validated technostress questionnaire adapted for Czech and German contexts. By comparing two banking institutions across these countries, the research aims to identify generational differences in technostress perception and coping strategies, while assessing the buffering role of resources. The findings will contribute to refining JD-R applications in digital work environments and provide actionable recommendations for fostering human-centered, sustainable workplaces in the banking sector.

Stop or Measure the Bleeding? Adaptive Judgment Across Scholar–Practitioner Oscillation

MC
Jennifer Seyler (Business Strategy and Operation Consulting, LLC)
Organization Development (OD) professionals navigate a persistent paradox: balancing scholarly rigor and theoretical contribution with practitioners' demands for contextual relevance, timeliness, and action. This scholar–practitioner identity requires managing ongoing tensions between diagnosing and intervening, reflection and responsiveness, and rigor and relevance. This study examines how these tensions unfolded as the author enacted dual roles as Executive Director and OD Consultant within a volunteer-led professional association. Through re-analysis of a longitudinal, mixed-methods insider– researcher dataset, the study investigates oscillatory movement between scholarly reasoning and practitioner action across an exploratory phase and seven iterative interventions. Seventeen distinct oscillations revealed patterned shifts in practitioner orientation. To interpret these dynamics, the study develops a Four-Domain OD Framework synthesizing OD theory, dialogic and diagnostic orientations, Lewin's change model, and paradox theory. This framework organizes practitioner activity into Diagnostic, Dialogic, Intervention, and Adaptation Practices, enabling analysis of oscillatory movement across inquiry, meaning-making, action, and recalibration across time. Adaptive judgment emerged as the coordinating mechanism enabling movement between orientations, while distinct organizing logics structured how oscillation unfolded across intervention phases. By theorizing adaptive judgment as a coordinating mechanism and identifying organizing logics underlying oscillation patterns, this study reframes the scholar–practitioner paradox as disciplined, patterned movement rather than role conflict. The findings provide a theoretically grounded account of navigating rigor-relevance tensions and offer a methodological framework for examining practitioner judgment in complex, uncertain organizational contexts, with implications for effectiveness in volunteer-led systems.

Digital Work and Employees' Technostress in Multinational Manufacturing: A Pilot Study

MC
Bianka Zakova (Prague University of Economics and Business); Zuzana Kreckova Kroupova (Prague University of Economics and Business); Oscar Rete (UTN - Facultad Regional San Francisco)
Accelerated digital transformation has intensified employee exposure to information and communication technologies (ICT), making technostress an increasingly salient organisational phenomenon. While technostress has been examined primarily in service, education, and IT contexts, little is known about how it manifests among office employees in multinational manufacturing firms. Addressing this gap, this pilot study investigates the prevalence, dimensionality, and sociodemographic variability of technostress in a Liechtenstein based family owned manufacturing company operating across five countries. This pilot study investigates technostress among office employees of a Liechtenstein based family owned manufacturing company with international operations. Data were collected using a validated 17-item technostress questionnaire administered to 63 daily ICT users. Non parametric statistical tests were employed to examine differences across gender, age cohorts, and countries of employment. Exploratory factor analysis complemented by hierarchical cluster analysis was applied to validate the underlying structure of technostress in this organisational context. The findings confirm the multidimensional nature of technostress and identify six distinct dimensions. The results further reveal significant sociodemographic and cross national variations. Based on these insights, implications are derived for international human resource management and the design of sustainable digital work environments.

Silent Servants or Speaking Machines? How Robots’ Social Responses to Abuse Shape Value Co-creation

MC
Ruchika Sharma; Nazia Gera (FORE School of Management); Aashu Aggarwal; Pratibha Singh (Birla Institute of Management Technology)
The proliferation of artificial intelligence and service robots has transformed frontline service delivery, yet incidents of robot mistreatment– verbal and physical– pose new challenges for human–robot interactions. While prior research has explored functional and efficiency outcomes of robot usage, little is known about how observers of robot abuse respond in terms of value co-creation. Across four studies, this research investigates how exposure to robot victimization influences individuals’ willingness to co-create value with service robots. Study 1 establishes that witnessing robot abuse (vs. control) increases intentions to co-create value. Study 2 examines the differential effects of physical versus verbal robot abuse and finds that both forms independently enhance value co-creation intentions, with no significant difference between them. Study 3 introduces robot social response capability (present vs. absent) as a moderator and finds a significant interaction: when a robot responds socially to physical abuse, value co-creation increases; however, when the same response occurs following verbal abuse, value co-creation decreases. Study 4 investigates the psychological mechanisms-specifically moral agency and error responsibility attribution- that underlie the impact of robot abuse type (physical vs. verbal) and social response capability (present vs. absent) on value co-creation. The findings reveal that while a robot’s social response to physical abuse heightens moral agency and increases value co-creation intentions, the same response following verbal abuse increases error responsibility attribution, leading to reduced value co-creation intentions. The findings advance understanding of robot victimization and underscore the importance of designing socially responsive robots that encourage ethical human–robot engagement.

Protecting Digital Talent: The Role of Eudaimonic Well-Being in Worker Loyalty in the FinTech Sector

MC
Fco. Javier Moreno Arrebola (University of Málaga); Jesus Molina-Gomez (University of Málaga); Mariano Garrido-Lopez (Western Carolina University); José Manuel Núñez-Sánchez (University of Málaga)
In a current environment characterized by rapid technological disruption and the accelerating effects of Moore's Law (Moore, 1998), the FinTech sector is experiencing high talent turnover and widespread anxiety regarding skills obsolescence. Through the perception of Corporate social responsibility (CSR) activities, organizations could positively affect employee loyalty, according to Guzzo et al.’s (2022) sequential mediation model. In this study, we apply this model to examine whether, despite the use of a one-dimensional CSR signal, the Eudaimonic well-being mediator will become the predominant mechanism driving employees’ loyalty boosterism within high-obsolescence, technology-intensive financial organizations. We argue that the threat to the psychological need for Competence, as a core pillar of Eudaimonic Well-being, is primarily mitigated by the CSR to Employees Dimension (Turker, 2009). Because CSR signals are cognitively filtered through competence-based needs, their relational effect is expected to be significantly stronger than that associated with Hedonic Well-being. This article develops the theoretical framework for a subsequent statistical study using PLS-SEM, through which we expect to demonstrate that the Eudaimonic causal pathway is statistically superior to the Hedonic pathway in driving loyalty boosterism. These findings will provide FinTech organizations with a new perspective on the design of CSR policies and may justify reframing Employee-related CSR expenditures as strategic investments in the retention of qualified human capital.
