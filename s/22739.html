Autonomy- Versus Dependency-Oriented AI Usage and Their Differential Effects on Employee Creativity

OB
Maona Mu (Renmin University of China); Wenhao Luo (North China University of Technology)
As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly prevalent in employees’ daily work, employees have two paradoxical tendencies when using it: a desire to maintain their agency to avoid being replaced by AI, and an inclination to rely on AI to reduce their cognitive burdens. This tension gives rise to two approaches to AI usage in the workplace: autonomy-oriented and dependency-oriented. Specifically, autonomy-oriented AI usage refers to employees utilizing AI to assist them in better performing their tasks autonomously, whereas dependency-oriented AI usage involves employees relying on AI and uncritically adopting its outputs to complete their tasks. Study 1, conducted with 300 employees across various industries in China, develops valid measures for these two approaches to AI usage. Moreover, we draw upon self-determination theory to explore whether and how these two approaches to AI usage exert differential effects on employee creativity. Study 2, using three-wave field data from 113 leader-follower dyads, shows that autonomy-oriented AI usage enhances employee creativity via autonomy need satisfaction, whereas dependency-oriented usage does not. By doing so, this research advances the literature on AI usage and offers practical guidance for managers and employees on how to effectively use AI at work.

When and From Whom: How AI- vs Colleague-Generated Examples and Their Timing Shape Creative Ideation

OB
Hyun Sun Chung (Seoul National University); Hyunjee Hannah Kim (Korea Aerospace University); Jeong Hyun Lee (Korea Aerospace University)
Generative AI is increasingly embedded in everyday work, reshaping how individuals generate creative ideas while viewing AI-generated ideas or colleagues’ prior ideas. Yet organizations lack evidence-based guidance on when and from whom examples should be introduced, because example exposure can both stimulate creative exploration and constrain it through fixation and conformity. Drawing on dual-process theory, social comparison theory, and construal level theory, we argue that AI- versus human-generated ideas change the motivational and evaluative context of ideation, and that these source effects depend on whether examples are shared early or late. We test this in a 2 (AI vs. Human source) x 2 (early vs. late exposure timing) between-subjects online experiment with 233 participants using a scenario-based idea generation task. Results showed that AI-generated ideas increased novelty and human - generated ideas increased feasibility, and timing moderated these effects such that early AI exposure strengthened novelty via divergent thinking, while late human exposure strengthened feasibility via convergent thinking. This research opens the “black box” of AI-generated creativity, offering actionable insights for designing human–AI collaboration processes that leverage AI's strengths without undermining human originality.

When Generative AI Helps—or Hurts—Creativity at Work: The Role of Daily Work Engagement

OB
Dominik Zahs (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf); Lynn Schmodde (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf); Marius Wehner
As generative AI tools become increasingly embedded in daily work, understanding their impact on employees is essential. From a work design perspective, generative AI can function either as a job resource or as a job demand; however, it remains unclear under which conditions each interpretation emerges in everyday work. To address this gap, this study adopts an experience sampling approach to examine how daily generative AI use relates to work engagement and creativity. We collected data from 120 employees across five workdays (480 observations). Drawing on the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, we examine generative AI use at the day level and consider daily workload and AI literacy as key boundary conditions. Multilevel analyses showed that generative AI use per se did not affect daily work engagement or creativity. Instead, the extent of daily use was positively associated with work engagement, which in turn fostered daily creativity. At the same time, a negative direct effect on daily creativity emerged, moderated by daily workload and AI literacy, suggesting that the benefits of generative AI unfold only under certain conditions. Overall, our findings show that the motivational and creative effects of generative AI depend on its extent of use and contextual conditions.
