Burning Bright or Burning Out: Understanding Outcomes of Self-Leadership Through Emotion Regulation

OB
Sinje Mierke Naß (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz University of Hanover)
Contemporary work increasingly relies on self-leadership as employees are granted high autonomy and are expected to organize their own tasks, priorities and boundaries. Self-leadership is typically portrayed as a positive resource that enhances motivation, performance and well-being. Yet emerging studies show that self-leadership can also be associated with emotional exhaustion, strain and burnout, particularly when individuals “help themselves” into chronic overcommitment and insufficient recovery. These mixed findings point to a missing process account of when and how self-leadership protects versus undermines mental health in autonomous work. In this conceptual paper, we develop an integrated self-leadership × emotion regulation model that explains divergent outcomes of self-led work. Merging self-leadership cycles with Gross’s extended process model, we derive five Co-stages (Co-Design, Co-Detection, Co-Selection, Co-Enactment and Co-Learning) and represent self-leadership episodes as a decision tree of regulatory choices. We theorize how context contingencies (autonomy, ambiguity, isolation), regulatory flexibility, and explicit versus implicit modes shape which branches are taken and how episode-level outcomes accumulate into cumulative mental-health load, sometimes triggering awareness-driven redesign. The model differentiates three trajectories – “burning bright”, “overextending” and “burning out” – and yields propositions about when each is more likely. We outline implications for self-leadership and emotion-regulation theory and for designing self-led work and interventions that target critical decision points to sustain mental health under high autonomy.
