Does Overqualification Facilitate or Hinder Career Attainments? A Career Mobility Perspective

OB
Weipeng Lin (Shandong University); Li Yao (Peking University); Qi Zhang (Oregon State University)
Prior research has offered competing perspectives on the career-related consequences of overqualification, yielding mixed and often contradictory findings. To move beyond this theoretical impasse, we integrate the contest- and sponsored-mobility perspectives and overqualification literature to develop a more comprehensive account of how and when overqualification may benefit or harm individuals’ career attainments (i.e., promotion and salary progression). Using data from a multi-source and multi-wave design across a time span of three years, the results indicated that overqualification predicted career attainments negatively via job dedication, but positively via sponsorship network building. Moreover, perceived organizational support for career development (POS-CD) played a moderating role, such that the indirect effects of overqualification on career attainments through job dedication and sponsorship network building were stronger when employees experienced low (vs. high) POS-CD. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the findings.

Employee Overqualification and Promotability: A Social Network Perspective

OB
Limei Chen (Chongqing University); Yiwen Zhang (The University of Hong Kong); Xiao Jun Zhan; Yirong Guo (Xiamen University)
Despite the prevalence of overqualification, little is known about how employees navigate this qualification–job mismatch to advance their careers. Integrating social network and career mobility theories, we examine how overqualified employees engage with advice networks to influence their promotability. We propose a dual-pathway model: overqualified employees are more likely to give advice, attaining more central positions in the advice-giving network, which then enhances promotability by strengthening leader–member relationship quality and improving task performance. Conversely, they are less inclined to seek advice, reducing their centrality in the advice-seeking network, which undermines promotability through weaker leader–member relationship quality and lower task performance. We further argue that gender moderates these pathways: compared with overqualified men, overqualified women face greater barriers in converting overqualification into central positions in the advice-giving network, which constrains their promotability. However, overqualified women face fewer penalties than overqualified men in attaining central positions in the advice-seeking network. Multi-wave, multi-source survey data across organizations in China supports most of our hypotheses. These findings offer nuanced insights into how different types of social networks can either facilitate or constrain career advancement for overqualified employees.

Rising Perceived Overqualification Among Newcomers: Implication and Mitigation

OB
Qiongjing Hu (Zhejiang University); Yijun Chen (Zhejiang University); Wei Wu (Zhejiang University); Yuyang Gan (Zhejiang University)
Perceived overqualification (POQ) reflects employees’ judgments that their skills and abilities exceed job requirements, yet research has typically treated POQ as a relatively static condition. Building on person-job fit theory, we conceptualize POQ as a dynamic, within-person demands-abilities appraisal that can evolve as newcomers learn and gain efficiency during organizational entry. We further argue that the developmental trajectory of POQ has consequences beyond initial levels and that growth in enacted task demands can mitigate POQ growth. We test these ideas in a six-month longitudinal study of 228 newly hired nurses in a large public hospital in Eastern China. Using latent growth modeling, we find that newcomers’ POQ increases in a positive linear trajectory during early socialization. Importantly, the growth rate of POQ predicts turnover intention at the end of the study beyond initial POQ. Moreover, increases in job complexity and outcome responsibility mitigate the upward trajectory of POQ over time. These findings shift attention from POQ as a fixed mismatch to POQ as a developing fit appraisal shaped by both newcomer learning and job design, and they highlight task progression as an actionable organizational lever for retaining and utilizing high-capability newcomers.

Antecedents of Newcomers’ Perceived Overqualification in the Adjustment Phase

OB
Yoko M. Negoro (The Pennsylvania State University); Heyao Yu (The Pennsylvania State University); Michael J. Tews (The Pennsylvania State University)
Perceived overqualification poses a persistent challenge for organizations, yet little is known about how such perceptions emerge, particularly during organizational entry. Drawing on person-environment fit theory and a sense-making in social influence framework, this research examines how coworkers’ organizational and occupational perceptions shape newcomers’ perceived overqualification during the adjustment period. Across two studies, we investigate distinct pathways through which coworkers influence newcomers’ evaluations. Study 1 uses scenario-based experiments to establish causal evidence that coworkers’ organizational identification and occupational commitment shape newcomers’ liking toward the organization and career aspirations, respectively. Study 2 validates and extends these findings through a multi-source, multi-wave field study of newcomers and their coworkers. Results indicate that coworkers’ organizational identification enhances newcomers’ liking toward the organization, and coworkers’ occupational commitment strengthens newcomers’ career aspirations. Importantly, coworkers’ occupational commitment indirectly reduces newcomers’ perceived overqualification via enhanced career aspirations, whereas coworkers’ organizational identification, operating through organizational liking, does not significantly influence perceived overqualification. In addition, perceived social distance moderates these relationships, such that coworker influence is stronger when newcomers experience closer relational ties. Overall, the findings demonstrate that perceived overqualification is not merely a static individual assessment but a socially constructed perception that develops through coworker interactions during early socialization. By shifting attention from outcomes to antecedents, this research advances understandings of how perceived overqualification emerges and highlights the importance of occupational cues and relational factors in newcomer adjustment.
