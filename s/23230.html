When Dragon Slayer Warriors Grow Dragon Scales: Board Chair-CEO Power Gap and Firm Goal Setting

STR
Qiang Zheng (South China University of Technology); Weiwen Li (Sun Yat-Sen University); Manli Huang (South China University of Technology)
Research suggests that setting ambitious goals for CEOs enhances shareholder value and mitigates agency problems, with board power playing a critical monitoring role. But is more board director power always better for ensuring rigorous oversight? Drawing on a unique dataset of 4,504 firm-year observations from China’s listed state-owned enterprises, where board chairs typically hold substantial power over CEOs, we challenge the conventional linear view. We find an inverted U-shaped relationship between the board chair-CEO power gap and the difficulty of corporate goals. While a moderate power gap in favor of the board chair promotes more ambitious goals, an excessive gap becomes detrimental, leading entrenched board chairs to set conservative goals that serve their self-interest. We theorize that this curvilinear effect is driven by two underlying mechanisms: the level of conflict between the two strategic leaders and the clarity of their respective accountability. Furthermore, we find that this relationship is contingent upon key relational and institutional factors: the inverted U-shaped curve is attenuated (flattened) by (1) board chair-CEO demographic similarity and (2) career horizon asymmetry, but amplified (steepened) by (3) institutional ownership and (4) government subsidies. Our study contributes to the strategic leadership and corporate governance literature by providing a more nuanced, non-linear perspective on how power dynamics within the top management dyad shape critical strategic outcomes.

The Agency Vortex: Why Social Dominance Matters for Strategy Research

STR
Natalie Shefer (Copenhagen Business School); Ithai Stern (INSEAD)
Agency theory traditionally tries to limit self-interested managerial behavior using incentive misalignment and weak governance. We advance a complementary perspective by theorizing how agency problems are generated through executive selection. We argue that organizations systematically select and promote individuals high in social dominance, a personality disposition associated with agentic behavior, because such behavior is interpreted as indicative of leadership capability. As these individuals ascend organizational hierarchies, the same agentic tendencies that facilitate their advancement also increase the likelihood of self-interested behavior once they occupy executive positions. Over time, this self-reinforcing process produces what we term an “agency vortex,” whereby executive selection amplifies agency problems. As a result, upper echelons become disproportionately populated with individuals high in social dominance, impacting how strategic decisions are made at the top of the organization. We theorize the implications of this on executive compensation, succession, and corporate development decisions, and identify boundary conditions under which social dominance may attenuate or intensify agency problems. By shifting attention from controlling agentic behavior through governance mechanisms to the selection processes that determine who reaches top management positions, our theory reframes the persistence of agency problems as rooted in the selection of certain executive personality dispositions.

Cybergovernance: How CEO Duality, Firm Diversification, and CEO Ownership Shape Breach Outcomes

STR
Rohan Karri (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Pankaj Kumar (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
This study examines how CEO duality influences a firm’s vulnerability to cybersecurity breaches and how this relationship is shaped by firm diversification and CEO equity ownership. While prior research associates CEO duality with weakened oversight, we argue that unified leadership can enhance responsiveness in the cybersecurity context. Dual-role CEOs prioritize cybersecurity investments owing to their heightened reputational concerns, authority and added insight, thus reducing the likelihood of a cybersecurity breach. Using a sample of S&P1500 firms, we find that CEO duality is associated with fewer cybersecurity breaches. Interestingly, this effect is amplified in more diversified firms, where organizational complexity heightens the value of unified leadership, but attenuated when CEOs hold high equity, as they prioritize other proximate projects over intangible peripheral investments such as cybersecurity. These findings offer new insights into how leadership structure and executive incentives jointly shape strategic responses to digital threats.

Residual-Claim Ambiguity and Strategy Selection Under Shareholder-Centric Governance

STR
Adam Clark (Chapman University)
Agency theory has long shaped strategic management by offering a logic for governing managerial discretion through shareholder-centric governance, which privileges shareholders as primary residual claimants and reduces goal conflict. Yet economic profits often depend on stakeholder-controlled resources whose marginal contributions cannot be fully specified or enforced ex ante. When such incompletely contractible inputs are essential to value creation, residual claims extend beyond shareholders, challenging the conditions under which shareholder-centric governance is efficient. This paper develops a theory of how governance shapes strategy selection in these multi-claimant settings by introducing residual-claim ambiguity: uncertainty over how economic profits should be allocated ex post among multiple contributors who bear residual risk. Residual-claim ambiguity expands managerial discretion over profit allocation and, under shareholder-centric governance, weakens the credibility of commitments to nonshareholder contributors. Anticipating unfavorable allocation, stakeholders respond strategically—through influence activities, bargaining frictions, effort shading, and reduced firm-specific investment—generating efficiency losses. These distributional agency costs are distinct from managerial agency costs, transaction costs, and bargaining costs because they arise from discretionary profit allocation under contested residual claims. This paper argues that distributional agency costs vary systematically across profit-generation mechanisms: novelty and unique complementarities heighten ambiguity because they rely on discretionary, hard-to-measure contributions, whereas scale relies more on standardized, contractible inputs that clarify expected returns. Consequently, shareholder-centric governance creates selection pressure toward scale-based profit generation and away from novelty and complementarities, even when the latter offer greater value-creation potential, reframing governance as a determinant of which value-creation pathways are sustainable.

Looking Under the Distributional Rug for Agency Problems

STR
Tyson Brighton Mackey (University of Wisconsin-La Crosse); Alison Mackey (University of Wisconsin-La Crosse); Jeffrey P. Dotson (The Ohio State University)
This paper resolves a longstanding empirical puzzle in agency theory research: why do studies often find conflicting or inconclusive support for agency theory predictions regarding governance and firm performance? We argue that previous empirical research overlooks a crucial contingency—the heterogeneity in the value of strategic decisions facing managers. We propose that governance mechanisms have differential effects across the distribution of strategy value, being most critical when strategies have modestly negative firm value but offer substantial private benefits to managers. By accounting for this heterogeneity, we demonstrate that governance has a significant impact precisely where agency theory predicts it should—when material agency conflicts exist. Conversely, when strategies clearly create or destroy substantial value, governance plays a minimal role as managerial and shareholder interests naturally align. We develop a novel methodological approach that has the potential to explain and resolve conflicting findings in the empirical governance / agency literatures. Our results indicate that governance matters significantly, but only under specific conditions of potential agency conflicts, explaining why averaging across all strategic contexts has yielded inconsistent findings.
