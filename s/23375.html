Know Your Role? Investor Perceptions of Executive Dismissal Following Signal of Financial Misconduct

STR
ZIHENG ZHANG (Nankai University); Matthew Mount
Drawing on role theory, this study proposes that the within-role difference between CEO versus CFO power—the difference in power relative to that expected for their role—can provide insights into investors’ reactions to CEO versus CFO dismissal following signalled financial misconduct. We argue that, because CEOs and CFOs are both functionally responsible for financial misconduct, the within-role difference between CEO and CFO power will be a salient signal to investors influencing their attributions of blame. Through an event study analysis of 743 dismissals (CEO vs. CFO) in U.S. firms between 2005 and 2021, we show that when the within-role power of the CEO is higher than the within-role power of the CFO, investors react more positively to CEO dismissal. Conversely, when the within-role power of the CFO is higher than the within-role power of the CEO, investors react more positively to CFO dismissal. We discuss the implications of our results for the literature on executive dismissal, upper echelons theory, and role theory. Key words: CEO dismissal, CFO dismissal, role theory, power, financial misconduct

A Wake-Up Call? How CEOs’ Prior Experience with Shareholder Unrest Shapes Subsequent CSR Engagement

STR
Abhinav Gupta (University of Washington); Adam Wowak (University of Notre Dame); Michelle K. Lee (Queen's University)
As corporate social responsibility (CSR) has become increasingly central to firm strategy, scholars have sought to understand why organizations differ in their CSR engagement. Much of this work has examined external stakeholder pressures—most notably activist investors—as drivers of CSR, while a growing body of work has explored how executives’ values and dispositions shape CSR decisions. What remains unclear is whether and how such stakeholder pressures shape the executives who experience them. Integrating insights from strategic leadership, stakeholder activism, and the attention-based view, we develop a theory linking CEOs’ experiences with stakeholder pressures in prior roles to their decision making in subsequent ones. Our central argument is that these experiences can produce lasting shifts in attention, such that CEOs who encounter CSR-oriented unrest at one firm are more likely to prioritize CSR in future leadership roles. We further theorize that the effect of such experience is stronger when shareholder unrest can be perceived as a legitimate critique of CSR underperformance, when the CEOs espouse more conservative ideologies, and when CEOs transition to firms in the same industry where CSR norms remain similar. Testing our theory using a sample of S&P 1500 CEOs and two distinct CSR indicators—an omnibus CSR measure and a measure of executive gender diversity—we find consistent support for our predictions.

Shareholder Voting Dissent and Non-CEO Executive Turnover

STR
Jiatao Li (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Yuan SHUI (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology)
This study examines how voting dissent from institutional investors affects non-CEO executive turnover. While research on shareholder dissent mostly focuses on director or CEO turnover, it ignores the effect on other top executives. Using a data sample from S&P 1500 firms between 2004 and 2019, we posit and find that when institutional investors withhold votes in director elections, non-CEO executive turnover increases. One reason could be the amplification of directors’ reputation concerns, triggering personnel change with non-CEO executives often becoming scapegoats. We also find that board network size and CEO power strengthen the scapegoating of non-CEO executives. Our findings move beyond the classic two-level framework of agency theory, expanding the understanding of how institutional investors influence corporate strategy.

How Target Executives and Directors Can Prevail in Activist Hedge Fund Campaigns

STR
Matthias Brauer (University of Mannheim); Marc J. Mertens (HEC Montréal); Louis Vandepoele (University of Mannheim)
Prior research on shareholder activism has yielded valuable insights into the far-reaching consequences of activist hedge fund campaigns on target firms, their executives, and directors. However, we know little about how target executives and directors might mount effective defenses against such activists. We advance the argument that executives and directors can repel activist hedge funds by deploying both negative and positive impression management tactics to secure the pivotal support of other shareholders. Specifically, we theorize on how and why discrediting an activist hedge fund enhances the likelihood of defense success. Moreover, we posit that target executives and directors’ self-promotion is positively associated with target defense success. Notably, our theorizing suggests that these two impression management tactics operate as complements rather than substitutes, such that their combined use further amplifies target defense success. Yet, we theorize that the potency of these defense tactics diminishes when proxy advisors oppose management’s position and endorse the activist instead. Empirical analyses of activist hedge fund campaigns directed at U.S. firms between 2000 and 2023 fully support our theoretical predictions. Our work advances impression management theory, offers phenomenon-driven contributions to shareholder activism research, and provides practical guidance for managers and directors.

Reinterpreting Executive Exits: Ascriptive Dissimilarity and Executive Labor Market Outcomes

STR
Hatameh Ahmadi Harandi (University of Florida); Zhefan Huang (University of Florida); David Gaddis Ross (University of Florida)
Extensive theory and evidence suggest that executives are more likely to leave their firms when they work on top management teams (TMTs) with ascriptively dissimilar colleagues (that is, individuals who differ from them in terms of race or gender). Prior research largely interprets such exits as reflecting a preference for similarity or an aversion to dissimilarity. We challenge this assumption by proposing and testing an alternative explanation: that executives depart because experience working with ascriptively dissimilar colleagues is valued in the executive labor market. As such, we also propose that executives who exit under such conditions are more likely to join new TMTs where they remain ascriptively dissimilar. Using Execucomp data from 2000 to 2019, we find strong support for our predictions. That is, we find prior experience working on ascriptively diverse TMTs, not currently working with ascriptively dissimilar peer executives, predicts the likelihood of both executive exit and of subsequently joining TMTs with ascriptively dissimilar peer executives. In post hoc analyses, we also find that executives with such experience receive higher compensation in their subsequent roles, which is also consistent with such experience being valued in the labor market. Together, these findings suggest that experience with ascriptively dissimilar colleagues can enhance executives’ market value and prompt career advancement, rather than simply causing discomfort or aversion.
