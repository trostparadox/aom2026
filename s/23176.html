Stabilization Without Convergence: How Collective Sensemaking Stabilizes in Digital Publics

CTO
Tim Risse (EBS University of Business and Law); Matthias Waldkirch (EBS University of Business and Law)
Digital publics are central arenas for collective sensemaking, yet most online discussions frag- ment rather than stabilizing into sustained, coordinated interaction. While prior research high- lights the roles of discourse, affect, and participation, we know little about how sensemaking be- comes interactionally scalable in open, weakly governed environments. We reconceptualize sensemaking outcomes as interactional stabilization—the emergence of sustained interactional order through the structural concentration of attention, without requiring agreement or consensus. Building on sensemaking and framing theory, we develop a configurational framework and ana- lyze conversational episodes from a large sample of Reddit news discussions using fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA). Our results show that interactional stabilization hinges on the persistence of interpretive frames over time. Stabilization can emerge through alternative pathways, including endogenous frame coherence without reliance on initial public cues and dis- tributed interaction without centralized sensegiving. By identifying frame stability as the core condition underpinning interactional order, this study advances sensemaking theory by explain- ing how meaning becomes scalable in digital publics and demonstrates the value of configura- tional approaches for studying large-scale online interaction

Not Everyone Shares the Same: Unpacking Factors of Willingness to Share in Data Ecosystems

CTO
Giulia Renzi (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Paula Ungureanu (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Selini Hadjidimitriou (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia)
Data ecosystems are fundamental to address relevant societal challenges, by leveraging multi-stakeholder participation. However, our understanding of what motivates or hinders individuals and organizations from sharing data within such ecosystems remains limited. While existing studies have emphasized many factors such as data quality, participants’ motivations, and governance mechanisms, an integrated perspective is missing. This study focuses on the interplay between motivational, cognitive, and relational factors that influence data sharing behaviors in data ecosystems. By conducting a qualitative study in a multi-stakeholder data ecosystem, we adopt a practice-based approach to investigate how data sharing perceptions, concerns, and relational expectations shape the willingness to share data and contribute towards different sharing practices. Our findings document a constellation of data sharing practices within a data ecosystem – namely driving, pulling, and scaffolding. These practices depend on the social construction of willingness to share data resulting from stakeholders’ motivational orientations (social vs. utilitarian), their cognitive evaluations of the risks and opportunities associated with data sharing, and their social evaluation of the ecosystem’s conditions. Our practice-based view integrates motivational orientation with socio-cognitive evaluation and relational processes of data sharing, advancing the literature on the governance of data ecosystems and providing new insights into the factors shaping the willingness to share data.

Divide and Contribute: Stratified Hybrid Infrastructuring in Firm-Sponsored Open Source AI Software

CTO
Savindu Herath (ETH Zurich)
Open-source artificial intelligence (OS AI) software, a key infrastructure for building and scaling downstream AI innovations, is increasingly being developed through hybrid arrangements that combine firm sponsorship with community-based contributions. While contributions to open-source software (OSS) have been extensively studied, we know little about how contributors with high interest and resource heterogeneity engage in the development of firm-sponsored OS AI software, and how those contributions collectively shape the underlying infrastructure. Drawing on the information infrastructure innovation (III) perspective, I conceptualize this development as the cultivation of an installed base and employ a computationally intensive, inductive mixed-methods approach to investigate nearly a decade of GitHub commit and pull request data from PyTorch and TensorFlow, two widely adopted OS AI libraries. Findings reveal a complementary division of labor: Firms contribute more complex, interdependent, and low-level infrastructural code, while individuals focus on high-level, modular tasks such as debugging, documentation, and interface development. This pattern of differentiated stratified engagement shapes the infrastructure by enabling parallel innovation at both the infrastructural core and interfaces. I contribute to III literature by theorizing on how top-down, bottom-up, and grafting innovation strategies co-exist within hybrid infrastructures, and how coordination and self-organization unfold in such contexts. I also contribute to OSS scholarship by uncovering how coordination and self-organization unfold in OS AI software. For practitioners, the findings offer guidance on how to balance influence and openness to foster community-driven innovation in firm-sponsored OS AI infrastructures.
