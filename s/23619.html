Gendered Visibility, Embodiment, and Support at Work

Session overview

This session explores how women's workplace experiences are shaped by visibility, embodiment, support, and social expectations. The papers investigate visibility among female scientists, body image concerns and self-marginalization, male allyship responses to sexism, women's participation in unions, and role model-based financial literacy interventions. Together, they reveal how women's opportunities and well-being are influenced by both interpersonal dynamics and broader cultural assumptions about gender, competence, and legitimacy.

The Price of Visibility: Good for Society, But Bad for Female Scientists?

DEI
Alexandra Julia Abler (Technical University of Munich); Nadja Mirjam Born; Isabell Melanie Welpe (Technical University of Munich)
Visibility is a critical factor for academic success, shaping access to resources, recognition, and advancement opportunities. Yet, visibility in academia remains unevenly distributed. Female scientists often perceive themselves as less visible. Moreover, while visibility can enhance access to resources and opportunities, it may also entail costs. We examine how visibility relates to key personal and social resources, namely self-esteem, self-efficacy, sense of belonging, and perceived social support. Based on a survey of academic personnel at 86 German universities (N = 1,739), our results show that female scientists report significantly lower levels of visibility than their male peers, but not a lower desire for increased visibility. Across the full sample, higher visibility was positively associated with self-esteem, self-efficacy, sense of belonging, and perceived social support. We found no evidence that women derive fewer resource gains from visibility than men. Scientists who feared backlash showed a stronger link between visibility and self-esteem and belonging. In contrast, individuals with higher narcissism showed a slightly weaker link between visibility and belonging. Across an exploratory follow-up, participants’ self-descriptions revealed no gender differences in agency or communal expression, suggesting a high willingness among female scientists to engage in visibility-enhancing practices. Hence, female invisibility in academia may be better explained by structural norms than motivation or ability, namely less willingness for visibility. Our findings advance Conservation of Resources theory and gender research by conceptualizing visibility as both a psychological resource and a resource amplifier. We discuss institutional strategies to promote equitable visibility in academic careers.

Confront in Public, Validate in Private: Effective Male Allyship Responses to Sexist Remarks at Work

DEI
Hsuan-Che (Brad) Huang (The University of British Columbia)
Knowing how to respond to sexist comments and counteract their harmful consequences remains a challenging task for male allies. In this mixed-methods research, our pre-registered qualitative study of 82 male leaders from the United States (Study 1) reveals that most intend to confront perpetrators of sexist remarks, with a preference for doing so privately rather than publicly; relatively few intend to validate the experiences of harmed targets. We contend that allies need to consider actions beyond confrontation—a response commonly recommended by scholars and in practice—as well as appropriate social contexts for these allyship behaviors to ensure the inclusion of women who are directly harmed by such remarks. We hypothesize that targets’ sense of belonging and voice intentions are optimally supported when allies not only (a) confront transgressors’ sexist remarks in public (vs. private), but also (b) validate targets’ harmed experiences in private (vs. public). Three pre-registered and one follow-up experimental studies (Studies 2–4; N = 1216) from the perspective of women support our theory. Our findings suggest that confrontation should be enacted in public because it directly reinforces gender civility norms, whereas validation is better provided in private to demonstrate concern for dignity. In our final study (Study 5; N = 253), we provide critical insights into how public confrontation—targets’ preferred response—might adversely influence the attitudes and behaviors of confronted perpetrators (e.g., bias regulation). By incorporating multiparty perspectives, our research provides actionable recommendations for potential allies, especially men in leadership roles.

The Influence of Personality, Relative Deprivation and Efficacy on Women’s Participation in Unions

DEI
Rashmi Maini (Amity University, Uttar Pradesh); Mahima Thakur (University of Delhi); Gurinder Singh (Amity University, Uttar Pradesh)
A huge proportion of women enter the workforce globally, and they face various challenges in the organisations, including unequal pay, mental and physical harassment, lack of childcare facilities, and low representation in leadership positions (Ledwith, 2012). To address these challenges and assure their special needs as women, they seek help from other women associated with unions (Dean, 2006). Despite knowing several reasons, such as patriarchal environment, rigid socio-cultural norms and family burden for low women’s participation in trade unions (WPTU), previous studies have categorically overlooked the influence of specific psychological attributes and social cues that might influence WPTU (Beadles et al., 2023; Gregory & Milner, 2009). This study fills the research gap by exploring the role of three factors, personality traits, relative deprivation and efficacy in the context of WPTU. The study utilises Structural Equation Modelling to analyse the data collected from 725 women unionists affiliated to three major global unions. Results indicate that personality traits, extraversion & emotional stability; relative deprivation, and both political & self-efficacy, have a significant positive influence on the participation of women in trade unions. Adding more women to union positions would bring a fresh perspective and make them more diverse & inclusive in nature. By actively participating in union-related tasks, women gain confidence and feel socially empowered. Such findings help policy-framers and trade unionists in building effective strategies for greater women’s participation in unions, aligning with the larger goals of diversity, equity and inclusion. Enhanced participation of women in unions reshapes union thoughts and pushes them towards an inclusive culture and broader social justice aspect.

Embodied Inequality at Work: Women’s Body Image Concerns, Self-Marginalization, and Career Striving

DEI
Anna Carmella Ocampo (ESADE Business School); Natalia M Lorinkova (The Pennsylvania State University)
Physical body features carry important meanings that propel or compromise people’s careers. In this article, we integrate insights from stereotype threat and self-regulatory theories to develop a workplace model of women’s body image concerns. We identify two distinct response pathways through which body image concerns direct the course of women’s career outcomes. Along an internalization pathway, women accept appearance-based stereotypes as valid, evident in their self-regulatory breaking responses characterized by self-marginalization and lowered self-expectations. Along a rejection pathway, women engage in self-regulatory compensatory and resistance responses that redirect effort, challenge negative appearance-based assumptions, and preserve agency. Our model further identifies key personal (e.g., personality, appearance centrality, and demographics) and contextual (e.g., aesthetic labor requirements, physical employment standards, organizational wellness climates) factors that influence the activation and consequences of the internalization and rejection pathways, thereby highlighting when and how body image concerns become consequential for women’s career aspiration and satisfaction. By conceptualizing body image concerns as an instigator of self-marginalization and a source of action, our work advances theories of embodiment, marginalization, and careers by proposing how gendered appearance norms are reproduced or contested through self-regulatory processes at work.

Closing the Gender Gap in Financial Literacy? The Effects of a Role Model-Based Intervention

DEI
Simon Hochstrasser (Technical University of Munich)
Despite the growing visibility of “female finance” in public discourse and initiatives explicitly targeting women, the gender gap in financial literacy remains a persistent concern for long-term well-being. Drawing on the Motivational Theory of Role Modeling, we examine whether role model cues improve the effectiveness of a brief, social media-based financial education intervention and whether gender congruence moderates these effects. In a randomized controlled trial (N = 986), participants viewed an Instagram-style post on risk diversification featuring a female role model (“Anna”), a male role model (“Thomas”), or no role model. Outcomes included objective financial knowledge (primary), subjective financial knowledge, marginal propensity to invest, and retirement saving intentions. Our results show persistent gender gaps, with little evidence that merely adding a gendered role model shifts outcomes on average. Where patterns did emerge, they were concentrated in men’s subjective financial knowledge, mediated by financial self-efficacy. These findings provide a cautious reality check for “female finance” initiatives. While adding a gender-congruent role model can signal inclusion, it does not reliably translate into higher financial literacy scores or reduce the gender gap. We highlight theoretical boundary conditions, suggesting that role model effectiveness depends on factors beyond gender matching and may trigger countervailing processes.
