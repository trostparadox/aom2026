A Dilemma or a Paradox? Rethinking the Chicken-and-Egg Challenge in B2B Digital Platforms

TIM
Khaled Jamal Abed Alghani (University of Vaasa); Håkon Osland Sandvik (University of South-Eastern Norway); Marko Kohtamäki (University of Vaasa); Vinit Parida
Platform owners face a variety of challenges as they create and manage their platform ecosystems. Among these, the chicken-and-egg dilemma, that is, deciding which side to attract first, is one of the earliest and has received extensive attention. Although the literature identifies several additional challenges, these are often examined in isolation and remain disconnected from the chicken-and-egg. A common denominator among these challenges is their connection to the diverse ecosystem actors, or, in other words, to the dynamics of network effects. As network effects fuel the momentum of digital platforms, we lack a clear understanding of when these challenges emerge and how platform owners address them as platforms evolve. We draw on a longitudinal analysis of a B2B platform owner in the maritime industry, through which we identify three central paradoxes and their associated coping mechanisms. We argue that the chicken-and-egg challenge comprises a sequence of paradoxes that emerge at different stages of platform evolution. To cope with these paradoxes, platform owners rely primarily on non-pricing mechanisms, while pricing plays only a complementary role, with both sets of mechanisms evolving as the platform matures. Consequently, we extend paradox theory by demonstrating that paradoxes unfold over time and that the mechanisms used to cope with a specific paradox evolve as systems develop.

Incentive Removal, Inequity Concerns, And User Content Production: Evidence From A Digital Platform

TIM
Patrick Hallila (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester)
When users manipulate reputational incentives, platforms must often decide whether to remove these rewards. I leverage the unanticipated removal of Kaggle's discussion-based reputational incentives as an isolated exogenous shock to examine how removing manipulated incentives affects users’ tendency to produce low- and high-quality content. Using a Regression Discontinuity in Time (RDiT) design to identify causal effects and a fine-tuned Large Language Model (LLM) to classify message quality, I find that users reduced their low-quality content production by 32% following the removal of the incentives. However, the level of high-quality content production remained unchanged and may have even increased in the short term. Furthermore, users with greater abilities and stronger social reputations increased their high-quality content production relative to their peers. Overall, my findings suggest that removing heavily manipulated incentives can benefit platforms by reducing low-quality content while preserving high-quality contributions from their most capable and reputable users.

Ghosting the Bazaar: Strategic Enclosure and the Silent Exit of the Digital Commons

TIM
Basudeo Dubey (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Saurav Snehvrat
Open Innovation models have existed to fix internal cognitive limits, which lead to distributed knowledge. Generative AI internalizes this search function. It moves problem-solving from the crowd to the machine. As per the Search Substitution Theory, if these AI lower internal search costs, then firms will replace external broad search with internal machine-augmented labor. We use a longitudinal sample of 1029 GitHub repositories, which comprises 64864 monthly observations and a triple difference (DDD) model to study the data around the launch of GitHub Copilot. We find that interest has increased across the board. However, for competitors of the Platform owner (Microsoft), engagement has actually collapsed by 34.9%. Internal output has increased by 93.7%, so these firms have outrun the crowd. Firms have also reduced their internal headcount by 37.4% while increasing output and Centralization has spiked by 14%. Unsupervised semantic clustering of 43,484 human interactions finds coordination and legal challenges are now present and rejection rates are also higher now. Contrary to expectations, this has not led to increased frustration, complains have decreased by 14.9%

Privacy Regulation and Digital Public Goods: A Natural Experiment from GDPR and Wikipedia

TIM
Chengdi Fa (Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich); Puyu Yang (University of Amsterdam)
This study investigates the relationship between the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and contributions to digital public goods. We theorize that the policy may increase participation costs for anonymous editors by creating compliance frictions and prompting self-censorship, thereby reducing content contributions on digital commons platforms such as Wikipedia. Analyzing a comprehensive page-region-week panel of 2018 Wikipedia edits, we compare editing patterns before and after GDPR between EU and non-EU regions, employing a matched Difference-in-Differences approach. We document a significant decline in anonymous contributions, content additions, and deletions from EU regions following GDPR, with the largest reduction observed in content additions. On privacy-sensitive pages, the decline in contributions was attenuated, but the composition of edits shifted toward more deletions relative to additions. This pattern aligns with theoretical expectations of self-censorship, where anonymous editors reduce engagement and prioritize content moderation over creation. Our findings highlight a potential tension between privacy protection and the sustainability of digital knowledge commons.

The Effect of Information Source and Digital Exposure on Decision Bias and User Discrimination

TIM
WANXIN WANG (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen); Xuchang Zheng (Dublin City University)
We examine the implications of platform information disclosure design for user behavioral norms and fundraising outcome in digital equity crowdfunding (ECF). Drawing on social conformity theory, we hypothesize a conditional effect of predecessor visibility. We investigate whether investor sophistication (expert vs. crowd), decision type (self-disclosure vs. monetary contribution), and risk context uniquely shape investor focus and shift decision-making on open platforms. Using a dataset of 1,140 projects and over 160,000 investments, our analysis reveals that in higher-risk, consequential investment decisions, investors defer to visible expert opinion whereas consistently discounting pronounced crowd visibility. By contract, both investor types emulate the self-disclosure patterns within their own investor group. Larger equity offers further intensify the negative impact of crowd visibility on investment choice and the positive influence of experts on subsequent identity disclosure. Furthermore, we find that while greater visibility of expert investors improves the likelihood of fundraising success, crowd exposure harms project’s funding performance. These findings help reconcile previously contradictory research regarding whether investor visibility fosters or hinders subsequent user engagement and funding outcomes. We suggest that what appear as herding behavior among ECF users is preceded by a rational assessment of situational risk factors .We summarise that the dual mechanism demonstrates that the “norm” of information disclosure in digital entrepreneurial finance are shaped by user role and task stakes and can be managed through platform architecture. First, the discrepancy between the demand of crowd finance and the marginalization of crowd opinions and identities highlights the need to balance between crowd welfare and the effective utilization of crowd resource to safeguard the long-term feasibility of crowd-based finance. Second, platforms and policymakers may address this tension by engineering user behavioral norm through platform governance tools, such as tiered information disclosure mechanisms or the provision of targeted investor education in future ECF designs.
