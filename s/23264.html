Diagnosing Diaspora Startup Readiness

ENT
Zeinab Hmama (ESCA Ecole de Management)
Entrepreneurial readiness is widely discussed in the literature, yet most models reduce it to resource possession or linear planning, overlooking how competencies and mindsets evolve within complex institutional contexts. Existing diagnostic frameworks, though valuable, remain largely decontextualized and static, offering limited insight into how entrepreneurs learn, adapt, and act across fragmented ecosystems. This limitation is particularly salient for diaspora founders, whose ventures unfold within dual institutional logics that challenge conventional assumptions of autonomy, structure, and strategic intent. This study addresses that gap by examining how readiness develops among nine transnational entrepreneurs operating between France and Africa, using a triangulated diagnostic approach combining the Entrepreneurial Mindset Profile (EMP), the EntreComp framework, and qualitative inquiry. The results reveal a readiness profile that is motivationally strong yet structurally and strategically underdeveloped, shaped by institutional duality and identity negotiation. The paper advances theory by proposing entrepreneurial readiness as a temporally staged, identity-informed, and contextually embedded capability system. It introduces three novel constructs, adaptive hybridity, alignment audits, and diagnostic adaptivity, to explain how entrepreneurs reconcile identity with action, transform reflection into learning, and translate hybridity into strategic advantage. Practically, it reframes diagnostics as developmental infrastructures that enable capability formation across diverse entrepreneurial ecosystems.

Entrepreneurial Production of Space: An Investigation of the U.S. Tiny House Industry

ENT
Indunil Prabodha Dharmasiri (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Rick Hunt (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Adam K. Frost (University of Southern Denmark); Shuang Frost (Aarhus University)
Entrepreneurship research has recently begun to explore space as a product of entrepreneurial action, following developments in management and organizational studies. Yet, the role of entrepreneurs in crafting or reimagining space remains underexplored. Our study investigates how entrepreneurs play an active role in producing novel spatial arrangements and generating value. Using a question-driven inquiry, we theoretically propose that the entrepreneurial production of space can be understood through four behavioral dimensions: novelty, generativity, reproducibility, and multidimensional benefits. We empirically test this framework by conducting a case study of the tiny house movement in the U.S. residential industry. Our findings validate that spatial production exhibits the entrepreneurial behavioral dimensions, revealing specific mechanisms including assumption-challenging, technical compression, principle-based scaling, and comprehensive value creation while managing inherent tensions. Using these validated findings, we develop a taxonomy of entrepreneurial production of space. Our study contributes to entrepreneurship theory by establishing entrepreneurial production of space as a distinct form of entrepreneurial behavior and offers practical insights for entrepreneurs seeking to create value through spatial innovation.

Retirement and the Mental Health of the Self-Employed

ENT
Simon C Parker (Ivey Business School)
Does retirement improve or worsen the mental health of the self-employed? This article proposes that the behaviors and actions entailed by entrepreneurship make it likely that the self-employed will experience lower mental wellbeing after retirement – an outcome that is ameliorated by better physical health and greater education. I test and find empirical support for these ideas using a panel of Health and Retirement Survey data; strikingly, the adverse impact of retirement on self-employed mental health persists well into retirement. If workers can anticipate worse future mental health, that might explain the puzzle why many self-employed continue to work well beyond conventional retirement ages, despite being wealthy enough to retire. I go on to explore implications for: entrepreneurship researchers who study health and retirement; practitioners advising entrepreneurs; and policymakers grappling with challenges entailed by ageing populations.

The Retirement Boom: An Analysis of Entrepreneurs Selling Their Businesses

ENT
Curtis Sproul (Georgia Southern University); Andrew Garofolo (Georgia Southern University); Kevin C. Cox (Florida Atlantic University)
This study investigates how retirement motivations influence entrepreneurial exit outcomes, focusing on asking price, time to sell, and acceptance of employment agreements. Drawing on entrepreneurial exit literature, we theorize that retiring entrepreneurs exhibit distinct exit behaviors compared to non-retiring counterparts, shaped by both financial and non-financial objectives. Using a novel dataset from DealStats (2023–2024, N=1,695), we employ OLS, negative binomial, and logistic regression models to test our hypotheses. Results indicate that retiring entrepreneurs request higher asking prices, experience longer times to sell, and are more likely to accept training agreements. Seller financing significantly moderates these relationships, amplifying the effect of retirement on asking price and time to sell, but not training agreements. These findings highlight the importance of retirement as a driver of exit heterogeneity and underscore the role of deal structuring in shaping outcomes. The study contributes to the entrepreneurial exit literature by emphasizing retirement-induced motivations and providing new evidence on target-level financials and seller intentions. We also address the broader implications of the retirement boom for entrepreneurship ecosystems and capital markets, where the transfer of entrepreneurial wealth will reshape opportunities for both buyers and communities.

The Toxic Trio of Internal Deception: Processes of Entrepreneurial Deception for Employees

ENT
Tea Lempiala (University of California Merced); Taylor Fugere (University of California Merced)
In this paper, we aim to understand how entrepreneurial deception happens internally in the organization, and how it is achieved by leadership through organizational and interactional practices. We join recent research that has drawn attention to the processual nature and escalating processes of entrepreneurial deception (e.g. Garud et al., 2023; Wood et al., 2022), and adopt the employees' perspective, which has been largely neglected in entrepreneurial literature. Currently, much of the existing research has examined entrepreneurial deception as an intentional act undertaken by entrepreneurs and targeted towards external audiences, such as investors, customers, or media. However, caught up in such escalating cycles are also the employees of the failing enterprise, potentially without their own choice or knowledge. This group is heavily impacted by fraud in terms of emotional strain, reputation and financial loss while also essential to running the organization. In this paper, we examine the organizational and leadership practices that enable internal deception to continue and deepen in a failing enterprise, and how employees make sense of the organizational reality during deepening cycles of deception. We do this through a qualitative case study of a social enterprise that operated as a startup company in the technology industry and was able to attract over 100 million in investment funding prior to its catastrophic demise and the sentencing of the leadership in prison for severe financial fraud.
