Who Benefits from the Belt and Road? Colonial History and Uneven FDI Network Integration

IM
Shuo (Luna) Yang (The Pennsylvania State University); Asli Musaoglu Arikan (Kent State University); Ilgaz Tahir Arikan (Kent State University)
Can state-led geo-economic initiatives rewire global investment networks, or do historical legacies constrain their transformative potential? We address this question by examining how colonial history conditions the network effects of China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). Drawing on path dependence theory and network analysis, we argue that colonial-era "investment corridors"--comprising inherited legal systems, relational infrastructures, and metropolitan-centered financial circuits--limit the extent to which contemporary connectivity strategies can restructure global capital flows. Using IMF bilateral FDI data, we construct a global investment network and compute five embeddedness measures: in-degree, in-strength, eigenvector centrality, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality. We link these to a continuous BRI Index and colonial legacy indicators. Three findings emerge. First, colonial legacies remain embedded in contemporary FDI structures, with former colonies occupying central but narrowly concentrated network positions. Second, BRI engagement enhances host economies' network embeddedness by broadening investor diversity and elevating systemic prominence. Third, these upgrading effects are significantly attenuated for former colonies, which experience weaker gains and evidence of positional drift from the network core. Our study contributes to international business scholarship by demonstrating that efforts to reconfigure global investment architectures are systematically filtered through historically entrenched institutional structures.

When Politics Shape Risk-Taking: Political Affinity and Early-Stage Venture Capital Investment

IM
Jiawen MA (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); Jian Hu (Singapore Management University)
The study examines how political affinity between home and host countries shapes venture capital firms’ risk-taking in cross-border investment. Adopting an institutional perspective, we argue that political affinity enhances the perceived legitimacy of foreign ventures by signaling institutional compatibility and political acceptance, thereby increasing investors’ trust and willingness to invest at early stages of venture development. We further theorize that the influence of political affinity is contingent on information conditions: it is amplified for digital ventures characterized by heightened uncertainty and attenuated when venture capital firms possess greater investment experience or denser syndication networks in the host country, which provide alternative sources of localized information. Using data on US–based venture capital investments across multiple countries, our findings support these arguments. This study advances research on venture capital and international entrepreneurship by identifying political affinity as a critical determinant of stage-specific risk taking in cross-border investment.

How and When to Please a Populist in Charge: Populism and Foreign Firms’ Investment Mode Choices

IM
Michael H. Wolfesberger (Vienna University of Economics and Business); Arjen Slangen (KU Leuven); Jonas F. Puck (WU Vienna); Leah Madita Fischer (Universität Innsbruck)
Populism is a phenomenon in which political leaders employ rhetoric that emphasizes a claim to speak for the common people and delegitimizes actors whose actions are not aligned with this agenda. This study examines how and when country-leader populism in a target country affects firms’ inward investment mode strategies. We argue that foreign firms prefer greenfield investments over acquisitions when investing in countries governed by populist leaders. Greenfield investments confer pragmatic legitimacy on firms due to the positive employment effects and limited national security concerns, thereby serving the populist leader’s agenda. We expect this effect to be stronger in host countries characterized by economic malaise and home-host country dyads with higher political or economic dissent. We further suggest that this effect is more pronounced for bigger firms and less (more) for firms with previous greenfield projects (acquisitions) under the populist leader. Analyzing a dataset of foreign investments into the European Union, we find support for our main and moderating hypotheses on the country level. Interestingly, our firm-level moderating hypotheses are not supported. Collectively, our results shed light on the role of populism in foreign firms’ investment strategies and highlight the mechanism of pragmatic legitimacy in dealings with populist country leaders.

Response to Institutional Pressures: How Trade War Reshapes Basic research in Chinese High-Tech Firms

IM
Xu Li (Tongji University); Wenjia Du (Tongji University)
This study examines how Chinese high-tech firms respond to institutional pressures arising from the US-China trade war and technology blockade. Drawing on Oliver's (1991) institutional response theory, we propose that firms with high international exposure face delegitimization of their traditional technology acquisition strategies, compelling them to adopt acquiescence responses by increasing basic research and development (R&D) investment. Furthermore, we theorize that two contextual factors moderate this relationship: patriotic cultural exposure and resource redundancy. Using a difference-in-differences design with firm-year observations from Chinese listed high-tech companies between 2015 and 2021, we find that firms with substantial overseas revenue (>30%) significantly increased their basic research following the 2018 trade war escalation. This effect is strengthened in regions with greater patriotic cultural infrastructure and among firms with higher financial slack. Our findings contribute to institutional theory by demonstrating how external geopolitical shocks reshape organizational legitimacy imperatives, and to innovation literature by revealing how firms pivot toward autonomous basic research when foreign technology acquisition pathways become delegitimized and how cultural and resource contexts shape these responses.

Strategic Relocation of R&D Internationalization for Firm Innovation amid Technological Decoupling

IM
Zaiyang Xie; Liang Wang (University of San Francisco)
This study explores how firms reconfigure global R&D network in response to technological decoupling impact, and examines its effect on their innovation performance. Taking the sample of Chinese listed firms from 2016 to 2022 and our results reveal that, firms suffered from technological sanctions are prompted to relocate their R&D resources by the way of pivoting towards political friendly and innovative countries (“relation” reconfiguration logic) and diversifying across different countries (“space” reconfiguration logic), and these strategic actions ultimately improve firms’ innovation performance. Also, the effect of R&D relocation strategy depends on different contingencies, while state-owned enterprises are inclined to source alternative R&D collaboration with friendly countries, firms with stronger foreign R&D experience are more motivated to diversify R&D assets across different countries. Further research found that adopting international R&D relocation strategy contributes positively to firms’ long-term innovation growth; however, it exerts no significant impact on their innovation quality. These nuanced findings advance the literature by providing a new insight that firms can to some extent benefit from relocating their R&D resources across countries to achieve sustainable innovation in the era of neo techno-nationalism.
