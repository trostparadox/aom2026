Total Compensation and the Adoption of Family-Friendly Workplace Practices: An Unintended Trade-Off

HR
Junting Li (Tsinghua University); SU CHEN (Xiamen University)
Despite extensive research on the consequences of family-friendly workplace practices (FFWPs), considerably less attention has been devoted to understanding the organizational-level determinants of FFWP adoption. Drawing upon resource dependence theory and signaling theory, we theorize a trade-off between total compensation (per employee) and FFWP adoption. We propose that organizations offering higher total compensation signal expectations of work intensity, thereby reducing both workforce demand for and organizational incentive to invest in FFWPs. We further develop a dual-buffering framework, identifying organizational slack as a resource-based buffer and female representation in top management teams (TMTs) as an agency-based buffer. Using panel data from 2,044 Korean establishments (2015-2021), we find that total compensation is negatively associated with subsequent adoption of FFWPs. Organizational slack attenuates this negative relationship. Moreover, a three-way interaction reveals that organizational slack attenuates this negative relationship primarily when female TMT representation is low; when female representation in TMTs is high, female executives champion FFWP adoption regardless of resource availability. Our study bridges the micro-macro divide in work-family research by identifying compensation structure as an antecedent of FFWP adoption and revealing the interplay between resource conditions and TMT gender composition in shaping organizational investment in employees’ family domain.

Unequal Pay, Fairness Perceptions, and Performance: Experimental Evidence from China

HR
Valeria Galchenko (University of Konstanz); Nick Zubanov (University of Konstanz); Xiang Zhou (Xiangtan University); Wong Ho Lun (Wheaton College)
In a field experiment in China, we paid different hourly rates for the same work and informed randomly selected lower-paid workers that others received more. Compared to the uninformed but equally paid workers, the informed perceived their pay as less fair, but, surprisingly, increased their output without reducing quality. Although we provided no reason for the pay difference, a post-experiment survey revealed that workers developed their own, predominantly benign, explanations, the leading one (53%) being higher quality of the better-paid workers. We validated our experimental results with a follow-up survey of 1100 people of working age in China whom we briefed about our findings and asked for their explanations. 58% believed that the informed workers perceived their higher-paid peers to be better workers and aspired to match them, and 75% stated that they would also work harder in the same situation. Our results question several theoretical predictions on reactions to unequal pay and hint at the importance of culture in moderating these reactions.

The Diligence Shield: How Workers’ Pay is Shaped by Gender and Nationality Stereotypes

HR
Rakoon Piyanontalee (The Pennsylvania State University); Hee Man Park (The Pennsylvania State University)
Pay disparity remains a persistent global challenge, yet inequality between domestic and migrant workers is comparatively underexamined. Drawing on the research on stereotypes and attribution theory, we argue that identical workplace behaviors may be evaluated differently depending on workers’ migrant status and gender. We focus specifically on absenteeism and theorize that allocated pay can vary because decision makers attribute attendance violations differently across groups. We theorize that identical workplace behaviors are evaluated differently across social groups due to persisting stereotypes regarding industriousness and reliability. Using personnel records from a migrant-worker-intensive workplace where performance assessment allows for subjective evaluation, we test these interactive predictions. Results demonstrate support for the hypothesized moderating effects: absenteeism is most strongly associated with lower pay for domestic men, highlighting how pay inequality can be reproduced through differential evaluation of the same behavior. This study underscores how “objective” metrics like attendance can still be subjectively interpreted, perpetuating pay inequality in diverse labor markets.

Working From Home and Gender Pay Gap: The Roles of Household Financial Resources and Human Capital

HR
Jiangyu Chen (Nanjing University); Xiaoyue Wu (Nanjing University); Wei He (Nanjing University)
As working from home (WFH) has become an increasingly prevalent alternative work arrangement, it remains unclear whether and how it reshapes the gender pay gap. Building on existing theoretical perspectives on WFH and gender inequality, this study suggests WFH as a double-edged work design for the gender pay gap. Based on the performance interruption perspective, we propose that WFH can increase work–family interruptions and biased performance evaluations, thereby widening the gender pay gap. However, we further found that this negative effect is contingent on women’s household financial resources, which can buffer the disruptive consequences of work-family interference. Based on the opportunity equality perspective, we propose that WFH can expand women’s job opportunities, thereby narrowing the gender pay gap. We further identify human capital as a key boundary condition that shapes the relationship between WFH and the gender pay gap. By foregrounding pay gap as a central outcome of alternative work arrangements, this study advances both research on gender pay gaps and flexible work design.

Pay Range Disclosure and Organizational Attraction: Gender Pay Inequity and Self-Efficacy

HR
Xinyi Zhou (Nanjing University); Xiaoyue Wu (Nanjing University)
Increasing women’s attraction to male-dominated occupations has been a central challenge for organizations seeking to improve workforce diversity. Although pay range disclosure is intended to mitigate gender pay inequity and reduce perceived discrimination, its effectiveness in enhancing women’s attraction to male-dominated occupations remains theoretically unclear. Drawing on fairness heuristic theory and expectancy theory, we examined how disclosed pay ranges shape female applicants’ reactions in male-dominated hiring contexts. Through a field study, we found that female applicants’ gender role orientation moderates the effect of pay range on anticipated gender pay inequity, such that wider pay ranges reduce anticipated gender pay inequity among women with lower gender role orientation, which in turn enhance their organizational attraction. Meanwhile, pay range interacts with female applicants’ general self-efficacy to predict job search self-efficacy, such that wider pay ranges enhance job search self-efficacy primarily among women lower in general self-efficacy, which subsequently increases their organizational attraction. These findings offer important theoretical and practical implications for pay information disclosure policies and diversity initiatives.
