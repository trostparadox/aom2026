Firm Complexity and Stock Price Crash Risk: Disentangling the Role of Fundamental Investors and Spe

STR
Adnan Ashraf (Shenzhen University); Muhammad Saleem (University of Wollongong)
Utilizing the firm complexity measure based on contextual analysis of 10-K reports, we document that complex firms tend to have lower probability of stock price crashes. Our results hold true across alternative proxies for crash risk, firm complexity, different model specifications and sample selection criteria. Furthermore, our findings withstand a series of endogeneity tests, including first difference models, Propensity Score Matching (PSM), Entropy Balancing, Generalized Method of Moments (GMM), and instrumental variables approaches. We uncover a dual channel driving this result: complexity attracts fundamental investors, who engage in greater information acquisition, while simultaneously deterring speculators by reducing short-selling activity. Additionally, our cross-sectional analyses indicate that low litigation risk and firms with non-Big4 auditors experience a greater reduction in crash risk due to firm complexity. Our findings recast firm complexity not as a tool for obfuscation, but as a marker of a rich information environment that promotes market stability.

Strategic Design Under Adversarial Pressure: Organizational Slack, Governance, and Cyber Breaches

STR
Suman Banerjee (Stevens Institute of Technology); Mengfang Sun (Stevens Institute of Technology)
Why do some firms repeatedly withstand cyberattacks while otherwise similar firms experience severe breaches? We theorize cybersecurity not as a discrete IT expenditure, but as a strategic design choice in an adversarial environment where external actors intentionally target valuable and visible firms. We develop a pressure--capacity view in which adversarial pressure scales with target salience and information intensity, while defensive capacity reflects a firm's strategic commitment to resilience---sustained by operating slack and enabled by authority-based risk governance. Using 2,898 publicly disclosed breach events among U.S. listed firms from 2006--2017, we show that leverage is associated with higher breach likelihood, whereas operating profitability substantially reduces risk. Firms with an executive-level Chief Risk Officer (CRO) are markedly less likely to experience breaches, consistent with governance authority increasing the coordination and productivity of protective effort. Exploiting the SEC's 2011 cybersecurity disclosure guidance, we find that governance becomes more predictive of breach prevention once concealment becomes harder. We contribute by explaining breach prevention as strategic organizational design under adversarial pressure, identifying boundary conditions where threats are persistent and protection requires sustained commitment. Because these features extend beyond cybersecurity, our framework generalizes to how firms build resilient capabilities and sustain competitive advantage under hostile external environments.

Strategic Rivalry Disclosure in 10-K Filings: A Dyadic Analysis of Competitor Naming

STR
Mohammad Nasir Nasiri (University of Amsterdam); Ana Mickovic (Amsterdam Business School); Amin Khodabandeh Amiri
Firms routinely discuss their competitive environment in regulatory filings, yet the decision to explicitly name competitors remains a voluntary and theoretically underexplored disclosure choice. Unlike mandated descriptions of market conditions, naming specific rivals reflects managerial perceptions of rivalry, proprietary cost considerations, and incentives to shape public narratives. We build a comprehensive, publicly available dataset of competitor mentions extracted from all 10-K filings of U.S. public firms from 1996 to 2024 and manually validate a subset of these dyadic links. Using the pharmaceutical industry as a detailed test setting, we examine the structural and strategic forces that shape competitor naming. We find that firms are substantially more likely to name rivals that are proximate in product-market space, and that this tendency intensifies when those rivals are larger, more visible, or more central in the competitive landscape. Naming is also strongly reciprocal: firms are significantly more likely to identify competitors that previously named them. Taken together, our evidence indicates that competitor naming is a selective and relational disclosure practice that reflects both structural market conditions and strategic considerations and offers new insight into how firms shape public perceptions of rivalry in mandatory reporting environments.

The Impact of Economic Policy Uncertainty on Customer Dependence: Evidence from Chinese Companies

STR
HUA ZhiBing (Tongji University); Danyang Zhu (Jiangnan University)
The double-edged nature of customer dependence leads to the possibility that firms may increase or decrease the level of customer dependence in response to economic policy uncertainty(EPU). Using panel data from Chinese listed firms between 2009 and 2021, and based on transaction cost theory, we find:1) EPU positively affects firms' customer dependence, and this positive relationship remains robust after a series of robustness tests. 2)The mechanism analysis reveals that firms' motivation to increase customer dependence is driven by proactive choices rather than passive responses; in terms of the form of customer dependence, the focus is on group customer dependence, rather than individual customer dependence. 3)The heterogeneity analysis find that the positive effect is stronger in contexts where business activities emphasize Relationship-Specific Investment, firms have a lower market position, and the market development level is lower.

Third-Party Signaler: The Signaling Effect of Government Certification on Trade Credit Financing

STR
Ying Zhao (City University of Hong Kong)
Although signaling theory has expanded to third-party signaling, the role of the third-party signaler in the signaling process is less explored. This paper extends the study of third-party signaling by investigating how the characteristics of the third-party signaler and its relationship with both the initial signaler and the signal receiver may affect the signaling efficacy through the credibility and legitimacy perceived by the audience, as well as the information needed by the audience. In the context of China’s Specialized, Refined, Differential, and Innovative (SRDI) certification policy on small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), this paper examines the impact of government certification as a third-party signal on firms’ trade credit financing from their supply chains. Using data from A-share listed firms between 2013 and 2024 and a staggered PSM-DID approach, this paper identified the significantly positive signaling impact of SRDI certifications on the trade credit received by certified firms. The analysis further reveals that government transparency strengthens the signaling effect, while the transactional relationship of focal firms with government agencies and the political connections of suppliers have negative moderating effects. Overall, this study expands the third-party signaling studies and provides new empirical evidence on the policy impact of Chinese SRDI certifications.
