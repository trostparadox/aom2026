Going Through the Motions: Sacrality Drift and the Interactional Work of Resacralization

OMT
Aminat Muibi (University of Victoria)
Recurring interaction rituals are central to organizational life because they can generate solidarity, emotional energy, and a protected, affectively charged center that makes participation feel consequential. Yet organizational rituals often persist even when their sacrality quietly thins: meetings continue on schedule, roles and turn-taking remain intact, and compliance is publicly displayed, while shared intensity, obligation, and repair increasingly fail to materialize. I argue that explaining how interaction rituals are sustained over time requires theorizing how resacralization occurs within organizational situations when sacrality drift occurs. Building on Interaction Ritual Theory and the interaction order, I treat sacrality as an interactional accomplishment that must be enacted in situations and renewed across chains. I theorize how drift becomes perceptible on different timelines due to deference and emotional-energy asymmetries between participants and stewards. Participants respond to perceived drift through frontstage deference, backstage counter-rituals, and deference disruption, producing interactional consequences of order maintenance, short bursts of emotional energy, and a threshold crossing that opens a repair window. Stewards respond through reasserting order, reattunement, and restewardship, yielding corresponding consequences that range from maintaining governability to rebuilding durable emotional energy across the chain. Resacralization emerges not as a single repair event but as an intersituational trajectory dependent on repeated reciprocal uptake across linked encounters.

Integrating and Differentiating Culture in Paradox Theory

OMT
Mirjam Roswitha Langenbacher (University of Liechtenstein); Josh Keller (UNSW Sydney)
Organizational paradoxes are persistent, interdependent tensions whose experience and navigation are shaped by both micro-level cognitive and emotional processes and macro-level values, norms, and institutions. Culture should therefore be central to paradox theory: it is the connective tissue linking how actors think and feel about contradictions with the institutional contexts that render particular tensions salient, legitimate, and actionable. Yet paradox research has not developed a sufficiently integrated understanding of culture’s role. Existing work has typically examined isolated elements of culture, such as e.g., national values, individual-level reasoning styles, or local norms, rather than theorizing culture as a complex set of interconnected features aligned with paradox theory’s own commitments to complexity, process, and multilevel dynamics. In this paper, we develop an integrative and differentiating framework for examining culture within paradox theory. We conceptualize culture as simultaneously (1) enduring and situational, capturing interactions between cultural imprints and contextual cues; (2) cognitive, affective, and normative, recognizing that paradoxes are interpreted, felt, and evaluated; (3) enabling and constraining, shaping both the capacity and authorization to act; and (4) distant and proximate, operating across interacting levels of socialization and audience evaluation. The framework clarifies when paradox scholars analytically differentiate cultural elements (either/or) versus theorize their integration (both/and), and what each choice reveals about paradox salience, framing, legitimacy, and navigation. By positioning paradox episodes as sites where cultural mechanisms are activated and contested, the framework offers a richer account of how paradox engagement can reproduce or transform cultural repertoires over time.

Where to Inhabit: Cultural Sedimentation Under Persistent Espoused–Reward Misalignment

OMT
Xin Lucy Liu (Columbia Business School)
Organizations often espouse values that remain symbolically salient yet weakly connected to what is materially rewarded. Although such espoused–reward misalignment is widely documented, existing theories offer limited explanation for how organizational cultures remain stable when misalignment persists. This paper develops a dynamic theoretical framework to explain cultural emergence and stability under persistent espoused–reward misalignment. The framework repositions the relationship among espoused values, reward systems, and enacted practices. Espoused values articulate legitimacy claims, reward systems function as evaluative environments that shape attention and viability, and enacted practices reflect how organizational members respond to enduring inconsistencies between what is espoused and what is rewarded. Misalignment is thus treated not as a temporary implementation failure but as a recurring structural condition of organizing. Under persistent misalignment, organizational members confront competing evaluative signals and must selectively allocate attention to construct workable interpretations of organizational reality. These conditions generate heterogeneous attentional sensemaking orientations rather than uniform responses to espoused values. Over time, differences in the viability of these interpretations are differentially retained, centralized, or marginalized through organizational processes of participation and positioning. Cultural stability thus emerges through the population-level sedimentation of sensemaking modes that can be sustained under prevailing reward conditions.The framework extends attraction–selection–attrition theory from compositional stability to interpretive dynamics and introduces interpretive fit as a micro-foundation linking individual sensemaking to cultural stabilization.

Travels of Social Values: A Value Regime Work Perspective

OMT
Mac Nguyen (University of Victoria)
Scholars often treat organizational values as determinate and value infusion as an unintended consequence of organizational adaptation. However, values tend to be relational, frequently latent or sedimented in the institutional environment, and can be strategically brought into organizations as shared meaning. Drawing on a longitudinal, inductive case study of BA (pseudonym), a prominent Vietnamese private conglomerate, I analyze archival, observational, and interview data spanning 1993–2023 to explain how patriotism—a high-order societal value regime—was accentuated and internalized. I identify three interrelated processes: selecting carriers of value, socializing members through script saturation and affective synchronization, and converting value into obligation via social control. Together, these processes constitute value regime work—deliberate processes that resurrect, reconfigure, and transmute societal values into a coherent organizational meaning system. The study reframes value infusion as proactive and evolving and advances research on values in organizations and value regime theory.

Mission Fit: A Strategic Utility Theory of Person–Organization Relations

OMT
Lemaro R. Thompson
We develop a strategic-utility theory of person–organization relations that complements the dominant belonging-based logic of fit. Specifically, we argue that person–organization fit scholarship, grounded in value congruence, explains employee attitudes but often mispredicts consequential behaviors— persistence under ideological misfit, voluntary departure among satisfied employees, and long-run constructive dissent. We introduce Mission Fit—the perceived alignment between an individual’s identity-central personal mission and the instrumental capacity of an organizational role to advance that mission, independent of value congruence or perceived organizational purpose. Mission Fit is an approach-oriented state (leveraging) rather than an avoidance-oriented state (entrapment). We theorize a platform-utility calculus comprising access to populations, resources, legitimacy, skills, networks, and beneficiaries. Two mechanisms—strategic cognitive reframing and identity shielding—sustain leveraging under misalignment, while authenticity debt bounds it over time. Six propositions predict selective effort allocation across task types, milestone- and platform-obsolescence-driven turnover net of satisfaction and pay, weakened compensation–retention links under high mission utility, and constructive deviance among credible mission-fit insiders. By adding a logic of strategic utility alongside belonging, Mission Fit Theory expands P–E fit and purpose scholarship and offers testable signatures distinguishing leveraging from identification, proactivity, and calculative commitment.
