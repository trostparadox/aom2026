Tailor Your Approach: How Peers Affect ESG Practices of a Firm?

STR
Yang Lan (University of International Business and Economics); Heli Wang (Singapore Management University); Xiaoyu MENG (Nankai University)
Firms increasingly look to peers when navigating the ambiguity of ESG strategy, yet prior research has paid limited attention to how different peers shape ESG choices in distinct ways. Drawing on the optimal distinctiveness perspective, we argue that firms respond differently to general peers—industry actors with low product-market overlap—and close peers—direct competitors with highly similar products—because these groups serve different evaluative functions. ESG actions by general peers establish category-level legitimacy expectations and encourage alignment, whereas ESG actions by close peers activate competitive comparison and motivate differentiation. Using a panel of U.S. public firms from 2003–2022, we find consistent evidence of this dual pattern: firms align their ESG practices with general peers while moving in the opposite direction of close peers. Examining exogenous environmental policy shocks, we further show that when close peers improve their environmental performance, focal firms reduce their own environmental efforts but reallocate investment toward social and governance dimensions, consistent with strategic ESG rebalancing. Overall, the findings highlight ESG as a multidimensional strategic space in which firms balance legitimacy and competition by selectively conforming to and differentiating from different peer groups.

Symbolic Commitment: Female CEOs and Uncertified ESG Disclosure in Family Firms

STR
Junghyun Mah (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Yishu Li (Washington University in St. Louis); Chi-Nien Chung (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
This study examines how female CEOs in family firms navigate the tensions between ESG transparency and family control. Drawing on self-construal theory, we propose that female CEOs adopt a strategy of "uncertified disclosure," voluntarily reporting ESG activities without certification, to balance external accountability with internal autonomy. Using data from publicly listed Taiwanese firms, we find that female CEOs are more likely to engage in uncertified disclosure than male CEOs, especially in firms with higher family ownership. However, this tendency diminishes when firms face negative ESG media coverage, prompting female CEOs to pursue certified ESG disclosures to restore legitimacy. These findings contribute to gender, family firm, and ESG literatures by highlighting the strategic flexibility of female CEOs and introducing uncertified disclosure as a nuanced symbolic management practice. Our research underscores the complexities of gendered leadership behaviors and offers insights into the evolving role of ESG transparency in family firms.

Peer Pressure and Firm Strategic Certification Behavior: Evidence from China’s Listed Companies

STR
Liang Ping (Hangzhou City University); Zongke Bao
Nowadays, numerous firms apply for various certifications to demonstrate their product quality and social responsibility. However, existing research has primarily focused on the internal and external factors influencing firms’ application for a single certification, with insufficient attention paid to the motivations behind firms applying for multiple certifications and their certification behavior. Utilizing a comprehensive dataset of Chinese listed companies spanning 2009 to 2023, this study employs Jieba lexical analysis and large language model (LLM) semantic techniques to extract certification data from corporate annual reports. Using fixed-effects models with instrumental variables, the empirical results indicate that peer pressure significantly increases the number of certifications acquired by firms, with effects being more pronounced among non-state-owned, non-polluting, and mature firms. However, the peer pressure reduces the quality of firms' certifications, leading them to pursue certifications that are unrelated to their main business and have less stringent requirements, thereby weakening the signaling effect of certification. Moreover, heightened pressures arising from Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) ratings amplify these distortions. The findings underscore the unintended consequences of peer pressure and provide a detailed portrayal of firm certification practices, offering critical insights for policymakers to design subsequent certification mechanisms and corporate managers to adjust their certification strategies.

All That Glitters Is Not Gold: Status and the CSR Gap

STR
Yuzheng Wu (Central University of Finance and Economics); Linchen Zhong (Central University of Finance and Economics); Xiaoyuan Liu (Central University of Finance and Economics); Zixu Liu (Central University of Finance and Economics)
Differences in firms’ attention to various stakeholders lead them to prioritize external corporate social responsibility (CSR) over internal CSR, creating a phenomenon known as the CSR gap. However, the firm-level heterogeneity underlying this gap remains underexplored. This study established a link between firm's status in the market hierarchy and CSR gap. Using a longitudinal dataset of Chinese listed manufacturing firms from 2010 to 2020, we find that the middle-status firms are more likely to exhibit the CSR gap than their high- and low-status counterparts, showing an inverted U-shaped relationship between firms' status and the CSR gap. Moreover, internal and external contextual factors moderate these effects: positive performance feedback and institutional ownership attenuate, whereas media coverage amplifies, the inverted U-shaped relationship between status and a firm’s CSR gap. Our findings have important implications for CSR and status studies.

Predicting CEO Commitment to CSR Initiatives: The Role of Changing Legal Regimes

STR
Seok-Hyun (Stephen) Hwang (The Hang Seng University of Hong Kong); Edward J. Zajac (Northwestern University); Georg Wernicke (HEC Paris)
We examine how changes in managerial legal liability shape firms’ corporate social responsibility (CSR) policies and initiatives. Exploiting Nevada’s 2001 corporate law reform, which reduced managers’ personal legal exposure at Nevada-incorporated firms while leaving firm-level liability unchanged, we study how this exogenous reduction in top managers’ downside legal risk affects CSR. Using a difference-in-differences design with matching, we show that reduced managerial liability is associated with a significant decline in CSR initiatives at Nevada-incorporated firms relative to comparable firms incorporated in other states. Consistent with CEOs’ central role in CSR decision making, we find the decline is stronger for firms led by CEOs with shorter career horizons and for firms led by CEOs with more conservative political orientations. Our findings suggest that misaligned exposure to downside risk between CEOs and their firms can weaken managerial incentives to sustain CSR. These results advance the CSR-as-insurance perspective by linking managers’ personal downside legal risk to the incentives for CSR engagement and by showing how shifts in managerial liability reshape CEO incentives and firms’ social engagement.
