“I Dare” and “I Will”: How Does Leader Trust Motivate Employees’ Reverse Mentoring Giving?

OB
Qiuping Zhang (Nanjing Institute of Technology); Lufeng He (Southeast University); Chen Zou (Nanjing Institute of Technology)
Reverse mentoring is a critical initiative for activating bottom-up knowledge flow in the digital and intelligent era. Yet, as knowledge providers, employees often face dual psychological barriers: being “not dear” (due to fear of authority) and “not will” (due to psychological entitlement), which hinders the effective implementation of this practice. While leader trust is considered a vital influencing factor, its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Drawing on social exchange theory and self-determination theory, this research investigates how felt leader’s trust promotes reverse mentoring giving by enhancing employees’ felt obligation and reducing their psychological entitlement. We also examine the moderating role of power distance belief. Through two studies —a three-wave field survey (NStudy1 = 405) and a four-condition scenario-based experiment (NStudy2 = 292)—we find that felt leader’s trust facilitates reverse mentoring giving by increasing felt obligation and decreasing psychological entitlement. Furthermore, power distance belief moderates the strength of these dual paths: for employees with high power distance belief, the enabling path via felt obligation is stronger, whereas the obstacle-clearing path via reduced psychological entitlement is weaker. By adopting an employee-centric psychological perspective, this study deepens our understanding of the micro-foundations of upward knowledge sharing and provide theoretical insights and practical implications for managers seeking to implement reverse mentoring effectively.

One Breach After Another: Sequential Psychological Contract Breach and Employee Reactions

OB
Kevin E. Henderson (University of St. Thomas); Erica Waldera Diehn (University of St. Thomas); Rama Kaye Hart (University of St. Thomas)
This study examines sequential psychological contract breach, which is defined as patterns of breach and fulfillment that unfold over time within the same organization, such that earlier events shape responses to subsequent ones. Using an experimental design, we manipulated breach sequences across two time periods among 329 college students. Participants either experienced no breach, breach early, breach later, or breach twice. Results show that breach negatively impacts trust and job satisfaction and positively impacts violation and intention to quit. In addition, participants not experiencing breach had the most positive attitudes, while participants experiencing two breaches had the most negative attitudes, but declines were less pronounced following a second breach. Timing also mattered. While attitudes were significantly worse at the end of the study for those who experienced breach only later, early breach had a more significant negative impact on attitudes, but attitudes were able to recover following subsequent fulfillment. Together, these findings underscore the importance of breach history and timing in shaping employee reactions, contribute to the limited research on post-breach processes, and highlight the dynamic nature of psychological contracts, including conditions under which organizations may recover from breach.

Fixed, Fickle, or Flexible: How Followers Perceive Dynamic Leader Behavior

OB
Sten Delvaux (Royal Military Academy); Sjir Uitdewilligen (Maastricht University); Bert Schreurs (Vrije Universiteit Brussel)
Adaptability is widely regarded as a desirable trait for organizational leaders. However, effective leadership requires balancing agility with consistency – a tension that raises the question: how can leaders retain the benefits of flexibility without appearing inconsistent? Using a between-subjects experimental vignette design, we manipulated behavioral consistency to examine how followers perceive fluctuations in leader behavior and the resulting effects on perceptions of leader effectiveness. Participants read one of four vignettes, each depicting a leader’s decision-making across eight situations. These vignettes portrayed three leadership styles varying in consistency: systematic variability (adaptive leader), random variability (random leader), and static behavior (directive and participative leader). Results showed that the adaptive leader, who adhered to a decision rule, was perceived as more effective than random or directive leaders. The adaptive leader’s consistent behavior increased trust and reduced uncertainty, leading to higher effectiveness ratings. Surprisingly, the participative leader was perceived as equally effective as the adaptive leader, despite facing dynamically changing situations. Contrary to expectations, preference for consistency did not moderate the direct or mediated effects. These findings highlight the critical role of leadership dynamics – such as behavioral consistency – in shaping leadership perceptions. Implications for theory and practice are discussed.

Valuable Ally or Emerging Rival? Follower Informal Leadership and Formal Leader Empowerment

OB
Xiaofei Teng (Peking University); Yang Bai (Peking University); Hui Wang (Peking University); Sun Xiuli (Capital University of Economics and Business)
Although informal leadership is widely recognized as influential in teams, existing research has primarily focused on self-managed contexts where formal authority is absent. Yet informal leadership also emerges in teams with formal leaders, and how these leaders cognitively interpret and behaviorally respond to the emergence of followers’ informal leadership remains unclear. We theorize that followers’ informal leadership constitutes an inherently ambiguous signal, simultaneously conveying trustworthiness and potential status threat, thereby shaping formal leaders’ empowerment decisions. Furthermore, drawing on system justification theory, we propose that formal leaders’ status-striving motivation functions as a critical cognitive lens shaping their interpretations. Study 1, using longitudinal data from English Premier League (EPL) teams, shows that formal leaders perceive followers’ informal leadership both as trustworthy and potentially threatening. Study 2, a field survey of 89 teams, tests the full model and demonstrates the moderating role of leader status-striving motivation. Study 3 employs an online experiment with 357 managers to replicate these findings and establish causal relationships. Collectively, this research offers new insights into leadership dynamics and advances understanding of how formal and informal leadership structures interact within teams.

How Jokes Beget Trust: Building and Testing Theory About Leader Humor and Employee Trust in Leaders

OB
Shubha Sharma (University of Tulsa); Jason Colquitt (University of Notre Dame); Claudia Cristina Torres (University of Notre Dame)
An emerging stream of research suggests that leader humor can have beneficial consequences for employee trust in the leader. However, scholarly understanding of the humor-trust connection is limited in three important ways: (1) the mediating mechanisms that can explain the connection remain unclear; (2) existing work has utilized coarse and incomplete operationalizations of trust; and (3) support for the connection is based on same-time, same-source data. The purpose of our research was to build and test a model capturing why leader humor might relate to employee trust. Drawing on theorizing in the trust, social cognition/person perception, and rapport literatures, we identified ascriptions and sentiments that can flow from leader humor while also fostering trust. The testing of our model drew distinctions between trustworthiness, trusting intentions, and trusting behavior while employing design choices to address common method bias. Our results showed that humor led employees to view leaders as creative, humble, and sources of positive affect. Those mechanisms, in turn, predicted trustworthiness, trusting intentions, and trusting behavior. Moreover, the effects of leader humor were more positive when it was affiliative rather than aggressive. We discuss the implications of our results for future research on the humor-trust connection.
