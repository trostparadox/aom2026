The Tradeoffs of Communicating Entrepreneurial Strategy

STR
Nataliya Langburd Wright (Columbia Business School); Fulton C. Eaglin (Duke University)
What tradeoffs do startup founders face in deciding whether to communicate their strategy to employees? While prior research emphasizes the value of clarity and alignment for execution, we find that only about half of employees across 24 entrepreneurial firms can accurately describe their company’s strategy. We tested the effects of strategy communication through two field experiments and follow-up interviews with nearly 600 employees and executives in 24 growth-oriented ventures across 14 countries. Communicating firm strategy increased employees' idea differentiation from other companies. Yet many founders refrain from communicating strategy. Interviews reveal three underlying reasons: underestimating communication frictions, limited managerial attention, and a desire to preserve flexibility. These findings reveal how communication frictions might constrain strategy implementation and in turn shape strategic entrepreneurial choices.

Imitation and the Fate of Innovators: A Signaling Perspective

STR
Marco Balzano (University of Trieste); Claudio Giachetti (University of Bologna); Fernando Suarez (Northeastern University)
Since Schumpeter described innovation as “creative destruction,” research has examined how technology innovators generate competitive advantage. Much less attention has been given to the role imitators play in shaping industry outcomes, though industry evolution involves both imitation and innovation. We argue that imitators play an active signaling role: their decisions can legitimize or undermine the value of an innovation for consumers. Building on signaling theory, we suggest that the relationship between imitation and innovator performance depends on whether the imitator is a market leader or a non-leader rival with similar technological expertise. This relationship is further shaped by the speed of imitation and technology diffusion. We test our hypotheses using data from the mobile phone industry, offering insights into the interplay between innovation and imitation.

Twinkle, Twinkle, Market Star: Celebrity Success and Entrepreneurial Entry

STR
Wenbo Teng (University of Oregon); Christopher C. Liu (University of Oregon)
This study investigates how celebrity success functions as an exogenous demand shock to reshape entrepreneurial entry. Utilizing the meteoric rise of Chinese star pianists around 2000 as a quasi-natural experiment, we analyze a comprehensive panel of music firms from 1995 to 2010. Our Difference-in-Differences estimates reveal that the celebrity effect is structurally contingent across three critical dimensions. First, regarding domain specificity, the shock imposed an aggregate selection pressure that generally suppressed entry, yet the core piano domain exhibited significantly greater resilience compared to peripheral categories. Second, along the value chain, we observe a distinct decoupling where downstream service and retail sectors capitalized on the signal rapidly, while upstream manufacturing remained insulated. Third, concerning domestic legitimacy, we uncover a nuanced landscape: while the strategic pivot toward the home market proved to be a universal phenomenon, the mobilization of indigenous capital was structurally gated by regional resources. Finally, we identify a decisive "bifurcated mechanism" governed by regional development: in resource-scarce regions, the shock stimulated necessity-driven instrument trading; conversely, in affluent hubs, it amplified capability-driven music education and brand creation. These findings demonstrate that cultural icons interact with local institutional endowments to determine whether a cultural wave translates into a livelihood tactic or a strategic industrial upgrade.

Understanding Corporate Incubation: A Configurational View of Enabling and Signaling

STR
Wanqiu Shen; Wenyang Yin (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Yuchang Hwang
This study examines how incubation outcomes are jointly shaped by incumbent firms’ enabling and incubated ventures’ signaling in corporate-driven incubation. We develop a configurational framework that conceptualizes enabling as business- and knowledge-based support at the incumbent level and distinguishes signaling mechanisms at the venture level into signaling costs and penalty costs. Analysis of 57 Chinese A-share firms originating from corporate incubation reveals multiple pathways to high incubation performance. Strong incumbent enabling can mitigate early-stage uncertainty and support venture growth despite moderate signaling, whereas strong venture-level signaling can compensate for limited enabling by establishing external credibility. In contrast, low performance arises when both dimensions remain weak. These findings highlight incubation success as a configurational outcome of internal enabling and external signaling.

Breaking Barriers: Signaling Strategies of Female CEOs in Family- and Non-Family Firms

STR
Isabella Listl (University of the Bundeswehr Munich); Elisabeth F. Mueller (University of the Bundeswehr Munich)
This study examines the relationship between elite educational and employment credentials as signals of competence and leadership potential and the probability of female CEO appointment. Drawing on signaling theory, we hypothesize that elite credentials increase the likelihood of female CEO appointments, with effects varying across family firms, board diversity levels, and industry contexts. Using a sample of S&P 500 firms from 2006 to 2021, we find that both elite education and elite employment credentials are significantly and positively associated with female CEO appointment. However, the effect of elite education is substantially weaker in family firms, while elite employment credentials show significantly stronger effectiveness in family-controlled settings. Board gender diversity has a strong positive main effect on female CEO appointments, independent of credential considerations. In female-friendly industries, the positive relationship between elite education and female CEO appointment weakens substantially, becoming negligible in highly female-friendly contexts, while elite employment credentials remain significant though attenuated. These findings advance signaling theory by demonstrating that signal efficacy depends on receiver characteristics, such as family firm status and board diversity level, and environmental conditions, such as the female-friendliness of an industry. The study contributes to family firm research by considering family firms as signal receivers with distinctive interpretation logics, and extends gender diversity research by demonstrating how women can overcome barriers to CEO positions through the strategic use of elite credentials as signals.
