Team Curiosity and Performance: The Role of Transactive Memory Systems and Team Formalization

OB
Shengjun Zhang (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Gang He (Nanjing University of Industry Technology); Ye Jinhua (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Jinsong Li (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics)
While curiosity has been widely studied at the individual level, its relevance to team functioning remains largely underexplored. Drawing on transactive memory system (TMS) theory, we propose that team curiosity—defined as the average level of curiosity among team members—enhances team performance by fostering the development of TMS within teams and that this indirect effect is moderated by team formalisation (the extent to which team structures, roles and routines are explicitly codified). We test our hypotheses using multi-source, time-lagged data from 87 teams across multiple organisations. The results indicate that team curiosity is positively related to the presence of TMS, which, in turn, predicts team performance. Importantly, the indirect effect of team curiosity on team performance via TMS is stronger among teams with higher levels of formalisation. These findings contribute to the literature on curiosity by introducing team curiosity as a novel team-level construct, identifying its effect on team performance through TMS and revealing how structural conditions shape its effectiveness.

How Your Knowledge Sharing Keeps Changing My Team-Based Self-Esteem and Work Engagement

OB
Guozhen Zhao (Delta State University); Ying Chen (University of Rhode Island); Meng Yu Cheng (Feng Chia University)
Drawing on social information processing theory, we adopt a change-based, two-source perspective, which involves both focal employees and their coworkers, to examine the effects of focal employees’ initial knowledge sharing on coworkers’ trajectories of team-based self-esteem (TBSE) and work engagement over time. Using a longitudinal, multilevel sample of 365 employees nested in 72 teams across three time points, we find that focal employees’ initial knowledge sharing at Time 1 serves as a stabilizer, slowing the rate of decline in coworkers’ TBSE. We also show that Time 1 team-level resilience moderates this effect such that knowledge sharing’s stabilizer role becomes even stronger when team-level resilience is high, and that this effect indirectly extends to changes in coworkers’ work engagement through their changing TBSE. These findings reveal how early acts of a focal employee’ knowledge sharing can regulate coworkers’ psychological trajectories, highlighting the dynamic and interpersonal influence of knowledge sharing in shaping team members’ perceptions, self-evaluations, and engagement at work.

Information Use Over Sharing:Dynamic Allocation of Search Opportunities to Optimize Team Performance

OB
Lili Bao (Eastern Washington University); Shengmin Liu (University of Shanghai for Science and Technology); Hongguo Wei (Robert Morris University)
Team problem-solving performance depends critically on how information is utilized, not just shared. While research on information processing emphasizes information sharing, especially unique information, mixed findings suggest sharing alone is insufficient. We distinguish information sharing from information use, the broader construct of how team members use information to make decisions. Using agent-based modeling with an NK landscape framework, we simulate teams of performance-optimizing agents to examine how different allocations of search opportunities (time) based on members' information use performance affect collective problem-solving under time pressure. This dynamic performance-based allocation mechanism allows search opportunities allocation and information accessibility to adapt over time as performance contributions change, which closely mimics naturalistic group decision-making scenarios, addressing calls to examine how real-world contexts differ from static laboratory designs where search opportunities are usually equivalent. We compare three allocation strategies: equal (all members receive equal opportunities), concentrated (all opportunities to top performers), and hierarchical (top performers receive more but not all opportunities). Findings demonstrate that information use capability interacts with time pressure and task interdependence to determine team success. This mechanistic perspective reveals that team performance depends more on how information is used than how it is shared, providing new insights for theory and practice.

Does Giving (and Taking) Depend on Who’s Rating? Disentangling Reciprocity Perceptions in Teams

OB
Jane Shumski Thomas (Purdue University Northwest); David J. Woehr (University of North Carolina at Charlotte); Misty L. Loughry (Rollins College); Karen Ford-Eickhoff (University of North Carolina at Charlotte)
Although research in the organizational sciences frequently relies on reciprocity as the central theoretical mechanism underlying social exchange relationships, reciprocity itself is rarely examined directly. The present study sought to investigate perceptions of reciprocity within peer exchange relationships and to assess the extent to which these perceptions are attributable to individual characteristics versus relationship-specific factors. Results from a social relations analysis indicated that variance in peer ratings of reciprocity is primarily attributable to the rater (i.e., the perceiver) and/or the unique relationship between the rater and the target (i.e., the relationship). Findings also suggest that agreeableness is significantly associated with reciprocity perceiver effects. With respect to relationship effects, perceived similarity was significantly related to relationship-level variance in reciprocity perceptions. Overall, the results support conceptualizing reciprocity as both a relational phenomenon and an individual difference. Implications for aligning theory, methodology, and analytic approaches with these findings are discussed.
