Do Morally Humble Leaders Create Morally Flexible Followers? The Role Of Moral Relativism

OB
Maojiao Mei (Syracuse University); Joel B. Carnevale (Florida International University); Lei Huang (Auburn University); Yuhan Zhan (Florida International University)
Leader moral humility is typically viewed as beneficial for ethical functioning in organizations. We challenge this assumption by theorizing, from a social learning perspective, that morally humble cues can also signal that moral standards are negotiable and context dependent, thereby fostering followers’ work-situated moral relativism. Moral relativism, in turn, should increase followers’ willingness to engage in unethical pro-organizational behavior (UPB) by making rule violations easier to justify as acceptable “in this case” when they serve organizational goals. We tested these predictions in a scenario-based, between-subjects experiment (N = 404) in which we manipulated leader moral humility in an ethically ambiguous workplace scenario and assessed moral relativism and unethical pro-organizational behavior intentions. Results showed that leader moral humility increased followers’ moral relativism and produced a positive indirect effect on unethical pro-organizational behavior intentions via moral relativism. These findings qualify prevailing views of humility as uniformly ethical, identify work-situated moral relativism as a key mechanism through which leader cues shape followers’ moral cognition, and highlight a potential dark side of morally humble leadership.

Seeking Moral Help in Teams and Why It Matters: A New Lens on Workplace Ethics and Team Performance

OB
Mayowa T. Babalola (The University of Western Australia); Moazzam Ali (International University of Rabat); Shuang Ren (Queen's University Belfast); Joel M. Evans
Interest in seeking help for task-related problems is growing due to its potential to influence workplace behaviors. In this research, we integrate the help-seeking literature with research on behavioral ethics to introduce the concept of moral help-seeking in teams. We then examine its antecedents and consequences. Guided by social information processing theory, we theorize that leader humility facilitates the development of a moral help-seeking climate, leading to higher moral help-seeking in teams and, subsequently, impacting team-level outcomes (viz., lower team ethical conduct and higher team performance). Furthermore, we examine the role of collective moral attentiveness as a moderator of the relationship between moral help-seeking climate and team members’ moral help-seeking behavior. Across two multi-wave, multisource field studies, we find support for our hypothesized model even after controlling for ethical leadership, ethical climate, and general task-related help-seeking. We discuss the implications of these findings for theory and practice.

The Strategic Paradox of Narcissistic Humility in High-Context Cultures

OB
ChungJen Chien; Thi Hao Nguyen (Yuan Ze University); Emil Velinov (RISEBA University of Applied Sciences)
Although narcissism typically drives self-enhancement, leaders in Asian organizations face intense normative pressure to display humility. Drawing on self-monitoring and self-consistency theories, this study investigates the paradox of whether and at what cost narcissistic leaders enact humility. Using a three-wave dyadic survey of 238 leader–follower pairs, we identify public self-consciousness as the critical "gatekeeper" determining behavioral expression. Results reveal a divergent path: Narcissistic leaders high in public self-consciousness strategically ''shadow'' the big self—masking grandiosity with humility —to align with social norms. While this performance secures social approval (higher subordinate liking for leader), it creates internal dissonance that depletes the leader self-liking. Conversely, leaders low in public self-consciousness uninhibitedly ''show'' the big self, defaulting to arrogance. This preserves their internal consistency and leader self-liking but alienates subordinates. Ultimately, these findings reconceptualize narcissistic humility not as a trait paradox, but as a strategic choice to ''shadow'' the big self rather than ''show'' it—a choice that allows leaders to win the crowd but at the cost of eroding their own sense of self.

Humility as a Leadership Signal: Information Processing Perspective on Team Information Elaboration

OB
Jia-Chi Huang (National Chengchi University); Hanni Kuan (National Chengchi University); Peter Bo-Yu Pan (National Chengchi University)
Prior research on leader humility has focused largely on its role in facilitating information sharing and exchange within teams, leaving limited understanding of whether and how leader humility promotes effective information utilization. However, diverse information becomes a valuable team resource only when it is deeply processed and integrated (van Knippenberg et al., 2004; Zahra et al., 2020). Grounded in the information processing perspective (De Dreu & Carnevale, 2003; Hinsz et al., 1997), this study conceptualizes leader humility as a leadership signal that shapes teams’ interaction patterns and information-processing logic, thereby influencing how teams understand and integrate diverse information. Building on the distinction between information-driven and preference-driven processing pathways (Stasser & Birchmeier, 2003), we propose that leader humility simultaneously shapes teams’ processing orientations, with distinct implications for how information is utilized. In addition, we introduce team mindfulness as a contextual condition that influences how team members interpret and respond to humble leader behaviors. We test a team-level moderated mediation model using multi-source data from 106 work teams. Using reliability-corrected single-indicator latent moderated structural equation modeling (RCSLMS) in R, the results show that leader humility has a significant indirect effect on team information elaboration via team promotion focus, whereas the indirect effect via team psychological safety is not significant. Team mindfulness further strengthens the indirect effect of leader humility on team information elaboration through team promotion focus. Taken together, this study contributes to the literatures on leader humility and team information processing by clarifying the conditions under which leader humility facilitates effective information utilization. Specifically, leader humility is most effective when it directs teams toward outcome-focused, risk-tolerant information-processing orientations and when team members engage with humble leader behaviors in a less evaluative manner. However, the findings also indicate that although leader humility fosters team psychological safety, such relational benefits do not necessarily translate into effective information utilization. Theoretical implications, practical implications, and directions for future research are discussed.

Middle Managers in the Status Hierarchy

OB
Feng Bai (University of Macau); Yongliang Dong (University of Macau)
Middle managers occupy pivotal yet precarious positions in organizations—serving as conduits of executive strategy while leading frontline employees. Their effectiveness rests not only on formal authority but also on sociometric status, the degree to which others voluntarily confer deference and influence. We develop and test a dual perspective framework explaining how hierarchical vantage—viewing a manager from above versus below—shapes status conferral. Adopting a person centered approach, we identify three behavioral profiles associated with superior status attainment: humble champions (high virtue and competence, low dominance), triple achievers (high on all three dimensions), and unscrupulous bullies (low virtue, high competence and dominance). Across three studies and an initial profile validation pilot (total?N?=?1,677), we find a systematic asymmetry in evaluations. Subordinates, evaluating upward, confer higher status on humble champions, whereas superiors, evaluating downward, favor triple achievers. We further demonstrate that organizational threat magnifies this asymmetry: under threat, senior evaluators’ preference for triple achievers intensifies, while subordinates’ preferences remain stable. These findings reveal that status in hierarchies is not a single uniform judgment but a multi audience evaluation process.
