Psychological Microfoundations of Green Value Co-Creation in Climate-Disrupted Tourism Systems

ONE
Reshma Sandeep Kumar Dey (Indian Institute of Technology, Madras); V Vijayalakshmi (Indian Institute of Technology, Madras); Piyush Sharma (Curtin University - Perth); Robyn Ouschan (Curtin University - Perth); Julie Napoli (Curtin University - Perth)
Climate change influences organizational contexts and industries; however, limited research explains how employees' and consumers' psychological responses translate into collective sustainability actions. Drawing on Social Cognitive Theory, the study examines how environmental identity and climate emotions serve as microfoundations of green value co-creation through environmental efficacy, and whether climate change perceptions of self and others condition these relationships. Survey data collected from frontline tourism employees (n = 267) and tourists (n = 363) visiting climate-vulnerable, nature-based tourism destinations in India were analyzed using partial least squares structural equation modeling and multigroup analysis. The study found that environmental identity strengthens environmental efficacy but does not directly influence green value co-creation. Positive climate emotions have a strong influence on both environmental efficacy and green value co-creation, whereas negative climate emotions exhibit limited main effects. Environmental efficacy emerges as the central mechanism linking environmental identity and positive climate emotions to green value co-creation. Contrary to expectations, climate change perceptions do not moderate the efficacy-green value co-creation relationship. However, perceptions of others' climate awareness directly enhance green value co-creation, highlighting the social nature of sustainability action. Multigroup analysis reveals important asymmetries: environmental identity and negative climate emotions matter more for employees, while positive climate emotions more strongly shape tourists' efficacy beliefs. The study advances the Organizations and the Natural Environment scholarship by identifying efficacy as a key psychological bridge between climate disruption and value co-creation. It also demonstrates how identity, emotions and collective perceptions jointly enable collaborative environmental action in climate-vulnerable, nature-dependent service systems.
