When Integration Remains Symbolic: Partial Structural Hierarchy in China’s IDS Reform

Saturday, Aug 1 2026 · 1:00PM–2:00PM
Sheraton Philadelphia Downtown Hotel — Poster Wall 5

Author: Zili Huang Harvard University

Session overview

Integrated delivery systems (IDSs) are widely promoted as a solution to fragmented healthcare. In countries with public-dominant delivery, integration is pursued not through mergers or acquisitions but through policy-led reforms that seek to coordinate legally independent organizations via shared governance arrangements and administrative oversight. Yet despite formal consolidation, many reforms struggle to achieve functional coordination in practice. This form of integration – here conceptualized as partial structural integration – is increasingly prevalent in healthcare systems but remains under-theorized in organizational research. Drawing on a qualitative case study of an IDS in China, this study asks: How do organizational actors make sense of and enact integration reforms when authority is layered rather than unified? The analysis adopts an abductive approach, and data were collected through semi-structured interviews with healthcare providers and managerial leaders across primary, secondary, and tertiary facilities, triangulated with policy documents, governance charters, and internal meeting materials. Preliminary findings suggest that partial structural integration produces patterned organizational responses rather than implementation failure. Identity boundaries persist across organizational levels, power asymmetries remain largely intact, and coordination relies on negotiation and symbolic compliance rather than hierarchical command. The study contributes to organizational theory by conceptualizing partial structural integration as a distinct organizational form characterized by authority without ownership, and by explaining how identity, power, and coordination interact under layered hierarchy. For health management, the findings highlight the limits of administrative consolidation alone and the need to address relational and organizational dynamics in integration reforms.
