Generative AI and the Flattening of Case Voice

Sheraton Philadelphia Downtown Hotel — Salon 7
CAU

People

Participant: Carin-Isabel Knoop Harvard Business School

Session overview

This caucus invites scholars, educators, and practitioners to explore the implications of GenAI for writing about people, particularly in management case studies, leadership narratives, and qualitative scholarly work. With GenAI firmly embedded in academic and pedagogical practice, the challenge is how to realize the efficiencies these tools afford without diminishing narrative depth, author judgment, or the developmental value of case writing for case authors. Accordingly, the session focuses on preserving depth, voice, and meaning in cases rather than on specific tools or prompting techniques. Business school case studies are a distinctive literary and pedagogical form designed to engage learners in decision-making while shaping how leadership, agency, and responsibility are understood. They aim to surface complex organizational dilemmas to deepen learners’ analytical and empathetic capabilities. Over the past two decades, authors have increasingly relied on identifiable protagonists in cases to foster engagement and perspective-taking. More recently, greater attention to the distinctive characteristics of decision makers has raised questions about how people are portrayed and what narrative conventions teach about leadership. The rapid rise of GenAI introduces a new layer to these questions. GenAI systems are increasingly used—explicitly or implicitly—in ideating, drafting, editing, summarizing, and even generating narrative in cases. Doing so can speed up the case writing process but can also risk flattening narrative complexity by transforming rich, contextually grounded human experiences into statistically plausible representations. In doing so, they may reproduce dominant archetypes and smooth over idiosyncrasy and voice often without signaling where interpretation ends and fabrication begins. These dynamics raise fundamental concerns for management scholarship and pedagogy. Quotes attributed to or generated by protagonists may be partially synthetic, motivations may be inferred rather than observed, and fidelity to lived experience might be sacrificed for narrative coherence and polish. The cumulative effect goes beyond style to affect pedagogy, at a time of great concern around declining student engagement. This shift may contribute not only to a homogenization of narrative voice, but also to a narrowing of the developmental value of case writing as a scholarly and pedagogical practice. It has long served as a mechanism to enable faculty to engage with organizational actors, contexts, and decision processes. Overreliance on GenAI in the production of case outlines and narratives may attenuate this proximity to practice, reducing opportunities for sensemaking and learning that traditionally emerge through sustained human engagement with empirical material. For example, when scholars and case writers rely on GenAI to identify what matters in interview transcripts, rather than rereading, relistening, and engaging deeply with the raw material, they risk outsourcing nuance and meaning to systems that privilege pattern over context. Guiding questions include: • What happens to scholarly judgment and craft when we outsource narrative decisions about humanity to generative systems? • How do GenAI systems tend to represent protagonists and their dilemna, and which elements of narrative depth, voice, and context tend to be preserved or lost? • How do issues of verification, transparency, and authorship shift when narrative text is AI-assisted? • How can human authors intentionally intervene to preserve nuance and meaning without re-inscribing their own biases? The caucus will explore strategies for engaging GenAI as a generative partner while maintaining narrative integrity and authorial responsibility. Participants will also reflect on how their narrative choices shape archetypes of leadership and influence how organizations and organizational actors are understood in classrooms and beyond.
