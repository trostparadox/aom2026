The Dark Side of Industrial Intelligence: Evidence from Reduced Employee Treatment

HR
Manman Tu (South China Normal University); Xueling Yan (Sichuan University); Xiaoyang Zhao (Sun Yat-Sen University); Qiyang Zhu (Sichuan University)
Does the pursuit of industrial intelligence come at the expense of employees? This study investigates the impact of industrial intelligence adoption (e.g., robots and AI) on employee treatment. Utilizing detailed breakdowns of construction-in-progress and R&D expenditures from Chinese annual reports, we construct a novel measure of adoption and document a significant negative relationship between industrial intelligence and employee treatment. Mechanism analysis suggests that the substitutability of technology for labor erodes the workforce's bargaining power, prompting firms to reduce attention to human capital investment. This effect is particularly strong in non-state-owned enterprises (non-SOEs) and is corroborated by a parallel decline in employee-related CSR ratings. Our study contributes to the business ethics and human capital management literature by highlighting the dark side of technological diffusion in human–machine coexisting organizations. Practically, we caution against a sole focus on efficiency, advocating instead for a balanced digitalization strategy that upholds social responsibility and long-term competitiveness.

Healthcare Workers’ Job Performance Across Crisis Stages: A Machine Learning Approach

HR
Zhen Yao (Cornell University); Xinyue Ge (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Jeonghwan Choi (University of Maine)
This study examines how organizational agility and knowledge sharing behavior shape nurses’ job performance as human resource processes during healthcare crises. Using the 2024 South Korean nationwide doctors’ strike as a natural experiment, we analyze how these factors jointly influence performance across four crisis stages. Drawing on job demands–resources and conservation of resources perspectives, we combine structural equation modelling with double machine learning to estimate direct and mediated effects while addressing confounding and nonlinearity. Results show that organizational agility consistently enhances job performance, both directly and indirectly through knowledge sharing behavior. However, the mediating effect of knowledge sharing weakens as crises persist, indicating limits to informal knowledge exchange under prolonged strain. Cross-national validation with nurse samples from China and Japan reveals contextual variation across Asian healthcare systems. The study advances human resource management theory by demonstrating that the performance value of knowledge-based HR resources is time-sensitive across crisis stages.

Strategic-Institutional Tension in China’s Platform HR Practices: The Manifestation and Responses

HR
Meng Lei (Chongqing University of Technology); Julia Brandl (Universität Innsbruck); Ben Nanfeng Luo (Renmin University of China); Aidan Wang (Renmin University of China)
Digital labor platforms rely heavily on gig workers and manage them through HR practices characterized by nonstandard employment and algorithmic management. While these practices enhance efficiency and cost control, they also attract growing institutional scrutiny over social protection and gig autonomy. Drawing on paradox theory, we conceptualize the coexistence of strategic and institutional demands on platform HR practices as a strategic– institutional tension. Using an in-depth qualitative case study of a leading Chinese digital labor platform, we identify two sub-tensions: One between commercial efficiency and workers’ rights protection associated with nonstandard employment, and another between value empowerment and individual constraints arising from algorithmic management. We show that platforms respond to these tensions through integrated cognitive, behavioral, and discursive strategies, including paradoxical framing, socially responsible HR practices, corporate political activity, and sensegiving. This study contributes to HRM research by integrating strategic and institutional perspectives and by demonstrating how platform HRM functions as an active site of paradox management, offering practical insights into balancing efficiency and legitimacy in the platform economy.

Financial Fraud Exposure and Ethics-Related Value Signaling in Recruitment

HR
Winnie Wen (University of Miami); Kaifeng Jiang (Peking University); Wei Shi (University of Miami)
Recruitment strategy has been a central topic of human resource management (HRM) research, yet little is known about how firms communicate values when their legitimacy is under threat. Corporate misconduct exposure presents a unique dilemma: firms must attract talent while facing job seekers’ heightened skepticism. In such moments, what values firms choose to highlight or withhold in recruitment messaging becomes especially consequential. Drawing on reactive impression management and expectancy violation theory, we examine how financial fraud exposure influences firms’ ethics-related value signaling in job advertisements. We theorize that firms will avoid emphasizing ethics after financial fraud exposure in job advertisements because doing so could increase perceived inconsistencies between espoused values and recent behavior. This tendency is amplified when scrutiny is greater, such as under extensive media coverage and among Best Places to Work (BPTW) firms. Analyzing 88,250 accounting-related job postings from U.S. public firms between 2015 and 2022 provides strong evidence that firms reduce ethics-related value disclosures after fraud exposure. Our findings contribute to HRM research by shedding light on how corporate recruitment strategies evolve following the exposure of corporate misconduct.

Selective Protection: How ESG-Oriented Firms Reallocate Employment Quantity and Quality During Crisis

HR
Serin Bang (Ewha Womans University); Ji-Young Ahn (Ewha Womans University)
How do firms with strong ESG commitments adjust employment systems during crises? While stakeholder-oriented firms are often expected to protect employees during downturns, we argue that crisis adjustment involves strategic trade-offs across employment quantity and quality rather than uniform protection. Drawing on stakeholder theory and adjustment cost theory, we propose that ESG-oriented firms selectively reallocate adjustment pressures across different employment margins when facing external shocks. We examine employment adjustments following the COVID-19 pandemic using panel data on 318 publicly listed Korean firms from 2013 to 2023. To mitigate endogeneity concerns, we fix firms’ ESG orientation using pre-pandemic ESG ratings (2013– 2017) and exploit the pandemic as an exogenous shock in a difference-in-differences framework. Our analysis distinguishes employment quantity (new hiring) from multiple dimensions of employment quality, including workforce stability, gender composition, training, employee benefits, and compensation across job grades. The results reveal a clear pattern of selective adjustment. Firms with higher pre-pandemic ESG performance reduced new hiring more sharply than lower-ESG firms, indicating stronger quantity contraction. At the same time, these firms achieved greater workforce stability through lower voluntary turnover and increases in female regular employment. However, employment quality protection was uneven: training showed no differential effects, employee benefits declined more, and compensation growth was concentrated at managerial rather than entry-level positions. Overall, the study shows that ESG orientation reshapes how firms distribute adjustment costs across employment quantity and quality during crises, highlighting selective protection and its distributional consequences within employment systems.
