Before the Labels: How Art Galleries Organized Exhibitions in the Absence of Categories

OMT
Erwanghao Yu (Bayes Business School); Alessandro Lomi; Simone Santoni (Bayes Business School)
This study examines how market intermediaries achieve selection coherence when established categories are unavailable. We analyze curatorial decisions in the early twentieth-century European art market, a setting characterized by rapid stylistic innovation preceding the crystallization of canonical artistic movements. Using data from the Database of Modern Exhibitions documenting 3,790 exhibition records across 163 galleries between 1904 and 1916, we employ relational hyperevent models to estimate the probability that specific combinations of paintings are selected for co-exhibition. We find that galleries exhibit strong tendencies toward subset repetition: paintings previously exhibited together are significantly more likely to be bundled again, and this effect strengthens over time. This relational bundling is more pronounced for emerging styles lacking established categories than for mature styles, and operates beyond stylistic similarity, indicating that co-exhibition history constitutes an independent selection criterion. These findings demonstrate that selection coherence can emerge from relational mechanisms when established categories are unavailable. Intermediaries achieve coherence by repeating configurations that have worked before, generating consistent selection patterns without requiring explicit categories.

Embedding Meaning into Markets: Legitimacy through Value Infusion in the Video Game Industry, 2001–2022

OMT
Bryn Choi (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Olga Khessina (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Matthew Kraatz
Products in some markets are increasingly evaluated through an artistic lens, but the influence of this lens on large-scale market judgments remains underexplored. We theorize value invocation as a feature of audience discourse: the extent to which audience members invoke value-based criteria when appraising offerings. This shifts attention from products' objective attributes to how cultural frames function as standards of judgment tied to legitimacy, and how value infusion can come to shape everyday evaluation. We argue that greater value invocation is associated with higher appeal for both users and critics, with this relationship strengthening over time for users as certain vocabularies diffuse, while remaining comparatively stable for critics whose professional role is centered on interpretive work. We also examine two drivers of cultural framing: institutional recognition conferred by the ruling in Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association (2011) and references to adjacent institutionally recognized art forms such as film and literature. We test these arguments using Metacritic data. Results show that value invocation is positively associated with both user and critic appeal, with this association strengthening over time for users but remaining stable for critics. Brown v. EMA does not increase cultural framing in discourse overall, but strengthens the evaluative payoff of such framing among critics. References to adjacent art forms positively predict value invocation, particularly in critic discourse. These findings demonstrate how legitimation can be traced through shifting evaluative language.

The Evolution of Culturally Rooted Categories: Insights from Electronic Dance Music (1981 – 2014)

OMT
Eduardo Meléndez (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Matthew Wood (University of Oklahoma); Chad Navis (Clemson University)
Culturally rooted categories that emerge from movements at the margins of commercial markets derive their value from the authenticity of their foundational properties. Research largely depicts a trajectory from underground genesis to mainstream appropriation, whereby insiders construct authenticity for cultural purposes and then outsiders co-opt its value for commercial benefit, overlooking alternative trajectories that may produce distinct mechanisms and result in different outcomes. We conduct a microhistorical study of electronic dance music, a culturally rooted category that vividly illustrates an evolution from underground music scenes to mainstream music markets. Drawing from multiple archival sources and employing grounded theory principles for data analysis, we identify a group of insiders—DJs and ravers—embedded in a nonmarket ethos who construct, defend, and accommodate the category from the bottom up, resisting top-down co-optation attempts by outsiders—record company executives and established artists—embedded in a corporate ethos. We induce the underlying sociocultural and sociocognitive mechanisms through which insiders shape the culturally rooted category, enabling its partial integration into mainstream markets, even as it continues to reside in underground scenes. We organize these findings into a theoretical model of grassroots hybridization that explains how culturally rooted categories accommodate cultural and commercial interests while sustaining the authenticity of their foundational properties as they transitions into mainstream markets, offering contributions to research on market categorization and authenticity. Keywords: culturally rooted categories, market categorization, authenticity, category shaping

From Unfamiliar Issues to Commitment: Dialogic Interpretive Work in Evaluative Arenas (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Subhransu Sahoo (LUISS Guido Carli University)
High-velocity environments expose organisations to a continual stream of unfamiliar developments that cannot be readily absorbed into established interpretive templates. We argue that organisational responses to such developments are partly organised through the intersubjective settlement of novel issues in public interaction, where organisations stabilise a credible account of what is consequential and render prospective commitments intelligible to evaluative audiences that condition attention, resources, and latitude for action. We examine this process in earnings-call Q&A, a recurrent evaluative arena where analysts surface candidate problem definitions and press managers to frame issues and reorient forward commitment. Building on diagnostic and prognostic framing, we specify two routines: Recognition, the semantic uptake of audience framings that stabilises a working definition of what is at stake; and Direction, the temporal reweighting of forward-looking talk toward focal issues, signalling a shift in the firm’s projected activity and pace. Using earnings-call transcripts for U.S.-listed software firms (2001–2025), we construct text-based measures of these routines and issue novelty, relating within-firm variation to subsequent de-seasoned stock returns. We find that Recognition is positively associated with market evaluations when issue novelty is high, but this association is concentrated in episodes where Recognition is coupled with Direction rather than in episodes characterised by either routine alone. The results reveal a recognise-and-commit architecture in which managers resolve equivocality by stabilising a diagnosis while projecting a revised trajectory, specifying conditions under which public diagnostic uptake is associated with prospective commitment and more favourable near-term market evaluations when firms confront unfamiliar developments

Evaluating Without Agreement: How Conflicting Validity Cues Influence Audience Evaluation

OMT
Margarita Cruz; Yuliya Navrotska (EHL Hospitality Business School)
How audiences evaluate organizations has long been a central concern in organization theory, and legitimacy scholars in particular emphasize the role of collective validity cues in shaping individual evaluative judgments. However, much of this research examines single cues or assumes consensus within audience groups, overlooking the effects of disagreement among evaluators. In this paper, we address this gap by investigating how within-group disagreement among high-status authorities and peers influences individual judgments, and how evaluators’ regional cultural backgrounds moderate these effects. We analyze 15,058 attributed quotes from 1,900 news articles on Swiss microbreweries published between 1996 and 2020. Our results show that greater disagreement within both authority and peer groups reduces positive evaluations, with peer disagreement exerting a particularly strong negative effect on evaluators from culturally distinct regions. These findings advance legitimacy research by highlighting the consequences of within-group heterogeneity, showing how evaluators rely on culturally embedded frames to resolve ambiguity, and demonstrating that authority and peer cues operate largely independently in shaping judgments.
