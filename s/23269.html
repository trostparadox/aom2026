How the New Next Generation Differs from Other Societal Generations in Family Business Succession

ENT
Julia Süss-Reyes (WU Vienna University of Economics and Business); Kajsa Haag (Jönköping International Business School); Bart V.L. Henssen (Odisee University of Applied Sciences)
For family businesses to continue over time, new generations must accept the task of continuing the legacy. This is a significant concern for family business leaders and policymakers, as trans-generational entrepreneurship has demonstrated positive outcomes for both businesses and society. However, considering the societal level changes in job mobility and recent reports on the career aspirations of young people, the future of family business seems to be in jeopardy. To contribute new insights to succession, our research focuses on a generation whose intentions are crucial, yet quite unknown. Based on 42 interviews with next-gen family members belonging to the societal generation called ‘Gen Z’ (born 1997-2013), we explore their intentions (or lack thereof) for joining their family’s business. Findings indicate that Gen Z meets four challenges more pronounced in their generation than previously: (1) sustainability as a dual reason to stay away from and to join the family business, (2) well-being as a dual reason to join the business (to work with family) and as a repellent in pursuit of alternative life styles, (3) a tension between place attachment and desire for flexibility, and (4) delayed adulthood that postpones succession and career decisions.

Managing Loneliness Through Family Governance Practices: Incumbents’ and Successors’ Perspective

ENT
Izabela Koladkiewicz (Kozminski University in Warsaw); Lukasz Kozlowski; Marta Wojtyra - Perlejewska (Kozminski University in Warsaw)
Recognizing the growing problem of loneliness, this study investigated family governance prac-tices’ (FGPs) usefulness in coping with loneliness among managing incumbents and successors from family businesses. Drawing on insights from social support theory, 250 managing incum-bents/successors were surveyed. Analyzing these data revealed that FGPs and the ability to uti-lize the resources they provide are useful in mitigating the negative impact of managing incum-bents and successors’ loneliness on firm management. However, they do not influence incum-bents’ and successors’ subjective feelings of loneliness. Hence, this study demonstrates the two-fold character of FGPs.

Professionalization Family-Owned Organization As Successor’s Identity Work In Post-Colonial Context

ENT
Nimruji Jammulamadaka
This paper examines professionalization of a family-owned organization (FOO) from a post and decolonial perspective. It argues that professionalization can be understood as the introduction of eurocentric management practices which illegitimise native organizing practices in an alleged superior professional-inferior colonial binary. This paper examines successor’s professionalization attempts in a FOO by combining social identity theory with the colonial violence of instilling inferiority in the colonized. Through a detailed analysis of an archive that spans over two decades of the case study organization, the study traces the effects of colonial inferiority in the way it impacts successor’s identity and professionalization. It suggests that professionalization is deeply implication in colonial violence and involves identity struggles of recovering/reclaiming the native self. It proposes a three-phase model of professionalization while overcoming colonial inferiority. It contributes to literature on professionalization in family-owned organization, decolonizing management and decolonising praxis.

Relational and Temporal Building Blocks of Successor Entrepreneurship: A Dyadic View

ENT
Alisa Boguslavskaya Jno-Charles (Babson College); Patricia Josephine Guinan (Babson College); Keith Rollag (Babson College)
How can family businesses ensure that the next generation of family business leaders are entrepreneurial? Existing literature on transgenerational entrepreneurship focuses primarily on family and firm-level antecedents of successor entrepreneurship, ignoring the more pervasive, important and impactful dyadic relationships between parent-bosses and their child-subordinates. How do these long-term, dual professional-family relationships shape the entrepreneurial intentions and behaviors of successors? Drawing on interviews from 50 parent-child dyads and triads, this study presents a relational and temporal process model of entrepreneurial transmission. Coding analysis reveals that transmission effectiveness depends on both parental behaviors that nurture and enable entrepreneurship in their offspring and the offspring’s interpretation of those behaviors and of the dual relationship integration. We further highlight the mechanisms by which this dyadic process unfolds over time, from childhood through entrance into the business. Our grounded insights contribute to a deeper understanding of transgenerational entrepreneurship and successor behavior in the family business.

Toward an Understanding of “Extremist Entrepreneurship”

ENT
Kristen Fultz (The University of Texas at El Paso); Andrew E. F. Fultz
Religious extremism is on the rise globally, and extremist Christian Nationalism is growing in the United States in particular. Despite awareness of this growing social movement and increasing interest in the intersection of religion and entrepreneurship, there is little understanding of how extremist religion might shape entrepreneurship. In this study, we ask the question How do Christian Nationalists articulate collective action frames in relation to entrepreneurship within their published texts? To answer this, six texts published by two prominent Christian Nationalist social movement organizations are analyzed using collective action frames as a lens through which to categorize and understand emergent themes and directives. From this data we induct a conceptual model and a definition of extremist entrepreneurship. This study contributes to burgeoning research on religion and entrepreneurship by introducing extremist religious groups as an important context for entrepreneurship research.
