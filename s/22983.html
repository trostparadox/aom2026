Spillover-Robust Synthetic Control: Addressing Network Interference in Causal Inference

RM
Dohun Lee (Seoul National University); Hyunwoo Park (Seoul National University)
The synthetic control method (SCM) has fit itself to be a major player of comparative empirical case study research, yet its validity is heavily dependent on the stable unit treatment value assumption, or the "no interference between units" assumption. This assumption is easily violated in management, economics, and policy settings where spillovers propagate through geographic proximity, trade networks, and competitive dynamics. We propose a spillover-robust synthetic control framework that integrates geoeconomic contamination risk directly into the optimization objective. Our method introduces a gravity-based penalty function that down-weight donor units based on their proximity to the treated unit, with hyperparameters adaptively tuned via Bayesian joint optimization. Monte Carlo simulations using a realistic clustered economic geography demonstrate that traditional SCM exhibits bias escalating linearly with spillover intensity, while our estimator maintains consistently lower bias regardless of contamination severity, achieving a six-fold reduction in bias under extreme spillover conditions. The framework provides applied researchers with a principled and objective tool for robust causal inference when cross-unit interference is empirically plausible.

A Shapleyian Analysis of the Measurement and Consequence of the Power of Small Shareholders (WITHDRAWN)

RM
Zhonghui Wang (California State University-San Bernardino)
Utilizing Shapleyian analyses to study shareholder power, we answer two research questions in this study: 1) what are the measures of shareholder power in the context of influencing corporate governance decisions? and 2) how can the power of smaller shareholders other than the top two or three influence shareholder voting and governance choices? We further predict how the Shapley-Shubik power of smaller owners other than the top two or three influences a firm’s choice between the straight voting rule and cumulative voting rule, which are vote-casting methods of board election. Our predictions receive support from panel data analyses of S&P 1,500 firms between 2007 and 2017. Our cooperative game approach furnishes a new toolkit for examining the determinants and impacts of shareholder power with respect to voting for governance choices.

A PEEBI Testimonial Structure for Abductive Studies in Strategy

RM
Sandeep Pillai (Tulane University); Brent Goldfarb (University of Maryland College Park); David A. Kirsch (University of Maryland College Park); Seojin Kim (Drexel University); Evan Penniman Starr (University of Maryland College Park)
Though scholars employ abduction, there is no agreed-upon structure to reporting their findings. Moreover, the traditional hypo-deductive reasoning structure does not align with the epistemology of abduction. We propose an abductive testimonial structure, termed PEEBI, which consists of five sections in which the authors take prior knowledge and theories, establish the context and observations that are worthy of interest, identify and evaluate candidate explanations for the observed patterns, determine the best explanation and their reasoning for accepting it, and abstract the best explanation to a more generalizable theoretical contribution. Consistent with abductive epistemology, PEEBI advances knowledge in a modest, stepwise fashion. PEEBI foregrounds transparency and communication of the author’s judgment and elevates the role of the readers by giving them information to better make judgments.

Profiles of Investor Participation and Influence in Startup Funding Decisions: Latent Class Approach

RM
Kashika Sud (Masters' Union School of Business); Kathan Shukla (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
Person-centered mixture modelling approaches have become increasingly prominent in organizational research as a means of capturing heterogeneity that is obscured by variable-centered analyses. Yet, methodological guidance remains limited on how such approaches can be used to study participation dynamics within deliberating teams—contexts characterized by unequal expertise, asymmetric voice, and uncertainty about influence. In this article, we introduce a latent participation profiling approach, operationalized through a finite mixture model with mixed indicators, to recover qualitatively distinct configurations of participation among team members. The approach integrates objective behavioural indicators of participation, role-based expertise cues, and subjective experiential assessments, thereby moving beyond single-proxy measures such as self-reported involvement. We demonstrate this analytic workflow using data from 682 participants nested within 137 decision-making teams engaged in a simulated investment task. The model recovered three distinct participation profiles: Highly Experienced Low Participation (HELP), Less Experienced Moderate Participation (LEMP), and Less Experienced High Participation (LEHP). These profiles were systematically associated with critical decision-making outcomes. Participants in the LEHP profile exhibited significantly greater decision consistency—indexed by lower rates of switching initial funding preferences—and smaller disparities between their individual valuations and their team’s final valuation, relative to those in the LEMP profile. Importantly, profile membership was also associated with team composition: teams characterized by the LEHP profile had a lower proportion of female members, highlighting how participation configurations intersect with demographic composition in shaping collective decision processes. Although the empirical setting is entrepreneurial finance, our contribution is methodological. We show how latent participation profiling enables organizational researchers to identify, interpret, and link multidimensional participation configurations to outcomes while accounting for classification uncertainty and nested data structures. In doing so, the article advances person-centered methods for studying team dynamics, influence, and decision quality in organizational research.

Interaction Effects May Indeed be Artifacts of Common Method Variance

RM
Arturs T. Kalnins (University of Iowa)
Conventional wisdom in organizational sciences holds that effects of quadratic and interaction terms cannot be artifacts of common method variance (CMV) in ordinary least squares regression. CMV is believed only to attenuate such effects. As a result, researchers often conclude that they can ignore CMV when testing moderated relationships. In this paper, we disprove the universality of the conventional wisdom by deriving counterexamples that reveal overlooked boundary conditions. A common scenario lies outside these boundaries: when a regression includes both a quadratic and an interaction term involving the same primary term, and at least one of them influences the dependent variable (DV), one of the estimated effects may be artefactual. We conclude that the conventional wisdom holds only if (1) the interaction term affects the DV and researchers include it in their regressions, but quadratic terms do not and studies exclude them, and (2) neither term affects the DV, regardless of inclusion. We recommend that researchers apply standard CMV precautions in all moderated regression analyses and present multiple model specifications to assess robustness.
