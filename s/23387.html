Linking Resource Allocation Outcomes to Resource Schema Misalignment

STR
Peter Lee Altmann; Yashar Mansoori (Chalmers University of Technology)
Strategy making has been theorized as an iterative resource allocation process. Understanding the properties and uses of resources, i.e., resource schemas, influence how practitioners agree on the allocation of resources to implement strategy directives. This paper shows how everyday practices result in resource schema misalignments that escalate into cognitive conflicts about allocating a firm’s non-financial resources. Findings from a 16-month field study in a global medical technology company reveal how two types of practices shape the assessment of resources. In particular, ‘schema shaping practices’ condition how practitioners understand both the properties and the potential uses of a resource. Since resources and practitioners are embedded in functional fields of practice, disagreements emerge in resource interpretations. These disagreements become visible when practitioners with different field embeddings share their resource allocation proposals. ‘Conflict generating practices’ hinder these proposals to become reconcilable and lead to resource allocation related conflicts that thwart resource allocation efforts. These practices bring differences to the surface without shaping common resource understandings. This paper shines a light on how the subjective nature of resources can lead to a breakdown of resource allocations process and stymie innovation and strategy work.

Industry and Geographic Peer Effects and Strategic Imitation in Innovation

STR
Qinjie YANG (Ningbo University of Finance and Economics); Shengbo WU (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Delin DU; Jiaoe WANG; Cong Cao (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
The ambiguous or inconsistent categorization of peers has significantly compromised the reference value of existing studies in this field. By focusing on industrial and geographic peers and their interaction, our study introduces an integrated approach that simultaneously assesses geographic intra-industry and inter-industry peers. We tested our hypotheses by analyzing corporate strategic imitation in innovation, with particular attention to peer effects associated with the establishment of China’s academician workstations. In particular, we constructed a novel multilevel database encompassing academician-, firm-, and region-level characteristics across China’s prefecture-level cities from 2009 to 2019. Fixed-effects logit analyses largely supported our hypotheses and yielded the following results. First, 7,772 academician workstations were established during the study period. Second, the industrial diversity of academician workstations exhibited distinct regional variation, with the eastern region demonstrating significantly greater industrial variety than the northeastern, northwestern, and southwestern regions. Third, the scarcity of intra-city innovation resources substantially reduces the likelihood that geographic intra-industry peers will imitate the focal city’s innovation strategy. Fourth, geographic inter-industry peers are highly likely to imitate the focal city’s innovation strategy under institutional pressure. Fifth, heterogeneity tests indicated that academicians’ birthplaces, a city’s administrative level, its innovation vitality, its financial support for innovation, and the region in which it is located significantly influence firms’ innovation strategy imitation. In summary, we propose a fine-grained geographic–industry peer classification and provide a more nuanced theoretical lens for analyzing the complex drivers of enterprise strategic imitation, thereby deepening the research on peer effects.

Does Imitation Help or Harm Performance? Boundary Conditions and Evidence from S&P 500 Firms

STR
Erik Lang (Norwegian School of Economics); William Ericson (Stockholm School of Economics); Ivan Belik (Norwegian School of Economics)
Strategy research is divided on whether imitation erodes or builds competitive advantage, with contradictory theoretical assumptions and sparse empirical evidence. We address this impasse using text-based measures that capture latent information on imitation as strategic convergence around innovation topics. Analyzing nearly ten thousand annual reports from S&P500 firms across fifteen years, we provide novel large-scale empirical evidence reconciling static and dynamic imitation theories. Imitation benefits shareholder returns and short-term operational performance via capability building but erodes long-term profitability. Critically, we establish boundary conditions: sectoral uncertainty enables learning and amplifies imitation benefits, whereas global uncertainty creates strategic paralysis. Firm strength shows minimal moderation, rendering imitation as a democratic strategy. Our findings integrate temporal, environmental, and organizational contingencies governing when imitation builds versus erodes competitive advantage.

Middle Managers’ Shaping Technological Transitions: The Substitutive Executive Function

STR
Joakim Netz (Jönköping University and Chalmers); Patrick Reinmoeller
This study clarifies the function that middle managers play in dynamic capability theory regarding how corporations undertake technological transitions. Strategy literature on middle managers largely treats them as drivers of renewal with new technologies, but research on dynamic capabilities has highlighted that middle managers complement the CEO and other corporate executives during technological transitions . Our longitudinal case study of Asea Brown Boveri (ABB) reveals how middle managers increasingly led a major technology transition to shape business contexts by intensifying their use of historical artifacts. These findings suggest middle managers exert a substitutive executive function that extends dynamic capability theory. As a result, this study offers a better understanding of how firms can shape industries during long technology transitions. Specifically, when corporate priorities change frequently and threaten sustained technology transitions, middle managers can not only complement executive support; they can even substitute for a lack of such support.

Perspectives on Risk in Management: An Integrative Review

STR
Irina Stoyneva (Thomas Jefferson University)
In this paper I review the extant research on the topic of risk in the field of management. I develop an integrative framework that enables the extraction and synthesis of the main findings. Using the proposed framework, I review the literature and identify six distinctive categories of risk conceptualizations or perspectives on risk. Within each category, the review further explores differences in levels of analysis, theoretical frames used, antecedents, dimensions and outcomes of risk. This review establishes some future areas of research, and identifies the implications for managers. The review further addresses the need to integrate prior knowledge in a cumulative manner thus contributing to the development of a coherent scholarship. It also advances our understanding and interpretations of the nature of risk.
