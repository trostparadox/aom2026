How Green Training Shapes Employees’ Green Behavior? A Moderated Mediation Model Test

OB
Tsang-Kai Hung (National Changhua University of Education); Peilin Tsai (National Changhua University of Education)
Translating organizational sustainability initiatives into employee behavior remains a pivotal challenge in organizational behavior research. Drawing on Ability–Motivation–Opportunity (AMO) theory and a supplies–values fit perspective, this study examines green employee empowerment as a mediator between green training and employee green behavior, and perceived corporate purpose as a contextual moderator. Using time-lagged survey data from 300 employees, we find that green training positively relates to green employee empowerment, which in turn promotes green behavior. Empowerment fully mediates the training–behavior relationship. While perceived corporate purpose strengthens the individual paths from training to empowerment and from empowerment to behavior, the overall moderated mediation effect is nonsignificant. These results suggest that corporate purpose amplifies the motivational interpretation of practices but may be insufficient to convert empowerment into sustained behavior without complementary opportunity structures. The study clarifies empowerment as a core psychological mechanism and refines the role of corporate purpose as a contextual amplifier rather than a decisive trigger of behavior.

Organizational Routines and Pro-Environment Attitudes: Evidence from Eco-Tourism

OB
Sweta Singh (Kalinga Institute of Industrial Technology); Ayatakshee Sarkar
Eco-resorts operate at the frontline of climate change adaptation, yet little is known about how sustainability strategies are enacted by employees in everyday work. This study examines how organizational routines shape pro-environmental attitudes among eco-resort employees in vulnerable forest regions of India. Using thematic analysis of 25 semi-structured interviews across a leading chain of eco-resort, the study integrates organizational routine theory with the Theory of Reasoned Action to explain how climate goals become embedded in daily practice. Findings show that routines such as waste management, energy conservation, guest interaction, and environmental monitoring transform abstract sustainability policies into habitual behaviors. These routines are reinforced by positive employee attitudes, social norms, and customer recognition, generating spillover effects beyond the workplace. The study contributes to tourism and organizational scholarship by demonstrating that climate adaptation in eco-resorts is not driven only by formal policies but by routinized employee practices that institutionalize sustainability in everyday operations.

When Feeling Safe Reduces Green Action: Psychological Safety, Values, and Supervisor Support in Empl

OB
Duysal Askun Celik (Istanbul Topkapi University); Ece Yilmaz; Bahar Meric Atakan; Tuba Biyikbeyi (Kilis 7 December University); Cenk Sozen (Turkish Academy of Management)
Environmental sustainability has become a strategic imperative for organizations, and employees’ green behavior is a critical determinant of environmental performance. Drawing on Value-Belief-Norm theory (VBN), this study examines how green values and supervisor support shape employees’ green behavior and explores the moderating role of psychological safety. Data were collected from private-sector employees in Türkiye through a two-wave survey design (T1: 437 responses; T2: 292 responses). Using PLS-SEM, results reveal that both green values and supervisor support significantly predict employee green behavior. Contrary to conventional assumptions, psychological safety weakens the relationship between green values and green behavior, while showing no significant moderation between supervisor support and green behavior. These findings extend VBN theory into organizational contexts by demonstrating that supportive climates can act as a substitution mechanism, attenuating the motivational force of values. The study offers theoretical contributions to the pro-environmental behavior literature by highlighting the double-edged role of psychological safety and provides practical guidance for organizations seeking to balance safety with accountability in fostering sustainability initiatives.

What Knowing is Not Doing: Unpacking How Environmental Intelligence Translates into Green OCB

OB
ChangHan LEE (Korea University); DuckJung Shin (Thompson Rivers University); Yunxiao Zhou (Korea University); Jeeyoon Jeong (NUCB Business School, Nagoya University of Commerce & Business)
Organizations increasingly rely on employees’ voluntary efforts to advance environmental sustainability, yet “knowing” about environmental issues often fails to translate into “doing” green behaviors at work. Addressing this knowing–doing gap, we introduce environmental intelligence (EI) as a domain-specific, self-regulatory capability that integrates environmental sensemaking (understanding/diagnosing) with goal-directed enactment (planning, monitoring, adapting, and prioritizing environmental actions in daily work). Drawing on a domain-specific intelligence perspective, we conceptualize EI as a multi-faceted, self-regulatory capability that combines environmental sensemaking with goal-directed enactment—shaping how employees process environmental information, prioritize environmental goals, and plan, monitor, and adapt their efforts toward sustainability-oriented action.. Using a two-wave, multi-source survey of 366 employees nested in 55 supervisor-rated teams in Chinese firms, we find that employees’ EI positively predicts their green organizational citizenship behavior (green OCB). This effect operates indirectly through environmental commitment and green self-efficacy, suggesting that EI strengthens both identification with environmental goals and confidence in handling environmental challenges, which in turn foster discretionary green behaviors. Furthermore, organizational green capital and green work norms condition the strength of the EI–green OCB relationship, and green control practices further amplify the behavioral expression of EI in contexts with strong green norms. Together, our findings position EI as a core action-oriented capability that helps explain when and how employees’ environmental “knowing” becomes environmental “doing,” and highlight the individual and contextual levers through which organizations can better unlock sustainability-oriented behavior.
