Session overview

This symposium examines how the growing voice of lay publics reshapes professional work at the frontlines of service delivery. Across domains such as healthcare, policing, and emergency response, non-credentialed members of the public—clients, companions, advocates, and coaches—now exert consequential epistemic influence over what counts as relevant information, credible evidence, and appropriate outcomes. Bringing together five qualitative and mixed-methods studies, the symposium theorizes public participation as a double-edged phenomenon: destabilizing when it induces defensiveness and generative when carefully orchestrated to bridge boundaries and enhance coordination. The papers illuminate how interactional identities, ideal client types, relational moves, supporter configurations, and pro-social engagements condition whether public voice is resisted, accommodated, or productively integrated.
