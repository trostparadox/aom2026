How Organizational Interpretations of Stigma and Audience Power Shape Role Identities and Responses

OMT
Lai Si Tsui-Auch (Nanyang Technological University)
Prior studies on organizational stigma often view stigmatization as a unilateral exercise of coercive power by stigmatizing audiences, aimed at marginalizing deviant actors. Yet, a complication arises in disputed sectors, where organizations frequently encounter conflicting audience evaluations amid contending values and norms. This presents both a concern and an opportunity: such diverging judgments complicate response strategies, while opening space for organizations to interpret stigma and audience power differently and to enact divergent role identities within the field. This paper addresses a critical but underexplored intersection in stigma theory: audience power, organizational stigma, role identity, and strategic responses. It proposes a model that draws on sensemaking theory and the poststructuralist view of power. It formulates propositions that clarify the relationships among key concepts, including: (a) the dual-edged power of dual-role audiences, (b) the tripartite interpretive agency—normative, cognitive, and emotional—of targeted organizations, and (c) the enactment of role identities (victim vs. reformist) and their corresponding responses (stigma resistance, mitigation, and legitimacy extension). The model further posits that the relationship between role identity enactment and strategic responses is shaped by an organization’s field position, which influences how it interprets dual-role audiences’ power and mobilizes associated resources. This paper contributes to stigma theory by advancing an interpretive lens on audience power and stigma, and by revealing how role identity enactment informs organizational responses. It outlines future research directions and offer implications for organizations and their stakeholders aiming to design nuanced interventions that avoid entrenching stigmatization.
