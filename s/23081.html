Understanding the Influence of Remote Work-Enabling HRM Practices on Organizational Outcomes

HR
Susan Zhu (University of Kentucky); Thomas Ptashnik (Providence College); Michael A Campion (Purdue University); Alexander Alonso
Remote work has become increasingly prevalent, reflecting an important shift in the labor landscape. Although not originally envisioned as an empowering HRM practice, remote work increases flexibility and autonomy and is linked to beneficial outcomes. Yet these benefits may not emerge from implementing remote work arrangements alone, as complementary HRM practices that enable employees to work effectively in these settings may be needed. Because remote and hybrid work were rare pre-pandemic (6% of U.S. workers) and peripheral to HRM systems, there is limited theoretical and practical guidance on such HRM practices to support remote work at scale. To address this, we conceptualize remote-enabling practices as a distinct set of HRM practices intended to support, accommodate, and enhance the effectiveness of remote arrangements. In Study 1, we develop a taxonomy using qualitative data from HRM professionals (N = 795) recruited through the Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) and identify seven categories of remote-enabling practices. In Study 2, we test their effects on organizational outcomes along with remote work arrangement using a four-wave longitudinal SHRM sample (N = 1,191–2,063). Results show practices differentially predict objective outcomes (e.g., unemployment, layoffs, reduced hours), subjective employee well-being, organizational recovery, and adaptation to remote work. These practices have additive (rather than interactive) effects that emerge both immediately and with a time lag and are more effective when adopted early. The findings have theoretical and practical implications for remote work.

Beyond Digital Advantage: When Gamification Modality Matters for Training Outcomes

HR
Ekin Irfanli (Maltepe University); Sevim Oztemurlenk (Baruch College, CUNY); Murside Ozgeldi (Maltepe University); Erkan Erdemir (Anadolu University)
Organizations increasingly adopt digital gamification for workplace training, often assuming it is more effective than non-digital approaches. Yet, limited research explains when different gamification modalities support distinct training outcomes. Drawing on gamified learning theory and behavioral transfer research, this study examines boundary conditions of gamification modality effectiveness. Using a field experiment in the automotive industry, employees were randomly assigned to training programs employing either digital or non-digital gamification while holding content constant. Training effectiveness was assessed across satisfaction, learning, behavioral transfer, and objective performance outcomes. Results indicate no significant differences between modalities for satisfaction or learning. However, non-digital gamification was associated with stronger behavioral transfer and performance outcomes. These findings challenge assumptions of digital superiority and suggest that gamification effectiveness depends on alignment between modality and targeted outcomes.

From Digital to Workforce Transformation: a Multilevel Process Model and Research Agenda for HRM

HR
Edvalter Holz (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute); Marianne Cruz
Digital transformation (DT) is reshaping how work is organized and governed, yet Human Resource Management (HRM) research still lacks an integrative explanation of how DT translates into workforce transformation and why its outcomes diverge across contexts and groups. This paper develops a multilevel process model of Workforce Digital Transformation (WDT) based on an inductive, grounded-theory literature review of 96 peer-reviewed studies. The model specifies a process sequence in which environmental stimuli prompt organizations to implement digital technologies, generating skill disruption and triggering HR/workforce architecture realignment through managerial response portfolios. These responses reshape emergent workforce characteristics and cascade into impacts at individual, HR functional, organizational, and societal levels. Platform labor markets and regulation are theorized as boundary conditions that shape pathway strength and outcome heterogeneity. By shifting the focal explanation from technology adoption to HRM mechanisms and multilevel consequences, WDT provides a unifying framework and a research agenda for cumulative empirical tests of when DT produces workforce resilience versus inequality, identity strain, and wellbeing costs.

Employee Competences for Responsible Generative AI Use at Work: A Personal Resource Perspective

HR
Dhruv Pratap SINGH (NEOMA Business School); Anuragini Shirish; Helena Gonzalez-Gomez (NEOMA Business School); Shiva Taghavi (NEOMA Business School)
Organisations are rapidly deploying generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) across core work activities, yet employees are often expected to use these systems responsibly without clear guidance on what such competence entails. This gap is already visible in practice, as employee misuse of GenAI has resulted in data leaks, biased decisions, and the submission of unreliable AI-generated content in high-stakes organisational contexts. While HRM research has examined GenAI adoption and governance, it has paid limited attention to the employee competences required for responsible GenAI use in everyday work. In this study, we develop an employee-centred account of responsible GenAI use. Drawing on holistic competence theory and the Job Demands– Resources framework, we conceptualise Responsible Generative AI Competences (RGAIC) as a multidimensional personal resource encompassing cognitive, functional, social, meta-learning, and ethical dimensions. We empirically ground this construct through a multi-stage research design with working professionals. Our findings show that GenAI-related training practices strengthen RGAIC, which in turn predicts GenAI-enabled job engagement. RGAIC explains variance beyond AI literacy and general professional competence, highlighting the limits of existing approaches that focus narrowly on technical skills or awareness. By centring employee competence, this study redirects HRM scholarship toward the lived realities of GenAI-enabled work and offers a foundation for research on responsible human–AI collaboration, workforce development, and risk mitigation as GenAI becomes embedded in organisational life.

Telework and Perceived Organizational Justice - A Multilevel Analysis

HR
Philip Gubernator (Helmut Schmidt University - University of the Federal Armed Forces of Hamburg); Sven Hauff (Helmut Schmidt University - University of the Federal Armed Forces of Hamburg)
Telework alters structural and social conditions that may shape employees' perceived organizational justice. Drawing on the Job Demands-Resource model, we examine reduced visibility and communication difficulties as key mechanisms, and justice-oriented leadership as a contextual compensatory resource, differentiating contextual, situational, and chronic exposure effects. Two-wave survey data were collected from employees in Germany (N = 1,673). We combine SEM comparing full-time teleworkers and non-teleworkers (N = 734) across stable contexts, and multilevel SEM among hybrid workers (N = 939) to analyze situational differences between telework and office days and chronic exposure effects of telework intensity. Our results show that within hybrid workers, POJ was lower on telework days than on office days, supporting a negative situational effect of telework. In contrast, employees in high-intensity telework reported higher POJ than non-teleworkers, indicating a contextual fairness penalty for non-teleworkers. Telework intensity was unrelated to POJ and communication difficulties. Communication difficulties were strongly associated with lower POJ at contextual and between-person levels, but did not mediate telework effects. JOL did not moderate telework intensity effects, but showed a robust positive main effect on POJ.
