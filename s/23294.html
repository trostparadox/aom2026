Approaching Grand Challenges Across Scales Through Orchestrated Bricolage

ENT
Haitao Yu (University of Macau); Tianjiao Xu (Tilburg University)
We examine how an under-resourced organization confronts grand challenges by navigating the persistent tension between scaling up across space and scaling deep within local communities. Through a decade-long case study of TFF, we show that TFF simultaneously achieved geographic expansion and deep local embeddedness through what we conceptualize as orchestrated bricolage—a multi-level process in which individual acts of bricolage by distinct organizational actors are coordinated into a mutually reinforcing system of collective action. Our findings contribute to the literature on grand challenges by demonstrating how global problems can be addressed through distributed, locally grounded experimentation without relying solely on large-scale, top-down solutions. TFF’s experience illustrates how juxtaposing scales—linking situated local knowledge with global coordination—enables grassroots organizations to respond to uneven local manifestations of system-wide problems. In doing so, the study highlights the importance of organizational designs that allow for experimentation across space while maintaining coherence over time. The study also advances research on entrepreneurial bricolage in several important ways. While prior work has emphasized bricolage as a survival-oriented or early-stage strategy that may hinder long-term growth, we show that bricolage need not trap organizations in cycles of improvisation and fragmentation. Instead, when bricolage is orchestrated across organizational levels, it can generate scalability. This reconceptualization views bricolage not as a temporary workaround, but as a dynamic, multi-level process that can sustain growth over extended periods.

Exploring the Effect of Entrepreneurial Phronesis on Personal Initiative in Non-WEIRD Context

ENT
Michael Asiedu Gyensare (University of Kent); Joseph Amankwah-Amoah (Durham University); Maria Elisavet Balta (University of Kent); Stephen E. Lanivich (The University of Memphis); George Oppong Appiagyei Ampong
This study advances the resource mobilisation theory and bricolage literature through the development and examination of a moderated mediation model of entrepreneurial phronesis on personal initiative. By advancing the concept of entrepreneurial phronesis and empirically verifying it with three-wave data from 301 entrepreneurs in a non-WEIRD context, we found support for the mediating mechanism of bricolage in the positive relationship between phronesis and personal initiative. Resource mobilisation self-efficacy was enacted and found to strengthen the relationship between bricolage and personal initiative. Accordingly, this study contributes to the literature on cognition and entrepreneurial behaviour by identifying resourcefulness as the mechanism that transmits the beneficial effects of entrepreneurial phronesis on personal initiative. We will later discuss the implications for theory and practice, as well as the limitations and potential avenues for future research.

Making Do or Building Up? A Meta-Analysis of Bricolage, Resource Accumulation, and Firm Performance

ENT
Mehrsa Ehsani (University of Calgary); Hadi Fariborzi (Mount Royal University); Mohammad Keyhani (University of Calgary); Piers Steel (University of Calgary)
Research on entrepreneurial bricolage has expanded rapidly, yet cumulative evidence regarding its performance implications remains unsettled. While many studies portray bricolage as a source of adaptability and advantage under resource constraints, others report null or negative associations, raising questions about when and how bricolage contributes to entrepreneurial outcomes and whether its effects are distinct from related strategic constructs. We address these issues through a comprehensive meta-analysis of 202 independent samples encompassing approximately 55,000 firms. Correcting for sampling and measurement error, we find that entrepreneurial bricolage is, on average, positively associated with firm performance. However, this average effect masks substantial heterogeneity. Analyses of variance and prediction intervals indicate that bricolage is systematically associated with greater dispersion in performance outcomes, particularly in resource-constrained contexts. This pattern reflects the underlying mechanisms of bricolage, localized recombination, improvisational problem solving, and uneven learning, which generate both high-performing solutions and fragile or unsustainable outcomes. Meta-analytic structural equation modeling further shows that bricolage retains an independent association with performance when entrepreneurial orientation and opportunity identification are considered simultaneously, while also functioning as a behavioral mechanism through which strategic posture and opportunity beliefs are enacted under constraint. By shifting attention from mean effects to performance distributions, this study reconciles previously mixed findings and resolves concerns about the redundancy of bricolage relative to adjacent constructs. The results advance entrepreneurship theory by positioning bricolage as a variance-generating mode of entrepreneurial action and provide a more precise foundation for understanding value creation under persistent resource scarcity and uncertainty.

Using the Materials at Hand: A Citation Context Review of Entrepreneurial Bricolage

ENT
Carlos David Valladares (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Marc H. Anderson (Iowa State University)
Baker and Nelson (2005) defined entrepreneurial bricolage as “making do by applying combinations of the resources at hand to new problems and opportunities,” and their article is the seminal work on this important topic. We present a citation context analysis of 900 citations to Baker and Nelson (2005) that appear in 376 journal articles published in 12 top management and entrepreneurship journals. We categorize each citation context – the specific passage or statement in the article that contains the citation – into one of 29 specific categories within eight broader areas that represent the content citing authors used in their arguments. We find that more than one-quarter (27%) of the citation contexts refer to the idea that entrepreneurs operate in resource-constrained environments. Another 10% of the citation contexts present the definition of bricolage, either in a direct quote or paraphrased. A smaller number of contexts concerned the distinction between parallel and selective bricolage and their nature. We also examined the extent to which citation contexts were critical of the assertions and ideas in Baker and Nelson, and those citation contexts that extended their ideas in novel and interesting ways. Our analysis presents a fuller view of the contribution this foundational article has made to management studies.

Who Gets to Make Do: Culture, Status and Stakeholder Appraisals of Bricolage

ENT
Jaume Villanueva (Rutgers Business School); Erin Powell (North Carolina State University); Ana Maria Bojica (University of Granada); Thomas Joseph Zagenczyk (North Carolina State University); Ted Baker (Rutgers University)
We argue that founders who engage in bricolage influence stakeholder assessments of various aspects of their ventures negatively (via the Inappropriateness Effect) and positively (via the Underdog Effect. Results from two experiments show that when founders engage in bricolage and have high social status in high power distance cultures, their choice (1) does not match the behavioral expectations of angel investors; (2) makes the opportunity presented to angel investors less appealing; and (3) makes their ventures seem less prestigious to prospective employees. Our work provides a starting point for considering how bricolage is assessed by external stakeholders.
