Economic Policy Uncertainty as a Double-Edged Sword: Evidence from Startups’ Survival and Growth

STR
Ruilu Yang (University of Science and Technology of China); Weiliang Zhang (University of Science and Technology of China); Yiwen Zhong (University of Pittsburgh); Joseph T. Mahoney (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Government policies have a profound influence on startups. While prior studies have extensively examined the effects of isolated policy events, relatively limited research has considered how overall uncertainty in the policy environment shapes entrepreneurial survival and growth. Using a unique dataset and large language model techniques, we develop a novel text-based measure of economic policy uncertainty (EPU) and uncover a theoretical tension: EPU negatively affects startup survival while positively influencing startup growth. Our mechanism tests suggest that this tension arises because EPU makes it more difficult for startups to acquire critical resources, thereby undermining survival. At the same time, EPU creates new opportunities, along with the resources released by failed startups that exit the market, increasing the likelihood that surviving startups achieve growth. We also find that these effects are contingent on the development of local market institutions.

Rethinking Startup Performance Management Systems: Processual Approach to Measurement in Uncertain

STR
Luiz Guilherme Rodrigues Antunes (Federal University of Itajubá); Hamilton Luiz Correa (University of Sao Paulo); Rafael de Freitas de Souza (University of São Paulo); Davi Acácio Lopes Silveira Sarmento (Federal University of Itajubá); Andrea Aparecida da Costa Mineiro (Universidade Federal de Itajubá - UNIFEI); Rodolfo Ribeiro (University of São Paulo)
Startups operate in highly uncertain environments in which performance management systems must continuously adapt to shifting market, technological, and organisational conditions. However, dominant approaches to performance measurement remain grounded in static causal models that overlook how such systems evolve across a venture’s developmental trajectory. We develop a processual and adaptive model of startup performance management that explains how assessment practices are reconfigured over time through critical temporal milestones. Drawing on a multidimensional process perspective and dynamic capabilities theory, we conceptualise performance management not as a stable design choice but as an evolving organisational capability shaped by strategic adaptation. We employ a mixed-methods research design combining in-depth interviews with founders of Brazilian unicorns and a structured forum with scaleups and deep-tech ventures. Using Gioia-based qualitative analysis together with t-tests and Monte Carlo simulations, we identify six evolutionary phases in startup performance systems—accelerated growth, initial refinement, transformative peak, continuous growth, residual disruption, and stabilisation. These phases are triggered by milestones such as changes in market conditions, increasing product complexity, and the entry of institutional investors, which progressively formalise and integrate performance practices. Our model advances theory by introducing a temporal lens to performance management in entrepreneurial firms and extending dynamic capabilities research into the microfoundations of measurement systems. Practically, it offers founders a roadmap for aligning metrics and managerial routines with each stage of venture growth.

Fast and Furious: Managing Speed and Task Complexity at a Luxury Sports Car Company

STR
Can Alice Wang (University of Michigan); Yue Maggie Zhou (University of Michigan); Sendil Ethiraj (London Business School); Stefano Turconi (London Business School)
This paper examines how task complexity introduced by customization influences productivity. We propose that customization often introduces a bundle of new tasks, increasing task variability and thus reducing productivity. While task re-bundling may serve as a potential solution, it faces tighter constraints when the new tasks introduced by customization are more interdependent, because it creates a trade-off between within-module task variability and between-module task interdependence. Using proprietary assembly line data from a luxury sports car manufacturer covering approximately 21,000 cars between 2023 and 2025, we find that task variability introduced by customization is associated with longer end-of-line (EOL) time. In addition, controlling for task variability, we further show that customization that introduce interdependent tasks is also associted with worse productivity, and such effect is stronger when these tasks are performed by multiple assembly stations. Together, our findings provide large-scale, fine-grained evidence on the task-level mechanisms through which customization influences productivity.

A Bid Strategy for Earlier Payment: Evidence and Implications of Front-End Loading in Procurement

STR
Justin Marion (University of California Santa Cruz); Benjamin Rosa (University of Michigan)
Liquidity management is key in industries with variable cash flows. We study how businesses in the highway construction industry manage cash flow by strategically bidding more on work with an earlier payout---a practice known as front-end loading. We find that small contractors, infrequent bidders, and Disadvantaged Business Enterprises front-end load more intensely, indicating a stronger preference for earlier payment. An equilibrium bidding model highlights a novel pathway through which this preference leads to a disadvantage in procurement and its managerial and policy implications.

Parsimonious Theorising

STR
Diego Jannace (Bocconi University); Saeid Kazemi (Bocconi University)
This paper advances the theory-based view by formalizing entrepreneurial “theories of value” as Bayesian networks and modelling parsimonious theorising under bounded rationality. Entrepreneurs choose theory complexity to maximize expected prior belief gains from non-redundant causal links net of cognitive costs. The resulting optimal complexity rises with problem novelty (theory value potential), cognitive capability, and domain knowledge, and decreases with redundancy among candidate causes. Our framework microfounds the architectural properties of novelty and simplicity, unifying them as parsimony; delivers testable comparative statics; and offers practical guidance for structuring, pruning, and sequencing theory-driven experimentation. We outline empirical strategies to operationalize complexity, redundancy, and belief updating, enabling cumulative evidence on when and how parsimonious theorising creates competitive advantage.
