Consulting Without Consultants: How University Social Responsibility Organizes Grand Challenge Work

MC
Jau-Rong Chen (Ming Chi University of Technology); Rung Wei Po (Ming Chi University of Technology)
Organizations increasingly rely on consulting to address complex and persistent grand challenges. Yet existing management consulting research remains largely centered on professional consultants and corporate clients, offering limited insight into how consulting unfolds in hybrid, non-corporate settings. Addressing this gap, this study explores how consulting occurs without professional consultants by reconceptualizing University Social Responsibility (USR) as an emergent organizing process through which problem-solving for grand challenges is enacted. Drawing on a longitudinal qualitative case study of a university-led USR initiative, this research analyzes data collected over a three-year period (2023-2025) through participant observation, semi-structured interviews, and archival materials. Adopting a process perspective, the study traces how universities, students, and community stakeholders collectively organize problem-solving through iterative cycles of problem framing, role negotiation, and solution development. The findings suggest that, in the absence of formal consulting contracts or professional consultants, consulting work is conducted through sustained, trust-based interactions rather than short-term, efficiency-driven interventions. Students emerge as boundary-spanning junior consultants, mediating between academic, community, and institutional logics while facilitating coordination and capability development across stakeholders. Value creation unfolds gradually through ongoing stakeholder co-creation, as social, cultural, and economic objectives are continuously aligned and reconfigured over time. By conceptualizing consulting as an organizing process rather than a discrete service, this study extends management consulting research beyond corporate contexts and demonstrates how problem-solving for grand challenges can be organized in university–community collaborations. More broadly, the findings contribute to research on process studies, hybrid organizing, and engaged scholarship by illuminating how consulting practices support the development of capabilities, stakeholder alignment, and sustainable value creation in complex social settings. Keywords: University Social Responsibility (USR); Management Consulting; Consulting as Process; Hybrid Organizing; Boundary-Spanning Roles; Grand Challenges

From Lecture-Based Pedagogy to Interactive Pedagogy. An Existential Transition for Education in MC

MC
Laurent Cappelletti (CNAM); Soleil Farah (University of Balamand); Yasmine Medhat Elhenawy (The American University in Cairo)
In the age of generative AI, the thesis defended within the article posits that academics will engage in interactive approaches to teaching or they will cease to exist. Moreover, it argues that digital technology and artificial intelligence, when utilized effectively, will facilitate this critical transition from traditional magisterial pedagogy to interactive methodologies. To this end, the article initially delineates the general characteristics of interactive pedagogy and the heightened quality it affords regarding knowledge acquisition. Subsequently, it explicates the distinguishing epistemological principles that the techniques of interactive pedagogy, whether digital or physical, must incorporate. Finally, to exemplify the mentioned points, it recounts a teaching experience of the author in MC, who repurposed the time saved from lecture-based pedagogy, facilitated by generative AI, into interactive pedagogy, thereby enhancing the epistemological value added to the educational process.

Patchy Synthesis, Brittle Advice: Temporal, Thematic, and Geographic Gaps in Systematic Reviews

MC
Oskar Kosch (Krakow University of Economics); Marek Szarucki
Management scholarship now operates in a high-volume synthesis era, with systematic reviews (SRs) becoming routine and approaching one thousand publications per year in the early 2020s. Yet the synthesis layer remains uneven across time, topic, and geography—an issue with direct implications for evidence-based management and for management consulting as a key intermediary translating research into organizational decisions. We provide a large-scale map of SRs by analyzing 8,018 reviews (2002–2025) in the Web of Science “6.3 Management” category using bibliometric, statistical, and language model–assisted techniques. Results show that SRs rely on newer references than management publications in general, but still systematically underrepresent the newest 1–3 years of research due to indexing and production lags, which reduces their value in high-velocity decision contexts. Thematic patterns are strongly concentrated in innovation, strategy, digital communication, and environmental management, while other domains—including management consulting—remain sparsely synthesized. Geographically, synthesis production is globally distributed but concentrated in a limited set of national research systems, with substantial cross-country variation in international collaboration. Together, these gaps create predictable risks for consulting and evidence use: blind spots where decision-critical questions lack synthesis, overconfidence where repeated reviews amplify familiar areas, and misapplied generalizations where boundary conditions and context alignment are weak. We propose pragmatic checks for responsible advisory use of evidence (recency, transferability, boundary conditions, triangulation) and outline an agenda for more decision-relevant synthesis that discusses timeliness, contextual fit, and targeted investment in under-synthesized domains.

Interdisciplinary Place Leadership: Cross-Sector Coalitions for Regional Economic Development

MC
Cheng Kidd Sun (Western Michigan University)
Regions face intertwined economic, social, and environmental challenges that are deeply rooted in place. In response, governments, businesses, nonprofits, universities, and civic groups increasingly form cross-sector coalitions to design and implement place-based development strategies. Yet coalition outcomes remain uneven. Some coalitions sustain a shared vision, coordinate investment, and reshape institutional arrangements over time, while others stall or dissolve without shifting underlying trajectories. This semi-systematic literature review argues that a key reason for this variation is that coalitions’ integrative capacity, which is their ability to organize, govern, and institutionalize interdisciplinary knowledge integration as part of place leadership. Collaborative governance scholarship explains coalition design and process; place leadership scholarship foregrounds territorial embedding, institutional history, and distributed agency; and interdisciplinarity scholarship clarifies how competing problem frames and standards of evidence are negotiated and combined. Synthesizing these traditions, the review identifies a recurring “epistemic black box” in explanations of coalition performance and advances a focused research agenda on problem framing, legitimacy and power, integration practices, boundary-spanning roles and objects, institutionalization, and development outcomes. The review contributes an integrative framing of interdisciplinary place leadership and offers an agenda for future research on how coalitions translate diverse knowledge into durable place-based change. Keywords: Cross-sector coalitions; Place leadership; Collaborative governance; Interdisciplinarity; Knowledge integration; Regional economic development

From Frameworks to Field Impact: Student Consulting Deliverables and Partner Value in Carshare

MC
Rida Elias (California State University-Dominguez Hills); Bassam Farah (Loyola Marymount University); Kamal Hamdan (California State University-Dominguez Hills)
Affordable mobility is a complex, multi-stakeholder challenge in which equity objectives interact with behavioral adoption frictions, operational constraints, and interorganizational governance. This paper conceptualizes CREST (pseudonym), a university–community carshare strategy challenge, as a consulting translation system: a structured process through which novice consultants (student teams) convert codified strategy frameworks into recommendations that external stakeholders evaluate as credible and decision-useful. Building on knowledge-translation and boundary-object perspectives, we develop a CREST Consulting Translation Model in which translation infrastructure (templates, milestones, iterative feedback, and external judging) shapes artifact translation quality, which in turn predicts external legitimacy signals and partner-perceived value in a public-interest setting. Empirically, we analyze two semesters of implementations (Spring 2025 and Fall 2025; 26 team deliverables) using the standardized judging rubric and qualitative judge comments from end-of-semester presentations. Results show that evaluators distinguish framework coverage from decision usefulness and place a premium on feasibility packaging, stakeholder- and user-grounding, evidence-based justification, and professional delivery under questioning. We discuss implications for management consulting scholarship on how legitimacy is constituted in novice consulting systems and provide a replicable consulting-lab architecture for mobility-equity collaborations.
