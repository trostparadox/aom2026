Beyond the Western Lens: Rethinking Entrepreneurship Theory for the Arab World (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Petya Koleva (Heriot-Watt University)
This study examines the conceptualization of entrepreneurship in the Arab world, challenging traditional Western models that prioritize individual autonomy, commercial opportunity, and technological innovation. Drawing on Schumpeterian innovation theory, the resource-based view (RBV), and institutional theory, the study explores how entrepreneurial activity in the Arab world is shaped by relational factors such as familial obligations, community needs, and religious duties. It argues that Western frameworks of entrepreneurship, which emphasize linearity in the innovation process and the pursuit of individualistic opportunities, fail to fully capture the complexities of entrepreneurial behaviour in Arab societies. Instead, entrepreneurial trajectories in these settings are often influenced by bureaucratic hurdles, political gatekeeping, and unpredictable regulatory environments. The study supports its position through a series of propositions, which have been used to develop an indigenous entrepreneurial framework grounded in the unique contextual and institutional specifics of Arab entrepreneurship. In so doing, the study contributes to a deeper understanding of entrepreneurship in the Arab world by reconceptualizing opportunity, innovation, and the role of institutional factors in shaping entrepreneurial outcomes.

Customer Co-Creation and the Entrepreneurship Process of Counterfeit Ventures

ENT
Muhammad Aftab Alam (Edith Cowan University); Muhammad Abid (Charles Sturt University); Erik Lundmark (Macquarie University)
This paper presents a dynamic process model of the interlocking activities between customers and informal entrepreneurs as they co-innovate counterfeit products in impoverished markets. Such informal ventures are shown to depend on active customer involvement throughout the entrepreneurship process, on legitimacy within the local community, and on knowledge of the low-income customers with high brand aspirations. Drawing on the entrepreneurship and marketing literature and empirical data from 23 informal ventures in Pakistan and their customers (45 interviews and 15 member checks), we highlight unconventional modes of customer involvement as market informants, adaptive collaborators, and symbolic catalysts, and explain how these customers not only co-innovate counterfeit products but also develop markets for them in impoverished communities.

Opportunity or Threat? Exploring the Influence of Country Risk on Entrepreneurship

ENT
Wenjing Ye (University of Science and Technology of China); Wenyang Gao (University of Science and Technology of China); Xiujuan Wang (Nanjing Audit University)
To address inconsistencies in existing literature, this study, grounded in real options theory (ROT), explores the complex relationship between country risk and entrepreneurship. We contribute to the literature on the contextual determinants of entrepreneurship by analyzing cross-country data from 99 countries between 2001 and 2021. We show an inverted U-shaped relationship between country risk and entrepreneurship: low levels of risk create opportunities and stimulate entrepreneurship, whereas high levels of risk pose threats and suppress entrepreneurship. Our findings illustrate that entrepreneurial support system, including entrepreneurial policy support, education level, and information and communication technologies (ICT), moderates the relationship between country risk and entrepreneurship, which serves as an adaptable risk mitigation tool to maximizes the upside potential of country risk. We discuss the implications for theory and for proentrepreneurship policy.

The Art of Rituals and Religion’s Power: Revisiting Malinowski's Anthropology for Entrepreneurs

ENT
Laurenz T. Weiße (University of Augsburg); Erik Lehmann (University of Augsburg); Ondrej Dvouletý (Prague University of Economics and Business)
This research extends the current state of knowledge on the relationship among religious be-haviours, church donations in particular, risk-taking, and entrepreneurship, by utilising the the-oretical underpinnings of Malinowski, a famous anthropologist. Inspired by Malinowski's re-search and existing studies on religion and entrepreneurship, this study examines how entre-preneurs respond to uncertainty through ritualised behaviour, utilising rich German administra-tive tax data covering the years 2010–2018. The empirical strategy tracked the behaviour of taxpayers before the entrepreneurial entry and three years after the business start-up. The sta-tistical analysis tested the role of income volatility, serving as a proxy for risk, on the amount of church-related charitable donations among taxpayers. The key findings were in line with the theoretical foundations, showing that the sequence of giving was higher before individuals entered entrepreneurship, then they experienced a decline at the transition, and subsequently, from the second year onwards, they even exceeded their prior philanthropic behaviours. Fur-thermore, our findings suggest that higher income volatility is positively associated with chari-table giving, and this effect is even more pronounced for entrepreneurs. Overall, this study calls for more in-depth longitudinal research that links entrepreneurial habits, risk-taking, phi-lanthropy, and personal income.

The Price of Narcissism: A Neo-Institutional Theory Perspective of Entrepreneurs on Airbnb

ENT
Jeremiah Thomas McGhee (University of North Texas); Jeffrey Chandler (University of North Texas); Marcus Wolfe (University of North Texas); Jeremy Short (University of North Texas)
Drawing from neo-institutional theory, we examine how Airbnb entrepreneurs’ expressions of narcissism affect their listing prices across different national cultures. We suggest that moderate expressions of narcissism maximize the price an Airbnb entrepreneur can demand for their listing, but this effect is highly contingent upon the cultural values of the market in which the entrepreneur is venturing. Specifically, we argue that Airbnb entrepreneurs’ expressions of narcissism have a curvilinear (inverted-U) relationship with the price demand of their listings and that this relationship is weaker (stronger) in cultures valuing individualism (collectivism) and monumentalism (flexibility). Using a sample of 502,002 Airbnb listings across 32 countries, we find general support for theorizing.
