Explaining Variation in Interest in Modern Slavery: Evidence from British History

MH
Andrew D A Smith (University of Birmingham); Nicholas Wong (Northumbria University); Daniel Riddell (University of Birmingham)
Abstract This paper examines why policymakers’ focus on modern slavery in international supply chains shifts over time. An analysis of historical British parliamentary debates, press coverage, and civil society lobbying from 1897 to 2010 reveals that political interest was highly inconsistent, despite the ongoing presence of goods produced with unfree labour within British supply chains throughout this period. Existing frameworks, such as stakeholder salience theory, the public arenas model, and framing theory, do not fully explain the fluctuations in policymakers' interest. To address this, we introduce the Modern Slavery Geopolitical Salience (MSGS) Model, which identifies five interacting factors: geopolitical environment, domestic economic interests, civil society activism, media framing, and social memory of slavery and abolition. Geopolitics emerges as the primary factor, whilst periods of war or heightened security threats tend to diminish concern, whereas periods of relative peace encourage engagement. Media exposure and activist framing heighten attention when aligned with economic or nationalist narratives. Contrary to claims that ethical concerns decline during recessions, interest in modern slavery tends to be countercyclical. The MSGS Model advances theory by integrating structural and communicative dynamics and offers practical insights for advocacy strategies.

Architectural Weight: Disaggregating Inertia and Tracing Moving Trade-Off in Terminal Decline

MH
Peiran Su (Audencia Business School); Ying Ding (University of the West of Scotland)
The study addresses the liability of senescence by disaggregating structural inertia, which is traditionally treated as monolithic, into a more diagnostic framework. We introduce a 2x2 model based on source (internal vs. external) and scope of impact (operational vs. strategic), yielding four distinct forces: process rigidity, executive stasis, market friction, and systemic lock-in. Analyzing the 114-year history of A. F. Craig & Co. Ltd., we find that organizational decline is driven by a moving trade-off where successful historical competencies, rooted in internal strategic stability, harden into external strategic constraints. This dynamic creates a cumulative architectural weight that nullifies tactical agility and prevents core strategic renewal. This reframes senescence, demonstrating that the inability to change what matters—core strategic goals—is the source of terminal decline.

Belling the Cat: Historical Shift of Effectiveness as a Purpose of Business Organizations

MH
Bora Aksu (Marmara University)
A purpose based understanding of effectiveness reframes the organization's capacity to stay true to its enduring reason for existence while navigating complex, multi-stakeholder environments. Relying on four assumptions about how effectiveness can be thought as the main purpose of organizations, a process model derived from recent theoretical developments is proposed. The paper follows the assumption that the contemporary paradigm can be considered under a historical shift from neo-liberalist shareholder capitalism to sustainable capitalism and argues a redesigned effectiveness model which emphasizes the historical stages of the organizational purpose implementation. By investigating the case of global automotive industry ecosystem, paper concludes that four stages of purpose implementation emerged during the historical shift to the sustainable capitalism. organizational effectiveness as a purpose implemented by relying on authenticity, accountability, hybridity and value creation in different stages. Those are externally imposed and rewards organizations who are bold enough to undertake the risky tasks of purpose implementation while passing through the stages.
