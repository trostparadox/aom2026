Beyond Pecuniary Incentives: How Openness-Preserving IPR Shapes Cumulative Innovation

TIM
Jiahua Sun (Singapore Management University); Nianchen Han (Nanyang Technological University); Shaoqin Tang (University of Colorado-Boulder); Liang Chen (Singapore Management University)
A central assumption in innovation policy is that stronger intellectual property rights (IPR) hinder cumulative innovation. This view, however, largely rests on the premise that innovators are primarily extrinsically motivated. We challenge this assumption by examining how IPR affects cumulative innovation when contributors are intrinsically motivated and when IPR is designed to preserve openness. Using a quasi-natural experiment from a landmark lawsuit, we implement a difference-in-differences analysis on GitHub repositories. We find that strengthened copyleft enforcement increases both the quantity and novelty of external follow-on innovations. We further find that a stronger openness-preserving IPR regime can amplify the use of available knowledge in cumulative innovation. Our findings extend the dominant IPR framework built around rent maximizing agents and exclusion rights by highlighting how the impact of IPR depends on what is being protected and on the motivations driving those who contribute to follow-on innovation.

Patent Pledge and Technological Innovation: The “Good Faith” of Tesla

TIM
Ziming Wang (The University of Tokyo)
In June 2014, Tesla publicly pledged not to assert its patents against users acting in good faith, aiming to promote the diffusion of electric vehicle technologies. This study provides the first empirical investigation of a firm-initiated patent pledge covering the pledgor’s entire patent portfolio and evaluates its consequences for both the firm’s own innovation and follow-on innovation. The results show that Tesla’s patent pledge significantly increased technological similarity between Tesla and subsequent innovators and led to a marked rise in Tesla’s patenting activity. In contrast, the pledge did not increase the number of follow-on innovations. These patterns suggest that although the pledge lowered formal access barriers, the good faith condition introduced appropriability concerns that limited entry while steering innovation toward technological areas aligned with Tesla’s core business. Overall, the findings indicate that patent pledges with retained control can function less as pure diffusion mechanisms and more as strategic instruments for shaping innovation trajectories and ecosystem development. The paper provides implications for managers considering patent pledges as part of their strategic toolkit and for policymakers assessing the long-term competitive and social effects of conditional royalty-free licensing.

The Use and Protection of Maori Traditional Knowledge in Existing IP Rights

TIM
Egbert Amoncio (Goethe University Frankfurt); Alexander Cuntz; Alessio Muscarnera (World Intellectual Property Organization); Kiri Toki (The University of Auckland)
This paper examines how trademark governance shapes the use and protection of Indigenous traditional knowledge. We study New Zealand’s 2003 trademark reform, which established a Maori Advisory Committee to review culturally sensitive marks. We develop a model showing that cultural guardianship embedded in formal IP administration performs a dual role: it deters misaligned registrations through screening while enhancing the legitimacy of approved marks through certification. The model predicts a decline in new culturally linked registrations but greater survival of existing ones. Using TM-Link data from 1993–2013, we identify Maori-related trademarks through Maori dictionaries and estimate a difference-in-differences model comparing Maori-related registrations and expirations in New Zealand with Maori-related marks abroad. Consistent with the model, we find that stringent collective governance reduced Maori-related registrations by roughly 25 percent while lowering expirations by a similar magnitude. The findings highlight the role of institutional design in aligning private trademark incentives with cultural guardianship, contributing to theories of property rights and governance of traditional knowledge.

Transferability MATTRs: Towards Understanding Antecedents of Strategic Licensing

TIM
Dafna Bearson (Harvard Business School); Maria Roche (Harvard University)
This paper examines firms’ decisions to license intellectual property (IP). We propose a framework emphasizing transferability, defined as firm choices about how to describe their inventions. Leveraging a novel proxy for transferability—the moving-average type-token ratio (MATTR), which measures the lexical descriptiveness of patents—we analyze licensing of U.S. patents granted from 1980 to 2015. The results reveal that higher MATTR is associated with a greater likelihood of licensing, suggesting firms strategically enhance transferability for potential licensees. The relationship between transferability and licensing is moderated by the invention’s value and industry demand uncertainty. These findings contribute to research on innovation strategy and technology markets, highlighting the strategic selection of IP for licensing and the interplay of invention characteristics, firm strategy, and industry context.

When Knowing Less Is More: Evidence from Licensing and Sale Agreements

TIM
Dafna Bearson (Harvard Business School)
This paper examines firms' decision to license or sell intellectual property (IP). I develop a conceptual framework in which the choice to license or sell depends on (1) IP owners' ex ante beliefs about commercial value and (2) information friction, defined as the degree to which IP owners, licensees, and buyers share beliefs about commercial value based on the available information. Using novel data on U.S. patent licensing agreements linked to established data on patent sales, I analyze transactions across companies from 1995 to 2023. I exploit the Supreme Court’s 2007 decision in MedImmune, Inc. v. Genentech, Inc., which lowered the expected payoff from licensing, as an exogenous change to study how shifts in beliefs about commercial value affect the margin between licensing and sale. The results reveal the likelihood of licensing increases for patents with high expected commercial value particularly in industries characterized by high degrees of information friction, suggesting that firms retain ownership of seemingly commercially valuable assets when beliefs are heterogeneous. These findings contribute to the literature on innovation strategy and markets for ideas by identifying a setting in which information friction facilitates rather than impedes transactions.
