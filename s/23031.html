Meditation and the Making of Academic Identity: A Collaborative Autoethnography Across Contemplative

MSR
Diwakar Singh; Vineet Kaushik; Zahoor Paray (Entrepreneurship Development institute of India); Rajat Sharma (Entrepreneurship Development institute of India)
Meditation and contemplative practices are increasingly incorporated into academic and organizational life, yet research continues to privilege third-person outcomes over first-person meaning. Despite extensive evidence on psychological and neurological effects, little is known about how meditation is lived, interpreted, and woven into academic identity across spiritual traditions. Addressing this gap, we present a collaborative phenomenological autoethnography of four scholar–practitioners engaged in Sudarshan Kriya Yoga, Yogada Satsang Society, Islamic zikr–salat, and devotional meditation. Drawing on first-person narratives, reflexive dialogues, and cross-case analysis, we examine how contemplative practice is entered, embodied, challenged, and integrated into scholarly life. Findings reveal a recursive developmental process through which meditation responds to disruption, deepens phenomenological awareness, catalyzes emotional and spiritual transformation, and contributes to the ongoing construction of scholar–practitioner identity. We advance a reflexive cycle of meditation-based identity formation and discuss implications for Management, Spirituality, and Religion (MSR) scholarship, contemplative pedagogy, and qualitative research.

Theorizing Identity Consolidation Under Liminal Spaces

MSR
Nishant Garg (Indian Institute of Management Indore); APARNA DWIVEDI (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Current research on entrepreneurial identity processes in liminal contexts has converged on two dominant theorizations: identity work, which assumes preserving continuity of the old through retrospective sensemaking validated by pre-liminal reference groups, and identity play, which assumes experimentation with new through prospective imagination validated by post-liminal groups in relatively safe environments. However, current theorisation has treated these as analytically distinct processes. We argue that liminality, characterized by disrupted continuity, ambiguity, and competing validation demands from both pre- and post-liminal groups, violates the core assumptions undergirding work and play, rendering an isolated study of either process theoretically insufficient. Hence, understanding this phenomenon requires an alternative but complementary theorization of identity consolidation. In this paper, drawing on realistic entrepreneurial experiences through an inductive qualitative study of 31 refugee entrepreneurs, we theorize consolidation based on the logic of integrative sensemaking, wherein retrospective and prospective meaning-making are co-constitutive. The emergent grounded model specifies three consolidation pathways: preservation-focused, integration-focused, and compartmentalized, each reflecting patterned responses to competing demands of identity validation without reverting to singular processes. We further examine the theoretical connections between consolidation and existing identity and sensemaking theories, demonstrating how it bridges the conceptual divide and differs fundamentally from work and play as currently theorized.

Identity Regression Theory

MSR
Nishant Garg (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Piyush Gopal (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
Current research on post-failure entrepreneurial identity has converged on two dominant theorizations: identity work maintains continuity through validation from pre-disruption reference groups, while identity play experiments with provisional selves through validation from post-disruption reference groups. Both theories assume unidirectional forward movement—whether through repair or experimentation. Although identity scholarship outside entrepreneurship acknowledges individuals may not always move forward, it still theorizes it through a deficit ontology as "identity paralysis" or "being stuck" in transitions like prisoner re-entry. We argue this unidirectional assumption renders study of post-failure identity processes theoretically insufficient. Understanding post-failure dynamics requires an alternative but complementary theorization: identity regression, wherein backward movement may be generative rather than failed adaptation. Through an inductive study of 33 post-failure entrepreneurs, we theorize regression on the logic of competing internal conviction and external social validation dynamics that nurture generativity despite loss. Our grounded model reveals three pathways—identity reversion, preparatory liminality, and identity experimentation—each reflecting patterned responses to validation breakdowns. We examine regression's theoretical connections with identity and validation scholarship, demonstrating how backward movement differs fundamentally from forward progression as currently theorized and reframes regression as generative reconstruction.

Heavenly Beliefs, Earthly Politics: Political Identity Mediating Religiosity and Political Views

MSR
Jeffrey Miles (University of the Pacific)
The assassination of American political activist, entrepreneur, and media personality Charlie Kirk brought renewed focus on the influence of religiosity on political identity, political views, and political activism on college campuses. Researchers have examined these variables for decades, but research findings have been mixed. To better understand the processes and mechanisms through which religiosity influences politics, some of the latest research efforts in this area have focused on finding variables that mediate these relationships. For example, work by Rowatt (2019) found that political ideology mediated the influence of religiosity on perceptions of immigrants. The main goals of the current study were to: 1) replicate the Rowatt (2019) finding, and 2) extend the use of political identity as a mediator of the influence of religiosity on two additional political views: addressing climate change, and raising taxes. In a survey study of 771 first-year undergraduate students in the western United States, religiosity was found to influence political identity, political views, and political activism. Political identity was found to influence political activism and to successfully mediate the influence of religiosity on three timely and significant political views: creating a pathway to citizenship for undocumented immigrants, addressing global climate change, and raising taxes to reduce the deficit. Lastly, this study found additional support for research showing that political activism tends to vary by political identity with liberals protesting more frequently than conservatives.
