Bottle Versus Breast: De-Institutionalizing Markets and Re-Institutionalizing Declining Practices

Author: Marselia Tan ESSEC Business School

Session overview

Extant research has largely overlooked how mature, taken-for-granted market categories undergo de-institutionalization. Studying such phenomena is critical because increasingly, social movements motivated by wide-ranging social and environmental justice issues seek to challenge established markets ––not by creating new markets for alternative products emphasized in past studies, but by reviving craft, natural, communal, care, or other practices that eschew entrenched producer-consumer exchanges. To explore this puzzle, I build a historical case study of the U.S. infant formula market and pro-breastfeeding activism from 1960 to 2000, drawing from extensive historical sources including firms, breastfeeding activists, NGOs, pediatricians, federal agencies, parents, and mainstream media. This study contributes to literatures on market evolution by generating a process model that unpacks mechanisms through which movements oriented toward practice revival delegitimize markets, and how incumbents strategically resist market de-institutionalization. Moreover, findings reveal an outcome of prolonged contestations between markets’ proponents and opponents that diverges from prior studies’ emphasis on disruption or substitution of incumbent markets: co-existence of product market and revived practice. The contested infant formula market restabilized and remains substantial, while simultaneously, breastfeeding as a non-market practice became re-institutionalized and diffused widely. This coexistence was accompanied by the emergence of ancillary markets—such as breast pumps and lactation consulting services—that support the revived practice without directly replacing the incumbent product market.
