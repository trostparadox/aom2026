Session overview

Across organizational, social, and self-regulatory contexts, individuals routinely choose not to engage—with information, with people, with perspectives, or with temptations—when engagement is expected to activate psychological, moral, or interpersonal threats (Caplin & Leahy, 2001; Hart et al., 2009; Golman, Hagmann, & Loewenstein, 2017). The fundamental decisions that people make about what and who to avoid versus approach shape important organizational outcomes, such as decision making, goal pursuit, justice, group performance, and trust. This symposium brings together four papers that examine avoidance as a response to perceived threat across distinct domains, revealing shared mechanisms that cut across health behavior, social interaction, judgment and decision-making, and self-control. Rather than treating avoidance as error, apathy, or inattention, these papers show how avoidance reflects forward-looking judgments about the threats posed by consequences of engagement—whether emotional, relational, cognitive, or moral. We explore when and why people avoid, whether avoidance has negative consequences, and how to intervene to reduce avoidance.
