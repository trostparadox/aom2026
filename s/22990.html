Translating Entrepreneurial Ecosystems Across Borders: A Process Model of Institutional Reassembly

IM
Siyu Fei (Concordia University); Alex B. Bitektine (JMSB – Concordia University)
International management research has extensively examined how firms transfer practices, technologies, and organizational forms across borders, yet we know far less about how complex institutional systems travel across national contexts. In particular, existing studies offer limited insight into how multi-actor, multi-level configurations such as entrepreneurial ecosystems are translated under conditions of institutional misalignment. Drawing on a 30-year historical study of China’s Z-Park, we examine how entrepreneurship support organizations (e.g., business incubators and accelerators) originating in Silicon Valley were translated into an emerging-market context. We show that EE translation unfolded through iterative reinterpretation and reassembly by diverse actors, producing new interdependencies and a locally distinct ecosystem configuration. We develop a process model of ecosystem reassembly, identifying two core mechanisms—syntagmatic editing and paradigmatic fitting—through which actors recombine, differentiate, and institutionalize institutional elements. We further introduce the concept of translation precision to explain variation in the degree of resemblance between translated elements and their foreign prototypes, highlighting the roles of institutional (mis)alignment and translators’ experiential familiarity. By theorizing how complex institutional systems travel, this study advances international management research on cross-border institutional change and extends institutional translation theory beyond the transfer of isolated practices.

Elite Coalition-Building as Nonmarket Strategy: Multinational Enterprises in Iraq’s Fragile Politic

IM
Hussein Salih Hadi Mamorry; Stefan Hielscher (University of Bath)
Multinational enterprises (MNEs) operating in fragile states confront not only weak formal institutions but also powerful local elites who regulate access to resources and stability. Building on the framework of limited access orders and principles of polycentric governance, we conceptualize elite coalition-building as a central form of nonmarket strategy in fragile political economies. Drawing on 22 in-depth interviews and documentary analysis from Iraq’s oil and gas sector, we develop a typology of three elite engagement strategies: inclusive, selective, and minimal. Inclusive engagement fosters multi-elite coalitions that share rents, reduce disruption risk and enhance operational continuity and local legitimacy, but at high cost; selective engagement privileges powerful gatekeepers, generating exclusion and fragile coalitions; minimal engagement preserves compliance but undermines legitimacy and long-term viability of elite coalitions. We also differentiate between community-embedded elites, who provide broader legitimacy, and detached elites, who may stabilize coalitions only in the short term but undermine cooperation in the long run. Our analysis shows how firms’ home-country institutional imprints— whether from open- or limited-access orders—shape default strategies of elite engagement abroad. By theorizing how MNEs co-produce (or destabilize) elite coalitions, the study advances research on nonmarket strategy, CSR, and international business research in fragile contexts.

Beyond Audits: A Supplier-Centric Framework for Reducing Inequality

IM
Yuka Fujimoto (Sunway University); Fara Azmat; Muhammad Faisal Kamal (COMSATS University Islamabad)
Global value chains (GVCs) raise enduring ethical concerns as multinational corporations (MNCs) shape who benefits and who is deprived within global production systems. Prior research shows that buyer-led GVCs allow lead firms to capture disproportionate value through pricing and governance control, while social-compliance and multistakeholder initiatives often fail to address suppliers’ economic inequities. This study advances management scholarship on global inequality by shifting the analytical focus from firm-level governance boundaries to supplier sites where economic value depletion occurs. Drawing on 94 semi-structured interviews and onsite observations with factory owners, managers, HR personnel, workers, and intermediaries in Bangladesh’s garment sector and Pakistan’s sports-goods sector, we inductively develop a supplier-centric cascading governance framework. We show that economic value depletion cascades across three interconnected levels: (1) macro—MNC headquarters’ disengagement from suppliers, enabling inequitable pricing and deceptive audits; (2) meso—underdeveloped HR systems, informal wage and promotion practices, and symbolic grievance procedures; and (3) micro—erosion of worker well-being, gendered vulnerability, and choreographed brand visits. Together, these dynamics explain how ostensibly “social” initiatives coexist with—and facilitate—persistent economic deprivation at supplier sites. We argue that reducing inequality in GVCs requires MNCs to internalize governance by engaging directly with suppliers through equitable pricing, independent auditing, and professionalized HR systems aligned with home-country standards. In doing so, this study reframes the boundaries of MNC responsibility across the entire supply chain and offers pathways toward reduced inequalities (SDG 10) and decent work and economic growth (SDG 8).
