An Agentic Theory of Work-Family Conflict Adjustment

OB
Bowen Shi (National University of Singapore); Wei Jee Ong (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Christopher Barnes (University of Washington)
Prevailing theories portray work-family conflict as having detrimental outcomes for employees, and also likely leading to loss spirals that amplify detrimental consequences. Challenging this view, we draw upon career construction theory to reframe employees as agentic constructors of their careers who actively manage their work-life interface through job mobility. We propose a theory of dynamic recalibration in employees in job transitions, in which employees experiencing high work-family conflict transition to roles with lower work-family conflict, whereas those in low work-family conflict roles pursue greater challenges. We further identify anxiety as the core affective mechanism driving this process. We present evidence from two national panel studies demonstrating this recalibration effect, plus a pre-registered experiment isolating anxiety as the causal mechanism translating prior conflict into future job preference. Our research thus reconceptualizes work-family conflict not as a mere driver of deleterious outcomes, but as a critical developmental signal that catalyzes agentic career construction. Ultimately, our findings reveal that individuals navigate their careers not by seeking to eliminate conflict, but by actively managing it toward a dynamic equilibrium.

Lifestyle Social Work as a Work Orientation: Conceptualizing Prosocial Work–Life Integration

OB
Dinesh Kumar (Woxsen University); Nidhi Suthar (Woxsen University)
Lifestyle Social Work (LSW) describes a work orientation in which prosocial commitments become identity central and are enacted across work and nonwork domains over time. This definition makes three construct claims. First, LSW implies cross-domain behavioral continuity that persists after incentive shocks. Second, LSW implies moralized self-definition that produces reputational and relational stakes. Third, LSW implies domain translation through sensemaking. These claims yield observable discriminant tests that separate LSW from calling, prosocial motivation and integration preference. Work orientation theory distinguishes career and calling as meaning systems tied mainly to the work role. It does not explain when prosocial meaning becomes a cross-domain standard for action. Building on Kumar’s conceptual origin of the term and translating it into organization scholarship (Kumar, 2024) we define LSW by three jointly necessary conditions: identity integration, boundary permeability and temporal continuity. We treat these three conditions as jointly necessary and jointly sufficient for LSW as a theoretical construct. If prosocial enactment is cross-domain but not identity-central, it reflects integration preference or volunteering. If prosocial identity is central but enactment remains role-bounded, it reflects calling. If enactment is cross-domain and identity-central but episodic, it reflects prosocial episodes. These exclusions make LSW falsifiable through observable discontinuities in prosocial enactment after negative work shocks or shifts in role demands. We develop a process model that specifies antecedents, mechanisms and outcomes. The model highlights identity internalization, cross-domain sensemaking and social reinforcement and it explains a coherence spiral that sustains meaning and an obligation spiral that elevates strain. We offer discriminant propositions and observable tests that separate LSW from calling, prosocial motivation, volunteering and work–life integration preference. The paper extends theory on work orientations, meaning and boundaries by adding enactment scope as a core dimension.

The Cross-Domain Self-Regulatory Process Toward Anticipated Pleasurable Off-Job Activities

OB
LUO QINGYE (Tsinghua University); YU KIARA HAO (Tsinghua University)
While prior work-nonwork interface literature has amply documented how employees’ past off-job experiences shape their subsequent workdays, it has largely overlooked the prospective influence of anticipating nonwork future. Addressing this oversight, we investigate how anticipated pleasurable off-job activities—such as a planned dinner or a movie night—proactively shape employees’ motivation and functioning during the current workday. Integrating self-regulatory theory and multiple goal research, we propose anticipated pleasurable off-job activities to serve as activated nonwork goals that imbue current work with instrumental value. Specifically, to safeguard their upcoming off-job pleasure, employees are motivated to exert work-related motivational control, which subsequently fuels their work engagement. Furthermore, we posit that this regulatory process is amplified by job instrumentality, a chronic value orientation that reflects the extent to which a worker views their job primarily as a means to other worthwhile goals. Evidence from a one-day survey (Study 1) and an experience sampling study (Study 2) supports the proposed main effect, and a rigorous survey (Study 3) is designed to test the full model. Collectively, our research extends the prevailing passive, resource-centric paradigm in work-nonwork interface literature, illuminating how employees act as strategic agents who proactively regulate their professional efforts to facilitate their personal lives.

Perpetual Liminality and Identity Fragmentation in Humanitarian Workers

OB
Matilde GUILHON (SKEMA Business School - Grand Paris Campus); Marcello Russo (University of Bologna); Claudia Manca (Technical University of Denmark); Elisa Villani (University of Bologna)
Humanitarian workers repeatedly alternative between missions and home, inhabiting a chronic “in between” state of perpetual liminality. Immersed in extreme environments that disrupt roles, relationships, and priorities, they then return to home settings that often no longer feel familiar or secure. Drawing on a qualitative study of Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) Italy, based on 43 semi structured interviews, focus groups, and informal conversations with managers, this paper investigates how humanitarian workers experience and manage identity evolution and fragmentation across recurrent post mission periods. We theorize three interconnected phases in the post mission trajectory: living fragmented identities between mission and home, navigating the ambiguity and insecurity of post mission life, and developing coping practices that enable to bridge both worlds. In doing so, the paper contributes to literatures on liminality and identity work in extreme contexts by conceptualizing home as a pivotal liminal site marked by post-mission emotional and identity disruptions, and by showing how ongoing identity processes underpin the longer-term sustainability of humanitarian careers.

Authentic Self-Presence in Role-Saturated Lives: A Grounded Theory of Me-Time

OB
Jessica Jamie-Lee Hechelmann (University of Kaiserslautern-Landau (RPTU)); Gisela I. Gerlach (University of Kaiserslautern-Landau (RPTU))
Work–life scholarship has richly theorized how individuals navigate multiple roles and domains, yet the self as a lived experience within roles remains conceptually underdeveloped. Based on an inductive grounded theory study of 31 interviews with German knowledge workers, we conceptualize me-time as a temporally bounded experiential state defined by two features: detaching from interpretive role expectations and acting in alignment with one’s momentary feelings and thoughts. We further develop a four-stage process model that explains how me-time emerges from a need to pursue inner thoughts and feelings unimpeded, how individuals enter me-time via detachment and alignment, how me-time is experienced as authentic self-presence, and how it yields immediate and accumulated effects on well-being. Finally, we show that me-time can occur within work roles when tasks are personally meaningful, autonomy enables self-directed enactment, and individuals can bring their capacities to bear with felt impact. Our findings extend work–life theorizing beyond domain-based accounts by clarifying the micro-dynamics through which the self becomes experientially available within roles.
