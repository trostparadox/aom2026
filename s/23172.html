The Contingency-Based Mirroring Hypothesis and its Application to the Video Game 'Cyberpunk 2077'

CTO
Claudia Loebbecke (University of Cologne); Cedrik Joshua Broschart (University of Cologne); Astrid Obeng-Antwi (University of Cologne)
This paper extends the mirroring hypothesis (Colfer & Baldwin, 2016) as a central theory for the coupling of product and technology architectures and organizational systems to digital production industries such as software development, the media and gaming. It proposes the Contingency-Based Mirroring Hypothesis Model, which accounts for five key dimensions of influence – product and technology related factors, knowledge and capability related factors, demand-side factors, institutional and environmental factors, and actor and organization related factors. Applying the model to the case of the development, turbulent launch, and a successful post-launch phase of the AAA video game 'Cyberpunk 2077', highlights that organizational effectiveness depends the ability to calibrate mirroring in response to technological, institutional, and market contingencies. This paper underscores the need to understand the original mirroring hypothesis not as a deterministic structural law, but as a contingent explanatory model. It concludes with theoretical contributions, practical recommendations and an outlook to future research.

Beyond Greed: Why Platforms Support Self-Exploitative Gaming, and When It Becomes a Trap

CTO
Chen E Liang (Massachusetts Institute of Technology); Vicky Chuqiao Yang (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
We develop a theory of gaming management in platform governance; that is, how may a platform strategically responds to actors gaming its algorithmic evaluation systems. We examine “data-making” (DM) fans in China. These fans coordinate large-scale engagement activities to generate traffic data, inflate platform metrics, and ultimately increase their idols’ business value. But while the data from DM may be monetizable to a platform in the short term, encouraging too much DM can hurt its long-term profitability as DM also speeds up to user disengagement. To explain why the platform in our case nonetheless encourages DM and when this strategy breaks down, we develop a system-dynamics model. We conceptualize gaming as a strategic interaction between platforms seeking to maximize total engagement data and complementors (here, idol agencies) seeking to maximize business value, from which both actors ultimately profit through sustained user participation. Our model explains when platforms and agencies converge on DM-dominant strategies, why platform-level regulation alone often fails, and why transitions away from DM-intensive regimes could be costly. Importantly, we show how platforms themselves may become trapped in suboptimal equilibria. We contribute to research on platform governance and algorithmic evaluation by theorizing gaming as a managed outcome of platform governance and by showing how “algorithmic imaginaries” (i.e., users’ heuristic interpretations on the platform environment) can coordinate collective action and reproduce market realities.

Customer-Centric Organizing: A Triangle of Tensions Framework

CTO
Jagdip Singh; John Paul Stephens (Case Western Reserve University); Gabriela (Gaby) Cuconato (Indiana University Kokomo)
Despite widespread endorsement of customer-centricity as a source of competitive advantage, organizations often struggle to translate customer-first principles into consistent practice. To address this challenge, we develop a framework for enacting customer-centricity, conceptualized as the interactional processes by which organizations enact, sustain, and adapt customer-first action across customer touchpoints. Drawing on a systematic review and synthesis of organizing theories, we propose the “Triangle of Tensions” (ToT) framework, which situates customer-centricity challenges within a triad of core relationships: management–employees, management–customers, and employees–customers. We demonstrate that rationality–relationality tensions are intrinsic to customer-centric organizing and manifest in varied configurations across organizations. Using case-study data from four prototypical configurations, we illustrate how the ToT framework yields theoretical and practical insights for understanding, diagnosing, and enhancing customer-centric organizing.

Understanding Born-Digital Firms: What They Are, What They Are Not, and Ways Forward

CTO
Sophie Gommers (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Svetlana Khapova (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Abbas Shahim (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Born-digital firms are increasingly seen as a powerful lens for theorizing the future of digital organizing and advancing understanding of the design principles that shape the digital economy. Yet, research on born-digital firms remains fragmented, with inconsistent definitions and conceptual boundaries. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review of the born-digital literature to clarify what these firms are and what they are not, and to examine how their organizing principles shape contemporary value creation. We propose a privative definition that delineates born-digital firms as entities free from pre-digital constraints, evident in their digital origin, nature of offerings, and extent of globalization, and distinct from legacy firms burdened by non-digital inertia. We develop an integrative input-process-output (IPO) framework showing how capabilities rooted in a digital core, complemented by selective non-digital capabilities, enable trust-based ecosystem orchestration and scalability by design. This review advances conceptual clarity and synthesizes fragmented insights into a coherent perspective on born-digital organizing. We conclude by outlining future research opportunities to deepen theoretical understanding and guide empirical studies of firms native to the digital age.

Just Trading Opportunists? How Crypto Token Airdrops Shape Platform Contributions

CTO
Georg Schiffner (RWTH Aachen University); Patrick Pollok (RWTH Aachen University)
Governance token airdrops have emerged as a prominent mechanism to transition from proprietary to collective platform governance. To incentivize external contributions, platform sponsors distribute (“airdrop”) governance tokens, some worth billions of dollars, across platform participants and contributors. However, such transitions raise concerns not just to reduce platform sponsor opportunism but to enable contributor opportunism instead. Hence, we study token airdrops in blockchain-based open-source platforms to examine how this form of transition to collective governance shapes platform contributions. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design that exploits airdrops as governance shocks, we analyze a panel of 615,979 quarterly contributor–platform observations that contain more than 10 million open-source platform contributions between 2015 and 2025. We find that governance token airdrops causally increase external contributions by 13 percentage points. Contrary to concerns, we find no evidence that airdrops increase opportunistic contributor behavior. We further examine how specific design features of governance token airdrops may have mitigated opportunism and discuss the dual incentive role of governance power and token value as a mitigation mechanism in platform governance transitions. Our findings contribute to property rights theory and dynamic agency theory and offer practical implications for platform strategies aimed at fostering sustained, value-enhancing external contributions.
