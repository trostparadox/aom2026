Feeling the Vibe? A Theory of Social Movement Resonance in Distributed Workplace Communications

OMT
Brittany Bond (Cornell University); Paul Merritt (Cornell University); Forrest Briscoe (Cornell University)
Prior research has examined how social movements can change organizational practices and policies through their influence on the decisions made by organizational leaders. Less is known about how organizations respond to social movements from the bottom up. Yet behavior among employees throughout an organization can shift in response to a social movement, suggesting a diffuse pathway for intra-organizational change. To shed light on this pathway, we consider the phenomenon of movement-resonant workplace communication. Drawing on social movement processes, we propose that after a movement gains traction in society, workplace communications will shift toward increased movement resonance. We further theorize that this increase in movement resonance will be greater under certain intra-organizational conditions: when communication authors belong to a movement-sensitive occupation; when communication recipients belong to a group that the movement targets for influence; and when communications occur in a locale with a prevailing political ideology that supports the movement. Using performance feedback texts from a Fortune 500 firm (n=118,833) before and after the #MeToo social movement, and a culturally-aware text embedding measurement method, we find considerable support for our predictions. Our research offers a new vantage for research on how social movements change organizations and also contributes to research on organizational communications and culture.

Middle Managers, Organizational Formalization, and the Diffuse Translation of Social Movement Goals

OMT
Ye Joon Lee (Northwestern University); Forrest Briscoe (Cornell University)
How do social movements induce organizational change from within? Prior research emphasizes top-down responses, such as organizational leaders revising official policies or practices, but movements may also diffuse through distributed behavioral shifts among middle managers. Drawing on theories of managerial discretion and formal structure, we argue that movements mobilize sympathetic middle managers to enact movement-aligned behaviors in their personnel decisions, while formal structures constrain these efforts. We test these ideas in the context of U.S. tech firms during the Stop Asian Hate movement, analyzing individual employment histories from 2019 to 2023 with a triple-difference design. Results show that firms with more Asian middle managers saw increased promotion prospects for Asian employees after the movement, while having a formal HR position within the organization mitigates the effect of movement-aligned managers. Our study identifies a distributed pathway through which movements reshape organizations and highlights the paradoxical role of formal structures in limiting managerial responsiveness to movement pressures.

Internalized Marginalization and Insider Activism

OMT
Eun Young Song (The Australian National University)
Existing scholarship on insider activists has primarily focused on their visible, coordinated efforts to influence organizations on social issues, assuming that marginalized workers would also openly act from within to challenge the status quo. However, their marginal status does not necessarily enable overt activism. Moreover, less-privileged minorities who internalize marginalization often present themselves as part of the dominant group, thereby complicating their own group members’ insider activism. This study addresses these notions with a new theory built on the premises of internalized marginalization and hidden transcripts. Focusing on workers hoping for change in everyday interactions with their own group members who exhibit internalized marginalization, I theorize these workers’ furtive activism; that is, an apparent non-resistance to the existing rules of marginalization to undermine those very rules and influence their organizations. I posit tactics of furtive activism followed by a nuanced model of distributed readiness for action, illustrating how such activism may be linked to the rise of individualized activism against the rules while simultaneously reinforcing the existing status hierarchy. By addressing a key omission in current scholarship, my theory offers a fresh perspective on the role of marginalized workers in insider activism and broader social change.

From Voice to Protest: How Employee Activism Campaigns Escalate

OMT
Hadi Shaheen (College of Charleston); Jean-Baptiste Litrico (Smith School of Business, Queen's University)
Employee activists increasingly challenge employers on social and environmental issues, yet we lack understanding of how these insider campaigns escalate over time. Analyzing two campaigns at large U.S. technology firms, we develop a model of claim-tactic progression in employee activism. We identify five claim types: Problematizing, Commensurating, Unmasking, Stigmatizing, and Civicizing that employees deploy as information-based instruments that leverage insider knowledge and create pressure mechanisms on firms. These claims co-evolve with four tactical repertoires: Institutional, Persuasive, Confrontational, and Disruptive that escalate across campaigns as activists encounter organizational resistance. Critically, we reconceptualize organizational responses along two orthogonal dimensions: concession and sanction. This explains how firms simultaneously make policy changes and discipline organizers, producing mixed outcomes that accommodate demands while suppressing future mobilization. Our study shows how insider knowledge becomes escalating pressure through claim evolution, and how tactical repertoires shift in response to organizational reactions, leading to different outcomes. These contributions advance understanding of employee activism, tactical escalation, and organizational responses to insider-driven change.

Navigating the Classificatory Void: Why Activist Short Sellers Tell Messy Stories

OMT
Brad R. Turner (Massachusetts Institute of Technology); Georg Rilinger
Scholars tend to view narratives as heuristics that reduce complexity. Clear, consistent, and well-supported stories are seen as most effective in shaping market valuation. Yet activist short sellers who depend on rapid market revaluations frequently publish sprawling, rhetorically flamboyant reports backed by uneven evidence. Why? Using qualitative coding, machine learning, financial data from 708 U.S. short-selling campaigns (2008–2023), media coverage, and interviews, we explain this surprising pattern. We argue that short sellers face not only the task of simplifying complexity, but also a coordination challenge. This challenge becomes more severe under evaluative uncertainty - when stable standards for evaluating firm value are absent. In these situations, narrators must do more than inform. They must also help audiences make second-order inferences about how others will respond. To do so, short sellers use narrative complexity strategically: either by crafting modular stories that appeal to different evaluative frames, or by building a sweeping moral indictment that invokes a higher-order standard. When uncertainty is lower, they turn to more conventional or reclassification stories that emphasize clarity and fit with established categories. By linking narrative form to audience coordination under uncertainty, this paper contributes to theories of classification, narrative persuasion, and valuation in financial markets.
