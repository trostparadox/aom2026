The Making of a CEO: First-time CEOs and the Construction of CEO Identity

Monday, Aug 3 2026 · 8:30AM–9:30AM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 1
MOC

People

Authors: Ben Fagan University of Cincinnati; Joanna Tochman Campbell University of Cincinnati; Heather Ciara Vough George Mason University

Session overview

The role of the chief executive officer (CEO) carries unparalleled visibility, pressure, and symbolic weight, making leadership transitions consequential for firms and their stakeholders. These demands are heightened for first-time CEOs, who assume one of the most scrutinized positions in organizations without prior experience in the role. While upper echelons research has examined how CEOs’ backgrounds, values, and personal characteristics shape strategic actions, little attention has been given to CEO identity, despite research showing that identity has profound effects on the way we feel, think, and act. Our study addresses this need by showing how new CEO identity is constructed and how that process shapes their actions and others’ evaluations. We develop a conceptual process model of how first-time CEOs construct their identities across three interrelated stages. In the private stage, narrative identity work centers on optimal distinctiveness, as CEOs define who they are relative to predecessors and prior selves. In the public stage, discursive identity work focuses on articulating identity claims and interpreting early feedback to form a construed image. In the performance stage, dramaturgical identity work emphasizes consistency while CEOs align identity claims with enacted leadership behavior. Across these stages, identity work is tested and modified through social feedback. We theorize that this process lays the foundation for the CEO’s emerging reputation, shaping whether others view the CEO as trustworthy, credible, and authentic. Our model explains how first-time CEOs construct and stabilize a leader identity in a highly visible and precarious role.
