Platform Governance and Complement Growth in Plug-and-Play Marketplaces: A Configurational Analysis

STR
Erik Benin (EBS University of Business and Law); Markus Kreutzer (EBS University of Business and Law)
Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) providers increasingly rely on plug-and-play marketplaces where third-party developers supply complementary apps, extensions, and integrations. However, marketplaces with similar technical possibilities exhibit different patterns of complement growth. This study examines how marketplaces design contractual governance to „price“ access for complementors through distinct „bundles“ of transaction costs and how these bundles influence complement growth. Drawing on transaction cost economics and a configurational perspective, we conceptualize platform-complementor agreements as multidimensional, governance-induced transaction costs that primarily allocate four types of transaction costs: participation and set-up costs, exclusion risk and safeguarding costs, value-capture and protection costs, and coordination and adaptation costs. We analyze 43 SaaS plug-and-play marketplaces by combining archival data on complementor counts from 2022 to 2025 with systematic coding of platform-complementor agreement clauses. Using fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), we examine how configurations of four governance dimensions—access control, termination control, IP appropriation, and engagement—together with marketplace maturity relate to high complement growth. No single governance condition emerges as necessary. Instead, we identify three high-growth governance paths that differ in the primary “cost currency” borne by complementors: IP concessions, selective access, and operational control asymmetry. This study contributes to research on digital platforms by showing how specific governance clauses translate into transaction-cost bundles and demonstrating that multiple distinct “cost profiles” can support high complement growth.

Removability of Pre-Installed Apple Apps and the Implications for Third-Party Apps

STR
Joey Van Angeren (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Brian T. McCann (Vanderbilt University); Govert Vroom (IESE Business School)
Many platform owners compete with third-party platform participants by providing their own first-party products, raising concerns about fair competition on the platform. Unfortunately, the impact of potential interventions on platform competition remains unclear. This study investigates one such intervention, namely making pre-installed first-party products removable by users. Focusing on Apple's 2016 decision to make some of its iOS apps removable, we employ a difference-in-differences approach and find that a removability option significantly increases downloads and usage of competing third-party apps, suggestive of improved competition for third parties. Further analysis indicates that this effect likely stemmed from users experimenting with third-party alternatives. Our findings suggest that enabling removability for first-party products can foster greater competition on the platform to the benefit of third parties and enhances value creation for at least some consumers. This study has implications for platform research and regulation.

Unbundling Asset Rights: Rights Allocation and Value Co-Creation in Platforms

STR
Shijian Wei (University of Alabama); Devin Stein (University of Alabama); Paul L. Drnevich (University of Alabama)
Platform research has emphasized a tension between openness and control at the platform owner’s level, yet this very tension is also present in [DS1.1]complementor level when complementors making strategic choices to adopt platform enabled openness features[DS2.1]. This study examines how platform complementors’ choice to allow derivative-work creation to their end-users shapes value co-creation and product outcomes. Drawing on property rights theory, we theorize that granting users the right to create derivative works improves product performance by stimulating user investment in improvements and extensions to the product. We also argue that the effectiveness of this rights-allocation mechanism [DS3.1]depends on age inflicted value dissipation and intensity of controlthat affect the perceived returns to investing in the asset and the costs of exercising granted rights. Our theoretical model explains the complex [DS4.1]relationships between right allocation and performance outcomes. We test our arguments using a longitudinal dataset collected from the Steam videogame platform from 1997 to 2023. We find evidence that complementors who allocate right to create derivative works significantly increase user investment, which in turn drives superior product performance. However, the efficacy of this rights-allocation [DS5.1]mechanism is strongest early in the product’s lifecycle and, paradoxically, is dampened by high levels of governance oversight. These findings contribute to the platform strategy literature by identifying rights allocation as a dynamic lever for co-creation, and revealing excessive oversight stifles the user investments that openness seeks to cultivate.

Antitrust Intervention and Innovation at a Dominant Platform Firm

STR
Cuehyon Kim (Stanford University); Yuxiang (Jim) Liu (Stanford University); Riitta Katila (Stanford University)
How does antitrust intervention affect innovation within a dominant platform firm? We examine this question in the context of the 2001 U.S. antitrust settlement against Microsoft. Because Microsoft had no natural peer of comparable scale or ecosystem centrality, we construct a counterfactual for its innovation trajectory using the synthetic control method (SCM) and synthetic difference-in-differences (SDID), drawing on competitors identified in Microsoft’s own 10-K disclosures. Across both methods, we find a substantial and persistent increase in Microsoft’s post-settlement patenting activity and total forward citations relative to its synthetic control. In contrast, measures capturing the average impact and quality of individual patents do not increase. We also find that post-settlement innovation spans a broader range of technological classes while drawing more heavily on Microsoft’s existing knowledge base. Together, these results suggest that antitrust intervention is associated with an expansion in the scale of innovation within the dominant platform firm, rather than improvements in per-invention impact.

The Efficiency Paradox: How Open Boundary Resources Facilitate Multi-Homing and Supply Reallocation

STR
Vikas Agarwal (IE Business School)
This study investigates how the transition from manual, high-friction interfaces to open, programmable platform boundary resources (PBRs) reconfigures complementor engagement (CE). While the "resourcing" view of platform governance posits that resources like Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) accelerate innovation, I identify an Efficiency Paradox moderated by complementor heterogeneity. Utilizing the October 2017 Airbnb API release as an exogenous shock, I employ a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) and Triple Difference (DDD) design on a granular panel of 8.7 million property-month observations. I find that programmable interfaces trigger divergent behavioral responses: they increase engagement for individual multi-homing hosts by resolving coordination risks (Simple Enablement) but significantly decrease engagement for professionalized multi-homers (Strategic Arbitrage). These sophisticated actors exploit frictionless synchronization to treat the focal platform as an interchangeable commodity demand source, algorithmically reallocating inventory to whichever channel yields the highest marginal revenue. At the market level, this results in a systemic within-platform deprofessionalization of the platform’s supply structure as its most capable members become less platform-bound.
