Widely Adopted Financial Management Practices Under Pressure? State Grant Allocations to Nonprofits

PNP
ChiaKo Hung (University of Pennsylvania); SHUYI DENG (Indiana University Bloomington); Huirong Bi; Yuan (Daniel) Cheng
Nonprofits are commonly advised to minimize overhead, maintain fiscal leanness, diversify revenue streams, and avoid debt. The problem is that adhering to these financial management practices may not always result in positive outcomes; instead, it may lead to unforeseen consequences. Using a unique dataset that consists of 938 organizations from Minnesota, this study examines whether nonprofits adopting the four widely implemented financial management practices are associated with securing larger grant amounts and a greater number of government grants. Our analysis results show that although diversifying revenue streams (maintaining fiscal leanness) can attract larger amounts of state government funding (receive a greater number of state government grants), efforts to minimize overhead, and/or avoid debt do not consistently yield comparable funding advantages. Government responses to these financial management practices appear more complex than previously assumed.

Architecting Ecosystems: Government and Philanthropic Funding Distributions and Collective Capacity

PNP
Jung Ho Choi (University of Pennsylvania); Femida Handy (University of Pennsylvania)
This study examines whether equal or concentrated grant distributions across anti-poverty nonprofits relate to community-level poverty. Using panel data on government and philanthropic grants to anti-poverty nonprofits from 2010 to 2019, the study finds that more equal distributions of both government and philanthropic grants are associated with lower poverty levels. However, the association between equal philanthropic distribution and poverty decrease is stronger when government funding is concentrated, revealing that philanthropic and government funding may create interaction effects within the nonprofit ecosystems. These findings challenge prevailing “big bet” grantmaking strategies and suggest that funders act as “ecosystem architects” whose resource allocation decisions are connected to collective problem-solving capacity. This study suggests that how grants are distributed, not just how much, may matter in creating social impact, offering practical implications for governments and philanthropic funders.

Corporate Philanthropy and its Society Case: A Review and Research Agenda

PNP
Ane Casajus-Burutaran (University of Geneva); Arthur Gautier (ESSEC Business School); Marian Eabrasu (EM Normandie)
Corporate philanthropy (CP) research has grown rapidly, yet it remains dominated by a firm-centered “business case” emphasizing why companies give and how giving benefits the donor. We conduct a systematic integrative review of 354 CP publications to map constructs, theories, levels of analysis, and methods. We find that corporate antecedents and firm outcomes account for most studies, whereas beneficiary- and society-centered work is scarce and more ambivalent about CP’s effects. Building on this diagnosis, we advance a “society case” agenda that recenters recipients and social outcomes. We propose using logic-model thinking to theorize and measure direct effects on nonprofits and indirect effects on beneficiaries and grand challenges, across contexts and over time. We conclude with research questions to rebalance the field and strengthen causal inference.

Unified Payments Interface? Aligning Private Incentives and Public Goals in Finance Grand Challenges

PNP
Krishna Kumar Balaraman (IFMR GSB KREA UNIVERSITY); Venkatesh Krishnamurthy
The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) has revolutionized India's digital payment. This paper takes a multi-step approach based on media and archival data to explore the cooperative strategies among private and public players in the UPI ecosystem to solve finance grand challenges. Our theory is that, in early stages of the ecosystem, when the demand uncertainty is higher, the public players engage in both upstream and downstream innovation while private players engage in downstream innovation. In later stages of the ecosystem, when the demand uncertainty is lower, the private players engage in downstream innovation and, the public players continue to engage in upstream innovation but remain an impartial arbitrator of downstream innovation. We contribute to the literature on cooperative ecosystems involving public and private organizations, grand challenges, and financial technologies.

Theorizing the Institutional Logics Shift from CSR to ESG in Cross-Sector Partnerships

PNP
Ke Lu (The University of Hong Kong)
The rapid ascent of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) frameworks has fundamentally reshaped the interface between business and society. While management literature often views ESG as the maturation of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR), we argue that ESG represents a distinct institutional order governed by "investor logic"—a rationality predicated on financializing externalities and quantifying risk—rather than the normative or moral logics that characterized earlier cross-sector interactions. Drawing on institutional logics and the economic theory of internalization, this paper theorizes the structural transformation of Cross-Sector Partnerships (CSP) in the ESG era. We propose that the shift from CSR to ESG alters the resource exchange dynamic: civil society actors are no longer viewed as sources of moral legitimacy, but as specialized "vendors of materiality" required to validate corporate risk mitigation. This integration creates a "logic absorption" effect, where the instrumental rationality of financial markets displaces the value-based rationality of civil society. We identify three strategic responses to this institutional complexity—logic auditing, cross-logic translation, and the co-creation of accountability mechanisms. This study contributes to institutional theory and strategic management by elucidating how the financialization of social impact creates a paradox of isomorphism, potentially eroding the distinct value that civil society actors bring to the corporate ecosystem.
