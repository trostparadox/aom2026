Mitigating Hidden Drawbacks of Firm Specific Human Capital Perceptions

HR
Mengwei Li (Tilburg University); Clint Chadwick (University of Kansas); Zhongxing Su (Renmin University of China)
Strategic human capital literature predominantly views firm specific human capital (FSHC) as a supply-side constraint through which firms capture value from workers. However, recent attention to workers’ perceptions of firm specific human capital (pFSHC) indicates an overlooked downside for value creation. When employees perceive their skills as highly firm specific, they may feel “locked in,” fostering negative attitudes and reducing their willingness to contribute. Building upon strategic HRM literature, we consider whether high performance work systems (HPWS) can offset these negative effects. In Study 1, pFSHC was associated with lower commitment and higher turn-over intentions, whereas HPWS exhibited opposite effects, directly and indirectly through reducing pFSHC. Study 2 replicated these findings and demonstrated that transformational leadership oper-ated as an alternative, functionally comparable mechanism.

Hiring Does Not Mean Learning: The Detrimental Consequences of Firm-Level Rivalry

HR
Thomas Yoon (University of Liverpool)
By emphasizing the potential benefits of organizational learning, management research on strategic human capital generally supports the importance of learning-by-hiring highly capable talents for their highly valuable and also transferrering knowledge for the hiring firm. However, when a firm hires individuals from a firm that is considered to be a rival by incumbent members within the firm, the interaction after the human capital acquisition may also be negatively influenced by their previous affiliation. We propose that intense firm-level rivalry provide one such mechanism why individuals may fail to achieve expected outcomes upon their mobility due to their prior affiliations. Building on previous research on rivalry, we further suggest how such negative relationships can be avoided. By utilizing individual-level patent data on inventors and their performance within major U.S. technology firms, our empirical analyses suggest that mobile individuals from rival firms, compared to externally hired individuals from non-rivalry firms, have a more significant productivity loss upon mobility. Additional analysis further suggests why geographic proximity and successful integration may moderate this negative performance implication. The results of this study suggest a critical boundary condition to research on learning-by-hiring.

Navigating Digital Transformation in Chinese Workplaces: Developing the ESCQ Competency Framework

HR
Xiaoming Xu (Torrens University Australia); Paul Cerotti (Torrens University Australia); Claire Davison (Torrens University Australia); Caroline Mahon (Torrens University Australia)
ABSTRACT China’s rapid digital transformation is redefining workforce requirements, yet robust frameworks for core employee capabilities remain scarce. This qualitative study addresses the gap by investigating workforce needs across four key Chinese sectors (media, public service, high-tech manufacturing, supply chain & logistics), drawing on 20 executive interviews and validation sessions with 20 industry leaders to develop the Essential Foundations, Skills, Capabilities and Qualities (ESCQ) Framework. Findings reveal traditional Western KSA models inadequately reflect China’s workplace realities. The ESCQ Framework identifies four capability dimensions, while uncovering moderate skills gaps—driven by delayed skills renewal, uneven capabilities, education-industry misalignment and talent shortages—most acute in Industry 4.0 technical skills, which impair organizational performance and competitiveness. Critical Chinese cultural dimensions (collectivism, high power distance, achievement orientation) uniquely shape these requirements. Theoretically, this research extends capability frameworks beyond Western contexts; practically, the ESCQ Framework provides actionable tools for talent management and workforce development aligned with China’s unique environment. Keywords: digital transformation, workforce capabilities, China, Industry 4.0, skills gap, ESCQ framework, cultural dimensions, qualitative research.

Opening the Black Box of Human Capital Investment: Role of Perceived Human Capital Competitiveness

HR
Dahye Kim (Korea University); Johngseok Bae (Korea University)
We clarify the black box of human capital investment by theorizing a competitiveness-centered perception mechanism through which firm-side expenditures shape employee organizational commitment. Drawing on signaling and sensemaking perspectives, we argue that visible and costly investments in compensation competitiveness and training intensity shape employees’ commitment by elevating perceived human capital competitiveness, defined as employees’ comparative belief that the organization can attract, develop, and retain high-quality talent. Using linked employer-employee panel data from the Human Capital Corporate Panel (2020-2023) for manufacturing firms, we estimate a moderated mediation model with firm-year equalizing weights and year fixed effects. We find support for the mediation pathway, and we observe that firm-year information transparency attenuates the perceived competitiveness-commitment association, consistent with informational substitution. Firm-cluster bootstrap tests show that the indirect effect remains positive under both low and high transparency.

Organizational Talent Management Processes are no Longer Fair: The Pre–TM Opportunity Architecture

HR
Kousay Abid (University of Doha for Science & Technology); Thomas Garavan
Talent management (TM) has been heralded as a contributor to organizational performance. However, the conclusive findings of theoretical and empirical studies continue to privilege the bright side of TM and render the need for a consolidation of theory that has so far relied on positive assumptions of justice and meritocracy. Thus, we contrast this conventional view that masks a critical theoretical blind spot, that of the informal opportunity structures that precede and shape formal TM processes. Drawing on social stratification theory, boundary work theory, and informal networks perspective, we challenge the foundational assumption that TM processes consistently operate as fair and meritocratic systems. Through critical analysis, we introduce and theorize the pre-TM opportunity architecture. We then reveal three interconnected mechanisms through which this architecture systematically produces the stabilizes inequality: (1) informal networks functioning as gatekeeping infrastructure that determines who becomes visible, receives sponsorship, and accumulates developmental advantages before formal identification occurs; (2) gender-based structural disadvantages embedded in network segregation, sponsorship gaps, and stereotypical evaluations; and (3) exclusive TM practices that function as opportunity hoarding mechanisms, institutionalizing upstream advantage as formal talent status. Our integrative framework demonstrates that access to “talent” status is not determined by merit alone but is fundamentally shaped by relational capital, structural positioning, and boundary-making processes that operate well before formal TM identification begins. We contribute to critical TM scholarship by reconceptualizing talent eligibility as socially constructed through invisible relational mechanisms and outline implications for research and practice. We theorize that by the time formal TM systems evaluate individuals, network-based advantages have already produced observable capability differences that appear to validate meritocratic claims while actually reflecting structural privilege. We outline future research avenues with practical and policy implications and call for a fundamental reconceptualization of meritocracy in talent systems that addresses invisible relational mechanisms rather than merely refining formal evaluation processes.
