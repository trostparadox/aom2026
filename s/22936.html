Strategic Momentum in Organizational Growth Process: Evidences from the Chinese Real Estate Sector

ODC
Xuan Sun (University of Electronic Science and Technology of China)
Strategic momentum has long been recognized as a powerful force shaping organizational growth trajectories, manifesting through behavioral and structural mechanisms. However, existing research has tended to examine these dimensions in isolation, hindering a comprehensive understanding of the persistence and evolution of organizational strategies. This paper addresses the limitation by exploring the following puzzle: How does the complexity among a firm’s accumulated strategic choices shape the emergence and development of strategic momentum? To answer it, we develop and empirically test a framework that reconceptualizes strategic momentum as a dynamic and curvilinear phenomenon shaped by structural complexity. Specifically, this study employs a panel dataset on Chinese public real estate firms’ investment decisions to examine how the complexity of a firm’s concurrent investments within a submarket moderates the U-shaped relationship between its prior investment experience and current investment propensity. Crucially, the overall complexity of the established investment activity system moderates this curve by flattening the U-shaped relationship and shifting the turning point to the left.

Where to Next and How? How Firms Reconstruct Imprints when Growth Renders Them Deficient

ODC
Ivana Milosevic (College of Charleston); Erin Bass (University of Nebraska at Omaha)
While imprinting theory has extensively examined how imprints form and persist over time, less is known about how they evolve when growth renders them deficient. This study examines how imprinted organizing processes evolved through an ethnographic exploration of two growth-induced painful situations a tech firm navigated over 14 years. Using extensive archival, interview, and observational data, we develop a process model that explains the imprint reconstruction as a distinct path of imprint evolution occurring in response to growth-induced painful situations. Specifically, the model illustrates that growth generates a loop of reinforcing and negative feedback signals, pushing the firm into growth-induced painful situations. These painful situations trigger reflective moments through which employees recognize deficiencies in the imprinted organizing processes and then selectively contract, expand, or extend visible elements of the imprints to mend these deficiencies while preserving their core organizing logic. In analyzing this process, we advance imprinting theory by identifying the feedback loops between imprinted processes and their manifestations as triggers to imprint evolution, as well as imprint reconstruction as its distinct path. The emerging insights suggest that repeated cycles of imprint reconstruction can be a valuable tool for navigating painful situations in growth while simultaneously enabling the firm to retain its core organizing logic.

Built to Adapt: Relational Enablers of Dynamic Capabilities in Uncertain Environments

ODC
Sun Hye Lee (University of Surrey); Jungho Suh (Gachon University); Young Jun Han
This study examines how firms develop and deploy agility during periods of external turbulence. Specifically, we explore how organizations adapt to rapidly changing environments and exploit emerging opportunities through the strategic use of their resources. Using the COVID-19 pandemic as the empirical context, we analyze successful adaptation processes and the key challenges organizations faced during the crisis. Our findings demonstrate that relational capital—manifested through trust-based formal and informal networks with key stakeholders and collaborative exchanges—plays a central role in enabling firms to sense, seize, and reconfigure in response to environmental turbulence. Credible and timely information transmitted through these networks strengthens sensing capabilities, while direct and indirect enabling mechanisms activate both seizing and reconfiguring by enabling prompt, bold action and continuous reshaping of organizational practices. Based on in-depth interviews with industry practitioners in South Korea, we show that relational capital constitutes a critical foundation of firm agility grounded in dynamic capabilities. Building on these insights, we propose a conceptual model that explicates the antecedents of dynamic capabilities that foster organizational agility in times of crisis. This study contributes to the dynamic capabilities literature by deepening understanding of how firms sustain agility, navigate external uncertainties, and enhance long-term competitiveness.
