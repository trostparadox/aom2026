From Control to Attunement: Designing Innovation Policy for Fragmented Ecosystems

TIM
Zhe Cao (The University of Queensland)
Innovation policy has evolved through successive paradigms, addressing market failure, building national innovation systems, and steering transformative change through missions. While analytically distinct, these approaches share a foundational assumption: that innovation environments are sufficiently coherent and institutionally complete for coordination to be mobilized through markets, systems, or mission-oriented governance. Yet in emerging fields such as synthetic biology, governance breakdowns increasingly reflect a different condition, one in which actors are interdependent but unable to orient collectively because the institutional architectures that enable responsiveness, legitimacy, and shared sensemaking are weak, absent, or contested. This paper introduces ecosystem coordination failure as a meso-level governance condition distinct from market, system, or transformational failures. Rather than treating coordination as a downstream implementation challenge, the paper reframes it as a first-order policy design problem under institutional incompleteness. Drawing on qualitative fieldwork in Australia’s synthetic biology ecosystem and integrating innovation ecosystem and transformative innovation literatures, the paper advances an attunement-oriented theory of ecosystem governance, in which the goal is to cultivate resonance – the capacity for heterogeneous actors, infrastructures, and value systems to become mutually responsive without requiring consensus, stable roles, or centralized control. The paper develops a nested heuristic framework comprising five design heuristics across structural, processual, and motivational domains. Together, these heuristics offer a design logic for governing innovation under fragmentation, pluralism, and uncertainty, advancing a fourth paradigm of innovation policy centered on enabling attunement rather than enforcing coherence.
