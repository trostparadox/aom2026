Designing a Life: Career Crafting, Self-Authorship, and Identity Transitions

Tuesday, Aug 4 2026 · 1:15PM–2:45PM
Pennsylvania Convention Center — 105B
MED

People

Moderator: Allan Grogan Sam Houston State University

Session overview

In an era of portfolio careers and unprecedented occupational uncertainty, how do students and professionals construct purposeful identities, and what role can management education play? This session examines the agentic dimensions of student and professional development: career crafting among working students who navigate competing responsibilities, Life Design as a constructivist framework for narrative identity construction, liminal identity transitions in doctoral education, how learning contract conditions shape self-efficacy and development intentions, and how identity salience itself acts as a gating mechanism determining whether new skills translate into real-world performance. A session for scholars who place student agency at the center of management education.

Papers (4)

Working While Studying: How Career Crafting Promotes Positive Outcomes

MED
Allan Grogan (Sam Houston State University); Huda Masood (Sam Houston State University); Jason Joan Riley (Appalachian State University)
Working students encounter numerous obstacles and conflict when among their work and school responsibilities leading to negative career and academic-related outcomes. Little, however, is known about how this segment of the population may succeed in their career and academic goals. The current research seeks to examine how employed students proactively self-manage their careers through career crafting behaviors to attain success in both career and university. Utilizing the domain satisfaction framework of social cognitive career theory (SCCT), the current study sought to investigate how career crafting, a proactive career development behavior is related to employed students’ career satisfaction and university commitment. Structural equation modeling on time-lagged surveys (N=275) collected from employed students showed that career crafting is related to career satisfaction and university commitment both directly and through work-school balance (WSB). Our findings elucidate the significance of proactivity in career management to enhance career and academic outcomes as well as the need to promote WSB for working students. In doing so, we advance scholarship on career crafting among employed students while extending the utility of SCCT by applying the domain satisfaction lens. Implications geared toward facilitating university commitment and satisfaction among this segment of the working population are subsequently discussed.

Agentic Identity Workspaces: A Theory-Building Review of Life Design Interventions

MED
Radek Oros (Nova School of Business and Economics); Samantha Su-Hsien Sim (New University of Lisbon); Sebastian Kernbach (University of St. Gallen)
How can management education develop students' capacity to navigate careers that cannot be planned in advance? We address this question through a theory-building systematic review of Life Design—a constructivist approach to career development emphasizing narrative identity construction. Analyzing 120 peer-reviewed studies encompassing 3,713 participants across 20 countries (2008–2025), we identify a striking paradox: Life Design has been extensively studied in counseling and general educational contexts, yet business schools—arguably the settings most theoretically aligned with Life Design's foundations—remain entirely unexamined. Our analysis reveals that identity and narrative outcomes dominate the evidence base (63.33% of studies), exceeding even career adaptability measures—a pattern that fundamentally supports conceptualizing these interventions through an identity lens. We leverage these findings to develop agentic identity workspaces—a theoretical framework explaining how career interventions construct internalized capabilities for identity work that individuals carry across institutional contexts. Extending identity workspace theory, we propose three mechanisms— scripting, sense-making, and self-projecting—through which Life Design interventions build portable career adaptive capacity. We conclude with implications for management education practice and a research agenda addressing critical gaps.

Accounting for Practice: Expanding the Scope of Liminal Residing During Doctoral Education

MED
A Rostron (University of Liverpool); Paul Ellwood (Maynooth University)
Despite the broad ‘practice turn’ in business schools in which curricula, accreditation and funding increasingly valorise research relevance and societal impact, doctoral education largely remains organized around disciplinary theory-building and academic publication. This is problematical given that most doctoral graduates pursue careers in practice and policy rather than as tenure-track academics. In this conceptual paper we critique pedagogies of doctoral education which narrowly frame it as a rite of passage for candidates to master a scholarly discipline in preparation for an academic role, and which characterise the candidate as novice and the supervisor as master. In its place we conceptualise doctoral education as proceeding through a relational and reflexive pedagogy in which respective spheres of expertise in academic scholarship and context-specific practice are brought together. To do so we extend theories of identity work and liminality as they have previously been applied to doctoral education in two ways: as mutually constitutive processes of identity work and identity transition for both candidate and supervisor, in which each is enabled to embed and integrate new expertise; and whereby the goal of doctoral education is not the completion of a liminal transition but the expansion of liminal residing, characterized by the ability and willingness to generatively trouble one’s sphere of expertise through dialogue with the other. The pedagogy we advance positions doctoral education as a potential ‘engine’ for business schools’ engagement with practice, and as such we also sketch out implications for the design and evaluation of doctoral programmes, bringing doctoral education into conversations around innovative pedagogies, research impact, faculty growth and organisational learning and change within HE institutions.

When Coaching Doesn't Cash Out: Identity Salience and the Social Gate on AI-Taught Skills

MED
Xianglu Tang (Hong Kong Baptist University); Becca (Yihan) Wang (University of California Irvine); Xin Lucy Liu (Columbia Business School)
Large language models enable friction free coaching at scale, but a core question remains under answered: when do AI taught skills survive the jump from practice to real performance, and for whom? We examine an LLM driven negotiation tutor that delivers diagnostic feedback by identifying dialogue level negotiation errors with at least 90% accuracy. Across two studies, we uncover a sharp discontinuity between learning and payoff. In a practice context designed to minimize identity cueing, diagnostic feedback disproportionately improves performance for East Asian and White women, narrowing gaps. However, introducing a single identity cue, telling participants that their counterpart is a White man, changes the conversion of learning into economic returns. Learning persists as errors decline, yet outcome gains fragment along gender by ethnicity lines. These findings demonstrate that AI coaching can teach enacted skills, but whether those skills pay off depends on social context and identity salience. We conclude by outlining an identity savvy design agenda for AI coaching as socio technical infrastructure for equitable talent development.
