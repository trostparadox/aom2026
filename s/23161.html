Knowledge & Problem-Niches: How Metaknowledge Architecture Shapes Online Knowledge Production

CTO
Leo Pick Allen (HEC Paris); Sri Kudaravalli (HEC Paris)
How does an online community’s emergent epistemic structure shape its knowledge production outcomes? Drawing on population ecology, category theory, and online community research, this paper argues that communities’ metaknowledge architecture—the sociotechnical ensemble of classifying and organizing affordances that members use to orient their consumption and contribution of content—embeds a landscape of emergent problem-niches at the community level. Characterizing this landscape in terms of (a) the number of problem-niches and (b) their “sharpness" (i.e., internal cohesion, vs “fuzziness"), we hypothesize that those features of the problem-niche landscape engender a community-level tradeoff between speed and coverage. We operationalize communities’ landscape of problem-niches by applying the Leiden community detection algorithm to community-period co-tagging networks observed across 173 Stack Exchange communities over 183 months. Communities with a landscape of many, fuzzy problem-niches tend to produce accepted answers more quickly, but for a smaller share of questions, than communities with a landscape of few, sharp problem-niches. Theory about categories and niches is adapted to the emergent, revisable, implicitly negotiated structures that characterize online knowledge work.

Key Elements in Strategically Formulated Design Challenges for Value-Creating AI Innovation

CTO
Franziska Anna Roeckel (University of Liechtenstein); Benjamin van Giffen (University of Liechtenstein); Andreas Janson (University of St. Gallen); Jennifer Hehn (Bern University of Applied Sciences)
Artificial Intelligence (AI) innovations offer substantial value-creation opportunities, yet organizations frequently struggle to initiate them successfully. AI innovation is particularly difficult to organize and manage due to technological uncertainty and the need for interdisciplinary collaboration. A central but undertheorized artefact is the Design Challenge; the strategic problem formulation that defines innovation goals, aligns diverse stakeholders, and guides subsequent activities. Consequently, its strategic formulation constitutes a core managerial task shaping and directing innovation activities. Despite this importance, research has largely overlooked how Design Challenges are formulated and how they shape AI innovation. To address this gap, we conceptualize the Design Challenge as a boundary object and examine how its strategic formulation enables and steers AI innovation. We conduct a cross-sectional analysis of 42 design challenges from corporate AI innovation projects. Our analysis identifies seven key elements – verb, object of innovation, target group, context, internal data, technology and potential – that structure Design Challenges and balance robustness and interpretive flexibility. These insights advance the understanding of the Design Challenge as a managerial instrument. The results provide also a tangible schema for practitioners to enhance the likelihood of value-creating AI innovations.

Bridges and Chewing Gum: Understanding Boundary Spaces at the Intersection of Work and Technology

CTO
Gianna Raible (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Lauren Waardenburg (ESSEC Business School); Melissa Twemlow (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Maria Tims
Organizations, by design, have knowledge boundaries separating the communities within them. Such boundaries are commonly viewed as dividing lines between differing languages, expertises, and practices. With the aim of unpacking how boundary spanners attune their actions to the temporal flow along such knowledge boundaries, we present a single-case study about an intermediary department at a German insurance company. This department is tasked with connecting specialist areas and the IT department during project work, as well as pursuing overarching organizational goals. We find that this department, over time, developed two ‘attuning practices’, i.e., sensemaking and translating, through which they set the margins of the technical and specialist domains for each project. This created a boundary space, which they subsequently filled by enacting mediating practices. We propose to conceptualize boundaries as the space between the margins of two domains that do not overlap, the distance between which is highly dependent on the lines of actions of each domain, and which requires constant attunement from boundary spanners.

Knowledge Boundaries in Science—A Literature Review of How GenAI Shapes Boundary Spanning Mechanisms

CTO
Lebogang Nthoiwa (University of Warwick); Susanne Beck (Warwick Business School); Joe Nandhakumar (Warwick Business Schoool)
Scientific progress is increasingly associated with collaborations spanning diverse expertise and knowledge domains as a key lever to producing more novel breakthroughs. Yet, it is the knowledge distance that creates boundaries and makes these collaborations difficult. Thus, the very diversity that enables novelty simultaneously introduces friction, misunderstanding, and coordination challenges. This structured literature review synthesises how scholars across disciplines use generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) to overcome knowledge-distance-related challenges in scientific collaboration, while also surfacing potentials and drawbacks. Drawing on information systems literature, we conceptualise GenAI as a technology that may boost or undermine the power of boundary spanning mechanisms (i.e., translate, transfer, or transform) to overcome syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic knowledge boundaries in distant collaborations in science. We identify and analyse 44 articles published since 2022 that have used GenAI in distant collaborations or researched its role for such collaborations. Our synthesis shows that GenAI does not eliminate knowledge boundaries but reshapes how they are crossed by influencing how boundary spanning mechanisms are enacted, negotiated, and sometimes obscured. By positioning GenAI as a boundary spanning technology, this review contributes to research on information systems and the organisation of science and develops an interdisciplinary research agenda for understanding how GenAI reshapes distant scientific collaboration.

“Selling Dreams While Nothing Works” - Data Practitioners’ Dual-Position Brokerage in Organization

CTO
Menghan Cui (UCL School of Management); Amit Nigam (City - University of London); Aneesh Banerjee (Bayes Business School)
This paper investigates how a nascent occupation establishes organizational jurisdiction through dual-position brokerage. Focusing on data practitioners— a growing field where members of nascent occupation use data analytics to inform decision-making across business units— we address the overlooked role of multiple actors within jurisdictional contestation. While jurisdictional contests are often framed as conflicts between two groups, this study reveals how brokerage mediates persistent tensions within and across professions, particularly when status differences are not formally institutionalized. Limited research has examined how brokerage, influenced by dual social positions, supports jurisdictional claims over desirable work. Drawing on 18 months of fieldwork across six organizations, this study demonstrates empirically how data consultants - members of this nascent occupation, operating as organizational outsiders, foster collaboration amid conflicts to facilitate establishing jurisdictional claims of local data teams.
