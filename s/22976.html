The Contested Role of Deadlines in Event-Time-Based-Contexts

OMT
Carolin Müller (Witten/Herdecke University)
Deadlines are widely used to coordinate organizational activity and often function as temporal boundary objects. While prior research has examined their use in clock time-based settings, little is known about their role in event time-based contexts. This study explores how agile teams – characterized by cyclical temporal patterns that embody an event time conception of time – respond to deadlines imposed by their non-agile organizational context. Based on a multiple case study of eight agile teams in financial services, findings reveal three response patterns: shifting to plan-based practices, negotiating deadlines, and enacting supplementary flexibilization practices. The study contributes to research on entrainment and boundary objects.

Temporality as a Strategic Resource: How Actors Mobilize Time in Framing Contests

OMT
Shu-Chen Chang (National Yunlin University of Science and Technology); Hua-Wei Hung (National Tsing Hua University); Shih-Chang Hung (National Tsing Hua University)
How do actors strategically mobilize time in framing contests? Research on strategic framing shows how actors craft messages to mobilize support and legitimacy, yet it has largely treated temporality as a background condition rather than as a mechanism of contestation. We address this gap by theorizing temporality as a strategic, interactional mechanism through which actors advance, defend, and challenge frames in contested arenas. Drawing on a mixed-methods study of repeated confrontations among political actors in a highly politicized setting, we examine how framing contests unfold across distinct actor–audience interfaces over time. Combining topic modeling of longitudinal parliamentary debates with qualitative analysis, we identify five temporal framing moves—retrospective, progressive, juxtaposed, issue-centered, and contrastive—that function as contest tactics for allocating responsibility, managing accountability, and legitimating or destabilizing trajectories of action. We further show that the deployment of these temporal framing moves varies systematically with actors’ interactional positions and political alignments, revealing distinct temporal logics of counterframing and frame reinforcement. By conceptualizing temporality as an interactional mechanism of strategic framing, this study advances research on framing contests, temporal work, and strategic communication, highlighting how struggles over meaning are inseparable from struggles over time.

How Modes of Eigenzeit Form, Develop, Change and Persist in Processes of Temporal Structuring

OMT
Carolin Müller (Witten/Herdecke University); Mark Ebers (University of Cologne)
Blagoev and Schreyögg (2025) have recently proposed a theoretical consolidation of research on how organizations navigate temporal complexity by what they call Eigenzeit modes (entrainment, ambitemporality, pluritemporality, and agility). In order to complement their largely comparative-static explanation for the different Eigenzeit modes, they call for further research that sheds light on conditions and processes that might help to explain when and why Eigenzeit modes might change or persist. Answering this call, we report findings from longitudinal, multi-actor, episodic case analyses of how agile units respond to the temporal complexities of operating in a non-agile organizational context by enacting particular Eigenzeit modes. We find that Eigenzeit modes do not always result in stability; rather, they can be repeatedly unmade and remade in response to evolving temporal tensions. The results of present study complement Blagoev and Schreyögg’s (2025) theorizing by illuminating practices of temporal (re-)structuring of Eigenzeit modes. We extend their theorizing by offering an episodic process model that captures endogenously and exogenously driven dynamics of Eigenzeit modes and explanations for the change and persistence of Eigenzeit modes over time.

Temporality in Team Boundary Spanning Research: A Systematic Review and Integrative Framework

OMT
Shaoyang Li (Monash University); Mariano L.M. Heyden (Monash University); Véronique Ambrosini (Monash University)
Team boundary spanning (TBS) refers to the activities work teams use to manage relationships and interactions with outsiders, both within and outside their home organization. While TBS is vital for performance, research remains fragmented on how teams effectively organize TBS. We systematically review 69 studies from the past 15 years, identifying three predominant approaches. Social cognitive approach examines how boundary spanners’ personality differences and self-regulation shape teams’ construal of and responses to environmental dynamism over time. Social structural approach investigates how consistency across boundary spanners’ role-specific networks reduces cognitive loads and retains TBS effectiveness at discrete moments in time and context. Practice-based approach foregrounds ongoing negotiation and situated adjustment of boundary objects, explaining how shared environmental understandings are transformed in dynamic environments. These streams converge on pooling individual cognitive resources (e.g., time, attention) but diverge on whether and how these resources are constrained and allocated. Synthesizing these approaches, we develop a dynamic framework that reconceptualizes the organization of TBS as a process of aligning the gradual development of individual environmental interpretations with the periodic reconfiguration of shared team cognitive scaffolds (e.g., common lexicons, interpretive categories) that evolve at different speeds across dynamic environments. We outline how team leaders and intra-team processes can facilitate this temporal coordination of TBS with limited member cognitive resources, offering a roadmap for future research into the temporal dynamics of TBS effectiveness.
