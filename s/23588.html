Beyond Codification: Generative AI and the Integration of Tacit Knowledge in Organizations

TIM
Aiden Zheqi Xu (Peking University); Kun Zhang (Peking University); Luye Bao (Peking University); Yuchen Wang (University of Cambridge); Wei Wei (Peking University)
Generative artificial intelligence is transforming how organizations manage knowledge, but its implications for knowledge integration have yet to be fully understood. This study examines how generative AI enables organizations to integrate tacit knowledge at scale and identifies the conditions under which these approaches work well. We conducted a grounded theory study with 66 semi-structured interviews across 52 organizations implementing generative AI in knowledge-intensive work. Our analysis reveals a three-stage process: organizational preparation, AI-enabled integration, and capability emergence. We find that pattern-based mediation serves as a key mechanism in the integration stage—AI learns context-action patterns from observing how experts work and then applies this knowledge throughout the organization. This differs from traditional approaches that require experts to explicitly document their knowledge or spend extensive time coordinating with others. Our findings suggest this mechanism offers organizations new ways to leverage deep expertise more broadly while maintaining its strategic value. However, success depends on specific boundary conditions. Our study contributes to understanding how generative AI reshapes organizational knowledge integration possibilities while specifying where these mechanisms work effectively and where they face constraints.

Clockwork PhDs? What Doctoral Innovation Systems Reveal about Time-to-Degree

TIM
Karine Cécile Revet
Delays in completing a doctorate reflect not only individual challenges, but also structural inefficiencies in the organization of doctoral work within research systems. While previous studies have examined individual determinants within single institutions, there is still a lack of system-level evidence on how the social organization of the doctorate affects completion time. We conceptualize doctoral training as part of a doctoral innovation system and examine how supervisory design, networks, peer environments, partnerships, and disciplinary regimes jointly shape completion time. We test these mechanisms using administrative data from theses.fr covering 61,124 doctoral theses defended in France between 2008 and 2023, for which complete time-to-degree data are available. Accelerated failure time models with a log-normal specification are estimated as the main approach. Results show that co-supervision shortens doctoral duration, while heavy supervisory workloads and highly constrained network structures delay completion. Network centrality benefits progress mainly in SSH, whereas structural constraints hinder it in STEM fields. Completion time is also influenced by gender configurations, thesis format, and linguistic or international context, with SSH theses generally taking longer and being more sensitive to relational and organizational factors than STEM ones. These findings emphasize that doctoral outcomes are shaped by organizational and network configurations rather than solely by individual factors. They also highlight actionable levers that supervisors, doctoral schools and research policymakers can use to improve the efficiency, equity and transparency of doctoral education.

Innovation Under Resource Constraints: Supercomputing in Scientific Research

TIM
Justine Boudou (Harvard Business School); John McKeon (Syracuse University)
Should organizations concentrate scarce resources among a few knowledge workers or distribute them more broadly? The answer to this question depends on whether additional resources enable more ambitious work or simply allow lower-priority projects to move forward. We examine how the direction of innovative output changes as resource constraints are relaxed in the context of high-performance computing (HPC), a critical input to modern scientific research. Using data from XSEDE, an NSF-funded program that allocates supercomputing resources to researchers, we leverage system-wide capacity constraints to identify the causal impact of variation in resource constraints on scientific output. We find that relaxing constraints increases the number of publications and shifts the direction of research. Scientists pursue less popular and newer topics, explore areas beyond their prior expertise, and broaden the scope of their work. However, these directional shifts are associated with fewer citations, suggesting a trade-off between frontier-expanding innovation and impact. Our findings show that allocation strategies shape not just the volume but the trajectory of innovation, with direct implications for R&D managers and policymakers supporting innovation under resource constraints.

Tainted but Not Abandoned: How Status Buffers Competence-Based Stigma in Innovation Networks

TIM
Kiran Awate
Scholarship on organizational stigma has focused predominantly on moral transgressions, developing a character-revelation model that treats adverse events as disclosing hidden dispositional information. We extend this framework to competence-based stigma—the taint following technical failures that signal diminished capability rather than deficient character. We introduce attributional decoupling as the mechanism through which networks process such failures: when high-status actors experience competence-based stigma, networks encode the event as situational noise rather than dispositional signal, preserving collaborative ties that would otherwise dissolve. This implies a signaling switch: status operates as liability under integrity-revealing stigma but as buffer under competence-based stigma. We test these arguments using a difference-in-differences design applied to pharmaceutical inventors whose patents were challenged in federal courts or the Patent Trial and Appeal Board between 2009 and 2022. Analyzing over 20,000 inventor-period observations, we find that patent litigation precipitates substantial network erosion—a 30 percent reduction in brokerage and 42 percent decline in collaborative ties—but that superstar inventors are significantly buffered from these effects. Supplementary analyses reveal that inventors with more experienced collaborators exhibit greater resilience. These findings reorient stigma theory from uniform exclusion toward contingent network response, specifying when and how failure translates into relational consequence in innovation ecosystems.
