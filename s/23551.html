The Fragility of Robust Action Strategies: NGOs, Grand Challenges & Strategy Implementation in Slums

OMT
Aloysius Marcus Kahindi (University of Victoria); Andras Tilcsik
Robust action strategies—participatory architectures, multivocal inscriptions, and distributed experimentation—are recognized as effective responses to grand challenges. However, many organizations struggle to implement them. Drawing on a seven-year field study of six nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) in two East African slums, we identify three barriers to implementing robust action strategies: donor pressures, limited personnel continuity, and local ethnic divisions. These barriers limited the extent to which most NGOs in our sample implemented robust action strategies. Two NGOs, however, overcame these barriers through a set of organizational practices: reframing or decoupling donor expectations, investing in staff continuity, and engaging in sustained ethnographic immersion. These findings explain why robust action strategies may be fragile in practice and highlight the organizational practices that support their implementation.

Robust Action Revisited: The Hidden Liability of Legitimacy in Persistent Crisis

OMT
Alfredo Enrione (ESE Business School, Universidad de los Andes); Carmelo Mazza (LUISS University Guido Carli)
Management scholarship has overwhelmingly theorized legitimacy as an organizational asset—something to acquire, maintain, and defend. We challenge this assumption by revealing legitimacy's hidden liability: the consistency that sustains stakeholder trust constrains the flexibility that persistent crisis demands. This tension creates a fundamental dilemma that existing crisis leadership theories, with their implicit assumption of bounded disruption, cannot address. We develop a theory of leadership under persistent crisis by recovering Padgett and Ansell's (1993) structural logic of robust action. Their analysis of Cosimo de Medici demonstrated that the capacity for multi-vocal, ambiguous action emerged not from strategic choice but from structural position. We extend this insight by identifying three structural preconditions that enable robust action in contemporary organizations: centrality in networks of weak ties, boundary-spanning positions, and resource control without commitment. Our central contribution is a process theory specifying how these structural preconditions moderate a flexibility-legitimacy oscillation. We introduce the concept of accumulated inconsistency—the growing stock of apparent contradictions that stakeholders observe when leaders maintain strategic ambiguity, cultivate diverse coalitions, and calibrate disclosure. As inconsistency accumulates, legitimacy erodes until organizations must transition from flexibility-dominant to legitimacy-restoration phases. Four propositions specify how structural preconditions lower inconsistency accumulation rates, raise legitimacy thresholds, improve transition effectiveness, and enable oscillation learning that compounds advantages across cycles. We conclude that effective leadership in persistent crisis is not merely behavioral skill but structural position—and that legitimacy, typically theorized as enabling organizational functioning, becomes a liability when its maintenance requirements foreclose the adaptive capacity that survival demands.

Unions as Institutional Entrepreneurs Amidst Algorithmic Management & The Rise of Alt-Right Politics

OMT
Adarsh Kumar (Friedrich Schiller University Jena)
This paper presents a strategic choice analysis of union strategy in the gig economy of India and Britain. For that, it conducts study of twelve traditional and independent unions representing gig workers from 2022-2023. It relies on semi-structured interviews, review of secondary union related documents and non-participant observation of union organised protests. It argues that despite opaque algorithmic strategy of platforms and anti-union political climate, unions create and combine approaches to remain effective and relevant while bringing organisational and institutional change. In India, use of digital media, grassroot mobilization and lobbying with political parties is a key focus for changing workers’ circumstances. Meanwhile, in Britain, unions are moving past court-based efforts to emphasize collective bargaining, grassroots organizing, and direct action. Paper asserts that exercise of agency and associated institutional change is complex and interdependent phenomenon that depends upon micro-meso and macro environment of unions. It suggests that agency is both spatially distributed across organisational levels and temporally differentiated. It by studying marginalised actors such as Unions, advances organisation study, particularly, institutional entrepreneurship literature beyond elite, powerful actors such as MNCs. Moreover, by comparing union choices in developed and developing economy it offers a holistic picture of how unions navigate political pressures, resource constraints, and rapid technological shifts across borders in a dematerialized world of work to ensure gig workers’ rights in the global gig economy, contributing to platform economy research.

Adaptive Mission Pivoting: How Firms Navigate Mission-Action Alignment in Continuous Crisis

OMT
Jan-Friedrich Kulp (ESCP-Europe Business School - Berlin); Sönke Mestwerdt (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester); Trenton A. Williams (Brigham Young University); Jill R. Kickul (EGADE Business School); Dean Shepherd (University of Notre Dame)
Organizational missions guide strategic change, yet firms increasingly operate under conditions of continuous crisis. While prior research has focused on episodic disruptions, little is known about how persistent, open-ended disruption reshapes sensemaking, mission, and organizational adaptation. This study introduces adaptive mission pivoting to explain how organizations maintain legitimacy and coherence under continuous conditions. Drawing on 107 interviews with leaders across 87 Ukrainian firms operating amid wartime uncertainty, we develop a process model of adaptive mission pivoting, revealing how organizations iteratively interpret crises, pivot their missions, and transform their organizations. We identify two trajectories: Frontliners, who expand missions toward societal contribution, and Stabilizers, who narrow missions toward organizational continuity. First, we conceptualize mission pivots as legitimacy-enhancing responses that function as an adaptive anchor for organizational renewal under conditions of a continuous crisis. Second, we reframe entrepreneurial pivoting by positioning mission pivots as higher-order adaptations that redefine why organizations change, preceding strategic transformation that optimize what they do. Third, we extend sensemaking theory by demonstrating how continuous crises transform sensemaking from sequential equilibrium restoration into an ongoing, recursive process without closure.

Privatizing the Law? Contestation and Influence in Investor-State Disputes

OMT
Sukanya Roy (University College London); Maxim Sytch (University of Michigan); Jose Uribe (Kelley School of Business, Indiana University)
How organizations engage with the law is increasingly seen as a dynamic process in which firms not only comply with legal authority but also contest and shape it. Yet research has paid limited attention to direct disputes between firms and governments, where sovereign authority and private interests collide. We theorize and test the construct of asymmetric embeddedness as a structural mechanism shaping how such contests unfold. Unlike traditional accounts that emphasize ties to specific actors, asymmetric embeddedness captures adjudicators’ repeated alignment with one role (i.e., representing investors or states) in a contested arena. We develop this construct through the tension arbitrators face between appearing impartial in a given dispute and securing future appointments. Building on investors’ advantage in initiating such disputes, we argue that arbitrators with deeper histories of investor appointment are more likely to be selected as tribunal presidents, to favor investors in dispute decisions, and to award them greater damages. Evidence from analyzing 855 investor–state disputes between 1987 and 2020, supplemented by 27 interviews and 60 hours of fieldwork, supports our theory. These dynamics reveal how adjudicators embedded in transnational professional networks systematically align dispute outcomes with private interests, thereby privatizing the law and challenging core assumptions about the sovereignty of nation-states in global governance.
