Employee Referral in Digitally Expanded Networks: Exploring the Role of Idealized Self-Presentation

HR
Fangsheng Zhu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Yipeng Tang (Zhongnan University of Economics and Law); Shuhong Wang (Zhongnan University of Economics and Law); Xiaoping Pu (Wuhan University of Technology); Yizhen Cao (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Employee referral has traditionally relied on referrers’ knowledge of candidates within bounded social networks. The expansion of online networks, however, has altered the informational context of referral decisions, exposing employees to ties evaluated with limited direct interaction. This study examines how referees’ online self-presentation shapes employee referral decisions in digitally mediated networks. Drawing on impression management and trust-based risk assessment perspectives, we theorize that idealized self-presentation exerts a double-edged influence by increasing competence-based trustworthiness yet decreasing integrity-based trustworthiness, and thus affecting employee referral decision; and, these effects depend on tie strength. Using a two-wave survey, we find that potential candidates’ idealized self-presentation has direct positive effect on referral behavior but undermines integrity-based trustworthiness, generating a negative indirect effect on referral behavior. Tie strength moderates these relationships such that idealized self-presentation enhances competence-based trustworthiness and referral behavior for weak ties, but erodes trust and referral likelihood for strong ties. These findings extend employee referral literature to online networks and illuminate how referrers evaluate candidates and assess referral risk.

The Two Sides of a Coin: How Job Applicants’ Pride Expressions Shape Hiring Decision

HR
Xiujuan Wang (Nanjing Audit University); Liwei Feng (Nanjing Audit University); Yanfen Wang (University of Science and Technology of China)
Personnel selection decisions are critical to organizational efficiency and success, and job interviews serve as the primary venue for such evaluations. As signaling games, interviews involve candidates transmitting various cues to influence recruiters’ judgments. Although signals of competence are critical, emotional expressions constitute another channel through which candidates influence interpersonal judgments and, ultimately, hiring outcomes. To reveal the critical impact of candidates’ emotional expression on personnel selection, building on the emotions as social information theory, we theorize that candidates’ authentic versus hubristic pride expressions influence hireability through a chain mediation in which candidates’ pride expression first affects recruiters’ interpersonal liking, which subsequently shapes their perceptions of competence; further, we argue that recruiters’ moral attentiveness moderates the initial pride–liking link. We tested this conceptual model by a scenario-based experiment. The results showed that authentic pride expression enhanced hireability by increasing interpersonal liking and, in turn, perceived competence, with this process amplified among recruiters high in moral attentiveness. In contrast, hubristic pride expression reduced hireability by lowering interpersonal liking and, in turn, perceived competence, with this process amplified among recruiters high in moral attentiveness. Overall, this research demonstrates that candidates’ pride expressions shape hiring decisions by triggering recruiters’ affective reactions and cognitive evaluations, offering new insights into the interpersonal dynamics of personnel selection.

Signalling Agile Working in Stacked Recruitment Rhetorics: A Multi-Method Qualitative Enquiry

HR
Fangyuan Shen (Manchester Metropolitan University); XIRONG LI (Manchester University); Cara Mulholland (Manchester Metropolitan University); David Bamford; Iain Reid (Manchester Metropolitan University)
Job postings are a critical yet understudied signalling platform through which organizations communicate role identity to prospective employees. Drawing on signalling theory and management fashion literature, we examine how firms use agile working rhetorics in recruitment and how these signals shape interpretations. Using an abductive, multi-method design, we analyze 608 job advertisements and conduct a vignette experiment with 96 agile professionals. Our findings introduce signal stacking, the co-occurrence of multiple signals within a job post, as a structural attribute of recruitment communication. We identify three stacking mechanisms (explanatory, complementary, and contradictory)that influence signal ambiguity and prospective employees’ sensemaking. We theorize a recursive signalling process in which ambiguity during recruitment affects identity construction and probe subsequent employee responses, including identity correction or distancing. By conceptualizing agile working rhetorics as symbolic and informational signals, this study extends signalling theory to dynamic, multi-layered recruitment contexts and reengages with management fashion theory to explain how fashionable practices shape organizational identity claims. We offer implications for designing clearer recruitment communication and outline future research on evolving work practices.
