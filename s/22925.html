When the Kitchen Stopped Being a Battlefield: Building an Inclusive and Safe Workspace in Alamesa

OMT
Tomas Farchi (HEC Paris); Pablo Daniel Fernandez (IAE BUSINESS SCHOOL - AUSTRAL UNIVERSITY); Melodie Cartel; Natalia Weisz (IAE BUSINESS SCHOOL - AUSTRAL UNIVERSITY)
In this paper, we examine how a socially innovative solution built an inclusive organizational space to integrate neurodiverse individuals. Focusing on Alamesa, a restaurant in Buenos Aires that employs a majority of neurodiverse staff, we examine how a dissonant metaphor fostered radical redesigns of organizational spaces, artifacts, practices, and experiences. Our findings reveal, first, how traditional understanding of a kitchen as a “battlefield” was displaced. Second, this clearing allowed new routines and roles to take shape in practice. Heidegger’s notion of dwelling helps us see what this built space afforded: a workspace where coworkers could be at peace with things, move without being pulled by stimuli, and learn without their hands being guided. Rather than adding facilitation, Alamesa built infrastructures that make work safer, calmer, and jointly inhabited. This paper offers a novel understanding of how workspace configurations that inhibit the integration of neurodiverse staff can be radically reconceptualized to become inclusive through a specific metaphorical clearing.

The Omitted Space: Enacting Social Inclusion Through Spatial Work

OMT
Jadwiga Karolina Supryn (Saïd Business School, University of Oxford)
This study examines how peripheral organizational actors shape social inclusion by enacting and transforming spaces within contexts marked by inequality. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative investigation of a FinTech organization in Ghana, the research conceptualizes space as an active, relational, and sociomaterial site where inclusion is continuously accomplished rather than statically delivered. Using Schatzki's (2002) notion of the “site of the social” as an analytical lens, the study shows how practitioners situated between institutional centers and marginalized communities create, reconfigure, and connect physical, digital, and institutional spaces to enable participation. These actors engage in spatial work that combines relational practices, material arrangements, and interpretive efforts to translate global technological and financial logics into locally meaningful forms of engagement. The findings reveal that inclusion emerges through interdependencies across spaces: physical encounters ground digital adoption, community trust stabilizes institutional legitimacy, and organizational practices mediate between moral expectations and technological infrastructures. By illuminating how peripheral actors stitch together these diverse spaces, the study reframes inclusion as a process of continual spatial and relational reassembly. This perspective contributes to management research on sociomateriality, peripheral agency, and inclusion by demonstrating how actors at the margins actively reconstitute the infrastructures that make participation possible.

The Myth of Neutrality in Polarized Times: Applying a Power Lens to Unpack Inclusion Experiences

OMT
Linda Jakob Sadeh (Ruppin Academic Center); Johanna Mair (European University Institute)
This paper expands inclusion theory by examining how heightened societal polarization and “mega-threats”— large-scale, identity-relevant societal events — shape workplace experiences of inclusion. We focus on a particularly salient yet under-theorized organizational response: assumed neutrality—the widespread, often implicit belief that organizations can remain apolitical spaces—which serves as the organizational-level context for our theorizing. Drawing on Bourdieu’s concept of symbolic power, we theorize how individuals’ experiences of inclusion in these contexts are shaped by their positions within intersecting organizational and societal symbolic hierarchies, offering a typology of these configurations. Our framework contributes to the Equity Diversity and Inclusion (EDI) literature by problematizing neutrality as a managerial myth and foregrounding the significance of political-ideological identities alongside historically marginalized ones. More broadly, we bring inclusion theory into dialogue with research on societal polarization, revealing how power asymmetries structure organizational life and challenging prevailing views of polarization as a symmetric, two-sided phenomenon.

Techno-Babel: How Participation Architectures Amplify and Temper Polarization

OMT
Felipe Massa (University of Vermont); Evelyn Rita Micelotta (University of Vermont)
This article theorizes how participation architectures — sociotechnical frameworks that provision and mediate social interaction in digital spaces — embed and amplify affective polarization. Extending research that locates polarization in individual psychology or partisan media messaging alone, we argue that polarization emerges from the configuration of architectural, discursive, and affective mechanisms that structure participation itself. We develop a tri-partite framework that explains how (1) architectural mechanisms like algorithmic curation and structural segmentation produce insular publics, (2) discursive mechanisms such as rhetorical policing and stigmatization narrow acceptable speech, and (3) affective mechanisms including emotional validation and identity reinforcement harden group boundaries. These mechanisms create a self-reinforcing cycle in which dissent is stigmatized, exposure to difference is minimized, and group loyalty becomes fused with moral identity. We assess why common interventions to temper polarization often backfire, exacerbating grievance and driving exit rather than fostering inclusion. Our theoretical framework invites management scholars to examine how participation architectures can be reimagined to sustain pluralism in an era of ideological fragmentation. We propose a set of integrative design interventions to temper polarization by rebuilding shared spaces, pluralistic norms, and integrative pathways.

Institutional Renewal Through Space-Making: Reworking Inclusion in Canadian Public Libraries

OMT
Natalia Mityushina (SKOLKOVO Moscow School of Management); Asma Zafar (Brock University); Trish Reay (University of Alberta)
In this paper, we develop a spatial perspective on institutional renewal. Drawing on an inductive, comparative analysis of qualitative data—including interviews, archival documents, and observations from five Canadian public library systems (12 branches)—we show how institutional renewal is accomplished through space-making. We identify two overarching space-making practices: appropriating space as endorsement of renewal and appropriating space as moderator of renewal. These parallel yet intersecting practices balance openness to change with restraint for change by shaping how renewal is materially enacted and experienced. Through different combinations of these practices, we identify four spatial configurations, each associated with a distinct pathway of institutional renewal—in our case, the reshaping of inclusion within public libraries. We contribute to institutional renewal research by foregrounding the material foundations of renewal and explaining variation in renewal outcomes. We also show how configurations of space-making generate distinct experiences of space that channel renewal along different pathways. Finally, we propose a process model of institutional renewal through space-making that highlights how institutions adapt, endure, and remain socially meaningful over time.
