Talking Under Pressure: How Capital Market Feedback Shapes CEOs’ E&S Communication

ONE
Sonja Malik (Georg August University Göttingen); Michelle Lang (Georg August University Göttingen); Michael Wolff (Georg August University Göttingen)
This study examines how CEOs use environmental and social (E&S) communication as a strategic response to legitimacy pressures from the capital market. Building on impression management theory, we conceptualize E&S communication as a dynamic and interactive process shaped through repeated interactions between analysts and CEOs. We theorize a feedback loop in which analysts’ E&S-related questions influence CEOs’ subsequent E&S communication. We further identify two moderators of this feedback process: (1) analysts’ tone, capturing information exchange within conference calls, and (2) dedicated ownership, reflecting information exchange outside the conference call. Using computer-aided textual analysis of quarterly earnings conference call transcripts from U.S. S&P 1500 firms between 2008 and 2020, we provide empirical evidence that CEOs systematically adapt their E&S communication in response to analysts’ feedback. We show that CEOs interpret analysts’ E&S-related questions as signals of legitimacy pressure and adjust their E&S communication accordingly, because ESG claims - unlike most strategic topics - are evaluated simultaneously along performance-based and moral or societal dimensions. Overall, our study contributes to impression management and ESG communication literatures by introducing a dynamic, interaction-based perspective on how legitimacy pressures shape CEOs’ ESG communication in capital markets.

Sustainability Persuasion: A Toolkit for Unlocking Internal Support

ONE
Anthony J. Lockhart (UNSW Sydney); Peter A. Heslin (UNSW Sydney)
Environmental sustainability has moved from the margins to the core of strategic decision-making, yet many credible sustainability proposals still stall inside organizations. This article explains why internal persuasion around sustainability is so difficult and offers a practical framework for moving stakeholders from skepticism to support. Rather than focusing on public opinion, we concentrate on firm-level decisions. Drawing on research in persuasion, judgment and decision-making, social identity, and moral psychology, we synthesize evidence into three clusters of barriers that commonly derail sustainability initiatives: time and risk biases (e.g., temporal discounting, loss aversion), identity and belief defenses (e.g., ideological conflict, motivated reasoning, obstinate personalities), and mechanisms of moral disengagement that mute guilt about potentially environmentally harmful decisions. For each barrier, we translate research into concrete rhetorical strategies that managers and directors can use when framing proposals, structuring decision processes, and shaping conversations. We then outline overarching implementation principles—disciplines of preparation, framing, and timing—that make these strategies more likely to succeed. The article’s contribution is both integrative and applied. It combines fragmented insights into a coherent, inside-the-firm persuasion toolkit and shows how leaders can use psychological science not only to understand resistance, but to design more effective paths toward sustainability-oriented action.

When Doing Good Meets the Headlines: How Strategic CSR Shapes Media Favorability

ONE
Jennifer Karsh (West Virginia University); Olga Bruyaka (West Virginia University); Alanna Hirshman (Texas Tech University); David Dawley (West Virginia University); Anja Nadja Fessmann (University of Passau)
Understanding which corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives generate favorable media attention is essential for firms operating in environmentally sensitive industries such as oil and gas, where public scrutiny is high. This study investigates how three strategic CSR dimensions—centrality, specificity, and visibility—influence media favorability. Drawing on signaling theory, we argue that these dimensions independently and jointly shape media perceptions, with CSR initiatives aligned with firms’ core business activities expected to exert the strongest influence. Using data on 519 CSR initiatives announced by 12 multinational oil and gas firms between 2013 and 2020, we find that centrality generates the most favorable media coverage, followed by specificity, while the effect of visibility is negative. We also show that CSR initiatives do not need to be fully strategic to be effective; rather, the accumulation of strategic attributes—exhibiting at least two or all three dimensions—strengthens the overall signal and increases the likelihood of favorable media framing. Overall, the findings support the core premise of strategic CSR research—that the design of CSR initiatives matters—but challenge assumptions about the relative importance and uniform value of its defining dimensions.

Exploring the Use of Energy-Related Metaphors in UK and Japanese Newspapers

ONE
Elise FRANCOIS (KEDGE Business School - Marseille); Ralf Barkemeyer (KEDGE Business School)
The aim of this paper is to investigate how energy metaphors shape public discourse about fracking in UK and Japanese broadsheet newspapers. It addresses an important research gap in our understanding of environmental discourse by moving beyond the Anglocentric focus of metaphor analysis, exploring cross-linguistic and cross-cultural differences in environmental communication. We investigate metaphor use in Japanese, an underrepresented language. Using a specialized corpus, we examine 8,805 UK-based and 199 Japanese broadsheet and regional newspaper articles published between 2007 and 2024. Metaphors are identified using the Metaphor Identification Procedure developed by the Pragglejaz Group and classified through a Type Hierarchy Approach. Our findings show areas of convergence alongside clear divergence in the use of fracking-related metaphors. Notably, metaphor usage differs between the Japanese outlets depending on the language (Japanese vs English edition). As our main contribution, we advance metaphor theory by empirically demonstrating how metaphor deployment varies with linguistic structure, media system and institutional context.

Climate Change Policy and Climate Risk Disclosure: Evidence from Machine Learning and Text Analysis

ONE
Farhan Hussain (Central South University); Zhifang Zhou (Central South University); Tao Zhang; Mo Yuchen (Central South University)
The gap between climate policy ambitions and corporate accountability remains a defining challenge for emerging economies. This study investigates how national climate change policy (NCCP) influences corporate climate risk disclosure (CRD). We develop a hybrid computational linguistics framework that integrates Word2Vec embeddings with a multi-stage expert validation to build dynamic dictionaries for CRD. Our analysis shows that NCCP significantly increases disclosures related to physical risks. Importantly, this relationship is strongly influenced by analyst coverage and affected by corporate political connections. Firms with policy-relevant connections engage in strategic disclosure management, whereas those with legislative connections show delayed responses. Moreover, firms with high corporate social responsibility (CSR) intensity effectively leverage the policy to enhance transparency. Our findings show how corporate governance and political economy shape the operation of climate policy in fragile institutions. Policymakers and regulatory bodies are encouraged to bridge the gap between policy intent and corporate practice.
