Managing Uncertainty in Generative Artificial Intelligence Systems

CTO
Ingrid Bauer-Hänsel; TC Eley (Case Western Reserve University); Ivo Blohm (University of St. Gallen); Kalle Lyytinen (Case Western Reserve University)
Despite growing interest in Generative AI (GenAI), only a few companies are moving beyond pilots to operational use. A key barrier is the heightened uncertainty GenAI systems introduce due to their non-deterministic nature. Unlike discriminative AI, where uncertainty is primarily epistemic and manageable through traditional testing, GenAI systems exhibit aleatory uncertainty—both human- and machine-induced—that cannot be reduced through more data or deterministic validation. Drawing on uncertainty theory and 37 expert interviews, we compare uncertainty across GenAI and discriminative AI systems, identifying a novel form of machine-induced aleatory uncertainty in GenAI processing. We show that managing GenAI uncertainty requires: (1) a new mindset: reintroducing human oversight to reduce machine variability, (2) new processes: shifting from testing-adoption to restriction-context-narrowing cycles, and (3) new measures: moving from deterministic test cases to semantic similarity and confidence scoring. This study contributes to uncertainty theory, GenAI research, and responsible AI deployment by clarifying novel uncertainties and offering actionable mitigation strategies.

The Algorithmic Skill Acquisition Spiral: Trust, Skills, and Identity in Adaptation

CTO
Fayyaz Khan (Al Yamamah University); Faqir Taj (Al Yamamah University)
Technology adoption is typically conceptualised as progressing through discrete stages with clear endpoints, yet individuals experience adaptation as an ongoing, recursive process. This research develops and empirically grounds the Algorithmic Skill Acquisition Spiral (ASAS), a five-phase framework connecting trust evolution, skill transformation, and professional identity reconstruction as individuals adapt to automation technologies. Drawing on a five-year longitudinal case study of Course Management System implementation at a Pakistani university, we trace how administrative staff navigated adaptation through Initial Encounter (uncertain trust, manual backups), Delegation Expansion (feature-specific trust, selective task delegation), Skill Transformation (the critical fork where upskilling or deskilling occurs), Role Redefinition (identity reconstruction work), and Co-Evolution (dynamic equilibrium with ongoing adaptation). The findings reveal substantial within-organisation heterogeneity in adaptation outcomes. Some individuals experienced upskilling by reinvesting time freed from routine tasks into higher-value coordination work, while others experienced deskilling as traditional competencies atrophied without equivalent new skill development. Crucially, the framework demonstrates that from the user's perspective, the core adaptation processes—trust calibration, skill transformation, and identity work—operate similarly across automation technologies regardless of technical sophistication. Whether facing deterministic enterprise systems or advanced artificial intelligence, users who cannot see inside the "black box" must navigate fundamentally similar psychological and social challenges. The framework thus explains divergent adaptation trajectories through mechanisms of experiential learning, time reinvestment, and social validation of reconstructed identities. Theoretically, the research integrates literatures on trust, skills, and identity that have developed independently, provides process insight into phase transitions, and challenges linear stage models by documenting recursiveness. Practically, it demonstrates that successful adaptation requires multi-year time horizons, deliberate job design to exploit complementarity, and organisational support for identity reconstruction.

Power by Promise- An Ethnography on Organizing for Generative AI Through Hype Work

CTO
Jana Retkowsky (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University); Anne-Sophie Mayer (LMU Munich School of Management); Joeri Van Hugten (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
While digital technologies are typically introduced by an organization’s management for a clear purpose, we know little about how organizations respond when technological change enters outside of formal channels and diffuses bottom-up. Drawing on a 22-month ethnography of a large Dutch media company, we trace how the organization adapted to the emergence of Generative AI (GenAI) through a variety of practices centering around the construction and regulation of hype around AI. We find that management initiated hype-installing practices, which framed AI as urgent and inevitable and embedded it into goals, and HR processes. These practices mobilized collective involvement which channeled organizational attention into engagement practices that generated visible activity which spread and evidenced the hype. In response to those practices, we observed two countervailing sets of practices at the level of the individual employee: (1) decoupled conforming, which sustained hype around AI despite the often perceived uselessness of the main company-wide implemented GenAI tool, and (2) reality proving, which tempered hype by interrogating specific GenAI use cases. Together, these dynamics show that organizing for GenAI was accomplished not through simply adopting a technology but through collectively enacting belief in AI’s promise. We contribute to research on AI at work by revealing hype as an organizing device that both propels and constrains AI-related transformation.

Augmentation or Automation? Dual Motivations for Gen-AI Usage and Employee Creativity

CTO
Yujie Shi (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Jiang Xu (Beijing Sport University)
The growing use of generative artificial intelligence (Gen-AI) in the workplace has sparked debate about its impact on employee creativity. While these tools can offer support and inspiration, over-reliance may reduce critical thinking and hinder creative performance. This study examines two employee motivations for engaging with Gen-AI: augmentation motivation, reflecting the desire to complement one’s abilities, and automation motivation, reflecting the desire to offload tasks. Drawing on dual-process theory and self-regulation theory, we propose that employees with stronger augmentation motivation are more likely to analytically integrate external knowledge, enhancing creativity, whereas those with stronger automation motivation tend to rely intuitively on external outputs, which can impede creativity. Conscientiousness is identified as a key boundary condition shaping which cognitive pathway dominates. A multi-wave, multi-source field survey and a scenario-based experiment tested these hypotheses. Results show augmentation motivation positively influences creativity via knowledge integration, while automation motivation negatively affects creativity through knowledge dependence, with conscientiousness amplifying the positive pathway and mitigating the negative one. Theoretical contributions of the research findings, practical implications, limitations, and directions for future research are discussed.
