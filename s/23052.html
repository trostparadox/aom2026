Experimental Design in International Business: A Critical Review and Research Agenda

IM
Jelena Cerar (WU Vienna University of Economics and Business); Christian Troester (Kühne Logistics University); Phillip C. Nell (WU Vienna)
Experiments are a powerful method for establishing causal inference and have increasingly been used in international business (IB). Yet the method remains in its infancy, standards remain unclear, and IB is particularly challenging for experimental designs despite its promise. IB experiments are also scattered across a wide range of (non-IB) journals. We therefore conduct a comprehensive review of experiments in IB to assess how, where, and with what rigor experimental methods are currently being used, and where methodological weaknesses may limit their contributions to IB and adjacent fields. We identify and analyze 212 experiments since 2000 across IB and management journals. Our review highlights both the opportunities and methodological challenges of experimentation in IB, with particular attention to issues of external validity, internal validity, and transparency. We provide an IB-specific design guide to address these challenges, illustrate how experiments can strengthen theory building in cross-border contexts, and outline opportunities for impactful future research at the intersection of IB and experimental methods.

Pitchfork Bifurcation and the Emergence of Multinational Enterprises

IM
Haruo Hagiwara Horaguchi (Hosei University)
This paper develops a nonlinear dynamical model of firm evolution to explain the endogenous emergence of multinational enterprises. As a discontinuous organizational transformation, it departs from standard approaches that treat time as an exogenous and smooth index. The model provides a generative theory of temporal structure in which distinct regimes of firm behavior arise endogenously from continuous underlying dynamics. Firms accumulate organizational capability through bounded domestic learning while interacting through competitive imitation. They gain access to foreign markets via the gradual accumulation of relational capital. Internationalization is modeled using the mathematical concept of a pitchfork bifurcation. The analysis shows that the symmetric domestic equilibrium loses stability through a pitchfork bifurcation when small asymmetries in learning intensity outweigh the homogenizing force of domestic competition. The equilibrium structure changes discontinuously, generating persistent asymmetries in international expansion. As a result, foreign subsidiary formation appears as a sudden and clustered event rather than a gradual adjustment process. The model explains why seemingly similar firms diverge sharply in internationalization outcomes and why multinational enterprises emerge only after prolonged periods of incremental change.

Adapting to Changing Global Landscapes: Interdependence, Coordination and the Performance of MNEs

IM
Mo Chen (New York University Shanghai); Nianchen Han (Nanyang Technological University)
Recent deglobalization-related formal institutional shocks reshape the constraints and incentives facing multinational enterprises, making adaptation contingent on how firms coordinate interdependent activities across countries. However, international strategy research offers limited guidance on when coordination mechanisms facilitate or obstruct adaptation under different types of institutional shocks. We address this gap by developing a simulation model of cross-country adaptation in which an MNE searches over interdependent activity choices across two national landscapes while coordinating decisions by allocating decision rights. The analysis shows that coordination can generate a system-level advantage when country environments are relatively similar, but this advantage erodes as environments become more dissimilar and as interdependencies among activities rise. Moreover, shocks that constrain feasible adjustments and shocks on payoffs through incentives produce distinct post-shock trajectories, revealing when coordination supports adaptation and when it becomes a constraint. Professional Associations as Transnational Actors: A Maturity Model of Internationalization

IM
Priscilla Lambert (University of York)
Professional associations are increasingly engaged in cross-border standard-setting, credentialing, and professional governance, yet their internationalization remains undertheorized in both internationalization research and the sociology of professions. Dominant theories continue to privilege for-profit firms, offering limited insight into how nonprofit, member-governed professional bodies extend authority, standards, and professional logics across borders. In this study, I examine how professional associations internationalize their professionalization frameworks and ask what strategic patterns emerge when internationalization is pursued outside the corporate domain. Drawing on an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design that combines a survey of UK professional associations with large-scale documentary analysis of qualifying bodies, I develop a maturity-based framework capturing three distinct strategic orientations toward internationalization. The analysis reveals that professional associations do not internationalize through linear progression or substitution of strategies, as assumed in firm-based models. Instead, internationalization unfolds through layered and hybrid configurations of practices reflecting varying levels of organizational capacity, institutional embeddedness, and strategic intent. By conceptualizing professional associations as active transnational actors rather than domestically bounded institutions, this study reframes how professional authority and governance are constructed in global professional fields and opens new directions for theorizing internationalization beyond the firm. Keywords: Professional associations; internationalization strategy; transnational professional governance; maturity model; sociology of professions

Student Sample Generalizability in International Business: A Meta-Scientific Review

IM
Frank A. Bosco (Virginia Commonwealth University); Isaiah James Lynch (Virginia Commonwealth University)
Student sample generalizability remains a concern in IB research. However, the degree of generalizability is currently unknown because no large-scale studies on the topic exist. We address this gap by assessing the magnitude of the culture × student status interaction in 88 existing meta-analytic databases. For each, we estimate the interaction for 50 country-level culture variables. We then summarize the resulting interaction effects using second-order meta-analysis. The mean absolute difference in observed summary effects is roughly r = .05 comparing students and nonstudents. Relative importance analysis indicated that the magnitude of the interaction was determined slightly by the specific culture variable, but primarily by the substantive bivariate relation studied in the meta-analysis. Bivariate relations involving job attitudes were associated with the strongest culture × student status interactions, with many student-nonstudent differences around ?r = .10; relations involving psychological traits were associated with the weakest, with many ?r < .03. We present analyses and visualize the interaction in various ways, all leading to essentially the same conclusions. We discuss implications for navigating student sample generalizability concerns in international business research.
