Proximity is Similarity? Identity Unmasking and Competition in Markets

OMT
Laura E. Dupin; Filippo Carlo Wezel (USI Lugano, Switzerland)
A rich literature is devoted to understanding how the perceived similarity among organizations is inferred from their categorical position in a cognitive space. An organization’s categorical position is consequential for competition and, under the assumption of minimal membership tests of audience members, it is assumed to be relatively stable over time. Thus, categorical freeriding, or the inflation of similarities across organizations that belong to the same category, is pervasive and the conditions under which audiences may re-evaluate category freeriders remain underexplored. In this paper, we theorize that the comparison to organizations that belong to an adjacent category, and are spatially located nearby it, is likely to unmask subtle differences among organizations that are initially associated to the same category. We name this mechanism “identity unmasking” and argue that it may be activated by incumbents or new entrants, with new entrants generating the strongest effect. Identity unmasking leads audiences to revisit the identity of organizations, resulting in evaluative penalties for some and rewards for others. Empirically, we illustrate how the failure rates of modernist and traditional artisanal French bakeries are differentially affected by their spatial proximity both to an incumbent and to new entrant industrial bakeries. Further experimental evidence corroborates these results.

So the SEC Won't Let Me Be: Regulator's Jurisdictional Battle Over Cryptocurrency

OMT
Jack Sadek (IE Business School); Robert Nason (McGill University); Anand Bhardwaj (McGill University)
Extant literature has shed insight into the roles of producers, intermediaries, and audience members in the formation of new market categories. However, little is understood about the critical role that regulators play in this process. Regulators are important as their judgments are often used to classify ambiguous new categories, and their guidance shapes whether and how laws are enforced in and around the category. To better understand the role of regulators, we examine their role in the history of cryptocurrencies. Cryptocurrency emerged from the informal economy and has faced persistent stigmatizing events, from association with drugs and terrorism to chronic incidents of fraud. Furthermore, the category itself is considered ambiguous, having developed several labels and use cases. We contend that examining a category with these characteristics accentuates the role of regulators, providing an opportunity to generate theoretical insights into their roles in category emergence. Through a combination of topic modeling and abductive qualitative methods, we examine the discursive strategies used by United States regulators, specifically the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Commodities Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). Interestingly, we find that rather than providing clarity on the category, the SEC and CFTC developed diverging lenses as to how to view the category. As a whole, we theorize that the SEC represents an expansionary regulator, using a Discourse of Jurisdictional Spanning to extend its control into the nascent category, while the CFTC represents an umbrella regulator, using a Discourse of Jurisdictional Lobbying, it defines the category in its image but is hesitant to claim full authority over it. As a whole, this study builds theory about the role of regulators in category emergence by identifying the potential for conflict within and amongst regulators, discursive strategies utilized to manage regulators’ jurisdictional boundaries, and the instrumental use of stigma.

Functional vs. Supra-Functional Distinctiveness in Post-Consumption Product Evaluation

OMT
Bryn Choi (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Olga Khessina (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Samira Reis (Carlos III University of Madrid)
A firm’s success in the market is shaped by how well its products appeal to consumers. The literature on optimal distinctiveness suggests that to appeal to consumers, products should be distinctive enough to stand out, but not so distinctive as to undermine audience recognition and evaluation. Prior research has largely treated products as unidimensional, measuring distinctiveness as the overall distance between a product and its peers. In contrast, we consider the multidimensional nature of products and examine whether optimal distinctiveness varies across product dimensions. Because products are experienced through use, we posit that their distinctiveness can arise along two dimensions: a functional dimension, consisting of features essential to how a product operates, and a supra-functional dimension, consisting of features that enhance appeal without being necessary for functionality. We argue that the effects of distinctiveness may differ depending on the focal dimension. Specifically, we hypothesize a negative relationship between functional distinctiveness and consumer appeal, and an inverted U-shaped relationship for supra-functional distinctiveness. Using data on 19,854 video games reviewed on Metacritic between 2001 and 2022, we find support for both predictions. By centering the analysis on product multidimensionality and post-consumption evaluation, our study highlights how the nature of products—as offerings that must be used, not just observed—shapes the dynamics of distinctiveness in ways that are complex and not always intuitive.

Quantum Information Science Belongs to Us: Credibility Contests and Hype Boundaries Construction (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Anne Sophie Barbe (Toulouse School of Management); Nina Granqvist (Aalto University)
The existing research on jurisdictional contests provides extensive insights on the defensive boundary work developed by a single group of actors to protect its jurisdiction—but neglects the reaction of other communities involved in the field in which such a contest happens. To explore these reactions, we propose to analyze how communities engaged in a nascent science-based industry shape the boundaries between hype and science. Drawing on an extensive, inductive qualitative study of the nascent quantum computing industry between 2017 and 2023, we develop a process model of the credibility contests underpinning the construction of the boundaries of hype in nascent science-based industries. We show that in these settings, three main communities engage in boundary work to distinguish hype from science, contesting the allocation of epistemic authority—and related resources—between science and business. We contribute to the literature on hype by unpacking the process of hype boundaries construction. In doing so, we expand understanding of credibility contests by identifying two types of reactions that may emerge, when a community uses boundary work to protect its monopoly over epistemic authority.

Toward a More Dynamic, Evolutionary, and Systemic Theory of Market Categories

OMT
Tomé Guerreiro D O Salgueiro (University of Delaware); Eduardo Meléndez (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Duygu Phillips (University of Delaware)
In this paper, we advance a dynamic, evolutionary, and systemic perspective on market categories. We first synthesize foundational definitions and organize the literature around three enduring tensions: environmental constraints versus social construction, stability versus change, and differentiation versus isomorphism. Building on this synthesis, we articulate three thought provocations. First, we argue for an explicitly evolutionary perspective that reconciles ecological and socio-cognitive views by treating them as partial accounts of a single, evolving system. Second, we propose and elaborate this systems perspective, conceptualizing categories as feedback-rich, nested systems shaped by recursive interactions among producers, intermediaries, audiences, and institutional environments. Third, we adapt the phylogenetic map as an integrative analytical framework for tracing category histories, relational positioning, and multi-level change over time. Together, these provocations reposition categories as living systems rather than simply static classificatory devices and generate an agenda for future research on category emergence, evolution, and decline.
