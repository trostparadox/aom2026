‘Taking a Leap of ’Faith’—Superstitious Beliefs Driving Action Upon Serendipitous Situations

OMT
Mauli Kundlia (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Although serendipitous opportunities are widely recognized as important sources of strategic innovation and entrepreneurial action, much less is known about why decision makers sometimes fail to act upon them. This paper explores how superstitious beliefs, defined as non-rational causal beliefs about luck, fate, or supernatural forces, shape managerial sensemaking of serendipitous situations and superstitious events, consequently influencing the likelihood of acting. Drawing on the sensemaking literature, I propose that superstitious events alter the actor’s risk perception and significantly reduce the anxiety associated with outcomes, leading to an enhanced belief about the possibility of future success. These beliefs can alert individuals to fortuitous cues and encourage attributing special significance to coincidences, thereby enabling action and opportunity materialization. However, they can also cause fatalistic interpretations that discourage deliberate action. To illustrate these dual effects and the mechanism involved, I present hypothetical examples highlighting how the varying valence of superstitions influences the sensemaking process and subsequent enactment of opportunity. The resulting conceptual model extends theory on serendipity and managerial cognition and action by showing how beliefs – particularly those rooted in culture and superstitions – affect the enactment of serendipitous situations in strategic contexts.
