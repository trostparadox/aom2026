Performance Feedback and Goal Hierarchy Evidence

OMT
Mengzhe Zou (Bocconi University)
Organizations simultaneously pursue multiple goals within shared work environments, where actions taken to improve one goal often reverberate across others. How do organizations allocate attention across goals in response to performance feedback? Prior research grounded in the behavioral theory of the firm has emphasized sequential attention to goals and self-enhancement processes, but these perspectives assume stable goal hierarchies and offer limited guidance when primary goals fall short and secondary goals do not clearly outperform. This study develops a theory of goal activation in multi-goal settings, arguing that when goals are interdependent and hierarchically ordered, performance above aspirations enables sequential expansion toward secondary goals, whereas performance below aspirations induces compensatory activation of secondary goals. I further argue that this correlation is contingent on the perceived importance gap between goals. Using qualitative evidence to establish goal hierarchies and longitudinal data on U.S. venture capital firms from 2015 to 2022, I find general support for the predicted V-shaped pattern. I further discuss how the hierarchical gap influences the interpretation of performance feedback, affecting the behavioral margin of activation versus intensity through which responses are manifested. The study augments existing research on multiple goals by emphasizing the nature of dynamic goal hierarchies and its implications for feedback interpretation.

Missed It By ‘That Much’: Near Miss Performance and Firm Risk-Taking

OMT
Owen Nelson Parker (The University of Texas at Arlington); Ryan Adam Krause (University of Iowa); Mandy Johnson (University of Iowa); Spencer James Fraseur (Texas Woman's University)
This paper revisits a core assumption in research on performance feedback and the Behavioral Theory of the Firm (BTOF): that when performance falls below aspirations, firms are presumed to take risks at a level commensurate with the shortfall. We challenge this notion, drawing on gambling research to argue that when performance falls just short of the aspiration—a phenomenon we term ‘near miss performance’—decision-makers become frustrated, engage in counterfactual thinking, and ultimately pursue disproportionate risk-taking due to ‘situational irrationality’. We distinguish this from bounded rationality and outline implications for current theory and future research.

To Whom Do We Compare? Reference Groups, Social Performance Feedback, and Problemistic Search

OMT
William C Zhou (Sacred Heart University)
A central premise of the behavioral theory of the firm is that organizations evaluate performance through social comparison and initiate problemistic search when outcomes fall short of aspiration levels. Yet many studies overlook the fact that firms simultaneously observe and attend to multiple peer sets that differ in similarity, salience, and diagnostic value. We don’t know much about the impact of reference group selection on problemistic search. In this study, we examine how narrow versus broad peer groups shape the direction of firms’ innovative search. We argue and find that underperformance relative to narrow peers induces deeper search within familiar knowledge domains, whereas underperformance relative to broader peer groups encourages firms to expand the scope of their search into more distant knowledge areas. We further theorize that inconsistent performance feedback between narrow and broad peer sets introduces attributional ambiguity and attenuates firms’ responses.

Organizational Reconnaissance: Opening the Black Box of Search with Behavioral Simulation

OMT
Alex Tyulyupo (Yale School of Management); Balazs Kovacs (Yale University)
How do organizations make sense of unfamiliar strategic environments when they lack established mental models? Existing theories focus on search within known dimensions but offer limited insight into how actors define relevant parameters in novel contexts. Using a behavioral simulation with 211 participants identifying competitors for a venture via a search platform containing 500,000 companies, we examine how actors construct initial understandings of competitive environments. Even when evaluating an identical venture, participants varied significantly in their search approaches and identified different competitor sets. Identifying stronger competitors increased perceived market competitiveness, revealing consequential differences in how participants made sense of the competitive landscape. We identify different reconnaissance strategies, finding that narrowing search queries yields more effective competitor identification. The process involves substantial cognitive revision as participants repeatedly adjust their understanding of the competitive landscape. Our findings contribute to theories of organizational learning and strategic cognition by showing how variation in initial search processes leads to divergent understandings of new strategic environments.

The Forgotten Dimension: Structural Distance in Organizational Search

OMT
Taeho Kwak (ESSEC Business School); Maciej Workiewicz (ESSEC Business School)
Cyert and March (1963) proposed that organizational search follows two rules of locality: search begins where the problem occurred and close to the current solution. While the organizational search literature has extensively examined the latter, the former dimension remains largely unexplored. The question we tackle in this paper is whether it matters where in the organizational structure search occurs, or only what solutions are explored? Through computational experiments, we examine the performance implications of structural distance, which is the organizational distance between where search occurs and where problems originate, and compare its effects to those of solution distance. We find that, from search efficacy point of view, structural distance can be as consequential as solution distance, but the relative importance of each depends critically on task interdependence. As interdependence increases, structural distance becomes increasingly important and can even substitute for insufficient solution exploration, while the converse does not hold. We also identify synergies when both dimensions expand jointly. These findings suggest that the dominant focus on solution distance may have overlooked a comparably important dimension of organizational search, with implications for how we understand the internal dynamics of problemistic search and the relationship between organizational structure and adaptive capability.
