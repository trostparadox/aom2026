Fixing the System to Keep the Talent: HRM, Gender Equality, and Workforce Sustainability in Medicine

Author: Alexandra Pfisterer Friedrich-Alexander University of Erlangen-Nuremberg

Session overview

Across Germany, Sweden, and the Netherlands, women constitute the majority of medical graduates, yet remain markedly underrepresented in senior academic and clinical leadership roles - despite decades of equality initiatives. Many interventions continue to target individual women—through work– family reconciliation measures or training programs—rather than addressing the institutional logics, power structures, and gendered expectations that shape career opportunities. As a result, informal practices, patronage, and persistent cultural norms—such as parental leave being framed primarily as a women’s issue—continue to influence career trajectories and ultimately limit the ability of hospitals to retain medical talent. Building on research on pediatric surgery, gendered organizations, and embedded analyses of medical careers, this study investigates how gender equality and work–life balance measures are framed, implemented, and embedded within organizational processes. We conduct a cross-country mixed-methods comparison of three university hospitals in Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden. Semi-structured interviews with equality officers and clinic leaders explore institutional logics and governance structures. An anonymous survey of pediatric surgery physicians employs validated instruments to assess job demands and resources, perceived organizational support, career fairness, and the perceived inclusiveness and efficacy of organizational measures. Our study advances Strategic HRM by demonstrating how equality policies succeed or fail depending on their integration into core HR processes and everyday organizational routines. By examining how institutional configurations shape gendered career trajectories, we identify transferable mechanisms that can enhance workforce sustainability, strengthen talent retention, and reduce structural inequality in high-pressure professional settings.
