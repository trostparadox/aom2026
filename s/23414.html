Under Pressure: How Environmental Shareholder Activism Drives Strategic Change

ONE
Sarina Ritter (Technical University Dortmund); Julie Saesen (Technical University Dortmund)
Firms face intensifying climate-related risks that threaten long-term competitiveness, yet they often continue to treat environmental issues primarily as a reporting or compliance obligation. At the same time, shareholder activism increasingly confronts corporations, urging improvement in climate-related practices. However, although prior research shows that environmental shareholder activism influences disclosure practices, we lack insight into whether such pressure shapes the strategic orientation that guide a firm’s environmental positioning and practices. We address this gap by drawing on organizational learning theory to examine how environmental shareholder activism is associated with a firm’s environmental orientation and how absorptive capacity moderates the relationship, which is a dynamic capability enabling firms to recognize, assimilate, and apply new external knowledge. Using a multisource data panel of S&P 500 firms, from 2014 to 2024, we find a positive association between environmental shareholder activism and subsequent environmental orientation. Moreover, the relationship is stronger for firms with high absorptive capacity. Our study contributes to the shareholder activism and organizational learning literatures by clarifying how and when environmental shareholder activism relates to firms’ environmental orientation.

Catalysts of Change: A Systems Perspective on Emission Reduction Potential in Collaborations

ONE
Isabel Maria Schöberl (Technical University of Munich); Claudia Doblinger (Technical University of Munich); Jeff York (University of Colorado-Boulder)
Climate change is one of the most pressing challenges of our time, requiring collaborative action across public and private actors. However, we know little about how (public-private) collaborations can potentially act as stewards of systemic change. We adopt a system perspective to examine how collaborations translate into environmental potential and conduct fixed-effects regression with 1,992 European climate-tech companies (2000-2023), along with several robustness checks to mitigate concerns about endogeneity. We link company and collaboration data with a forward-looking Emission Reduction Potential (ERP) metric, capturing the yearly potential reduction (percentage) in emissions to incumbent baselines. Three main findings emerge: First, system embeddedness through collaboration stock is positively associated with ERP, with effects being stronger in the company's early life. Second, public-private collaboration shows directionally higher ERP, with the strongest associations found in government laboratories and agencies. Third, collaboration stock today predicts additional collaboration partners tomorrow, consistent with reinforcing system dynamics. We contribute to the literature on collaboration and public-private collaborations in the context of grand challenges by reframing collaboration as a dynamic systems strategy and by linking ties to a forward-looking environmental potential. The evidence further informs practitioners seeking to design a collaboration system to accelerate decarbonization and combat climate change.

Corporate Sustainability Pledges

ONE
Amirsalar Jafari Gorizi (The University of Texas at Dallas); Mike W. Peng (The University of Texas at Dallas); Sahar Fozmoslemian
Despite growing scholarly interest in corporate sustainability, we still have limited insight into what motivates corporations to make sustainability pledges and why the market reacts differently to these pledges. This study examines the motivations driving corporations to join the United Nations Global Compact (UNGC) and make public pledges, as well as how the market reacts to them. We find that the market reacts more positively to the participation of companies that have either a high or well-below-average sustainability performance. This inverted U-shaped relationship is moderated by the extent of a corporation’s predominant regulatory focus. The severity of ESG controversies also directly (and negatively) affects market reaction. We uncover a temporal shift in membership patterns: while the UNGC was originally populated by distressed firms seeking legitimacy, high-performing corporations emerged as the dominant source of new membership as it matured. Lastly, we find that even these high-performing members generally join the UNGC following a decline period—joining the compact is a symptom of a turnaround already in progress, not the original driver of improved behavior.

Comparative Advantage of Organizational Forms: How Local Logics Inform Value Creation

ONE
Patrick J. Callery (University of Vermont)
Organizational scholars have demonstrated a renewed interest in understanding comparative advantage of varying organizational forms in addressing social and environmental issues, notably, whether or when a profit motive enables greater efficiency in provision of public value. Whereas recent theoretical developments have rigorously documented contextual factors that determine relative efficiencies of alternative forms of governance, there has been limited attention given to the perceptions of local community stakeholder preferences, despite their substantial interest in and influence over the actions of local organizations that create public value. This study addresses this oversight through an examination of public versus private ownership structures in public service organizations and comparative efficiency in adopting innovations that both create public value and allow for capture of private value. We integrate theory on comparative advantage with the institutional logics perspective, considering the role of two distinct yet complementary logics in the context of local public services provision: a prevailing local ideology and the strength of local community social capital. We examine the diffusion of methane recovery and energy generation from municipal solid waste landfills in the United States, a phenomenon that blends global sustainable value creation---abatement of greenhouse gas emissions---with private value creation through local electricity generation. Our theory and findings have important implications for knowledge in the fields of comparative governance, institutional logics, and addressing global challenges.

Implementing Sustainability Reporting: System Integrity In Materiality, Transition,and Stewardship

ONE
John Finch (University of Glasgow); Li Xiang
This paper examines how mandatory sustainability reporting is implemented as a governance mechanism in the UK and the EU. Drawing on a three-year applied programme using policy and regulatory documents and contemporaneous meeting records, we deploy a comparative case research design to identify and analyse three interdependent subsystems that are being assembled through reporting practice: Materiality assessment in corporate reporting; transition planning in corporate strategy; and investor stewardship in fund management. We compare the three subsystems along the dimensions of system openness, coalition power in producing and acting on reporting outputs, contests in the qualification of sustainability data, and the exchanges enabled by sustainability reports. We find that materiality is the most stable subsystem, producing relatively portable depictions of sustainability risks and opportunities with clearer expectations of the data and evidence to be gathered and reported. Transition is the most open and contested, shaped by evolving templates and multi-level policy commitments that extend reporting into corporate strategic commitments. Stewardship operates through principles-based guidance that legitimises investor engagement with those companies in which they invest – voice rather than exit – but relies on less settled evidential conventions for outcomes. We develop an implementation framework of systems integrity—coherence, information integrity, boundary control, accountability, and adaptive capacity—and show how interactions between the three subsystems progressively assemble sustainability policy in practice.
