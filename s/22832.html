Organizations and Values

SIM
Pranav Dalmia (University of Oxford); Alan D. Morrison
We present an account of organizations that foregrounds the role of values. We conceptualize the value separability claim, which holds that one can consider an organization independently of the values that it pursues. We argue that this claim is incorrect and, hence, that any organizational theory that incorporates it overlooks important facts about organizations. On our account, organ-izations are social structures within which relatively thin understandings of values are thickened and made concrete; where values are instantiated; and where logics for comparing incommen-surable values are created. We use our theory to discuss methodological questions in social sci-ence, to criticize value monistic approaches to corporate governance like the shareholder centric approach, and to identify implications for the study of organizational purpose.

Perceived Personal Benefit and Materialism in Ethical Fashion: Evidence from the US and Japan

SIM
Zara Berberyan (Leuphana University of Lüneburg); Sarah Margaretha Jastram (Hamburg School of Business Administration); Stefan Rose (University of Cologne); Lucas Philipp Heinrich Hugo Krassau (Hamburg School of Business Administration); Mark A Heuer (Susquehanna University); Kanji Tanimoto (Waseda University); Stefan Schaltegger (Leuphana University of Lüneburg)
In view of increasing social and environmental issues, shifting consumers towards ethical consumption remains a challenge of scientific and practical importance. Notwithstanding the demonstrated relevance of personal benefit for materialistic consumers, the literature does not offer precise answers as to how materialism and personal benefit are interlinked in the context of ethical consumption as well as on the role of a national context in this relationship. Drawing on a nationally representative sample of 2,618 respondents from the US and Japan, this study provides novel perspectives on the interplay between materialism and personal benefit in the field of ethical fashion consumption and demonstrates the impact of individualistic versus collectivistic contexts. The results yield a more nuanced understanding of this hitherto neglected relationship, thereby contributing to the ethical consumption discourse as well as wider discourses on communication and advertising appeals. In addition, the findings are of managerial and societal relevance as they provide novel insights into how personal benefit appeals can be leveraged to mobilize materialistic consumers towards ethical consumption in two major consumer markets.

Organizations as Focal Points of Social Norm Dynamics: A Process Perspective

SIM
Hans Jaich (Hamburg School of Business Administration); Sarah Margaretha Jastram (Hamburg School of Business Administration)
Changing social norms are increasingly seen as necessary for solving the most pressing environmental challenges facing our societies. Scholars therefore emphasize the need for studying how social norms dynamically interact with each other and thereby change, persist and disappear. One pressing question in this emerging research field is which actors, under which contextual conditions, could contribute to setting these dynamics in motion and accelerate their dissemination. Thus far, however, organizations have seldom been viewed as privileged contexts of agency in social norm dynamics. In this paper, in one of the first steps to address this gap in the literature, a process model was developed to shed light on the interweaving of social norms in and around organizational contexts and how this influences their evolvement and dissemination. By integrating theoretical knowledge from different disciplines into an organization-specific perspective, this paper establishes an interface with the literature on social norm dynamics and supports the identification of leverage points for social change.

Personal Values and the Public Interest in Accounting Professionals’ Client Acceptance Decisions

SIM
Seamus Dufurrena
This study examines how accounting professionals engage in ethical deliberation when formal norms provide limited or ambiguous guidance. Drawing on qualitative sources including 22 interviews with Certified Public Accountants (CPAs) who accepted clients in the newly legalized U.S. cannabis industry, the paper explores how professionals reason through the ethical complexities of servicing a legally and morally contested sector. We find that, under conditions of normative opacity – where legal contradictions and vague professional guidance preclude risk-based reasoning – ethical deliberation becomes primarily values-driven. CPAs invoked three distinct value systems to justify client acceptance: 1) pro-cannabis political conviction and social justice advocacy, 2) political agnosticism emphasizing fairness and consistency, and 3) commercialist ideals of opportunity and service provision. These values were reconciled with the profession’s ambiguous “public interest” principle, allowing practitioners to interpret compliance flexibly while maintaining ethical legitimacy. The study contributes to the literature on professional ethics by demonstrating how, when normative guidance is indeterminate and risk-based assessments untenable, professionals reconstitute ethical reasoning by finding alignment between disparate value systems and key professional principles that are pliable enough to accommodate such alignment. In doing so, it refines the understanding of logics of action in ethical decision-making and reveals the interpretive elasticity of the public interest principle.

The Ethics of Bank Runs for Non-Banks

SIM
Andrew Allison (West Virginia University)
Bank runs are ubiquitous parts of financial crises. Managers of non-financial businesses withdrawing their deposits play a major causal role in the bankruptcy of banks and the system-wide crises that follow. Yet, normative guidelines for how managers of these firms should act during bank runs has gotten relatively little attention from business ethicists. In this paper, I aim to provide an outline for an ethics of bank runs that draws on existing normative frameworks in environmental ethics (Jamieson 2007; Nefsky 2017; Talbot 2018; Nye et al. 2024). I use frameworks because there is a structural similarity between the collective action problem that arises in bank runs and in climate change mitigation. Relying on the Diamond-Dybvig model of bank deposits and bank runs (1983) as well as recent game theoretic work on bank runs (Vassilyev 2023), I draw out an analogy between incentive structures in bank runs and climate change mitigation: Managers of non-bank firms face the same game-theoretic incentives during bank runs as large countries do when deciding whether to enact climate change mitigation policy climate change. That is, they are made better off by not withdrawing their deposits only if they have good reason to believe that other firms will not withdraw their deposits, just as countries are made better off by limiting carbon emissions only if they have reason to believe that other countries will limit theirs. Both stockholder (Friedman 1970) and stakeholder (Donaldson & Preston 1995; Freeman 1998) theoretic accounts of fiduciary duty give strong normative reasons for managers to want to withdraw their money from insolvent banks when they are likely to face a run. But, managers also have strong normative reasons to want to avoid bank runs because of negative consequences that many stakeholders will face from the system-wide effects that a bank bankruptcy can have. In light of these considerations, I suggest that managers of non-bank firms that hold large portions of deposited funds have very strong normative requirements to leave funds deposited on the grounds that it not only plays a large causal role in avoiding a bank run, but also produces a credible signal to other non-bank firms that a bank run is unlikely. For managers of smaller non-bank firms, I point to a virtue ethical framework (Jamieson 2007) that grounds a responsibility to keep funds deposited grounded in an obligation to be virtuous financial community members.
