One Step Forward, Two Steps Back: Healthcare Inequities Across Care Pathways for Minority Patients

HCM
Trisha Bugra; Darby Roland (Toronto Metropolitan University); Ellen Choi
This qualitative study examines how visible minority patients encounter, experience, and navigate healthcare inequities through routine care delivery within a publicly funded healthcare system. We conducted 16 semi-structured interviews and 25 open-ended survey responses with visible minority patients. The findings illustrate how inequities emerge through a combination of stigmatizing interactional dynamics and discretionary decisions and processes that accumulate over time, contributing to divergent care pathways. Across accounts, variability in clinical decision making, disruptions in continuity, and the invocation of social and cultural stereotypes appeared as routine features of care delivery that patterned how care unfolded across encounters. Rather than treating emotional distress, silence, or switching providers as downstream reactions to isolated encounters, the analysis shows how these experiences clustered as patterned adaptations to how organizational processes were enacted across care pathways. The findings contribute to healthcare management scholarship by specifying how discretion operates within care pathways and by reframing patient experience as a diagnostic indicator of how care processes are organized and managed. This process-oriented account clarifies how healthcare inequities emerge through routine features of care delivery and highlights organizational and managerial levers – particularly related to follow-up reliability, coordination, and accountability – that shape care trajectories under conditions of system strain.
