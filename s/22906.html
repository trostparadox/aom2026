Attention Saturation Beyond the Organization: Societal Sensemaking and Task Coordination

OMT
Alexandru Roman (University of Maine); Haibo Liu (Keck Graduate Institute, Claremont Colleges)
Employees do not interpret events solely within organizational boundaries; they also participate in society-wide interpretive processes that shape attention and coordination. We examine how large-scale collective interpretation, in interaction with contextual conditions, influences individual task performance. We conceptualize societal attention as a finite capacity and argue that periods marked by intensive interpretive activity impose cognitive demands that compete with coordination at work. When externally driven meaning-making intensifies, employees must devote attention to understanding unfolding developments, increasing coordination friction and impairing performance on tasks that rely on tight temporal and interpersonal alignment. We exploit variation during the 2016 U.S. presidential campaign, distinguishing between periods of elevated attention associated with interpretive disruption and periods of comparably high but conventionally framed attention. Using data from more than seven million emergency medical service responses, we show that interpretively disruptive periods are associated with slower response times, whereas high-attention periods lacking such disruption are not. These effects are stronger in socially dense environments, including urban locations, larger organizations, and fully professional units. Notably, the effects weaken on days with localized political events, when interpretive activity concentrates in physical gatherings rather than diffuse societal discourse. Together, the findings indicate that performance declines arise from competition over attentional resources rather than from generalized distraction or operational interference.

Long-Term Adoption of Social Innovations in Institutionally Distant Contexts

OMT
Johanna Fischer (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz University of Hanover); Christiana Weber (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz University of Hanover); Bart Clarysse (ETH Zürich)
Why do some social innovations persist while others fade, even when they appear to create tangible benefits? We address this question by examining the long-term adoption of social innovations transferred across high institutional distances from the Global North to the Global South. Drawing on translation theory, we conducted a longitudinal qualitative study of five agricultural social innovations introduced to self-employed farmers in rural Tanzania, using 41 focus group discussions and 20 interviews. Our individual-level analysis identifies three distinct adoption behaviors—material adoption, immediate adaptation, and symbolic adoption—and theorizes the value-based mechanisms that shape them. We show that lasting adoption hinges on three interrelated mechanisms: value–preference congruence (alignment between innovation values and recipients’ value hierarchies), value–action consistency (resonance between innovation use and individuals’ lived experiences), and innovation–value–context fit (alignment of innovations with broader environmental and social conditions). We further introduce the concept of symbolic adoption to explain why innovations that appear initially successful often fail in the long run. By highlighting not only the value-based mechanisms of translation but also when and why one translation trajectory and its ensuing actions take place rather than another, our findings extend translation theory. Finally, they contribute to the social innovation literature by uncovering the micro-foundations of lasting adoption, adaptation, and rejection in institutionally distant contexts.

Translating Interventions Across Sociocultural Worlds: Evidence from Mini-Grids in Rural Kenya

OMT
Mohammed Bendaanane (Technical University of Munich); Angelique Slade Shantz (University of Alberta)
Many organizations from the Global North intervene to address societal problems affecting populations in the Global South, yet these interventions often fail to achieve their desired outcomes. We argue this often stems from an institutional translation problem, whereby solutions need to be adapted when introduced across sociocultural distance. While prior translation research has focused on documented failures across distance, or successful translation within relatively similar contexts, we know little about processes enabling successful translation across fundamentally different sociocultural worlds. We explore this through a qualitative multi-case study of five mini-grid ventures bringing electricity for the first time to unelectrified rural communities in Kenya, where the sociocultural differences between ventures led by affluent foreign entrepreneurs and the rural communities they aimed to service were vast. Drawing on interviews, field observations, archives, and satellite data measuring electricity adoption, we examine 16 mini-grid projects with varying translation outcomes. We find that successful translation is driven by, first, ventures’ critical self-reflexivity and awareness of value asymmetry regarding electricity, and second, by collaborative positioning during translation. Collaborative positioning associates electricity with local autonomy and opportunity, fostering peer-driven adoption that emerges organically from within communities. We make three contributions. First, we extend institutional translation theory by revealing processes enabling successful translation across sociocultural worlds. Second, we contribute to literature on organizational interventions to address social problems by showing how organizations can translate interventions across extreme sociocultural distance. Third, we offer methodological contributions by demonstrating the value of combining qualitative fieldwork with satellite data to investigate organizational social impact in hard-to-reach contexts. Practically, our work carries important implications for social entrepreneurs, nonprofits, and development organizations to take culture seriously when intervening to help disadvantaged populations.

Toward a Multidisciplinary, Meta-Theoretical Framework for Understanding Health Behavior

OMT
Nicholas Rosemarino (Teachers College of Columbia University); Charles Jones (Independent Researcher); Marie Beitelshees; Andrew Hill
This paper introduces the Public Health Systems Ecology Model (PHSEM), a meta-theoretical framework that reconceptualizes health behaviors as emergent outcomes of complex social systems rather than individual decisions. PHSEM’s tri-level structure explains how macro-level infostructures (public health authorities, legacy media), meso-level institutions (workplaces, educational institutions, communities, and digital platforms), and micro-level psychological processes interact to shape health behavior. Drawing on seminal theoretical traditions with foundations in organizational science and sociology, we integrate Open Systems Theory and Complexity Theory as overarching scaffolding while employing level-specific theories, such as Institutional theories and Behavioral Intention Modeling. Of note, is our conceptualization of the meso level, where institutions adaptively reinterpret macro-level guidance. We espouse that effective communication must operate at the “edge of chaos”, balancing structure with flexibility to align interventions with evolving institutional priorities. Through case examples spanning a range of health behaviors, we demonstrate the model’s ability to trace causal pathways across system boundaries while providing guidance for both macro-level authorities communicating to institutional contexts and leaders operating within these contexts. By shifting the analytic focus from individuals to systems, PHSEM advances theoretical discourse and offers a more sophisticated account of how health behaviors emerge within and across complex social environments.

Cooperation Beyond Institutions. The Case of the Lodi Model

OMT
Marco Maria Daprà
Institutions underpin the taken-for-granted norms and belief systems that enable cooperation in everyday organizational life. However, what fosters cooperation when institutional frameworks collapse remains insufficiently understood while sustaining cooperation is essential to forming new normative frameworks, particularly in extreme contexts where collective action toward a shared goal becomes imperative. This study investigates these dynamics through an in-depth empirical analysis of Lodi Hospital, the first healthcare institution in the Western world to confront the COVID-19 emergency. Our findings reveal that a shared commitment to an overarching interpretive framework and persistent ethics of care were foundational to enabling cooperation and facilitating a broader organizational paradigm shift. We contribute to the literature on institutional collapse and extreme contexts by showing (1) that cooperation can precede and underpin the reconstruction of institutional norms and (2) that institutional collapse and emergence are heterogeneous processes across institutional orders.
