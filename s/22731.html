Human-Machine Decision-Making Structures and Decision Quality: Dual Psychological Pathways

OB
Yinshan Zhao (Southeast University); Lulu Zhou (Southeast University); Yaru Wang (Southeast University); Shuming Zhao (Nanjing University)
As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly embedded in organizational decision-making, how different human–AI collaboration structures shape decision quality remains underexplored. This study seeks to unpack the underlying mechanisms and boundary conditions through which human-machine decision-making structures—specifically, human-led versus AI-led arrangements—influence decision quality. A mixed-method research design combining an experiment and a survey was employed. The final sample consisted of 130 valid experimental observations and 341 valid survey responses. The results show that human-machine decision-making structures enhance decision quality by stimulating employee reflection, while simultaneously undermining decision quality by eliciting job replacement anxiety. Organizational AI strategy moderates these relationships: a replacement-oriented AI strategy strengthens the positive effect of human-machine decision-making structures on decision quality, whereas a collaboration-oriented AI strategy weakens this effect. This study advances the human–AI collaboration literature by adopting a human-machine decision-making structure perspective to uncover a dual-path mediation mechanism linking decision structures to decision quality.

Understanding Dynamics in Repeated Rounds of Advice in the Judge–Advisor System

OB
Ian Mizuki (York University); Xian Zhao (Ohio University)
Organizations now deploy decision aids that interact with users repeatedly, not once, and people are increasingly revise judgment after viewing advice from either human or an AI system, yet most evidence comes from a single encounter. Integrating prior research, we develop a Dynamic Utilization and Calibration (DUC) framework that extends the Judge Advisor System (JAS) by building on a temporal egocentric discounting perspective, in which the self serves as the central reference point for advice utilization. DUC explains how egocentric discounting unfolds dynamically across multiple rounds of advice-taking. We test hypotheses derived from the DUC framework in a confirmatory reanalysis of a Human AI advice dataset. Adults (N= 1,088) completed 32 trials in one of four tasks that required judgments based on image, text, and tabular information. On each trail, participants made an initial judgment, viewed advice labeled as human or AI, and could revise their response. Four results stand out. First, advice utilization declines modestly with repeated exposures, consistent with a habituated form of temporal egocentric discounting. Second, higher self-reported trust toward advisor coincides with greater trial-to-trial fluctuation in advice utilization, suggesting experimentation when the advisor is trusted. Third, belief-inconsistent advice carries a sizeable distancing penalty that is stronger for human-labeled advisors and larger in reasoning tasks than in memory-based tasks, with effects on advice utilization that carry over to subsequent trials. Furthermore, Advisor–preference fit yields the strongest effect but only when judges who prefer AI receive AI-labeled advice. These findings support DUC and extend JAS from static underweighting to a dynamic account of how advice use evolves across repeated interactions. Designs that consider temporal egocentric discounting, provide timely calibration cues and outcome feedback, and repair credibility after disagreement may better support appropriate advice reliance.

Effort Substitution in AI-Enabled Work: Implications for Challenge-Seeking Behavior in Organizations

OB
Muhammad Qasim (Southwest Jiaotong University); Minxi Wang (Chengdu University of Technology)
Artificial intelligence (AI) systems increasingly perform core work tasks. This shift raises an important motivational tension: tools that enhance efficiency may simultaneously weaken workers’ engagement in effortful cognitive struggle. Drawing on effort regulation and learned industriousness, we examine how patterns of AI use shape employees’ willingness to engage in challenging, developmental work. We focus on perceived effort–competence returns, defined as workers’ beliefs about whether investing cognitive effort continues to yield developmental value in AI-enabled contexts. We theorize that repeated AI task substitution (where AI generates complete solutions) alters effort–outcome contingencies, weakening the learned association between exertion and successful outcomes and thereby reducing challenge-seeking behavior. In contrast, AI task augmentation (where AI provides scaffolds while workers retain responsibility for core reasoning) maintains this association and sustains engagement with challenge. We further argue that task ownership conditions these effects, such that high perceived autonomy preserves the developmental value of effort under AI use. We derive testable hypotheses and outline a multi-study research program spanning construct validation, experimental manipulation, and longitudinal field designs. Our framework specifies boundary conditions under which AI-enabled efficiency may undermine, rather than support, the motivational foundations of learning and adaptation in organizations.

Learning from AI, Believing in Yourself: The Double-Edged Effects of AI Coaching on Self-Efficacy

OB
Wenpu Li (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Xin-an Zhang
As AI coaching becomes common in employee learning, a core unanswered question is how learning from AI shapes employees’ beliefs about their capability. Drawing on self-efficacy source theory, we argue that AI coaching reallocates efficacy cues: it expands availability to responsive mastery experiences (raising mastery self-efficacy) while devaluing vicarious cues that hinge on interpersonal cues (lowering vicarious self-efficacy). We further propose that an intervention highlighting AI knowledge from human restores the devalued vicarious cues. We test hypothesis in a randomized field experiment with newcomers in a fintech call center, combined with a 10-day daily diary design (in progress, current sample includes 52 newcomers and 457 day-level observations). Compared to learning from colleagues, learning from AI strengthens mastery self-efficacy and yields a positive indirect effect on objective performance. At the same time, learning from AI decreases vicarious self-efficacy; this deficit is attenuated when the AI is framed as drawing on experienced employees (origin-known AI). These findings extend self-efficacy source theory to AI-era coaching and identify design levers that help organizations capture AI’s scalability benefits while reducing vicarious costs.
