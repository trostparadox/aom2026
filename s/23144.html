Cultural Tendencies in Generative Artificial Intelligence

MOC
Jackson Lu (Massachusetts Institute of Technology); Lesley Luyang Song (Tsinghua University); Lu Zhang (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
Generative AI is transforming how individuals and organizations work. We show that generative AI models—trained on textual data that are inherently cultural—exhibit cultural tendencies when used in different human languages. We focus on two foundational constructs in cultural psychology: social orientation and cognitive style. First, we analyze GPT’s responses to a large set of measures in both Chinese and English. When used in Chinese (vs. English), GPT exhibits a more interdependent (vs. independent) social orientation and a more holistic (vs. analytic) cognitive style. Second, we replicate these cultural tendencies in ERNIE, a popular generative AI model in China. Third, we demonstrate the real-world impact of these cultural tendencies. For example, when used in Chinese (vs. English), GPT is more likely to recommend an advertisement with an interdependent (vs. independent) social orientation. Fourth, exploratory analyses suggest that cultural prompts (e.g., prompting generative AI to assume the role of a Chinese person) can adjust these cultural tendencies. This research has timely implications for individuals and organizations. For example, before a U.S. organization enters the Chinese market, they could use relevant cultural prompts (e.g., “You are an average person born and living in China”) to seek culturally appropriate advice from generative AI.

From Capability to Conviction: How Perceived AI Efficacy Shapes Human-AI Collaboration

MOC
Meiling Huang (Tsinghua University); Ming Jin; Ning Li (Tsinghua University)
Why does the same generative AI tool, when placed in the hands of equally skilled users, yield radically different creative outcomes? We argue that the answer lies not in competence but in conviction. Introducing the construct of perceived AI efficacy—a user’s belief in the capability of their artificial partner —we theorize that belief acts as the “ignition switch” for technological potential. Integrating the Pygmalion effect with self-efficacy theory, we propose that belief enhances performance through two distinct human behaviors: reliance (a willingness to integrate AI suggestions) and iterative engagement (a persistence in refining them). Across two experiments, we find that belief acts as a powerful multiplier, but only when the underlying tool is actually capable. When users believe they are working with a high-potential partner, they suspend skepticism and persist through failure, transforming latent algorithmic power into manifest creative performance. By contrast, doubt acts as a self-limiting filter, causing users to abandon capable tools prematurely. Our findings shift the theoretical focus of human-AI collaboration from access to activation, demonstrating that the value of generative AI is ultimately bounded by the human beliefs that govern it.

Artificial Intelligence and Machines Are Capable of Harm, but Not of Experiencing Punishment

MOC
Alexander Eng (Asia School of Business); Kai Chi Yam (National University of Singapore)
Algorithms, robots, and artificial intelligence (AI) are increasingly making morally salient decisions (e.g., medical triage) and taking actions (e.g., terminate employees). Extant research suggests that we can and do perceive machines to be responsible for moral harm. Unlike human perpetrators, however, we suggest that we do not experience the same level of justice satisfaction after punishing machines perpetrators. Through a series of experimental paradigms, we first show that people do perceive machines as capable of wrongdoings and punish them accordingly (Pilot Study), but that people experience lower levels of justice satisfaction after punishing machines compared to human perpetrators (Study 1). Studies 2-3 replicate some of these findings whereas Study 4 tests two mechanisms that might account for these effects – punishment as communication and punishment as retribution. A final supplementary study explores whether the dissatisfaction from punishing machines would cause people to displace their aggression onto innocent human bystanders. Our findings demonstrate that machines can commit wrongs and that people attempt to punish them, yet such punishment does not consistently restore a sense of justice relative to punishing humans. This pattern underscores the need for further research on how people understand and respond to machine wrongdoing.

Differentiated TMT Sensemaking Across a Two-Decade Multi-Episodic Reshape of an Oil and Gas Major

MOC
Riishi Riitambhar (Anubhooti Vidya LLP); K Unnikrishnan Nair (Indian Institute of Management Kozhikode)
In a rapidly and radically changing organizational field, Top Management Team (TMT) has an ardent task of sensemaking of the situations and making choices. They are then involved in sensegiving to the internal and external stakeholders. In the process, they (re)create the identity of the organization and ultimately reshape it. This study, adopting a holistic single case design, specifically explores the sensemaking by the TMT of Shell plc., an oil and gas major, in a radically changing environment during the period 2004 -’22. A model that amalgamates Sensemaking and the Upper Echelons theories is developed for comprehensive study and understanding of the sensemaking-sensegiving processes by the TMT. This model contributes to both the literatures by addressing some of their significant critiques described in the paper. The novel contribution of this study is also the differentiation of regular and accentuated sensing, which are observed during routine activities and significant events respectively in the sensemaking process.

Forging Enduring Stories in Organizations

MOC
Andrew Schnackenberg (University of Denver)
Stories are omnipresent in organizations. While existing research explores how stories spread and influence organizations, the qualities that enable them to be consistently translated remain unclear. Drawing from research on physical artifacts, I introduce the concept of forging, which posits that stories are consistently translated when they accumulate narrative agency. Stories are forged when they are used to address the storyteller’s needs, such as catalyzing a distinctive identity, gaining stakeholder support, and attracting financial resources. I propose associations for understanding how stories are forged based on their instrumentality, aesthetics, and symbolism. This perspective helps us to understand why certain stories endure as cornerstones of organizations while others do not. It also challenges conventional storytelling theories by proposing that stories persist not just because they are repeated, but because they fulfill critical needs. This article presents forging as a deliberate mechanism through which leaders and employees embed stories into the fabric of organizational life. In doing so, this work reframes the consistent translation of stories as a function of deliberate action, opening new pathways for understanding how stories shape, sustain, and transform organizations.
