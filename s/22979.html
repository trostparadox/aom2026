Unpacking the Member Check Process and Considerations for Future Research

RM
Ava Haddox (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Annabelle Gallup (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Christina Hymer (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Anne W. Smith (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville)
Member checking refers to the process of presenting data insights and interpretations to study participants within interpretive qualitative research. While originally proposed as a technique to clarify data interpretations, foster researcher credibility, and promote trustworthiness of data insights, papers citing member checking rarely provide details on how the process was conducted or how member checks affected theoretical insights. As a result, this important methodological technique remains largely in the shadows for readers and editors alike. To address this concern, we review 170 empirical qualitative articles that cite member checking, focusing on who was engaged in member checking, what can be learned from it, when it occurs, and how and why authors engage in member checking. From our in-depth review, we identified 20 exemplary uses of member checking. We provide considerations for future research towards how member checking can be incorporated into interpretive qualitative research projects.

Expanding The Qualitative Analytical Toolbox: Active Visual Mapping as an Advanced Analytic Approach

RM
Rehab Elkhiyat (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Yemisi Bolumole (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Anne W. Smith (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville)
Qualitative research often relies on retrospective interviews, archival oral histories, or documentary materials that are analytically rich but challenging to analyze. Qualitative analysis has primarily been conducted through coding, with recent efforts to clarify the analytical approaches that lead to theorizing. One such analytical approach has been visual mapping as a valuable process for reducing complexity and representing temporal dynamics. Yet scholars have devoted surprisingly little attention to explaining how visual mapping acts as an analytic artifact that can lead to theoretical insights. We build on previous use of visual mapping and propose active visual mapping (AVM) as an analytic artifact. Our approach decomposes qualitative data into analytically distinct layers that are then dynamically recomposed into visual structures to reveal cross-dimensional patterns leading to theoretical insights. Using oral histories from the 2016 Gatlinburg wildfire, we provide step-by-step guidance for implementing AVM. We specify two mechanisms leading to theoretical insights: juxtaposition (comparing layers to examine dimensional interactions) and zooming (examining how processes unfold across time periods). This paper contributes to qualitative methods by making explicit the interpretive mechanisms through which visual mapping supports theory building, offering transparent guidance for analyzing multidimensional qualitative data.

Re-Searching Organisations: Moving Organisational Ethnographies Online

RM
Elisa Montori (Venice School of Management - Ca' Foscari University)
Although interest in how digital platforms affect organising processes continues to expand, there is limited methodological debate about researching digitally mediated organising. One way for researchers to explore the impact of technology and algorithms in (re)shaping organisational communication, networks, and processes is through online ethnography – also labelled netnography, digital ethnography, virtual ethnography, or cyberethnography. The core principles of ethnography should remain consistent between online and offline settings. Drawing from neighbouring disciplines and traditional approaches, this study examines what organisational netnography involves and suggests ways to ensure rigorous online research. The investigation is structured around five major questions for conducting online ethnographies: the rationale for online research – that is, the ‘why’, the research settings – that is, the ‘where’, the informants that might be found in digital contexts – that is, the ‘who’, the types of data that can be gathered online – that is, the ‘what’, and, finally, the strategies for engaging with online environments, – that is, the ‘how’ of online researchers. Six exemplar cases are used to highlight current practices and challenges connected with the methodological approach.

Targeted Triangulation: a Framework for Reasoning with Multiple Data in Qualitative Research

RM
Jonas Spengler (University of Cambridge); Thomas J. Roulet (University of Cambridge)
Data triangulation – combining multiple, qualitatively different data to substantiate a claim – is a common technique for establishing good empirical grounding in qualitative research. While most studies claim to triangulate to some degree, it is often unclear how and why they do so concretely. We introduce a framework of triangulation that helps authors and readers employ triangulation in a more targeted manner. The framework distinguishes three different approaches to triangulation – comparing, complementing, and contextualizing – and identifies specific grounding problems that these approaches address. Accordingly, this methodological piece helps authors and reviewers alike assess the quality of empirical grounding and provides concrete guidance for ‘how’ and ‘when’ to triangulate in qualitative research.
