Purpose in For-Profit Companies: Purpose Implementation and Perceived Purpose Authenticity

STR
Henk W. Volberda (University of Amsterdam); Zahra Kashanizadeh; Pushpika Vishwanathan (University of Amsterdam); Jatinder Sidhu
Research on corporate purpose is on the rise with a proliferation of both empirical and conceptual work. A common approach to empirically studying purpose is to infer it from employee perceptions and experiences. As a result, our knowledge of how firms implement purpose and what determines its perceived success remains limited. To address this lacuna, we perform a qualitative comparative case study analysis of ten large, reputable, for-profit firms based in the Netherlands. Our data includes 58 interviews with CEOs, board members, employees, and external stakeholders, and case-specific archival data. Our inductive analyses reveal three approaches to purpose: ‘competitive differentiation’, ‘incremental integration’, and ‘stakeholder consultation’, which differ based on the drivers of purpose as well as the types of practices that are implemented. We develop the concept of perceived purpose authenticity to capture stakeholders’ judgments about the success of purpose implementation and find that it is strongest for firms where purpose was initiated inside, at the top or bottom of the organization, rather than outside. Our findings contribute to the purpose literature by offering an alternative framework to study purpose, presenting perceived purpose authenticity as a novel concept, and revealing connections between the drivers, practices, and perceived authenticity of purpose.

Profits, Stakeholders, or Purpose: How a Business Orientation Affects Stakeholders' Moral Boundaries

STR
Paul Simonis (University of Amsterdam); JW Stoelhorst (University of Amsterdam); Alfred Zerres (University of Amsterdam)
While economic theory, stakeholder theory, and the literature on purpose each take a position on how firms ought to define their moral boundaries, little research examines how stakeholders perceive what the moral boundary of a firm is. Based on literature, we first distinguish three orientations on what a firm’s moral boundary should be: a ‘profit-orientation’, a ‘stakeholder-orientation’, and a ‘purpose-orientation’. Then, we conceptualize three psychological modes (i.e., a self-interested mode, in-group moral mode, and out-group moral mode) among a firm’s stakeholders. In two preregistered studies (total N = 864), we empirically test the effect of the three orientations on the salience of the three psychological modes, as well as their downstream behavioral consequences. The results of our studies suggest that both stakeholder- and purpose-oriented firms are more likely to trigger moral behavior towards in-group-members through the increased salience of an in-group moral mode, while only purpose-oriented firms are more likely to trigger moral behavior towards out-group-members through the increased salience of an out-group moral mode. Our results suggest that adopting a stakeholder-orientation can help firms to behave morally towards transacting human stakeholders, but that purpose-driven companies may be better at addressing grand challenges that involve marginalized and non-human stakeholders.

Integrating Norms of Fairness into Resource-Based Theory’s Model of Profit Generation

STR
Steven Kofford (Mississippi State University); Adam Clark (Chapman University)
Strategic management scholars increasingly argue that firms generate economic profits by gaining access to stakeholder-controlled resources whose value and marginal contributions are difficult to specify or verify ex ante. Stakeholder-oriented extensions of resource-based theory (RBT) therefore suggest that firms often must treat key stakeholders as residual claimants, allocating a “fair” portion of ex post profits to sustain cooperation under incomplete contracting. Yet these arguments leave a core governance problem undertheorized: stakeholders differ in what they regard as fair, so which fairness norms can efficiently anchor implicit contracts when profit allocation is decided only ex post? This paper integrates fairness directly into RBT’s model of profit generation by theorizing how stakeholders’ ex ante expectations about fair ex post profit allocation condition whether they will contribute potentially profit-generating resources. Drawing on relational models theory, we argue that the relational model enacted between a firm and a stakeholder structures the stakeholder’s distributive and procedural fairness expectations (communal sharing, equality matching, authority ranking, or market pricing). We then specify how two features of the stakeholder’s position—opportunity costs and shared identity with other residual claimants—systematically shape which fairness norms stakeholders consider appropriate for determining their residual claim. The resulting framework predicts when each relational model will be most efficient for attracting stakeholder-controlled resources and offers a contingent, mechanism-based account of profit generation in stakeholder RBT.

Purpose Relatedness, Coordination Strategies, and Economies of Scope

STR
Harrison John Munro-Clark (HEC Paris)
When does diversification create value? While research has extensively examined activity relatedness as a driver of coordination costs, it has overlooked an equally fundamental dimension: purpose relatedness. When business units pursue distinct purposes, coordination costs intensify because tradeoffs become incommensurable. Using a computational model, we first confirm that under incommensurable objectives, coordination systematically destroys value. We then show how firms can overcome this challenge through specific coordination strategies. A strategy of temporal rotation, alternating decision-making control over shared activities, engenders a generative friction that allows firms to escape suboptimal performance peaks, yielding positive economies of scope. These "adaptation benefits" are so significant that temporal rotation outperforms even when fully commensurate tradeoffs are possible. Adaptation benefits diminish as more business units are coordinated. This paper reintroduces purpose relatedness as a critical dimension in diversification research and offers a more complete answer to when diversification creates value through the concept of "coordination economies of scope."
