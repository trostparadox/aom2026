Neurodiversity, Inclusion, and Invisible Barriers

Session overview

This session examines how neurodivergent employees experience stigma, inclusion, and organizational support across varied work contexts. The papers address workplace reactions to neurominority employees, autistic adults’ hidden barriers, employee-led inclusion through self-narratives, and organizational tensions in neuroinclusion. Together, they offer a timely and nuanced look at how organizations can better understand and support neurodiverse workers while rethinking assumptions about inclusion.

Papers (5)

Revisiting Stigma and Inclusion: A Neurodivergence Perspective In Saudi Arabia

DEI
Mahmoud Fallatah (Alfaisal University); Malak Malibari (Prince Sultan University); Cheryl K. McIntosh (Northwest Missouri State University); Myrtle P. Bell (The University of Texas at Arlington)
Across the past decade, neurodiversity and inclusion of those who are neurodivergent have moved from the margins of corporate diversity discussions to the forefront of organizational inclusion concerns. Neurodivergent individuals represent an estimated 15–20% of the global population. Labor-market outcomes for neurodivergent individuals are markedly worse than those of their neurotypical peers. This paper replicates and extends McLaughlin, Bell, and Stringer (2004), testing whether the stigma–attitude–fairness–discrimination mechanism originally applied to physical disability generalizes to neurodivergent conditions in Saudi workplaces. They identified six stigma dimensions particularly relevant to workplace evaluations of disability: performance impact, social impact, controllability, unpredictability, course, and attractiveness. Consistent with the original framework, we found that stigma exerted a strong and reliable influence on attitudes, fairness perceptions, and discriminatory judgments for people with three different neurodivergences: autism, ADHD, and dyslexia. Participants who endorsed more stigmatizing beliefs reported more negative evaluations across all three acceptance constructs, closely mirroring the central mechanism identified by McLaughlin et al. Stigma again showed its strongest association with discriminatory employment judgments. This study contributes contextually grounded evidence from Saudi Arabia and underscores the need for education, inclusive leadership, and clearer communication about accommodations to foster more equitable workplaces worldwide.

How Do Neurominority Employees React to HR Practices Support Perceptions? Better, Worse, or Same

DEI
Jarrod Haar (Massey University); David Brougham; Candice Harris
There is growing attention to neurominority employees, and this is important given estimates that this group might represent one fifth of workplaces. But how do they react to workplace experiences? Are these the same as everyone else or do neurominority employees experience workplace factors different, and is this better or worse? From an organizational perspective, are some experiences superior and if so, which ones? This paper presents two studies and four samples exploring HR practices, and support perceptions of the organization and supervisor on key worker outcomes: job satisfaction, turnover intentions, organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs), and work-life balance, with neurominority status as a moderator. Study 1 tests three samples (including time-lagged data) and Study 2 expands the way neurominority status is captured. Overall, all direct effects are as expected, with modest support for interaction effects. When evident, neurominority employees typically react to workplace factors less beneficially, although to OCBs, effects are superior. Overall, it appears that neurominority employees react typically like neurotypical employees although with a few exceptions. Implications for organizations, theory, and researchers are offered.

Navigating Hidden Barriers: A Study of Workplace Experiences Among Autistic Adults

DEI
Kristen Raney (University of Virginia (McIntire)); Harry J. Van Buren (The University of Tennessee-Chattanooga)
Organizations increasingly recognize neurodiversity as a vital dimension of workforce diversity, with autistic and otherwise neurodivergent individuals representing a growing segment of employees across industries. Despite this rising interest, organizational research on autism in the workplace remains shaped largely by employer accounts, HR policies, and clinical perspectives. To advance understanding of autistic individuals’ workplace experiences, we conduct a qualitative, discovery-oriented analysis using a novel dataset of candid narratives from Reddit, a platform where users often speak openly under pseudonymity about identity-sensitive topics. This study offers several important contributions to research on neurodiversity, organizational behavior, and inclusion by uncovering how autistic employees interpret and navigate workplace challenges. First, we show that autistic workers experience work as a setting of social precarity, where routine interactions carry heightened risk of misinterpretation and exclusion. Second, we identify sensory and cognitive misfits where environmental stimuli and task structures interact with autistic processing styles to produce overload and burnout. Third, we demonstrate that organizational systems intended to provide support – such as HR processes and accommodation mechanisms – often function as gatekeeping structures, amplifying vulnerability rather than alleviating it. We conclude with implications for future research.

Inclusion Starts With “I”: How Neurodiverse Employees Initiate Inclusion Through Self-Narratives

DEI
Xinxin Li (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Hantai Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Despite growing recognition of neurodivergent employees as valuable organizational assets, genuine workplace inclusion remains elusive. Existing research predominantly focuses on top-down organizational inclusion initiatives, which have proven limited in effectiveness. While prior work has documented narratives’ stigma-reducing potential and alluded to identity narratives’ persuasive power in organizational settings, research explaining how neurodivergent employees can strategically leverage personal narratives to advance inclusion is absent. Drawing on narrative persuasion theory, we develop a theoretical model illuminating how neurodivergent employees can proactively initiate workplace inclusion through growth-oriented self-narratives. Our process model posits that neurodiversity growth narratives—integrating challenge, growth, and accommodation plots—advance workplace inclusion through two sequential mechanisms: recipients’ immediate narrative absorption and subsequent post-narrative pro-neurodiversity reflection. We further identify narrative content, quality, medium, recipient traits, and context as key moderators of this persuasive process. This research enriches organizational inclusion scholarship by shifting from the dominant top-down paradigm to center neurodivergent employees’ agency and extends narrative persuasion theory by contextualizing it within neurodiversity and workplace inclusion dynamics. The model offers actionable guidance for neurodivergent employees seeking to foster inclusion and for managers aiming to cultivate truly inclusive workplaces.

Cookie-Cutter or Tailor-Made? Navigating the Psychological Tension That Arises Amidst Neuroinclusion

DEI
Duysal Askun Celik (Istanbul Topkapi University); Ülfet Uzunkoca (Istanbul Atlas University); Gökçe Akdemir Ömür (Istanbul University); Anna Pawlowicz; Erman KILINÇ; Sarah Hathorn (University of St Andrews); Cenk Sozen (Turkish Academy of Management)
This study explores how HR leaders navigate the psychological tension between standardized policies and individualized accommodations in neuroinclusion. Grounded in paradox theory (Lewis, 2000; Smith & Lewis, 2011), sensemaking (Weick, 1995), and the transactional model of stress and coping (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), we conceptualize this tension as an enduring paradox. Through interviews with 10 senior HR leaders in multinational firms, we analyze how they interpret neurodiversity (Austin & Pisano, 2017; Singer, 1998), evaluate fairness (Bowen et al., 2004; Cropanzano et al., 2015), and translate ambiguity into action. Thematic analysis identifies three interconnected mechanisms: cognitive sensemaking, behavioral coping, and affective responses, specifically empathy. Two conditions—leaders’ neurodiversity awareness (Doyle et al., 2021) and organizational culture (Shore et al., 2011)— shape how these tensions emerge. We propose a self-reinforcing sensemaking–coping loop explaining why paradoxes persist in established organizations. This study provides a micro-level understanding of neuroinclusion and offers guidance for designing clearer frameworks and leadership practices that reduce reliance on ad hoc coping.
