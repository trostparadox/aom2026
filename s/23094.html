“How Subtle Was It?”: Role of Intensity of Incivility and HRM in Observer’s Response to Incivility

HR
Bhumi Mahesh Trivedi (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Aditya Moses (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
Workplace incivility is characterized as a subtle, ambiguous, and often covert form of interpersonal mistreatment in the workplace. Uncivil behaviors lead to negative work outcomes, low job satisfaction, and reduced well-being, ultimately inducing stress and posing a threat to overall well-being. While research on how individuals respond to workplace incivility has primarily focused on individual-level factors, organizational-level factors, such as HRM systems, may also influence how employees respond to incivility. This study examines the role of HRM systems on employees’ responses to workplace incivility. We use signaling theory to hypothesize the effect of an HRM system on three responses to workplace incivility: fight, flight or freeze. Using an experimental vignette methodology including 633 working professionals, we find that a combination of human resource configuration and human resource strength moderates the response to two levels of workplace incivility intensity. Furthermore, we find that employee-oriented configurations with high strength elicited a fight response, while market-oriented configurations with low strength elicited a flight response. Implications for workplace incivility and HRM systems within organisations are discussed.

Withdrawal Intentions of Military Personnel: A Systematic Review of Literature

HR
Prashant Sunil Borde; Sushma Verma (Dr. Vishwanath Karad MIT World Peace University, Pune); Ranjeet Nambudiri (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
The purpose of this study is to review and synthesise the literature on withdrawal intentions of military personnel, identify gaps and propose future research directions. The study employs a systematic literature review (SLR) approach, following the SPAR-4-SLR guidelines, to investigate the current state of research in withdrawal intentions of military personnel. The study uses 51 shortlisted articles from the Scopus and Web of Science databases. Using the Antecedents, Decisions, Outcomes, & Theory, Context, and Method framework and thematic analysis, the authors analyze the current status of literature and gaps. Using thematic analysis, the study identifies five main themes in the withdrawal intentions of military personnel literature, namely, leadership and supervisory dimensions, commitment and identification, work-family interface, psychological wellbeing and stress reactions, & career and opportunity. Furthermore, the current status and future research directions are highlighted.

How Does Geography Distance Affect the Complaints of Workplace Sexual Harassment and Discrimination

HR
Leyuan You
Employing a large-scale dataset of employee complaints collected from online job reviews, this study examines how geographic distance between the reviewer’s location and corporate headquarters impacts the propensity for workplace sexual harassment complaints. We find that when reviewers are located farther from headquarters, they are less likely to complain about workplace sexual harassment. This finding is consistent with the knowledge transfer hypothesis, which posits that physical distance increases information flow frictions. Using the pandemic as an exogenous shock to physical distance, we find that this effect disappears during periods when remote work is prevalent. The negative relationship between distance and workplace sexual harassment complaints is also more pronounced in regions with higher female workforce participation. Extending the analysis to a broader dataset of discrimination complaints, we find similar results, which are not driven by the reviewer’s proximity to EEOC headquarters offices (a proxy for regulatory enforcement). Overall, the results show that spatial factors influence the incidence and reporting of workplace misconduct, providing insights into organizational governance and human resource policy.

How Survivors Cope: A Typology of Responses to AI-Induced Layoffs in Organizations

HR
Oluwasegun Abraham Solaja (Georgia State University); yi yang (Ohio University); Likoebe Maruping (Georgia State University)
Organizations increasingly deploy AI systems that enhance productivity while simultaneously rendering certain human skills obsolete, leading to what we define as AI-Induced Layoffs (AIL), workforce reduction triggered directly by AI adoption. Despite rising AI adoption and evidence of AI-related job displacement, the existing theory do not explain survivorship when the layoff agent is an evolving AI system that simultaneously augments remaining workers and expands the frontier of substitutability. This study develops a typology of AIL survivors by integrating layoff-survivor research with cognitive appraisal theory. We classify survivors along two dimensions, attitude (optimistic vs. pessimistic) and behavior (proactive vs. reactive), yielding four archetypes of survivor response. We propose that organizational AI adoption strategy, and organizational support to adaptation shape survivors’ attitudes, while self-efficacy, and locus of control shape their behavioral orientation. The resulting framework generates propositions for understanding survivors’ psychological, attitudinal, and behavioral responses to AIL and offers implications for how organizations can manage AI-driven change while protecting workforce resilience
