From Grit to Well-Being and Career Commitment: Examining the Moderating Role of CQ for Expatriates

CAR
Trung Kim Nguyen (Phenikaa University); Angela Shin-yih Chen (National Taipei University); Guo-Hua Lin (Chaoyang University of Technology)
This study examines the impact of grit on workplace well-being and career commitment among self-initiated expatriates, and further investigates the moderating role of cultural intelligence. Drawing on the Conservation of Resources theory, we test how key personal resources shape expatriates’ work and career outcomes. Data were collected by a three-wave time-lagged design. 213 copies of questionnaires were obtained through an online survey of Vietnamese self-initiated expatriates working overseas. The results indicate that psychological availability mediates the relationships between grit and workplace well-being, as well as between grit and career commitment. Theoretically, this research enhances the comprehension of grit by clarifying the mechanisms through which mediating and moderating resources operate within the paradigms of expatriate employment contexts.

Holding the Sword of Truth: Authenticity and Employment Outcomes in Skilled Immigrants' Job Search

CAR
Shan Lu (York University)
This study explores the role of authenticity in the job search process of skilled immigrants, revealing a set of thought-provoking and even counterintuitive findings. While mainstream job search advice often endorses impression management and the strategic concealment of one's authentic self as practical ways to avoid bias and secure employment quickly, our research highlights the long-term value of authenticity. Skilled immigrants who remain true to their identities during the job search may face greater short-term challenges. Yet, this approach is more likely to foster deeper career satisfaction, professional self-esteem, and organizational commitment. Ultimately, authenticity may help skilled immigrants not as a vulnerability but as a powerful asset in achieving meaningful career integration and growth within new host countries.

Growing with Community: The Impact of Community Involvement on Refugees’ Objective Career Outcomes

CAR
Eun Su Lee (The University of Melbourne); Elise (Eun Kyung) Lee (Yonsei University-Mirae Campus); Mijeong Kim (Pusan National University)
How refugees overcome labor market challenges and achieve career success outcomes, such as increase in income, has been underexplored in international business literature and more specifically in international human resource management. This study aims to fill this literary gap by adopting a holistic perspective on refugee career rebuilding to investigate the importance of multi-level, contextual understanding around international careers and objective outcomes. Drawing on career construction theory and the broader international careers literature, we propose and test how refugees’ engagement in local community activities leads to objective career success outcome, i.e. income growth, via enhanced senses of belonging and cultural self-efficacy. Using five-wave longitudinal survey data collected between 2013 and 2018, our analysis includes a sample of 1,389 refugees in Australia. Our analysis reveals that a sense of belonging and cultural self-efficacy mediate the positive relationship between local community involvement and income growth. These findings extend international careers research by applying career construction theory to the unique context of refugees, offering insights into how community integration influences career outcomes in the face of global mobility. The study contributes to the theoretical understanding of refugee careers and provides policy implications for enhancing refugees’ career development and financial stability through community inclusion.

Privileged Roots, Creative Pursuits? Childhood SES, Work Values, and Creative Occupations in China

CAR
Danlei Zhen (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Huiru Lu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Jian Guan (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Who is more likely to pursue creative occupations? Individuals from higher childhood socioeconomic status (SES) backgrounds have been found to be disproportionately represented in creative occupations, a pattern often attributed to the notion that such occupations are less suitable for individuals from lower SES backgrounds. However, we argue that high- and low-SES background individuals may be drawn to enter creative occupations, albeit for different work values. Drawing on resource-based and cultural perspectives on SES, we propose that, within the Chinese cultural context, childhood SES is related with variation in work values (i.e., extrinsic, intrinsic, and prosocial values) and that these work values are differentially related to engagement in creative occupations. Using a nationally representative sample from China, we find a U-shaped relationship between childhood SES and prosocial value, which is in turn positively related to participation in creative occupations. We also found a negative relationship between childhood SES and creative occupations that is statistically accounted for by extrinsic value. However, we find no significant relationship between childhood SES and intrinsic value, nor between intrinsic value and engagement in creative occupations. These findings offer important theoretical and practical insights into how childhood SES related to career decisions in non-Western cultural contexts.
