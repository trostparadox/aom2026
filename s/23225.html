Agglomeration as Remediation: How Spatial Clustering Generates Legitimacy for Poverty Entrepreneurs

ENT
Juliet Osuji (North Carolina Agricultural and Technical State University); John Aiyede (University of Louisville); Philip Amankwa
Prior research suggests that the benefits of firm agglomeration may vary by firm characteristics such as industry, size, and geographic location, yet little is known about whether these benefits differ by entrepreneurs’ economic status, particularly in informal and poverty-affected markets. Addressing this gap, we examine whether entrepreneurs at the Base of the Pyramid (BOP) derive disproportionate benefits from firm agglomeration and why. Drawing on qualitative interviews with 46 entrepreneurs operating in Makola Market, Accra, Ghana, we find that while agglomeration benefits entrepreneurs broadly, those with lower economic status benefit most. Specifically, agglomeration generates organizational legitimacy through mechanisms such as normative governance, regulated pricing, firm density, stable market presence, and market cohesion. This legitimacy improves how entrepreneurs are treated by key stakeholders, including customers, suppliers, government officials, and local communities, enabling greater access to resources, protection from opportunism, and improved performance. By positioning organizational legitimacy as a critical and underexplored benefit of agglomeration, this study extends agglomeration research, advances resource-based perspectives on legitimacy, and contributes to entrepreneurship-in-poverty scholarship by identifying agglomeration as a contextually grounded mechanism through which BOP entrepreneurs can overcome institutional and resource constraints. The findings offer theoretical and practical insights into how spatial clustering can support inclusive entrepreneurship and poverty alleviation.

An AI Founder and its Potential for Legitimacy: A Meaningful Heterodoxy

ENT
Jeff Augugliaro (Montclair State University); Aakash Sapru
Entrepreneurship theory assumes founders are human—an assumption invisible because it has been universally true. We argue this anthropocentric foundation becomes visible and problematic when we consider the AI Founder: an autonomous artificial intelligence capable of fulfilling founder functions, either independently or as a core founding team member. Using the AI Founder as a theoretical limiting case, we demonstrate that legitimacy—the critical resource new ventures acquire through founder evaluation—depends on mechanisms calibrated to human characteristics. We delineate four dimensions of AI Founder otherness (ontological, cognitive, social/relational, and ethical/legal) and analyze how each creates paradoxical effects on legitimacy, simultaneously obstructing conventional paths while potentially enabling novel forms. Our contribution is analytical rather than predictive: by examining how legitimacy would function for a non-human founder, we expose assumptions embedded in current theory and identify the conceptual work required if entrepreneurial agency is not exclusively human. Additionally, this analysis advances the meaningful heterodoxy research program by extending the concept of otherness to its logical extreme and reveals that legitimacy theory, as currently constituted, cannot accommodate non-human founders—a finding with implications for the growing continuum of human-AI collaboration in venture creation.

Born Illegitimate? How Web Presence and Certification Repair Foundational Liabilities

ENT
Rajarshi Mukherjee (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
While the prevailing dominant view suggests that starting a business without formal registration undermines legitimacy and hampers long-term performance, we challenge this assumption by shifting attention from national institutions to firm-level strategies that help overcome legitimacy deficits. Drawing on legitimacy theory, we argue that, irrespective of their national context, firms can mitigate the disadvantages of non-registration at founding by engaging in two key legitimation strategies—building a strong web presence and signaling quality through internationally recognized certifications. Analyzing enterprise-level data on 102,778 firms across 134 countries, we confirm the negative association between nonregistration at founding and future firm performance and provide strong evidence in support of the contingent roles of having a web presence and possessing quality signals. Our study contributes to research on informal entrepreneurship and legitimacy by identifying firm-level mechanisms that enable informally founded ventures to overcome legitimacy barriers and perform effectively despite institutional constraints. Through our study, we move the conversation beyond macro-level institutional explanations and highlight actionable legitimation strategies that entrepreneurs and policymakers can adopt to bridge the formal–informal divide.

How Ventures Reconcile Divergent Legitimacy Demands When Higher-Level

ENT
Jue Wang (The Pennsylvania State University)
Ventures rely on narratives to translate new technologies into meaningful value propositions and gain legitimacy, yet preemption regulations, which shift authority from local governments to higher levels, can require them to comply with state or federal rules even when those legally compliant activities conflict with the moral expectations of local communities. Faced with the conflict between higher-level regulators and local communities, ventures tend to moralize their narratives through moral scaling (increasing the intensity of moral claims) and moral layering (invoking a broader set of moral foundations). Moreover, the effects of preemption on narrative moralization are stronger when ventures operate in local communities with high moral awareness or when higher-level regulators exhibit more divergent legitimacy demands, both of which amplify the interpretive challenges that ventures seek to navigate. Using the U.S. commercial drone industry (2014–2019), this paper used state-level preemption regulations as institutional shocks and analyze ventures’ website narratives every year for moral content. The findings strongly support the proposed hypotheses. This paper advances research on entrepreneurial narratives by showing that ventures moralize their stories to reconcile conflicting institutional demands that arise across and within governance levels.

Social Legitimacy as a Foundation for Female Business Creation in Entrepreneurial Ecosystems

ENT
Julian Schenkenhofer (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Filippo Marchesani (Gabriele d'Annunzio University); Alice Civera (University of Bergamo)
Why do some entrepreneurial ecosystems (EEs) remain exclusionary despite abundant resources, networks, and institutional support? Existing research often assumes that ecosystem maturity naturally leads to inclusivity. Challenging this view, we integrate entrepreneurial ecosystem and legitimacy theories to explain when and for whom ecosystems function effectively. We argue that the impact of ecosystem quality on female entrepreneurship depends on the social legitimacy of women as entrepreneurial actors—that is, the extent to which women are collectively recognized as appropriate and desirable entrepreneurs. In contexts of high legitimacy, ecosystem features such as dense networks, accelerators, and knowledge flows foster greater female participation. In low-legitimacy contexts, however, the same structures reinforce exclusionary dynamics. Our multilevel framework links micro-level legitimation efforts by female founders with macro-level discursive and performative legitimation processes within ecosystems. Using longitudinal data from 42 Italian cities (2010–2023) and dynamic panel estimations (System GMM), we show that ecosystem quality increases women’s entrepreneurial activity only where social legitimacy is high. These findings identify legitimacy as the key mechanism translating ecosystem resources into inclusive outcomes and advance ecosystem research by shifting attention from structural elements to underlying social mechanisms.
