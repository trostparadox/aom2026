Seeing and Shaping the Future: Encountering Multiple Futures Through Multimodality

Session overview

“The future” has become a central concern across organization and management research, including organization theory, entrepreneurship, and strategy-as-practice (e.g., Pettit et al., 2023; Wenzel et al., 2025). Yet much of this engagement remains conditioned by the assumption that the future can be planned, forecast, and in this sense, “disciplined” (Kornberger, 2013; e.g., Fergnani, 2022; Healey & Hodgkinson, 2024). While managers, entrepreneurs, and organizations continue to rely on practices of forecasting, planning, and prediction, a growing body of research challenges the notion of a single, coherent temporal trajectory, arguing that any attempt to anticipate “what is not yet” necessarily unfolds under conditions of fundamental or Knightian uncertainty (e.g., Alvarez & Porac, 2020). This raises challenges for mobilizing commitment and engagement around futures that, by definition, remain uncertain and only partially knowable. It also suggests that the future emerges as a plurality of futures that may be imagined (Logue & Grimes, 2022), simulated (Leonardi & Leavell, 2025), or speculated (Gümüsay & Reinecke, 2024); as well as negotiated, resisted, or imposed (Pettit et al., 2023). In a parallel development, scholarship in management has increasingly moved beyond a primary focus on verbal language to attend to non-verbal (e.g., visual, material) and multimodal (e.g., combinations of these) means of communication (e.g., Meyer et al., 2018; Paroutis & Knight, 2019). While still relatively limited compared to predominantly verbal approaches, such research recognizes that attempts of organizing and strategizing for the future are not only articulated and experienced in words but also rendered through a plurality of communicative forms and practices. These include temporal kinds, such as talk and gesture (Thompson & Byrne, 2022); more durable kinds, such as printed or digital notes, sketches, renderings (Comi & Whyte, 2018), flowcharts, matrices, and timelines (Kornberger, 2013; Knight et al., 2018); and ultimately, materialized prototypes and designs (Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012). Facilitated by new technological developments such as in 3D-renderings and AI-based tools, such multimodal resources have not only become more diverse but also more easily accessible (Kuric & Höllerer, 2025). What remains underexplored, however, is the nature of this co-development: to what extent these shifts toward plural futures and toward multimodality meaningfully intersect and how they shape one another. If both developments co-occur, are there conceptual and/or phenomenological reasons for this convergence, and if so, what can we learn from it? Emerging research already suggests that this connection matters. For instance, visual and material communication may afford multivocality, thereby opening futures to interpretation (Giovannoni & Quattrone, 2018; Quattrone, 2017). Digital representations such as dashboards or simulations may strengthen perceptions of calculability and control, with potential implications for mobilizing engagement around futures, yet they may also produce closure when futures appear definitive and other alternatives are obscured (Leonardi & Leavell, 2025). These and other dynamics have crucial implications for power, interpretability, and contestation (Whye et al., 2025), as futures may gain traction not necessarily because of their content, but because of the expressions through which they are communicated (Kuric & Höllerer, 2025). This symposium brings together scholars who come from diverse disciplinary perspectives yet share a common concern for both the future’s plurality and its multimodal articulation—some foregrounding one of the dimensions, others engaging both pluralities simultaneously. By placing these perspectives in dialogue, the symposium aims to deepen conversations across fields to illuminate how futures are produced, stabilized, and contested using multimodal means of communication. By advancing dialogue across fields such as organization theory, entrepreneurship, and strategy-as practice, this symposium does not seek to aim to reconcile perspectives into a single framework, but to explore how different approaches can productively inform one another.
