“It’s a Waiting Game:” How Organizations Address the Distant Future of a Decarbonized World

OMT
Oliver Hirschfelder (EBS University of Business and Law); Carina Keller (EBS University of Business and Law); Patricia Helena Hein (Ivey School of Business)
This study explores how the factors that impede or facilitate organizations from taking action to achieve distant futures as they employ waiting practices. Utilizing a longitudinal research design over 3.5 years, the study examines two major companies in the power generation sector. The study reconceptualizes waiting as a form of interorganizational temporal coordination, challenging the notion of waiting as passive. First, we contribute to a more relational and processual understanding of how organizations seemingly coordinate (in)action under conditions of uncertainty and interdependence, particularly in fields like climate action, where roles and temporal authority remain ambiguous. Second, we introduce resonance as a mechanism that renders distant futures experientially tangible and underscores the significance of ‘quitted waiting’ in instances where organizations relinquish previously held future orientations. Third, this research contributes to the understanding of how organizations navigate distant futures. It emphasizes the strategic role of (in)action and the dynamic, relational processes involved in coordinating temporal engagement across organizational boundaries. This extends the temporal research by illuminating how organizations transition out of a state of waiting, not as a collapse of vision, but as a shift toward new forms of engagement.

The Dynamic Present: Coordinating Time and Emotions in Collaborative Future-Oriented Work

OMT
Linda Mitrojorgji (NEOMA Business School)
Through a seven-month ethnography of an architectural project, I explore how a multi-stakeholder working group engaged in temporal work that simultaneously accounted for multiple, competing temporal orientations. I develop a model showing how the mechanism of "intermediate time frames” enabled actors to comply with externally imposed clock-time deadlines while maintaining focus on event-time processes oriented toward a desirable future. I demonstrate how temporal talk, temporal structuring, and temporal objects within these intermediate time frames enabled continuous construction of a "dynamic present" – a provisional, shared understanding of where the group had been, where it stood, and where it was heading. This “dynamic present” helped actors reconcile short-term feasibility with long-term desirability, coordinate across different organizational contexts, and manage emotional challenges following past failure. This study contributes to temporal work research by revealing how temporal coordination integrates practical coordination with emotional regulation, enabling progressive refinement rather than demoralizing iterations.

Leveraging Serendipity for Future-Making: The Influence of Organizational Climate

OMT
Maria Rita Micheli (IESEG School of Management)
The environment where organizations operate is increasingly characterized by uncertainty, making traditional forecasting insufficient for shaping futures. In such contexts, unexpected knowledge cues can play a generative role for future strategies. We develop a theoretical model on how organizations can cultivate serendipity potentiality - the capability to notice, interpret, and reframe weak cues. Building on prior research, we identify three cognitive micro-processes underpinning this capability: identification of distant cues, informed intuition, and novel problem formulation. We then theorize how two types of organizational climate - performance-oriented and high identification - activate these micro-processes through distinct pathways. Bridging research on serendipity, organizational climate, and future-making, our model conceptualizes serendipity potentiality as a cultivable strategic capability and identifies organizational climate as a lever for its activation.

What if Futures are Made Quietly? The Relational Dynamics of Organizational Future-Making

OMT
Clara Scheve (Hamburg University of Technology); Thomas Wrona (Hamburg University of Technology)
In this paper, we develop the concept of “ambient future-making” to theorize how organizational futures are shaped through opaque, unintentional, and routinized practices. Drawing on practice theory and social phenomenology, we delineate a distinction between ambient future-making and more visible, intentional, and agentic forms of “focal future-making”. By conceptualizing future-making as an ongoing process of temporal coordination rather than episodic strategic action, our approach offers a conceptual boundary that distinguishes between future-shaping effects and their explicit articulation in strategic episodes. We explain how ambient future-making prefigures organizational trajectories, operating through metrics, routines, and infrastructures that are largely unrecognized as sites of futurity. We further theorize the relational dynamics of future-making by examining four relational modes (amplification, multifrequency, reverberation, quietness) through which focal and ambient future-making intersect. Our work contributes to a more precise theorization of future-making, illuminating how organizational futures are shaped not only explicitly but primarily through the taken-for-granted background of organizational life.

Cross-Sectoral Narrative Modes for Desirable Future Making: The Role of Projective Objects

OMT
Ilaria Durante (Politecnico di Milano); Claudio Dell'Era (Politecnico di Milano); Stefano Magistretti (Politecnico di Milano); Juliane Reinecke (University of Oxford)
Current research on future-making has raised scholarly attention on the role of materiality in sparking desirable future narratives within single organizations and sectors, while emerging studies have recognized the value of cross-sectoral future-making in addressing complex and systemic challenges. Projective objects could facilitate collaboration between cross-sectoral actors with different sets of past experiences, interests and worldviews. Thus, we explore how projective objects facilitate the construction of narrative modes about desirable futures in the transition from organizational to cross-sectoral future-making. We conduct a nine-month qualitative study involving organizations that operate in different sectors in a Future-making Lab where practitioners produced desirable futures through projective objects. We gathered and analysed the narrative modes produced in conversations and reported in the diverse projective objects developed across time by the cross-sectoral participants in the study. Our research proposes a process model that unpacks three different narrative modes about desirable futures: (i) disentangling, (ii) metamorphosing and (iii) deliberating. The production of these narrative modes is enhanced by different kinds of projective objects (repositories of present and past knowledge, forms of future knowledge and maps of past, present and future knowledge) in the transition from organizational to cross-sectoral future-making. The contributions of our study are threefold. First, it advances future-making by showing the relevance of a cross-sectoral perspective. Second, it provides a prospective conceptualization of narrative modes about desirable futures. Third, it contributes to the literature on boundary objects by exploring projective objects as boundary objects in future-making, highlighting the future-oriented affordances of boundary objects.
