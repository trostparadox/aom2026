Rivals Watching Rivals: The Discipline of Competitor Transparency in ESG Reporting

SIM
Youde Yang (shandong university of finance and economics); Bakr Al-Gamrh (Rennes School of Business); Fei Jinhua (Southeast University); Xu Guanghua (Nanjing University of Science and Technology)
This paper explores a governance mechanism for ESG greenwashing from the competitors’ perspective. Using data of Chinese A-share listed companies from 2012 to 2022, we investigate how competitor disclosure impact corporate ESG greenwashing. We propose two explanatory frameworks: a symbiotic relationship and a competitive relationship. Results show that competitor disclosure significantly hinders corporate ESG greenwashing, with findings robust across various tests. Under the symbiotic relationship, the corporate size similarity enhances the inhibitory effect, and disclosure improves the accuracy of analysts’ earnings forecasts. In the competitive mechanism, competition intensity enhances the inhibitory effect, while competitor disclosure enhances the differentiation in corporate information disclosure. Furthermore, heterogeneity analysis shows this effect is significant only for firms in the growth or maturity stage and with high analyst coverage. These findings extend understanding of the interchange between competitors and focal firms, enrich competitive relations research and offer a novel market-based approach to mitigate ESG greenwashing.

Rainbow- Hushing or Washing? Institutional Complexity and Corporate Rhetoric

SIM
Yu Li (SKEMA Business School - Grand Paris Campus)
Firms frequently engage in corporate social responsibility (CSR) decoupling, which refers to the inconsistency between a company’s CSR actions and its communications. This paper investigates under what conditions firms engage in rainbow-hushing (understate actions on LGBTQ issues) and rainbow-washing (overstate actions on LGBTQ issues). Drawing upon the literature on institutional complexity and impression management, we suggest that institutional dispersion, defined as the variation of policies in a firm’s institutional environment, positively and negatively relates to firms’ rainbow-hushing and rainbow-washing decisions, respectively. Further, we propose that institutional tendency, defined as the average level of policies towards LGBTQ-supportive in a firm’s institutional environment, will negatively moderate these two relationships. Using machine learning-based measures and 2,461 observations of Fortune 500 companies during 2016 and 2022, we find strong support for the hypotheses. This research contributes to literatures on CSR decoupling, institutional complexity, and stakeholder management.

What's in a Name? Eponymy, Reputation, and Greenwashing

SIM
Weicheng Chiu (Tongji University); Yize Qin (Tongji University); Xuzhe Liu (Tongji University); Xiaojing Ni (Tongji University)
Greenwashing as a strategic decoupling between symbolic environmental disclosure and substantive environmental performance remains a persistent challenge to sustainable development, particularly in institutional contexts with weak regulatory enforcement and limited third-party auditing. While prior research has primarily focused on external regulatory pressure and market-based monitoring as constraints on greenwashing, far less is known about how ownership identity functions as an internal governance mechanism. Drawing on signaling theory and the socioemotional wealth (SEW) perspective, this study examines whether family eponymy constrains corporate greenwashing and how this effect varies across reputational environments. Using panel data on Chinese listed family firms from 2009 to 2021 and a text-based measure of greenwashing derived from corporate social responsibility reports, we find that family eponymous firms exhibit significantly lower levels of greenwashing than their non-eponymous counterparts. Moreover, this constraining effect is context dependent: it is amplified in affective evaluation environments characterized by positive media sentiment, but attenuated in technocratic evaluation environments with extensive analyst coverage. These findings advance research on CSR decoupling by identifying ownership identity as a governance mechanism and demonstrating that the effectiveness of identity-based signals hinges on the prevailing reputational regime.

Fresh at the Helm: Rookie CEOs and Corporate ESG Greenwashing

SIM
Yuanqiong He; Meng Yi (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); LINFEI ZHONG (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); Sana Chiu (University of Houston); Yong Lin (University of Birmingham)
Firms increasingly use ESG disclosures to signal responsible governance; however, ESG reporting may diverge from actual ESG performance, creating disclosure-performance gaps that are commonly interpreted as greenwashing. While prior research emphasizes specific CEO characteristics, we know less about how CEO career stage influences greenwashing behavior. Drawing on career concerns theory, we argue that rookie CEOs (i.e., executives serving in their first CEO appointment) face a credibility deficit and greater reputational vulnerability, which makes greenwashing riskier in their CEO careers. Consequently, rookie CEOs are more likely to pursue credibility-protecting disclosure strategies by lowering ESG decoupling. Using a panel data of 8,802 firm-year observations from Chinese listed firms during 2012-2023, we find that rookie CEOs are associated with lower ESG greenwashing and that governance mechanisms (internal controls and reduced agency costs) serve as a pathway through which CEO career-stage incentives increase ESG disclosure credibility. Furthermore, the rookie CEO effect is stronger in state-owned enterprises and weaker in heavily polluting industries. This study contributes to greenwashing/decoupling research by introducing CEO career stage as a crucial predictor and extends career concerns theory by highlighting when executive career stage may motivate credibility protection rather than impression management.
