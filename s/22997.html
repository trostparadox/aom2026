A Value-Risk Framework for Human Capital Under Geopolitical Industrial Policy

IM
Ao (Jessi) YAN (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Man-Nok Wong (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Prevailing institutional logic suggests that firms will conform to geopolitical industrial policies, such as the U.S. CHIPS and Science Act, by decoupling from rival-nation talent and consolidating domestic workforces. This view overlooks the tension between institutional pressures for legitimacy and the strategic imperative to retain competitively vital human capital. We develop and test a value-risk framework, extending strategic human capital theory, which posits that firms navigate this tension by reallocating talent based on a calculus of strategic value versus geopolitical institutional risk. Analyzing LinkedIn data from Revelio Labs and firm-level data from Compustat, we employ a quasi-experimental design to examine talent mobility. Contrary to the decoupling script, we find the CHIPS Act triggers increased outflow of U.S.-domestic talent from incumbent firms due to intensified competition, while firms actively retain high-value, globally embedded experts. Findings reveal strategic retention, not blanket exclusion, challenging the assumption that conformity overrides competitive logic. We contribute a microfoundational model of human capital reallocation under techno-geopolitical uncertainty and highlight critical unintended labor market consequences for policymakers and firms.

Geopolitical Conflicts and Social Skill Hiring

IM
Bingkun Zhang (University of Melbourne); Jingru YU (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
Whereas existing literature posits relocation/reshoring and political lobbying as the primary strategies for multinational enterprises (MNEs) to manage geopolitical risks, we propose an alternative market-based mitigation strategy: the strategic hiring of human capital with strong social skills for supply chain positions. Leveraging the U.S.-China trade war as a natural experiment, our difference-in-differences analysis of about 33 million U.S. job postings reveals that U.S. MNEs exposed to the trade war increase social skill hiring in supply chain positions, particularly in managerial positions. This effect is stronger for MNEs with highly dispersed Chinese supply bases and weaker for those in national security industries, and MNEs with more social skill hiring experience enhanced stability with Chinese suppliers in the post-trade-war period. Overall, our findings highlight the critical role of supply chain human capital in enabling MNEs to manage geopolitical risks.

Political Cognitions at the Top: Ideological Orientation, Distribution and Reshoring of U.S. Firms

IM
Guus Hendriks (University of Amsterdam); Tao Chen (University College Cork); Tazeeb Rajwani (University of Surrey)
Reshoring, the return of previously offshored activities to the home country, has become an increasingly salient strategic issue amid geopolitical tensions and calls for economic nationalism. Yet firms differ markedly in their reshoring activity, raising questions about the managerial and political underpinnings of such decisions. Building on upper echelons theory, we examine how the political cognitions of top management teams (TMTs)—both their ideological orientation and ideological distribution (i.e., the degree of like-mindedness among members)—shape reshoring behavior. Using panel data on U.S. firms from 2010 to 2021 and firm-level reshoring events, we find that firms led by more politically conservative TMTs engage in fewer reshoring activities. This negative relationship is weaker when there is greater female representation in TMTs and when media coverage of reshoring is higher. While ideological like-mindedness shows no direct effect, its interaction with media visibility is positive, indicating that cohesive TMTs respond to heightened legitimacy pressures when reshoring becomes salient in public discourse. Our study contributes to international business research by (1) advancing a microfoundations perspective on de-internationalization that integrates managerial political ideology, (2) enriching upper echelons theory by showing that strategic inertia arises through conditional, context-dependent mechanisms of ideology and homogeneity, and (3) identifying internal diversity and external scrutiny as boundary conditions that shape how political cognitions influence global strategy. More broadly, we extend the emerging micro-political perspective in international business by conceptualizing managerial political cognitions as a form of political embeddedness that links executives’ ideological orientations to firm-level strategic behavior.

Selective Identity Expression in Family Multinationals Under Geopolitical Tensions (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Mengyuan Lyu (Fudan University); Jiasi Fan (Beijing Technology and Business University); Xiaoping Zhao (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
In an era of escalating geopolitical tensions, growing attention has been devoted to how multinational enterprises respond to global risks. This study examines an underexplored question: how family multinational enterprises (FMNEs) strategically respond to home-country stakeholder concerns during periods of geopolitical tension. Drawing on organizational identity theory, we argue that FMNEs engage in selective identity expression through governance adjustments. Specifically, they emphasize the “family” dimension by increasing family representation on the board as a commitment to long-term orientation and strategic stability, while emphasizing the “business” dimension by reducing family involvement in the top management team (TMT) to demonstrate professionalism and effective risk management. We further show that these responses are shaped by heterogeneous exposure to stakeholder concerns: the board-level effect is stronger among FMNEs with higher pre-event reputation, whereas the TMT-level effect is more pronounced among FMNEs with higher levels of pre-event operational embeddedness. Using a sample of 1,099 Chinese listed family firms from 2009 to 2019 and exploiting three major geopolitical events, the 2012 Sino-Japan dispute, the 2016 Sino-South Korea dispute, and the 2018 Sino-U.S. trade war, we find robust support for our hypotheses. This study advances research by highlighting MNEs’ home-country responses to geopolitical tensions, extending understanding of organizational identity work as a strategic crisis response, and revealing the context-dependent nature of family involvement.

Decision-Makers’ Ideological Diversity, Geopolitical Risk, and Firms’ Geographic Scope

IM
Yannick Thams (Florida Atlantic University)
Why do multinationals spread their realm in heterogeneous locations while others remain confined in institutionally homogeneous environments? While international business research has devoted attention to this question, it has minimally addressed how firms’ decision-makers differ in their perceptions of countries and in their interpretations of host-country institutional conditions as a function of their political ideology. We propose a behavioral perspective that centers on the political ideology of the corporate elite and brings politics inside firms. We argue that the heterogeneity in political beliefs within a firm’s top management team and board is likely to enlarge the number of institutional contexts considered and deemed acceptable by the latter, which in turn is expected to increase the institutional heterogeneity of its international portfolios. We further demonstrate that this relationship is stronger when the level of global geopolitical risk is higher, given the increased complexity, ambiguity, and uncertainty that such supranational risk poses, which in turn increases the reliance on executive values to make strategic decisions. Our study provides a behavioral microfoundation for international footprint and endogenizes political ideology as a determinant of global strategy, and is the first to introduce ideological diversity as a cognitive dimension in explaining decision-makers' interpretations of foreign environments.
