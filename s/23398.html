Learning in the Age of AI: Cognitive Risks, Transfer Barriers, and Critical Reckoning

Session overview

AI promises to transform learning, but does it deliver? This session confronts the hard evidence and critical perspectives on what generative AI actually does to student cognition, skill transfer, and knowledge retention. From controlled experiments on long-term learning degradation, to a bold new framework of Symbiagogy that repositions teachers as meta-agents in human-machine co-learning, to a critical interrogation of how AI is reframing the very pathologies of management education, these papers offer a rigorous, necessary counterpoint to the AI hype dominating the field. Grounded in experiential learning theory, self-regulation, and sociomateriality, they ask not just what AI can do, but what it is doing to us.

Self-Regulated AI Use Hinders Long-Term Learning

MED
Stefanos Poulidis (INSEAD); Osbert Bastani (University of Pennsylvania)
There has been significant recent interest in leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) to aid student learning. Current systems enable students to control the timing and nature of AI assistance; however, this student- directed access risks short-circuiting the effortful practice that is essential for lasting expertise. To understand these risks, we conducted a field experiment with over 200 chess club students training intensively on a custom AI-assisted chess platform. Students were randomly assigned to either a system-regulated condition, where the platform automatically provided AI tips at key moments, or a self-regulated condition, where students could additionally request help at any time by clicking a button. After 12 weeks of training, we find that both groups improved their chess skills, but students in the self-regulated condition achieved less than half the performance gains of students in the system-regulated condition (30% vs. 64%). Mediation analyses suggest two mechanisms underlying this gap: diminished productive struggle (based on assistance requested in the Zone of Proximal Development) and reduced engagement. Self-regulated students trained less, reported a lower sense of accomplishment, and became increasingly reliant on AI, despite being aware of its potential harms. These adverse effects are mitigated among highly motivated students but are not moderated by skill level. Our findings demonstrate that while scaffolded AI assistance can accelerate learning, unrestricted access can undermine it.

Theorizing The Experiential Learning Cycle in AI-Mediated Management Education

MED
Federico Ruggiero (University of Bergamo); Ana Isabel Canhoto; Daniela Andreini (University of Bergamo)
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is rapidly entering higher education, yet much of the emerging research still focuses on adoption or outcomes rather than on how AI-mediated learning processes unfold over time in authentic course settings. Building on this gap, we introduced a personalized chatbot based on GenAI, as a free study companion within a UK university term and investigated how students incorporate it into everyday study practices, and how they describe AI-mediated learning through iterative movement across Kolb’s experiential learning cycle. We conducted end-of-term online interviews with nine PhD students, and analyzed transcripts using an abductive, thematic approach in NVivo, and iteratively refining categories in dialogue with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle. Findings show that students did not treat the chatbot as a standalone solution but they embedded it within a wider tool ecology and moved across tools to manage context, variable response quality, and ongoing worries about unreliable or invented references. A recurring tension ran between using the chatbot for answer-first retrieval routines and a learning-oriented use characterized by epistemic verification. The paper contributes by extending Kolb’s cycle to AI-mediated learning by theorising epistemic verification as an AI-specific form of reflective observation.

What is Wrong in Management Learning and Education? A Study of AI Use Among Business Students

MED
Kanika Meshram (University of Melbourne); Evonne L Irwin (University of Melbourne); Bill Harley (University of Melbourne)
The challenges which generative artificial intelligence (AI) poses to management learning and education are frequently framed in terms of its role as a disruptive external force, which challenges academic integrity and skill acquisition, thereby undermining educational processes and outcomes. This paper argues that while this is a legitimate view, it is only part of the story. Informed by analysis of interview data from postgraduate business students, we argue that it is the problematic nature of much management learning and education which makes ‘learning’ so amenable to outsourcing to AI. Drawing on post-structuralist theories of agency and technologies of the self, we explore how students interact with AI in their studies and illustrate how these interactions are shaped by a complex interplay of the technologies of AI and the nature of management learning and education. Addressing the challenges posed by AI requires not just a rethinking of how AI use is managed, but of the nature of curricula and learning and teaching practices in business schools.

New Paradigm in B-School Pedagogy: Insights from an AI-Avatar-Led Classroom

MED
Navyasree Yekkaluri (Indian Institute of Management Kozhikode); Anupam Das (Indian Institute of Management Kozhikode)
In the context of Generative AI, this study explores the evolving ‘pedagogical’ paradigm in Management education by proposing an alternative framework, ‘Symbiagogy’ that reflects co-learning between humans and machines. Theoretically we build on Experiential learning theory (ELT) and Self-Determination theory (SDT); framed within the philosophical lens of sociomateriality. To investigate how AI can co-create meaningful learning experiences, we draw our empirical evidence from a classroom intervention, involving an AI-Avatar-led instruction among 154 B-school students in a ‘Business-Communication’ course; followed by a Qualitative survey. The thematic analysis using MAXQDA unraveled five major themes - Cognitive structuring and experiential views, Relational disconnection and affective gaps, AI-Augmented autonomy and cognitive extension, Orchestrator of human-machine assemblage, Designing communicative Intra-actions for symbiotic learning. Findings offer practical directions to Symbiagogy that suggest the repositioning of teacher’s role as meta-agent orchestrating cognitive co-agency, reflexive autonomy, dynamic relatedness, situated co-design and ethical symmetry by acknowledging AI as ‘epistemic partner’.
