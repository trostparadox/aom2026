From Global Exposure to Strategic Transformation: The Role of Returnee Directors in AI Adoption

TIM
Muhammad Ahmed (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Chengli Shu (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is reshaping the competitive landscape, yet many emerging market firms (EMFs) struggle to integrate it due to institutional gaps, limited technological infrastructure, and organizational constraints. This study investigates how returnee directors—board members with international education or professional experience—enable EMFs to overcome these barriers and adopt AI effectively. Drawing on institutional theory and upper echelons theory, we argue that returnee directors act as both strategic leaders and institutional translators, leveraging global exposure to reframe AI practices for local contexts. Analyzing a panel of 3,680 Chinese A-share listed firms from 2011 to 2018, we find that returnee directors enhance AI adoption through two complementary pathways: strategic knowledge transfer and global network bridging. Strategic knowledge transfer embeds advanced managerial practices and AI-driven routines within firm operations, while global network bridging facilitates access to international partnerships, technological resources, and best practices. These mechanisms enable EMFs to adopt AI not merely as a technical tool but as a strategic capability aligned with long-term objectives. By integrating micro-level leadership characteristics with macro-level institutional dynamics, the study advances theory on technology adoption in emerging economies. Practically, it underscores the critical role of globally experienced leadership in driving digital transformation, offering insights for firms and policymakers seeking to leverage international human capital for competitive advantage.

The Impact of Temporary Student Visa Extensions on Patenting: Evidence from STEM OPT

TIM
Seungmin Yoo (Boston University)
Can foreign students at U.S. universities, with a brief work extension, become inventors without displacing native talent? This paper examines one of the most significant recent U.S. immigration policy changes, the 2008 STEM Optional Practical Training extension, which gives international STEM graduates an additional 17 months of work authorization. I show a 59.9%–86.8% increase in the number of first-time immigrant inventors and a 20%–23.1% increase in the share of first-time immigrant inventors in CBSAs with R1 universities relative to other areas after the policy change. I find no evidence of first-time native inventors being displaced.

Shielding or Stifling? How Restrictions on Foreign Acquisition Shape U.S. Entrepreneurial Innovation

TIM
Qing He (National University of Singapore); Kenneth G. Huang (National University of Singapore)
Governments increasingly restrict foreign acquisitions of domestic technologies to protect national security and technological leadership. While such policies directly target individual transactions, their broader implications for entrepreneurial innovation remain poorly understood. We study how foreign acquisition restrictions reshape innovation incentives among U.S. entrepreneurial firms by examining publicly announced foreign takeover blockages by the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS). Our core argument is that CFIUS blockages erode the expected value of acquisition-based exit opportunities—particularly for technology-oriented startups for which foreign buyers constitute an important class of potential acquirers—thereby weakening incentives to invest in publicly disclosed innovation. Using a novel dataset of CFIUS- blocked acquisitions and a staggered difference-in-differences design, we find that entrepreneurial firms operating in the same sector as a blocked target experience a significant decline in patented innovation output, even when they are not directly involved in the blocked transaction. Overall patenting declines by 18.1 percent, with a larger decline of 25.8 percent in the firm’s core technological domains. The quality of patents, measured by forward citations, also declines. Furthermore, firms adjust their innovation trajectories by shifting activity away from prior core domains and into new technological areas. However, these shifts are not immediately accompanied by improvement in innovation quality in the newly entered domains, indicating the presence of switching costs and transitional inefficiencies. Together, our findings show that foreign acquisition screening extend beyond directly blocked firms, reducing innovation among industry peers and imposing adjustment costs as firms reorient their technological focus.
