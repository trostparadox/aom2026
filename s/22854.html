Inclusive Leadership and Breakthrough Innovation Among Expatriate Employees

OB
Jayesh Pandey (Institute of Rural Management Anand); Yusuf Hassan (Heriot Watt University)
This study investigates how inclusive leadership influences breakthrough innovation among expatriate employees through the mediating role of knowledge quality and the moderating role of knowledge application. Drawing on the knowledge-based view of the firm, this study develops and tests a conceptual model using survey data collected from 273 expatriate employees across multiple industries in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Structural equation modeling and moderation analysis were used to test the hypotheses. The results indicate that inclusive leadership positively affects breakthrough innovation through enhanced knowledge quality. Furthermore, knowledge application strengthens the indirect relationship between inclusive leadership and innovation, suggesting that the conversion of high-quality knowledge into innovation is more effective when knowledge is actively applied in organizational processes. This study contributes to leadership and knowledge management research by identifying knowledge quality as a key mechanism and knowledge application as a boundary condition in the inclusive leadership–innovation link, particularly in the context of globally mobile talent.

Humanistic Leadership: The Catalyst for Meaning in Hospital Ancillary Work

OB
Boxiang Lin (Nottingham University Business School China); Ping Ping Fu (Nottingham University Business School China); Qing Qu (Tsinghua University); Xiaoyu Peng (Nottingham Univercity Business School)
Hospital ancillary workers, including cleaning, catering, maintenance, and security staff, support hospital operations but are not medical professionals. This group of people, due to the nature of their work, suffers from physical, social and emotional occupational stigmatization and is often regarded as a forgotten group. Prior research highlights the importance of outsiders’ affirmation for cultivating dirty workers’ work meaningfulness. However, there is still a puzzle regarding how to introduce dirty workers into positive interactions with others. This gap is particularly salient within people-oriented approaches, such as humanistic management and humanistic leadership. This study takes a case study approach to illustrate: 1) Humanistic leadership behaviors toward dirty workers; 2) Changes under the catalysis of humanistic leadership; and 3) The mechanism through which dirty workers earn affirmation under the catalysis. The results indicate it is possible for dirty workers to earn respect and recognition from others through their own efforts toward humanistic learning under the catalysis of humanistic leadership. The dirty work affirmation-acquisition mechanisms identified in this study may expand research on cultivating occupational meaningfulness in stigmatized work settings.

From Authoritative Parenting to Servant Leadership: A Life-Span Altruistic Developmental Pathway

OB
Xiaozhou Zhou (Yanshan University)
Drawing on Life Span Developmental Leadership Theory and Social Cognitive Theory, the study examines the developmental origins of servant leadership from an early family perspective. Specifically, the study argues that authoritative parenting style, as an altruistic parenting approach, fosters prosocial motivation as a core altruistic motivational mechanism, which in turn promotes servant leadership. Furthermore, identification with parents serves as a critical boundary condition that strengthens both the direct effect of authoritative parenting style on prosocial motivation and its indirect effect on servant leadership. Statistical analyses of 293 three-wave leader-subordinate dyads support a moderated mediation model consistent with these predictions. By integrating family and work contexts within a single framework, this study advances a process-oriented understanding of servant leadership as an outcome of early altruistic socialization and extends leadership development research beyond the workplace. The findings also offer practical insights for parents and organizations seeking to cultivate servant leadership.

Why and When Stars Help Peers Shine: Social Learning from Stars’ Proactive Helping

OB
Ying-Chun Chou (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Nai-Wen Chi (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Min-Hsuan Tu (University at Buffalo, School of Management)
Although scholars have theorized that star employees can produce constructive effects on non-star peers, it remains unclear how and when non-star peers can benefit from star employees’ proactive helping. Drawing on social learning theory, we propose that supervisors' servant leadership lead star employees to proactively help their peers, and star employees’ proactive helping enhancing non-star peers’ performance via increased job-related self-efficacy. Additionally, we also explore the moderating effect of the group star proportion (GSP) on these potential social learning processes. We conducted three studies to test our theoretical model using three distinct samples, including 97 insurance sales teams, 40 R&D engineer teams, and 65 automobile sales teams. The findings of multilevel path analysis revealed that (1) supervisors’ servant leadership positively correlated with star employees’ proactive helping even when transformational leadership or team-member exchange were controlled for; (2) star employees’ proactive helping had a positive indirect effect on their peers’ performance via peers’ job-related self-efficacy; and (3) a higher group star proportion intensified the positive relationship between stars’ proactive helping and peers’ job-related self-efficacy as well as the aforementioned indirect effect. Findings offer both theoretical and practical insights into how star employees benefit their peers and provide guidance on managing and leveraging the contributions of star employees.
