Damned if You Do(n’t): How Epistemic Technology Spoils Expertise

CTO
Ayman Ajaj (University of Ottawa); Mayur Joshi (Telfer School of Management, University of Ottawa); Mathieu Bouchard (University of Ottawa)
In both practitioner discourse and academic research, the role of epistemic technologies in shaping the expertise of knowledge workers is well recognized. While the literature has largely focused on the role of epistemic integration in this process, the role of social evaluations remains underexplored. In this study, we focus on a particular type of social evaluation, stigma, and theorize how it reshapes expertise when epistemic technologies are introduced into knowledge work domains. In particular, building on Goffman’s foundational work on stigma, we advance a concept of spoiled expertise which takes distinct forms based on two dimensions: knowledge workers’ stance of adoption of epistemic technology, and locus of evaluation of expertise. Together these two dimensions culminate into four forms of spoiled expertise: (1) slopped expertise, (2) lagging expertise, (3) shunned expertise, and (4) outdated expertise. We highlight the main transitions across these forms, and the moral rewriting of expertise under epistemic technologies. Our key contribution is to the literature on knowledge work by advancing a theory of spoiled expertise amid stigma. We conclude by outlining the implications for professionals, and by proposing a research agenda on spoiled expertise in the age of epistemic technologies and algorithmic knowledge production.

Towards Persistent Augmentation: A Three-Axis Model of Emergent Abilities in Human-AI Ensembles

CTO
Judy Rady; David Matthew Townsend (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
As AI systems grow increasingly advanced, organizational researchers debate whether augmentation represents merely a transitional phase preceding full automation. Drawing on emerging research on human-AI ensembles – socio-technical configurations in which human and artificial agents learn to collaborate to solve problems, make decisions, and create value – we challenge the presumed inevitability. We argue that under conditions of high task complexity, certain ensembles achieve a state of persistent augmentation, involving a stable configuration in which humans and AI systems remain jointly necessary. To develop this argument, we examine how variation in the structure, relational depth, and task performance outcomes between different types of ensembles creates a pathway towards persistent augmentation: adaptation, collaboration, extension, and integration. We further theorize that progress on this pathway is enabled by three critical factors: (1) task complexity, which refers to the degree to which a task resists decomposition into discrete, independently executable subtasks; (2) epistemic coupling, the structural alignment of knowledge between human-AI partners through which each member of the ensemble can reliably understand the other; and (3) agentic synchrony, the dynamic alignment and reciprocal steerability of action tempo and control handoffs that reduce coordination friction and amplify joint work. Combined together, these factors create emergent abilities, novel cognitive capacities that neither humans nor AI systems could achieve independently. We conclude by identifying organizational design principles for cultivating ensembles capable of sustaining these emergent abilities over time.

Of Tools and Teammates: A Typology of Human-AI Teaming Configurations

CTO
Tim-Christoph Engelhardt (University of Bremen); Julia Kensbock (University of Bremen); Benjamin Mueller (University of Bremen); Anna-Sophie Ulfert
Human–AI teaming (HAIT) leverages the complementary strengths of humans and artificial intelligence (AI) agents to achieve outcomes unattainable by either alone. While research and organizational interest in HAIT are rapidly growing, the literature presents highly diverse team configurations, varying in context, roles, and interaction patterns, without a coherent framework for describing or comparing them. This lack of classification hinders conceptual clarity, comparability across studies, and the development of cumulative knowledge. Existing classification systems are often context-specific or focus on narrow aspects of HAIT, limiting their general applicability. This paper addresses these gaps by developing a typology for systematically describing HAIT configurations. Drawing on typology-driven theorizing and inductive methods, we combine a review of the interdisciplinary HAIT literature with insights from an international survey of HAIT experts. The resulting typology comprises five dimensions and 23 sub-dimensions, each with characteristics that capture essential aspects of HAIT scenarios, including the configuration of human and AI agents, their roles, relationships, and operating conditions. The proposed typology offers three key contributions. Theoretically, it advances construct clarity in HAIT research by making configurations explicit and analyzable. Methodologically, it provides a structured vocabulary and set of dimensions that facilitate comparison and synthesis across studies. Practically, it supports researchers and practitioners in bridging disciplinary boundaries, fostering coherent discourse, and guiding empirical exploration. In doing so, the typology lays the groundwork for more cumulative and integrated HAIT scholarship.

The Double-Edged Sword Effect of Artificial Intelligence Usage on Employees' Relational Crafting

CTO
Yihua Wu (Zhongnan University of Economics and Law)
The frequent use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the workplace has led to the technological delegation of tasks that previously required interpersonal collaboration, profoundly reshaping employees' social experiences. This study, grounded in complementarity theory and the need-to-belong theory, constructs a dual-path model to examine how employees' use of AI influences relational crafting through two opposing mechanisms. On one hand, AI usage enhances employees' sense of competence, reduces their reliance on others, and thereby suppresses relational crafting. On the other hand, AI usage undermines employees' sense of belonging, motivating them to restore social connections, which in turn promotes relational crafting. Furthermore, this study proposes that interdependent (versus independent) self-construal exerts opposite moderating effects on these two pathways: it weakens the positive impact of AI usage on sense of competence or strengthens the negative impact of AI usage on sense of belonging. Through a two-phase questionnaire survey of 230 employees with AI usage, all hypotheses of this study were validated. This paper reveals the dual mechanisms and boundary conditions through which AI influences employees' relational behaviors, offering novel theoretical contributions to research on AI usage and job crafting, while also discussing implications for management practices and future research directions.
