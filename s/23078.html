Leader Gender and Evaluations of Follower Performance: A Meta-Analysis

HR
Zejun (Jenny) Zhao (Florida State University); Gang Wang (Florida State University); Janine Bosak (Dublin City University); Yingge Li (Not Associated)
Does leaders’ gender matter to their evaluations of followers’ job performance? Despite extensive research and convincing findings on evaluations of male and female leaders, we lack a compelling, cumulative understanding of the extent to which female and male leaders differ in their evaluations of their followers’ task performance, let alone under what conditions the difference may be stronger or weaker. To fill this important research gap, we conduct a meta-analysis to synthesize prior findings on the relationship between leader gender and evaluations of follower task performance and examine the moderating effects of three theoretical moderators: gender equalitarianism culture, leader-follower tenure, and female follower representation. Drawing from the role congruity perspective and the social identity threat perspective, we develop two competing hypotheses regarding the influence of leader gender on evaluations of follower task performance. Supporting the role congruity perspective, our meta-analytic regression results suggest that female leaders tended to give more favorable evaluations of followers’ task performance than male leaders. In addition, gender equalitarianism culture amplifies this effect, whereas leader-follower tenure attenuates it. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

The Current DEI Environment, Descriptive Norms of Equal Treatment and Female Leadership Selection

HR
Katie Manley (Monmouth University)
In the current U.S. political environment, Diversity, Equity and Inclusion (DEI) initiatives have come under scrutiny. Still, gender gaps in leadership positions persist. At the organizational level, predominant gender equality interventions (e.g., quotas) are prescriptive in nature and can result in backlash. At the individual-level, attitudinal support for women is intertwined with deeply engrained social identities and biases that can prove difficult to shift. Still there are other ways for organizations to impact the normative treatment of women and men in organizations. In this research we examine how perceptions of gender-equal treatment in organizations at varying levels of attitudinal support for women relate to the likelihood that a woman is selected as a successor. Specifically, we find that perceived gender-equal treatment in organizations has the largest positive female selection effect on those with low to moderate support for women. This research seeks to merge the literatures on descriptive norms and gender representation gaps, as well as provide future research opportunities to explore the antecedents of perceived gender-equal treatment in organizations, with the aim of improving leadership selection outcomes for women.

Unintended Consequences of Appointing Female CHROs for High-Investment Work Practice Adoption

HR
Junting Li (Tsinghua University); Joo Hun Han (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology); So Ri Park (Rutgers University)
Despite the growing presence of female executives in top management teams (TMTs), relatively little is known about how female executives in HRM roles— particularly Chief Human Resource Officers (CHROs)—shape organizational outcomes beyond diversity, equity, and inclusion and other social performance domains. Integrating the strategic HRM, gender, and TMT literatures, we theorize a potential downside of female leadership in the HRM context. We propose that female CHROs, who often face heightened scrutiny and pressure for short-term results, may be more reluctant to invest in costly high-investment work practices (HIWPs). We further advance a dual-threat framework, identifying organizational-level threat (low firm performance) and TMT-level threat (a critical mass of female executives) as amplifying conditions. Using panel data from 464 establishments (2015–2019), we find that female CHRO presence is negatively associated with the adoption of motivation- and opportunity-enhancing HR practices, but not ability-enhancing practices. The negative association with opportunity-enhancing practices is stronger when firm performance is low and when three or more women are present in the TMT; effects on ability-enhancing practices are also contingent on TMT composition. Overall, we identify CHRO gender as an antecedent of HIWPs and highlight unintended HRM consequences arising from threat-related contingencies faced by female CHROs.

Navigating Sisterhood and Solidarity: Women’s Career Advancement in Rwandan Women Led Organizations

HR
EMMANUEL BIGIRIMANA
Does having women leaders in an organisation guarantee career support for female subordinates? This study examines how subordinates and industry experts perceive the career support provided by top-level women leaders to their female subordinates in Rwanda. To gain a deeper understanding, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 37 respondents drawn from four distinct sectors. This qualitative study reveals four essential constructs that reflect our understanding of women’s leadership dynamics. Top-level women leaders perform Pre-emptive Pipeline Labor, proactively mentoring female subordinates to foster career advancement without compensation. Yet, interpersonal rivalry manifests as Competitive Sisterhood, fueling jealousy that fractures collaboration. Excluded subordinates contend with the Anxiety of Inclusion, uncertain belonging, but are still overlooked. Our findings reveal unintended exclusion, urging policymakers and HR to rethink inclusive leadership. This challenges the inherent assumption of empowerment and solidarity. This study extends social identity theory and reinterprets Queen Bee syndrome, revealing how top-level women leaders lead their junior women subordinates.

Employee Resource Groups for Single Women: Need, Design & Potential

HR
Prapti Aggarwal (Indian Institute of Management Kozhikode)
Singles, i.e., unpartnered individuals, are the fastest growing demography and yet single women at face stereotyping, biases and discrimination at work. Employee Resource Groups (ERGs) are known to support minorities such as individuals with different sexual orientation, race, gender, disability but research as well as practice remain silent on need as well as potential of ERGs for single women. This article addresses this gap by bringing together two currently disparate literatures, i.e., experience of single women at work & ERGs – and argues for need of ERGs for single women at workplace. At stage 1, it evaluates past success and associated factors for ERGs. At stage 2, it examines the current challenges of single women at work on the level of self, others and organization. Finally, it proposes a framework that can guide ERG implementation at work. The propositions address: (1) organizational contexts requiring single women's ERGs, (2) ERG emergence mechanisms in resistant cultures, (3) identity-based participation barriers, (4) organizational necessity contingencies, (5) executive sponsorship as legitimation, and (6) ally membership balancing psychological safety with power for advocacy. The article extends ERG literature to a new dimension of diversity, i.e., single individuals, who negotiate between an asset or a deficit identity. This framework provides practitioners actionable guidance while advancing diversity and inclusion literature by recognizing relationship status as legitimate diversity dimension deserving organizational attention.
