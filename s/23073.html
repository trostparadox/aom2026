“Off the Expected Path”: How Provider Networks Shape Cancer Care Trajectories and Outcomes

HCM
Luca Giorgio (European University of Rome); Daniele Mascia (Luiss university)
This study investigates how the organizational and network characteristics of first-access providers influence adherence to expected guideline-concordant care pathways and patient outcomes. Analyzing administrative data from 915 cancer patients in an Italian Local Health Authority, we reconstructed care trajectories through sequence analysis, comparing observed pathways against expert-defined benchmarks. A central innovation is the embedding of pathway adherence within the relational infrastructure of patient-sharing networks surrounding initial entry. Using a generalized structural equation model (GSEM), we modeled the probability of following expected sequences as a function of provider and network features (degree and diversity), while survival duration was modeled as a function of sequence type. Findings reveal that formal "hub" status is not significantly associated with concordance, suggesting that structural designations are insufficient without supporting routines, shared protocols, and interoperable information systems. In contrast, higher clinical complexity at the first-access facility significantly increased the likelihood of expected sequencing. Regarding network metrics, concordance decreases as providers have more patient-sharing ties (higher degree) but increases when partners are more heterogeneous (higher diversity). Notably, diversity attenuates the negative association between degree and concordance. Finally, adherence to expected sequences strongly correlates with longer survival. Overall, our findings highlight that the composition of patient-sharing networks—not only connectivity—matters for guideline-consistent care pathways.

How Physicians’ Advice Networks Reconfigure During Hospital Organizational Change

HCM
Mario Masiello (Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore); Luca Giorgio (European University of Rome)
Organizational restructuring in hospitals requires clinicians to adapt not only to new formal arrangements but also to evolving patterns of professional interaction. Drawing on social network analysis, this study examines how advice networks among physicians reconfigure following a transition to a process-based organizational model in a large Italian hospital. Using longitudinal network data collected before and after the reorganization, we apply exponential random graph models (ERGMs) to analyze the formation and persistence of advice ties. The results show that network change is shaped by a combination of demographic and professional attributes, endogenous relational mechanisms, and individual responses to change. Specifically, gender heterophily and shared clinical specialization influence tie formation and persistence, while reciprocity and triadic closure play a central role in structuring new advice relationships. Moreover, physicians’ cognitive resistance to change significantly inhibits the formation of new ties, whereas affective and behavioral resistance show no independent effects. By integrating ERGMs with a multilevel perspective on organizational change, this study highlights how hospital restructuring unfolds through the reconfiguration of professional networks and underscores the importance of relational dynamics for the implementation of integrated care models.

Hospital Acquisitions and Physician Flows (WITHDRAWN)

HCM
Bohan Li (Harvard University)
The rigorous ongoing policy debates on mergers and acquisitions (M&A) in healthcare is missing a critical dimension: the impact of hospital M&A on physicians. Despite the important role that physicians play in the hospital production function, there is a limited understanding of how hospital M&A affects where and how physicians practice. Specifically, little is known about the impacts of these events on the composition and practice patterns of the physician labor force at acquired hospitals. The theoretical impact is ambiguous and may vary across the nature of the acquisition (e.g., within-market vs. cross-market), characteristics of the acquiring entity (e.g., profit status, corporate strategy) and the characteristics of physicians themselves (e.g., quality, experience). This paper uses public use data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and a matched stacked difference-in-difference design to estimate the effect of hospital acquisitions on where and how surgeons practice. Preliminary results suggest that acquisition by a health system meaningfully increases surgeon entry into target hospitals without any effects on surgeon exits. This study contributes to the debate on consolidation in healthcare by shedding light on how acquisitions impact a key determinant of hospital performance: physicians.

Nephrologist Affiliation with Dialysis Facilities and Patient Outcomes for End Stage Renal Disease (WITHDRAWN)

HCM
Farbod Alinezhad (Northeastern University); Gary J Young (Northeastern University); Jianfei Cao (Northeastern University); Richard Hirth (University of Michigan); Brady Post (Northeastern University)
Despite its advantages for the treatment of end stage renal disease (ESRD), the utilization of home peritoneal dialysis (PD) remains limited in the U.S. compared to facility-based hemodialysis (HD). Theory and previous research suggest several organizational features of the dialysis industry contribute to this underutilization. We examined associations between nephrologists’ affiliation with dialysis facilities as well as facility characteristics (i.e., ownership status and PD service offerings) with two ESRD outcomes: PD utilization and kidney transplant surgery. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of dialysis patients in the Medicare program for a nationally representative 5% sample of all enrollees from 2013 to 2022. The adjusted 24-month cumulative incidence of PD utilization (versus (HD) was 9.9% for patients of affiliated nephrologists compared with 8.2% for those of non-affiliated nephrologists (1.7 percentage point difference; 95% CI, 0.8 to 3.0). In stratified analyses, the adjusted difference in PD utilization was larger for nephrologists affiliated with for-profit facilities and for those affiliated with facilities offering PD. Differences in kidney transplant surgery were small and not statistically significant. These findings underscore the potential role of organizational factors in promoting PD highlighting the need for policy reforms that expand home-based dialysis.

Peer Group Departures and the Performance of Those Left Behind: A Study of Primary Care Physicians

HCM
Gary J Young (Northeastern University); David Zepeda (Northeastern University); Md Mahmudul Hasan; Brady Post (Northeastern University)
We investigated how the permanent departure of a peer affects the work behavior of remaining peers in environments characterized by low task interdependence. While prior studies explored how workers adapt in new environments or how temporary departures impact colleagues in highly interdependent settings, we address two unexplored questions: how permanent departures differ from temporary ones, and what impacts occur when workers operate relatively independently. We investigated these questions through primary care physicians' (PCP) referral patterns for inappropriate diagnostic imaging—a significant healthcare cost contributor. We conducted difference-in-differences analyses of PCP departures in Massachusetts (2009- 2017). Our sample included 2,794 PCPs across 612 practices, focusing on inappropriate MRI referrals for back, knee, and shoulder pain. Using linear probability regression models, we assessed how differences between departing physicians' and remaining peers' practice styles affected subsequent referral patterns of the remaining peers. Results show significant peer effects: each percentage point difference between departing physicians’ and remaining peers' practice styles yielded a 0.29 percentage point change in a patient’s probability of an inappropriate referral (5.8% probability change relative to baseline). Financial status of departing physicians moderated this effect. Effects emerged three to four quarters after departure and were stronger in smaller practices. Importantly, effects were symmetrical—departures of physicians with either higher or lower inappropriate referral rates influenced remaining peers equally. Managerial implications: Our findings offer valuable insights for managing employee departures. The symmetrical effect challenges conventional wisdom— losing poor performers can positively influence remaining team members just as losing high performers may negatively impact them. This suggests managers should consider peer effects in retention decisions, as individual performance loss might be offset by improved team behavior. For healthcare administrators, physician departures represent critical moments that can either advance or hinder efforts to reduce low-value care, requiring proactive management strategies.
