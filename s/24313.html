When We Belong and Know Who We Are: The Syndemics of Minoritized Identity at Work

HR
Steven Kavaratzis (Toronto Metropolitan University)
This phenomenological qualitative study examines how race and sexuality shape experiences of burnout, with attention to intersectionality. We conducted 32 semi-structured interviews with racialized and LGBTQ+ employees. Interviews revealed two parallel experiences: 1) a negative identity spiral from choosing to mask identity.; and 2) a positive identity spiral where disclosures were welcomed and identities were affirmed. Across both experiences, participants described intersectionality as intensifying identity dynamics: in contexts of belonging and affirmation it was deeply resource-building— supporting self-esteem, well-being, and outward sharing with others—while in the absence of psychological safety it was especially resource-depleting, involving self-editing and vigilance, overcompensation, isolation, and eroded self-esteem. Authenticity was described as a context-dependent calculation, often involving prioritizing one identity over another to protect the self associated with greater risk. When both identities were safe to bring forward, identity took on new meaning and became a powerful source of motivation, engagement, and vitality. By novelly applying conservation of resources theory to syndemics— the study of interacting risks that amplify one another—these findings illustrate how participants experience intersectionality through resource and risk caravans. Together, these insights provide a theoretically grounded basis for better understanding burnout and intersectionality among minoritized employees.
