Do Justice, But Suffer From It? The Dual Effects of Whistleblowing on Whistleblowers’ Job Engagement

OB
Andong Dong (Shanghai University); Jun Yang (Tongji University); Zhenghao Xu (Shanghai University); Jun Ma (Shanghai University)
Although scholars widely agree that whistleblowing supports organizational ethical management, we still lack clear evidence about when and why it benefits or harms whistleblowers. Drawing from the theory of self-conscious emotions, we argue that whistleblowing creates an emotionally ambivalent experience: it can generate pride from helping the organization address wrongdoing, while also eliciting guilt because speaking up may disrupt relational harmony. We propose that these two emotions carry opposing implications for job engagement. We evaluate our model with 395 employees in a three-stage four-wave survey design. The findings suggest that whistleblowing activates distinct emotional responses, such that pride promotes higher levels of job engagement, whereas guilt reduces job engagement. Further, management support serves as a key boundary condition, shaping both emotional responses and the resulting conditional indirect effects on engagement.
