Linking Presenteeism Climate and Digital Mindsets to Employee Well-Being in Public Administration

PNP
Elisabeth F. Mueller (University of the Bundeswehr Munich); Julia Stephanie Thaler (University of the Bundeswehr Munich)
In times of accelerated digital transformation and rising expectations for responsiveness, public administrations face new forms of working and workplace pressures with important implications for public managers and the conditions under which they perform public service work. While these changes create opportunities, they also introduce new job demands that may affect individual well-being and job performance. One particularly salient job demand in this context is a presenteeism climate, defined as shared perceptions that working while ill is rewarded, supported, and expected. In public administrations, this climate intersects with longstanding professional norms emphasizing duty, continuity of service, and reliability. Drawing on the job demands–resources model, this study examines the negative relationship between presenteeism climate and individual well-being and investigates digital mindset as a personal boundary condition that may facilitate coping with such a climate. Individuals with strong digital mindsets may be better equipped to manage responsiveness and presenteeism climate-related pressures without compromising their well-being by leveraging digital tools and new working forms. Using survey data from 197 managers in German public administration, this study reveals that a stronger presenteeism climate is associated with lower job performance, mediated by greater emotional exhaustion and lower work engagement. Digital mindset factors relate positively to work engagement, yet they do not buffer the adverse effects of a presenteeism climate on emotional exhaustion. Overall, in highlighting the potential adverse consequences of contemporary workplace climates and the importance of developing individual mindsets, these findings offer new insights for theory, as well as for public management practice.

Digital Transformation of Work: The Rise of Post-Human Entanglements in the Civil Service

PNP
Aristito Febrianto; Adina Dudau; Anna Morgan-Thomas (University of Glasgow)
Digital transformation in government is commonly judged by efficiency, transparency and structural change. We instead ask two more micro-level questions: how does work emerge in the constellation of platforms, programs and devices that compose the digital workplace, and what kind of work emerges? Using a posthumanist lens and a two-year ethnography of a central ministry in the Global South, we focus on digitalisation as ordering rather than the impact of discrete digital tools. We trace three concurrent modes of ordering administrative work: platform-scripted (gateways, routings and signatures that authorise action), dashboard-signalled (timestamps, counters and invitations that summon and pace attention), and discretion-enacted (mentoring, embodied meetings and hybrid etiquette that recognise judgement and care). We argue that these orders enact three configurations of work: superhuman/extension when platform and dashboard orders reinforce one another; antihuman/extraction when summons crowd out discretion; and metahuman/repair when discretion recalibrates the mix to keep effort liveable. The configurations are co-present, not sequential, reflecting the multiplicity and ongoing becoming of administrative work.

How E-Procurement Systems Shape Sustainable Public Procurement in the U.S. and Japan

PNP
Yifan Chen (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Nicole Darnall (American University); Justin Michael Stritch (Arizona State University); Takuro Miyamoto (Kanto Gakuin University); Toshi Arimura
This study examines how the comprehensiveness of e-procurement systems influences sustainable public procurement. Although governments worldwide increasingly adopt e-procurement to enhance efficiency and transparency, prior research has not fully explored how the scope of system functionalities, its comprehensiveness, affects sustainability objectives. This research addresses three critical gaps. affect sustainability objectives or whether these relationships hold universally. Drawing on large-sample survey data from finance and environment officials in U.S. local governments and Japanese municipalities, this cross-national comparative analysis reveals divergent patterns. We find that, in the U.S., e-procurement comprehensiveness exhibits a direct positive effect on the breadth of sustainable public procurement, amplified under financial resource constraints; in Japan, however, system design alone proves insufficient absent high organizational openness to innovation. These findings advance digital government theory by demonstrating country-specific pathways through which technological capabilities translate into sustainability outcomes and offer practical guidance for public managers in different national settings.

Truth and Dare: Market Relations and Civic Sentiment Distance in Digital Space During COVID-19

PNP
Yan Long (University of California Berkeley); Wei Luo; Yue Zhang (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill); Rui Chen (Harvard University)
Under rising authoritarian pressure, civic organizations face intense pressure to align with state narratives. While existing research relies on state–society frameworks to explain civic compliance versus dissidence, this article foregrounds market embeddedness as a critical yet heterogeneous institutional force shaping autonomous organizational expression. We introduce the concept of sentiment distance—patterned departures from state-sanctioned emotional scripts—to capture a resilient form of civic contestation when overt oppositions become prohibitively costly. Analyzing over 20,000 digital posts by Chinese civic organizations during the COVID-19 pandemic combined with survey and interview data, we find that market instead of political relationships drive expressive civic voice amid amplified repression. Disaggregating market embeddedness into three dimensions, we show that relational and financial market embeddedness facilitated sentiment distance from state performative optimism, while managerial embeddedness exerted mixed effects. These findings complicate our understandings of civic resilience under constraint in affective terms which depends less on political alignment than on how organizations are anchored across state and market institutions.
