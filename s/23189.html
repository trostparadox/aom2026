Are Entrepreneurs More Intuitive Than Managers? Revisiting Cognitive Reflection with a Verbal CRT

ENT
Chris Hand (Kingston Business School at Kingston University); Jagannadha Pawan Tamvada (Kingston Business School at Kingston University London)
Entrepreneurship theory frequently portrays entrepreneurs as more intuitive decision-makers than managers. Recent empirical studies support this view using measures of cognitive reflection, reporting higher contemplativeness among managers and inferring greater intuition among entrepreneurs. However, these studies rely primarily on numerically framed measures, raising concerns about whether observed differences reflect decision-making style or domain-specific abilities such as numeracy. We revisit this relationship using an independent, preregistered, and conceptually extended replication with data from the UK Household Longitudinal Study Innovation Panel. Cognitive reflection is measured using a verbally oriented test designed to minimize numerical computation. Across multiple estimation strategies, we find consistent evidence that managers exhibit higher levels of cognitive reflection than non-managerial employees. By contrast, entrepreneurs do not score lower on cognitive reflection and do not appear more intuitive than managers or employees when cognition is measured using verbal items. Taken together, the results suggest that previously reported differences in entrepreneurial intuition may be sensitive to measurement choice and support a more context-dependent understanding of entrepreneurial decision-making.

Guiding Without Knowing: A Model of Entrepreneurial Support Practices Under Uncertainty

ENT
Thomas Houy (Télécom Paris)
The aim of this article is to examine the effects of uncertainty on the entrepreneurial support practices. A conceptual contribution is proposed to formalize the consequences of market unpredictability on the "entrepreneur-advisor" relationship. We present a model based on the notion of the "false-positive paradox". We show that uncertainty leads entrepreneurial support actors to produce a substantial number of erroneous diagnoses, which paradoxically reinforces certain properties already identified in the entrepreneurial support function. The model presented in this research also helps to shape ongoing debates on the future of entrepreneurial support practices.

Race to Opportunity: The Missing Minority Entrepreneurs

ENT
Ahmed Sewaid (MBSC)
Racial minorities remain persistently underrepresented in entrepreneurship, yet the mechanisms that perpetuate this disparity remain under explored. Drawing on structuration theory, we examine how structural forces and individual agency interact recursively to reproduce racial inequalities in entrepreneurial finance. We theorize that the historical fundraising performance of an entrepreneur’s racial group functions as a dynamic racialized memory trace that shapes legitimacy perceptions among fund providers and self-concept among entrepreneurs. Using data from Kickstarter, we show that while a positive fundraising track record for a racial group increases the likelihood of success for individual entrepreneurs from that group, fund providers respond asymmetrically. Specifically, for Black and Hispanic entrepreneurs, the positive effects of prior group successes are discounted, while the negative effects of group-level failures are amplified. In parallel, minority entrepreneurs internalize such dynamics by placing greater weight on their group’s failures and undervaluing their group’s successes, which lowers their likelihood of platform participation. Our findings suggest a feedback process in which biased funding decisions and reduced participation jointly reinforce persistent disparities. By integrating structural and agentic perspectives, our study advances theory on entrepreneurial inequality and offers a dynamic account of how racial disparities persist in decentralized market settings.

Too Proud To Fail: The Impact of Managerial Narcissism on Bankruptcy Initiation

ENT
Rachid Achbah; Marc Frechet; Ivana Vitanova
This study examines the impact of managerial narcissism on strategic decision-making during financial distress. Building upon the extended agency model of narcissism, we argue that financial distress activates narcissistic managers' self-regulation processes, leading to reduced willingness and extended delays in filing for court-supervised bankruptcy proceedings. We test our theoretical framework across two independent studies of financially distressed SMEs in France. Study 1 assesses the willingness to file for bankruptcy proceedings among a sample of 230 French SME CEOs who experienced financial distress in 2019. Study 2 analyzes delays in initiating bankruptcy proceedings in a sample of 228 SMEs who entered the bankruptcy procedure during 2023. Our findings support the idea that managerial narcissism significantly impedes initiating bankruptcy proceedings. Additionally, we demonstrate that the dynamic narcissistic self-regulation process, which leads to avoidance of bankruptcy proceedings, is not mitigated by other personality factors, such as emotional stability, and is exacerbated in severely distressed firms.

Well-Intentioned but Misguided: The Impact of Entrepreneurial Role Models on Cognitive Biases

ENT
Mingcheng Li (University of Science and Technology of China)
Prior research has largely emphasized the positive role of entrepreneurial role models in motivating entrepreneurial intentions and behaviors, while paying limited attention to how such role models are presented and their potential cognitive consequences." Drawing on framing theory, we examine whether the selective presentation of entrepreneurial role models—operationalized as presenting only positive entrepreneurial role models—shapes individuals’ entrepreneurial overconfidence and overoptimism, and whether implicit theories of entrepreneurial ability moderate these effects. We test our hypotheses using a scenario-based experiment with 593 university students and a survey of 161 entrepreneurs. The results show that, compared with presenting both positive and negative entrepreneurial role models, presenting only positive entrepreneurial role models increases individuals’ entrepreneurial overconfidence and overoptimism. Moreover, under conditions in which only positive entrepreneurial role models are presented, incremental theorists exhibit higher levels of entrepreneurial overconfidence, whereas entity theorists do not show a corresponding increase. By shifting attention from whether entrepreneurial role models are beneficial per se to how they are presented, our study identifies important situational factors influencing entrepreneurial cognitive bias and highlights the role of dynamic contexts rather than static personality traits in shaping entrepreneurial cognition.
