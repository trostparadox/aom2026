From General-Purpose Models to Domain Knowledge: How Trading Zones Translate AI Across Boundaries

CTO
Dongyeob Kim (Case Western Reserve University); Sarah Lebovitz (University of Virginia)
Despite rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI), many organizations struggle to generate domain-specific value from AI systems. Although AI models are widely diffused, their impact depends on how professionals interpret, adapt, and embed them within domain-specific norms and work practices. Drawing on research on professions and knowledge objects, we conceptualize AI as a family of general-purpose, evolving computational models that lack a fixed epistemic anchor in professional domains. Consequently, AI must be translated into local problem framing and reasoning routines through negotiation among heterogeneous professionals within emergent trading zones. We examine this process through a mixed-methods study of AI development and use at a major U.S. academic medical center, combining interviews with computational analysis. We identify distinct trading zones where translation takes place and trace their evolution over time, revealing three translation phases: technical exploration, experimental application, and cross-boundary convergence, which differ in how AI is positioned as a knowledge object and in whether trading zones stabilize, expand, or dissipate. By showing how trading zones around AI emerge and evolve, our study advances theory on AI, professions, and knowledge trading, and explains the conditions under which domain-agnostic AI models acquire domain-specific epistemic form.

Doing, Knowing, or Illusion with LLMs? Ethnographic Insights from a Leading European Telecom

CTO
Lara Abdel Halim (Ecole polytechnique)
The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into software engineering is reshaping professional practice by producing outputs that are statistically plausible yet decontextualized from local organizational rules. This decoupling creates a risk of "prosthetic expertise," where technical performance masks the reality of situated competence. Drawing on a two-year ethnography within a major European telecommunications company, this paper examines how engineers navigate these tensions through three collaboration regimes. The Functional Regime (1) applies to low-stakes repetitive tasks, prioritizing efficiency through executive delegation but fostering cognitive dependency. The Epistemic Navigation Regime (2) emerges when addressing polymorphic tasks deeply embedded in specific business rules. We identify two modalities within this regime. In Regime 2A (Augmented Exploration), the developer possesses domain expertise and exercises "top-down discrimination," leveraging their grasp of business rules to filter AI proposals. Conversely, Regime 2B (Epistemic Void Filling) occurs when domain knowledge is lacking. Unable to discriminate, the developer falls into a trap of "circular validation," relying on the AI to generate both the code and the tests intended to verify it, creating an illusion of robustness (Epistemia). To prevent this circular drift, we propose the Sovereign Regime (3). We argue for a theoretical shift from Aspirational Responsibility to Structural Responsibility, where defining "semantic rails" through formalized artifacts (ADRs, Packmind) acts as a prerequisite. This paradigm redefines the expert's role from a "super-coder" to a "technical journalist," building the Source of Truth infrastructure necessary to anchor AI outputs within the organization's sociomaterial reality.

Algorithmic Pragmatics: How Large Language Models Reshape Professional Communication

CTO
Tsehaye Haidemariam (BI Norwegian Business School); Guro R. Sanden (BI Norwegian Business School)
Large Language Models (LLMs) fundamentally transform human–machine communication, and in turn, professional communication in organizations. Unlike earlier systems that processed language without participating in interaction, LLMs generate context-sensitive discourse, modulate tone and stance, and filter information through selective summarization. This article introduces Algorithmic Pragmatics, a theoretical framework explaining how LLMs reshape communicative action despite lacking intentions or grounded understanding. The theory is developed within the context of international business communication by drawing on language-sensitive international business and management research. The Algorithmic Pragmatics framework identifies four mechanisms through which LLMs influence meaning-making: algorithmic generation of linguistic acts, algorithmic modulation of interpersonal tone, algorithmic filtration of salient information, and cybernetic interaction, whereby recursive human–model feedback loops normalize algorithmically patterned forms of communication. These mechanisms collectively reorganize the conditions under which linguistic meaning is produced, interpreted, and distributed in business communication. They also carry far-reaching societal and ethical implications, including shifts in epistemic authority, the emergence of algorithmically enforced linguistic norms that contribute to linguistic homogenization, widening accountability gaps in hybrid human–machine authorship, and new forms of communicative inequality shaped by differential access to AI literacy and the privileging of dominant language varieties. The article concludes with three implications for professional communication, specifically in relation to hybrid communication systems; cognitive offloading and sensemaking; and communicative convergence.

Problem-Solving in the Age of LLMs: From Routine to Edge Case in Crowdsourced Knowledge Production

CTO
Robin Nowak (University of Passau); Julian Just (Universität Innsbruck)
Crowds have long been central to creative problem solving and public knowledge production. However, as Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly provide instant solutions even for complex problems, the role of the crowd becomes uncertain. We study how LLMs reshape problem solving on Stack Overflow and which problems remain at the boundary of what LLMs can handle. Using the full set of Python-related questions on Stack Overflow spanning two years before and after the release of ChatGPT, we map the problem space into 651 semantic topic clusters and track activity using Interrupted Time Series analysis. We show a pronounced but uneven reallocation of activity. Many clusters decrease sharply, while a smaller subset remains stable or even grows. To explain this variation, we estimate Two-Way Fixed Effects models linking post-period changes to pre-period topic traits. Clusters with higher semantic drift, higher attention and more complex problem types increase or decrease less. In contrast, topics with many answers, a larger share of novice participation, and clusters dominated by simple how-to problem types decrease more. Finally, we identify 70 resilient and still prevalent edge case clusters. These fall into two broad groups. One consists of stable application domains that require contextual adaptation. The other consists of fast-moving integration challenges where knowledge depreciates quickly. Overall, the results suggest that crowds remain relevant for problem solving in the age of LLMs, but their role is shifting from broad participation to specialized high value contributions where human judgment, creativity, and collaborative sensemaking remain indispensable.

Large Language Models in Academia: Productivity Boost and Heterogenous Effects

CTO
YoungJin Kwon (University of Minnesota); Agnes Yang (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville)
Large language models (LLMs) have attracted significant attention for their potential to enhance knowledge worker productivity. In this study, we provide the first large-scale empirical evaluations of LLMs’ impact on academic research productivity. Leveraging a comprehensive dataset of 4,582 computer science scholars across 194 top U.S. universities and analyzing 218,723 research papers published between 2019 and 2024, we find that the introduction of LLMs is associated with about 8% increase in publication output; a gap that persists across alternative measures, including the first-author publications and top-tier conference papers. Our regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) analysis further reveals that LLMs not only shifted the average publication level but also accelerated the growth rate of productivity, rising to 3.2% in 2023 and 12.8% in 2024. Notably, junior scholars realize stronger gains than their senior counterparts, with the productivity benefit diminishing by roughly 1% for each additional year of experience. Recognizing that LLMs’ benefits may not be uniformly distributed, we also investigate their impact on non-native English-speaking (NNES) researchers, who have historically faced disadvantages in academic writing (Liao et al., 2024). Difference-in-differences and generalized synthetic control analyses indicate that, following LLM adoption, native English-speaking (NES) researchers produced more papers than their NNES counterparts. Overall, our findings indicate that while LLMs significantly boost scholarly productivity, they also exhibit dual effects, lowering barriers for junior scholars while potentially reinforcing linguistic inequities.
