Occupation Insecurity in Healthcare: Key Moderators of Career Satisfaction and Turnover Intentions

HCM
Rebecca J Lindgren (Washington State University); Tahira M. Probst (Washington State University); Alison Lucia Hunt (Portland State University); Lara Christina Roll (PricewaterhouseCoopers Belgium BV/SRL); Minseo Choi (Washington State University); Melissa R. Jenkins
Technological advances such as automation and AI can translate into better outcomes for patients as well as improved working conditions for healthcare professionals. However, there may be unintended consequences for workers when healthcare organizations adopt new technologies, particularly since these changes can prompt occupation insecurity (OCIS), i.e. fears that one’s occupation will be dramatically altered by or rendered obsolete by advanced technology. Moreover, it is important to identify ways that organizations can facilitate effective technology adoption to reduce unanticipated effects such as employee turnover. As such, this study looks to examine the effect of OCIS on career satisfaction and career turnover intentions, while examining the moderating effects of training climate and occupation mobility perceptions. Survey data was collected from N = 392 medical laboratory professionals to test our hypothesized moderated mediation model in which higher levels of OCIS lead to decreased career satisfaction and increased career turnover intentions. Training climate and perceived occupation mobility moderated these relationships, providing theoretical implications for the effects of OCIS on career-level outcomes as well as practical implications for healthcare organizations looking to adopt new technology while minimizing threat perceptions.

Caring for the Carers: A Mintzbergian Kaleidoscope for a More Caring Bureaucracy in Hospitals

HCM
Anne-Sophie Dubey (Conservatoire national des Arts et Métiers); Camille Toussaint; Matthieu Battistelli (Université Savoie Mont Blanc - IREGE); Caroline Mattelin-Pierrard (Université Savoie Mont Blanc - IREGE)
Contemporary hospitals face an enduring organisational paradox. Historically grounded in professional bureaucracies centred on clinical expertise and professional autonomy, they have increasingly incorporated New Public Management reforms that intensify administrative control, standardisation, and divisionalised financial logics. These dynamics produce what we term an infernal triad: the weakening of professional bureaucracy, the overextension of mechanistic coordination, and the dominance of divisionalised structures, with detrimental consequences for both patient care and staff well-being. While hybrid models—such as the caring bureaucracy—have been proposed as remedies, they remain weakly operationalised. This article advances a practice-based reconceptualisation of hospital coordination by introducing the concept of structural pluralism. Drawing on a single-case study of a Swiss hospital organised as a decentralised, community-oriented network, we theorise structural pluralism through a Mintzbergian kaleidoscope, showing how hospitals rely on the simultaneous coexistence of multiple, practice-specific bundles of coordination mechanisms rather than converging toward a dominant structural configuration. Our findings show that a caring bureaucracy emerges not as a stable form, but as a situated and adaptive organisational design grounded in the selective leveraging of complementarities and containment of tensions across coordination mechanisms for heterogeneous practice. We provide five detailed empirical examples of such coordination bundles.

Workplace Compassion Practices and their Associations with Patient-Provider Therapeutic Connections

HCM
Cheryl Rathert (Saint Louis University); Timothy J. Vogus (Vanderbilt University); Ibraheem Ahmed Jawadi (Saint Louis University)
Patient-centered care is a defining element of healthcare delivery. Both healthcare organizations and providers are interested in ways they can improve patient experiences. Previous research has demonstrated that better patient health outcomes and satisfaction are associated with patients experiencing a therapeutic connection (TC) with their providers; yet there is little knowledge about factors that may strengthen TCs. Related research has found positive associations between workplace compassion practices and outcomes for both patients and providers. Workplace compassion means that an individual notices someone in distress and acts to reduce their suffering. Compassion practices are ways of organizationally encouraging and normalizing expressions of compassion among coworkers and toward patients. We investigated the association between workplace compassion practices and healthcare clinicians’ perceptions of TCs and two mechanisms through which workplace compassion practices may operate: psychological safety (PS) and perspective taking (PT). A cross-sectional study of 341 healthcare providers (n=341) using serial multiple mediation regression models found that associations between compassion practices and TC were significantly mediated by PS and PT. Although we cannot infer causation from this study, it suggests that organizational compassion practices are associated with better patient-provider TCs through PS and PT. The study provides additional evidence to incentivize organizations to adopt workplace compassion practices.

A Temporal Conservation of Resources Perspective on Workforce Resilience Across Crisis

HCM
Jeonghwan Choi (University of Maine); Yob Ha (Texas A&M University); Kyung Nam Kim (Texas A&M University); Sumi Lee (Texas A&M University)
Healthcare crises expose workers to prolonged disruption, yet theory offers limited explanation of how workforce resilience is sustained over time. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we develop a Temporal Resource Activation perspective that conceptualizes resilience as a phase-dependent process shaped by shifting resource salience. We test this framework in a natural experiment arising from a 17-month nationwide medical doctors’ strike in South Korea, during which clinical nurses assumed expanded frontline responsibilities. Using four-wave panel data, we integrate wave-specific regression analyses, multi-group structural equation modeling, latent growth modeling, and cross-lagged panel models to examine how structural, psychological, and relational resources jointly shape job performance across crisis onset, escalation, peak, and recovery. Results reveal a systematic sequence of resource activation. Structural resources, captured by perceived organizational health and toxicity (POHT), are most salient early in the crisis, providing stability and enabling initial coping. Psychological resources, reflected in psychological capital (PsyCap), become dominant under peak strain and emerge as the strongest and most enduring predictor of performance over time. Relational resources, operationalized as knowledge-sharing behavior (KSB), gain importance during recovery, supporting coordination and re-stabilization. In contrast, demand-focused factors such as burnout and counterproductive behavior show limited explanatory power. Together, these findings advance COR theory by demonstrating that workforce resilience emerges through sequenced resource activation rather than static capacities, with implications for how organizations design interventions across crisis phases.
