Navigating the Interpersonal Dilemma: The Effect of Leader Perfectionism on Leader-Follower Moqi

OB
Xinhui Qin (Chongqing University); Cuilian Zhang (Chongqing University); Wei Yan (Chongqing University)
Drawing from the self-regulation theory, this study predicts the explanatory role of work reflection in the relationship between leader perfectionism and follower moqi with leader, and the potential of leader humility in enhancing the positive effect of leader perfectionism. Results derived from a three-time wave survey support most of our hypotheses. Specifically, follower work reflection mediates the relationship between leader perfectionism and follower moqi with leader. Meanwhile, leader humility moderates the positive relationship between follower work reflection and follower moqi with leader, as well as the indirect relationship between leader perfectionism and follower moqi with leader through follower work reflection. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed based on these findings.

Leader, You Are Wasting My Time: How Leader Perfectionism Triggers Employee Interaction Avoidance

OB
Yiru Wang (Chongqing University); Cuilian Zhang (Chongqing University)
Although research has begun to examine the interpersonal effects of leader perfectionism, it has largely overlooked employees’ agentic responses to such perfectionism within leader–employee interactions. Drawing on frustration–aggression theory, we examine how leader perfectionistic concerns and strivings differentially impact employees’ interaction avoidance toward the leader. Across a multi-wave field study and an online survey study, we found that leader perfectionistic concerns heightened employees’ wasted time perceptions, which subsequently led to interaction avoidance, whereas leader perfectionistic strivings lowered employees’ wasted time perceptions, thereby reducing their interaction avoidance. Moreover, employees’ perceived upward mobility buffered the positive association between leader perfectionistic concerns and wasted time perceptions, while amplifying the negative association between leader perfectionistic strivings and wasted time perceptions, ultimately shaping employees’ interaction avoidance. We discuss our theoretical contributions to the literature and practical implications for organizations.

When Leaders Become the Team: How Support Congruence Fuels Unethical Pro-Team Behavior

OB
Ji Hoon Lee (Ji Hoon Lee); Kyoungmin Cho (Pennsylvania State U); Ye Dai (Southern Illinois University, Carbondale)
This study examines how leaders’ self-concepts are constructed through their relationships with teams. Drawing on a social constructionist perspective and social identity theory, we introduce downward identification—a process through which leaders internalize their teams as part of who they are. Using multi-source, time-lagged data from 104 leader–team dyads within Korean police units, we show that congruence between leaders’ perceived team support and teams’ perceived leader support fosters leaders’ downward identification by creating shared meaning and mutual validation. In turn, downward identification predicts unethical pro-team behavior (UPTB), defined as leaders’ intentional violations of ethical norms to benefit their teams. This indirect effect is conditional on team organizational identification, such that the effect of relational congruence on UPTB via downward identification is stronger when teams identify more strongly with the organization. These findings advance a relational understanding of leadership identity and illuminate how collective identification shapes the ethical consequences of leader–team relationships, revealing how strong shared identities can unintentionally amplify ethically questionable pro-team actions.

From Gatekeepers to Overloaded Brokers: When Engaging Leadership Weakens Network Advantages

OB
Natalia Bagnoli (Politecnico di Milano); gabriele boccoli (Politecnico di Milano); Luca Gastaldi (Politecnico di Milano); Mariano Corso (Politecnico di Milano)
This study examines how occupying brokerage positions in digital communication networks influences brokers’ own job satisfaction and work engagement, and how this relationship is moderated by engaging leadership. In today’s hybrid workplaces, collaboration unfolds through both synchronous (audio/video calls) and asynchronous (emails) interactions, transforming the broker’s role and activities from primarily relational mediation into a cognitively demanding and technologically mediated process of coordinating information flows across digital channels. Drawing on social capital theory, media richness theory, and the job demands-resources framework, we theorize that brokerage can enhance job satisfaction and work engagement by providing brokers with access to diverse information, greater autonomy, and social recognition within the organization. These resources can make work more stimulating, meaningful, and rewarding. However, these potential benefits may weaken under high engaging leadership. When leaders actively stimulate collaboration and promote direct connectivity among employees, brokers - already highly involved and relationally exposed - may experience overwhelm and relational pressure, as well as a fear of disintermediation, that is, the perception that their unique role is being bypassed. Such conditions can weaken the sense of mastery, influence, and significance that typically sustain brokers’ satisfaction and engagement. We test these hypotheses using a longitudinal, multi-source design conducted in a medium-large Italian bank with a final sample of 497 employees. The study combines digital trace data - comprising audio/video calls and emails exchanged during two observation periods - with two survey waves measuring perceptions of engaging leadership (T1) and brokers’ job satisfaction and work engagement (T2). Brokerage positions were identified through betweenness centrality in synchronous (calls) and asynchronous (emails) communication networks, and relationships were analyzed using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). Results show that brokers that operate in digital networks tend to experience higher satisfaction and engagement, but these effects are significantly attenuated under strong engaging leadership. This study makes an important contribution to research on social networks and leadership by challenging the prevailing assumption that beneficial conditions necessarily reinforce each other. Instead, it demonstrates that even positive factors such as brokerage and engaging leadership can interact in ways that limit, rather than amplify, brokers’ work engagement and job satisfaction.

It's Not Me, It's You: The Effect of Alter's Brokerage Orientations on Ego's Leader Identity

OB
Steffen Triebel; Andrew Parker (Durham University Business School); Christian Waldstrom (Aarhus University)
Prior research has primarily focused on individual characteristics and experiences as drivers of leader identity construction, whereas the relational antecedents through which leader identity is shaped remain underexplored. This study examines the effects on individuals of the behavioral brokerage orientations of others within their network (i.e., ‘alters’). Specifically, we examine how alters’ tendencies to connect others (tertius iungens) or keep others apart (tertius gaudens) affect the focal actor’s leader identity. Drawing on leader identity theory and social capital theory, we propose that brokerage behaviors by alters within buy-in networks shape how individuals internalize leadership as part of their self-concept. We theorize that tertius iungens-brokering by alters has a positive effect on leader identity, whereas moderate tertius gaudens-brokering by alters has a positive effect that becomes negative as the brokering behavior gets stronger. Using data from 101 employees in a consultancy firm, we find support for a linear effect of tertius iungens-brokering and a negative curvilinear effect of tertius gaudens-brokering. The findings advance our understanding of leader identity construction and deconstruction by highlighting the role of others in an individual’s network and helps leadership research ‘zooming out’ to reflect the complex, interpersonal nature of leader identity with greater methodological plurality.
