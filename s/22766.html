An Attribution View of Disabled Employees’ Reactions to Anticipatory Help from the Nondisabled

OB
Xinxin Li (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Yanran Lu (University of Maryland College Park); Hui Liao (University of Maryland College Park)
People with disabilities (PWD) represent a growing talent pool that human resource management cannot overlook. However, existing research primarily focuses on managerial attitudes toward PWD recruitment, neglecting their daily working experience. Our research explores the PWD’s interaction with their colleagues in the context of interpersonal helping. Specifically, we asked the question of how PWD react to helping behaviors from their nondisabled colleagues. Drawing on attribution theory, we propose that employees with disabilities are less likely to ascribe anticipatory help (vs. reactive help) from nondisabled individuals to prosocial motives, but more likely to make benevolent discrimination attributions. These patterns of attributions lead to defensive reactions to anticipatory help, including rejecting help, reducing help-seeking behavior, and avoiding the helper. These effects are more pronounced for individuals with stronger stigma consciousness. Moreover, we propose that helpers’ sensitive self-disclosure is an effective strategy to inhibit the negative attributions of employees with disabilities and enhance the effectiveness of anticipatory help. Results from a qualitative study and two vignette experiments support our hypotheses. Our research enhances our understanding of the interactions between employees with and without disabilities.

Shine or Shadow? Examining the Enablers and Inhibitors of Social Acuity Among Autistic Leaders

OB
Nagapriya Vaidyanathan (Drexel University); Christian Resick (Drexel University); Liza Yasemin Barnes (Drexel University)
The current research employs a mixed methods design to understand the aspired leadership behavior of autistic leaders and the challenges and resources that hinder and enable them from successfully enacting these behaviors. In Study 1, we conducted an abductive study where we analyzed anonymous leader and employee data from online interactive platforms, along with 26 employee interviews, to understand the leadership behaviors of autistic leaders, the challenges they face, and the support they seek to overcome these challenges. This is followed by Study 2, where we developed a deductive model based on the findings from Study 1 and integrated the Empathizing and Systemizing theory (Baron-Cohen, 2009) to examine how these cognitive processes influence their leadership behaviors through perspective-taking. Results across both studies indicate that, while autistic leaders desire to engage in both agentic and communal leadership behaviors, difficulties with social acuity affect how their behaviors are perceived by employees. However, we find this challenge alleviated by leadership coaching tailored for autistic leaders, which alleviates their challenge with perspective-taking. Overall, our work advances theory and deepens understanding of the leadership demonstrated by autistic individuals in the workplace.

Unequal Intimacy: Relational Paradox and the Construction of Meaning in Prosocial-Dirty Work

OB
Jiaxin Lin (Tsinghua University)
Workers engaged in prosocial–dirty work occupy a uniquely paradoxical position: they are needed by society for their helping behaviors, yet devalued because their work carries physical, social, or moral “stigma.” Within these intimate yet unequal interactions, they continually experience the generation and erosion of meaning through daily contact with those they serve. Drawing on 15 months of ethnographic observation and in-depth interviews across four eldercare institutions in China, this study introduces the concept of the “relational paradox” to explore how employees and clients co-construct work meaning within this tension. By developing a two-dimensional analytical framework—“direction of dependence × relational symmetry”—we uncover how different interaction patterns shape caregivers’ experiences of meaning and identify four relational types of work meaning: separated, competitive, nested, and complementary. Our findings demonstrate that work meaning is not a linear, individual psychological product, but a relational process dynamically generated through shifts in power flow and emotional reciprocity. From an employee–client meaning co-construction perspective, we reveals how work meaning emerges within unequal intimacy and further elucidates the mechanisms through which employees and clients jointly mitigate stigma in their everyday interactions.

When Humor Sparks Creativity: The Role of Thriving at Work and Gender-Age Intersectionality

OB
Ranxin Liao (Hitotsubashi University); Weiwei Shi (Beijing Normal University); Ran Li (Beijing Normal University); Xiji Zhu
While prior research has extensively examined leader humor and its effects on followers, employee sense of humor as a personal resource remains underexplored. Drawing on conservation of resources (COR) theory and intersectionality theory, we investigate how employees’ sense of humor enhances creativity through thriving at work, and how this process is contingent upon gender, age, and their intersectionality in East Asian organizational contexts. Using data from 521 employees across 94 teams in Chinese organizations, we find that sense of humor positively relates to employee creativity through enhanced thriving at work. This indirect relationship is moderated by both gender and age, with stronger effects observed for men compared to women. Furthermore, we identify a significant three-way interaction, that age amplifies the indirect effect for men while attenuating it for women. These findings reveal that aging creates cumulative advantage for men but cumulative disadvantage for women in leveraging humor for creativity. By uncovering the mediating role of thriving and demonstrating how cultural constraints embedded in gender-age intersections shape humor’s effectiveness, this study advances understanding of how sense of humor, as a personal resource, operates within culturally structured social positions. Our research contributes to literature on workplace humor, thriving, creativity, and intersectionality while providing practical insights for diversity management in East Asian organizations.
