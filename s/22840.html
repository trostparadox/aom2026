The Interpersonal Consequences of Employee AI Dependence

OB
Jialin Liu (University of International Business and Economics); Mingpeng Huang (University of International Business and Economics)
As AI becomes more common and diverse, employees are increasingly depending on AI to perform their tasks, viewing AI as collaborative to achieve career goals. Although the consequences regarding employees’ dependence on AI to accomplish their work are relatively well established from the perspective of work outcomes, we argue that employees’ dependence on AI may also have an impact on their interactions with coworkers. Drawing on self-perception theory and based on a field study and two experimental studies, this study demonstrates employees’ dependence on AI to complete their work leads to an increase in workplace impostor thoughts. This self-perception about one’s competence affects subsequent employees’ helping behavior toward colleagues. Moreover, employee locus of control influences their perception of depending on AI, which in turn moderates the relationship between AI dependence and workplace impostor thoughts. These insights advance our understanding of the novel trigger of workplace impostor thoughts and helping behavior, as well as provide both theoretical and practical insights for organizations aiming to effectively integrate AI.

Not Just a Burden: How Workplace Impostor Thoughts Shape Help-Seeking

OB
Jingwen Wang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Rui Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Danlei Zhen (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Workplace impostor thoughts are prevalent in organizations, yet existing research offers an incomplete account of how they shape employees’ everyday work behavior. This research seeks to address this gap by shifting attention from the psychological consequences of impostor thoughts to employees’ strategic responses to perceived evaluative discrepancy. Drawing on impression management two-component model, we examine how, why, and when workplace impostor thoughts influence distinct forms of help-seeking through image risk. Using multi-wave, multi-level field data from 297 employees nested within 64 workgroups, we find that workplace impostor thoughts heighten image risk, thereby increasing autonomous help-seeking while reducing dependent help-seeking. These relationships are more pronounced when task interdependence is high. Overall, our findings reveal that impostor thoughts are not only intrapersonal burdens but also socially embedded cues that motivate behavioral regulation in evaluative contexts. The results also offer practical insights into how organizations can enable employees to take action in response to impostor-related evaluative discrepancies.

Leading Under Doubt: Follower Responses to Leader Impostorism in Service Work

OB
Muhammad Waheed Akhtar (Rabat Business School, International University of Rabat); Fauzia Syed (Rabat Business School, International University of Rabat); Jongwook Pak (The University of Auckland)
Leader impostorism has been predominantly framed as a maladaptive trait that undermines leadership effectiveness. Yet, empirical evidence on its consequences for follower outcomes remains sparse and conceptually fragmented. Drawing on social information processing theory, this study develops and tests a dual-pathway model to explain how leader impostorism influences subordinate behavior through divergent psychological mechanisms. Study 1 was a field study with a manipulation check conducted among employees (N = 153), and Study 2 was a time-lagged field study (N = 273). The results demonstrate that leader impostorism is positively associated with upward ingratiation, which, in turn, enhances subordinates’ perceptions of job resources such as autonomy and development opportunities. Conversely, leader impostorism also increases perceived leader hypocrisy, which predicts greater engagement in negative workplace gossip. Importantly, we identify followers’ political skills as a critical boundary condition that moderates both pathways: it strengthens the positive relationship between leader impostorism and upward ingratiation, while simultaneously weakening the positive relationship between leader impostorism and perceived leader hypocrisy. This research contributes to the leadership literature by examining impostorism implications and highlighting the role of follower agency in shaping leader–follower dynamics.

The “Double-Edged Sword” Effects of Workplace Impostor Thoughts

OB
Zhisong Cui (Jiangxi University of Finance and Economics); Mengran Li (Guizhou University); Chilombo N Nanyangwe (Jilin University)
The relationship between workplace impostor thoughts and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) is intricate. While some research suggests that these thoughts can enhance employees’ OCB, other studies indicate they may have a negative impact. Based on regulatory focus theory, we challenge the prevailing assumption that workplace impostor thoughts have universally positive or negative effects, proposing instead that they can yield mixed outcomes. We propose that the influence of workplace impostor thoughts is influenced by the leader-member exchange (LMX). Specifically, when employees with impostor thoughts perceive high LMX, they are more likely to engage their promotion focus, which subsequently boosts OCB. On the other hand, when impostors perceive low LMX, they tend to activate their prevention focus, resulting in a decline in OCB. Our hypotheses were supported by both a field study and an experimental study. Collectively, our findings offer a more nuanced understanding of how workplace impostor thoughts influence OCB and emphasize the crucial role of LMX in this internal process.
