Board Diversity in the Age of AI: Antecedents and Performance Consequences

STR
Qiang Li (China Europe International Business School); Yu Zhang (China Europe International Business School)
This study examines the impact of a firm’s intensity of artificial intelligence (AI) adoption on its board experiential diversity and firm performance. Although AI possesses superior data-processing capabilities, the “black-box” nature of the technology often obscures the underlying logic of its outputs and recommendations. This creates significant cognitive complexity and opacity for firms. Drawing on resource dependence theory and upper echelons theory, we theorize that firms respond to these challenges by configuring boards with greater experiential diversity—specifically across educational, industrial, and organizational domains. Using a sample of U.S. public firms from 2010-2024, we find empirical support for this relationship. Furthermore, our analysis suggests that the above relationship is attenuated by financial analyst coverage and competitive action frequency. Finally, we demonstrate that the potential for AI to enhance firm performance is more fully realized when the board possesses high experiential diversity. This research contributes to both the corporate governance and AI literatures by highlighting how technology necessitates shifts in leadership composition.

Data Empowerment: How OGD Redraws Corporate Investment Maps

STR
Lijun Pan (Zhejiang Gongshang University)
In the era of the digital economy, Open Government Data (OGD) has emerged as a key institutional mechanism for unleashing the value of data as a factor of production and empowering the real economy. However, existing research has primarily focused on its implications for public administration or localized economic effects, with limited exploration of its impact on corporate non-local investment patterns. To address this gap, this study investigates whether OGD can reshape corporate non-local investment behavior. Leveraging the quasi-natural experiment of Chinese prefecture-level cities successively launching OGD platforms between 2010 and 2020, we employ a multi-period difference-in-differences model for empirical analysis. The findings indicate that: First, the establishment of a city-level OGD platform significantly promotes the establishment of non-local subsidiaries by external firms in that city, confirming its spatial reconfiguration effect. Second, this effect is particularly pronounced in incentivizing firms to engage in cross-industry non-local diversified investments, suggesting that open data helps firms overcome industry-specific cognitive barriers. The contributions of this study are threefold: It extends the economic impact of OGD to the domain of cross-regional capital allocation, providing micro-level evidence for the concept of data empowerment. It reveals the mechanism through which data openness influences corporate spatial decisions by reducing information costs. Finally, it offers policy insights for optimizing capital allocation and promoting coordinated regional development through differentiated data openness strategies.

How and When Decision Openness Promotes Digital Innovation: Evidence from China

STR
Chongchong Lyu (Lanzhou University); Zixin Xia (Lanzhou University); Yufei Li (UNSW Sydney)
This study explores the role that decision openness plays in digital innovation. It examines how decision openness promotes an innovation culture and enhances dynamic capabilities, essential for adapting to rapid technological and market shifts. Through a two-wave, time-lagged survey involving 273 firms, the study reveals that decision openness not only directly influences digital innovation but also does so indirectly through the mediation of dynamic capabilities. Additionally, the study investigates the moderating effects of bridging ties and social media strategic capabilities (SMSC) on this relationship, revealing that both factors strengthen the impact of decision openness on digital innovation. These findings challenge the traditional view that digital innovation is mainly technology-driven, highlighting the significant role of internal decision-making process. The study contributes to existing literature by integrating dynamic capabilities theory with decision-making process, providing insights into the complex nature of digital innovation. For practitioners, the study emphasizes the importance of cultivating an open decision-making environment, developing dynamic capabilities, and strategically leveraging external networks and social media to drive innovation in the digital era.

Executives’ IT Background and Cross-Boundary Digital Innovation: Evidence from China

STR
WanShi He (Sun Yat-Sen University); Weiwen Li (Sun Yat-Sen University)
As traditional firms navigate the challenges of digital transformation, the strategic influence of executives with information technology (IT) backgrounds becomes increasingly critical. Drawing on upper echelons theory, this study examines how IT-background executives shape cross-boundary digital innovation. Using a panel dataset of Chinese listed firms, we find that executives with IT backgrounds significantly enhance cross-boundary digital innovation. This effect is further amplified by TMT functional heterogeneity and technology blockade, yet unexpectedly diminishes in firms with higher levels of enterprise digitalization. These findings advance the literature by elucidating how managerial characteristics interact with organizational, team, and environmental contingencies to influence strategic innovation. The study contributes to research on digital transformation and strategic leadership by identifying the boundary conditions under which IT-background executives drive cross-boundary digital initiatives.

Threatened by AI? Firm AI Adoption and Employees’ Approval of CEO

STR
Shihui ZHANG (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou)); Jinyu He (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou))
How does a firm’s artificial intelligence (AI) adoption shape employees’ approval of their chief executive officer (CEO)? Grounded in psychological contract theory, we posit that corporate AI adoption triggers employees’ perceptions of psychological contract violation, thereby eroding their approval of the CEO —the key decision-maker behind the firm’s AI adoption strategy. Furthermore, this relationship is contingent on three boundary conditions: CEO ownership, employee ownership, and firm financial performance. Empirical analysis of a sample of S&P 500 firms covering the 2010–2018 period provides robust support for our theoretical predictions. Collectively, our theoretical framework and empirical findings offer important implications for research and practice at the intersection of AI adoption and employee–management relations.
