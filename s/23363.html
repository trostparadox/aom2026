Diffuse or Focus? The Effect of ESG Disparity on Green Innovation

ONE
Zhe JI (Bordeaux University)
This study examines whether pillar-skewed ESG engagement affects firms’ green innovation and tests the moderating roles of analyst attention, market uncertainty, and slack resources. Using panel data on SBF 120 firms in France from 2014 to 2024, we find that ESG disparity is negatively associated with green innovation. The negative association is attenuated when analyst attention increases and when market uncertainty is higher, whereas the moderating effect of slack resources is not statistically significant. After accounting for potential endogeneity, the relationship remains consistent, and additional robustness analyses corroborate the main findings, including the attenuating roles of stronger external monitoring and higher uncertainty.

Cleaner Global Value Chains: Green Innovation and Environmental Performance in EU Agri-Food Sectors

ONE
Federico Zilia (University of Milan); Ivan De Noni (University of Milan); Luigi Orsi (University of Milan); Stefanella Stranieri (University of Milan)
Global value chains (GVCs) shape how industries innovate and pollute. This study tests whether, and under what conditions, GVC participation fosters green innovation and improves environmental performance in European agri-food industries. We combine multi-regional input-output data from the GLORIA database with sector-level green patents from OECD REGPAT, covering 28 European countries and 39 sectors from 1991 to 2021. Using fixed-effects panel regressions, we link backward and forward participation to green patenting and to CO2, GHG, and particulate emissions. Results show a clear asymmetry: backward participation (imported intermediates) is consistently associated with lower emissions, suggesting environmental upgrading through access to cleaner and more efficient foreign technologies. By contrast, forward participation, typical of upstream and resource-intensive stages, does not reduce emissions and is often related to higher local air pollution. Green technological capability moderates these effects: it lowers emissions only in forward-oriented segments, where baseline pollution intensity is higher and upgrading depends on domestic innovation. In conclusion, the environmental sustainability of GVCs depends jointly on the mode of integration and the strength of domestic green innovation, implying policies should pair access to clean intermediates with targeted support for eco-innovation in upstream activities.

Revisiting Green Innovation: The Path and Contingencies to Higher Performance

ONE
Ming-Chang Huang (National Yunlin University of Science and Technology); Guan-Ting Chen (National Yunlin University of Science and Technology); Chieh-Han Yang (National Yunlin University of Science and Technology)
In recent years, many firms have adopted green innovation as a strategic approach to enhance their environmental sustainability and competitive advantage. However, the results have been inconsistent despite numerous studies exploring the relationship between green innovation and financial performance. This study re-examines this contentious issue by investigating the pathways and contexts that influence green innovation and financial performance. Specifically, it addresses whether green innovation affects a firm's financial outcomes. The research focuses on 90 listed firms that have consistently published corporate social responsibility (CSR) reports and environment, social, and governance (ESG) reports from 2014 to 2021, resulting in a panel data set comprising 720 "firm-years" for empirical analysis. The findings indicate that green innovation has a positive impact on a firm's performance. Moreover, the disclosure of environmental information mediates the positive relationship between green innovation and financial performance. The study also reveals that when pressure from secondary stakeholders is higher, the positive relationship between green innovation and environmental information disclosure is strengthened. This, in turn, moderates the impact of green innovation on financial performance through the disclosure of environmental performance. Additionally, when pressure from primary stakeholders is greater, the positive relationship between environmental information disclosure and firm financial performance becomes more pronounced, further moderating the influence of green innovation on financial performance through environmental performance disclosure.

Green Incentives and Emissions: The Hidden Costs of KPI Complexity and Sustainability Reporting

ONE
Jan Philip Boeckers (RWTH Aachen University)
As investors and regulators turn to compensation design to drive decarbonization, green incentives, defined as executive pay tied to environmental KPIs, have become a prominent yet debated governance tool. This study examines whether, and under which conditions, such incentives lower corporate carbon emissions. Drawing on agency theory, the attention-based view, and institutional theory, we analyze 6,632 executive-year observations from S&P 500 firms between 2010 and 2022, integrating ISS Incentive Lab, Refinitiv, and Compustat data. Results show that a higher share of compensation linked to environmental KPIs is associated with larger reductions in Scope 1 and 2 emissions. However, this effect weakens when executives are evaluated on a higher number of performance objectives and when firms publish sustainability reports. The findings reveal a governance paradox: mechanisms intended to reinforce green pay can undermine it when incentive plans are complex or disclosure provides alternate legitimacy as a substitute for performance.

Geographic Luck and Environmental Performance: How Wind Patterns Bias ESG Ratings

ONE
Sreevathsan Sridhar
Environmental performance ratings conflate corporate behavior with geographic chance. When wind patterns carry emissions across state borders, downwind communities in neighboring states experience pollution without compensating economic benefits, intensifying stakeholder complaints independent of actual facility conduct. Using facility-level toxic release data matched with meteorological wind patterns and ESG ratings (11,229 facilities, 2006-2024), I demonstrate that wind-adjusted proximity to state borders systematically predicts reported environmental incidents and worse ratings despite no relationship with actual emissions. Facilities closer to borders along prevailing wind directions face significantly amplified rating penalties per unit pollution compared to identical facilities in state interiors. These findings reveal that "geographic luck"—exogenous atmospheric dispersion patterns— systematically contaminates environmental performance metrics, challenging how we interpret ESG ratings and highlighting persistent externality measurement problems even after regulatory interventions targeting cross-border pollution.
