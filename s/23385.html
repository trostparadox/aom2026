Orchestrating Fintech Partnerships: A Traditional Investment Bank

STR
Angélique VOLTAIRE (ESCP Business School)
Digitalization, regulatory tightening and the rise of fintechs are pushing investment banks to reconfigure how they build and mobilize capabilities. This paper examines how partnerships with fintech firms contribute to the dynamic capabilities and Meta-Capabilities of a large European investment bank. Drawing on Teece’s dynamic capabilities framework and Doz’s notion of meta-capabilities, we conceptualize bank–fintech partnerships as inter-organizational microfoundations of strategic agility. Empirically, we conduct a longitudinal embedded case study under an industrial contract, combining more than two years of participant observation, 36 semi-structured interviews and extensive document analysis. Our findings show that partnerships function as a distributed sensing device for technologies, markets and regulation; they support seizing through modular technological components and a flexible portfolio of partnerships, yet their scaling is constrained by legacy systems and prudential requirements. Partnerships also catalyse transforming via venture-building and platform initiatives, but these pockets of agility remain “prudentially bounded” by the incumbent’s organizational and regulatory apparatus. The paper refines the understanding of asset orchestration in systemic financial institutions and identifies key governance and boundary-spanning microfoundations for converting fintech partnerships into strategic agility under prudential constraints.

When Sustainability Meets Alliance: ESG Capacities and Alliance Types Across Industry Boundaries

STR
Chung-Jen Chen (National Taiwan University); Bo-Kai Chow (National Taiwan University); Yi-Ling Lee (National Taiwan University)
This study examines the effects of the ESG capacities and industrial environment on the firm’s choice of alliance type between intra-industry and inter-industry, as well as the endogenous implications of the alliance type for post-alliance performance. Our findings indicate that: (1) firms with stronger environmental capacity are more likely to form inter-industry alliances, whereas firms with stronger social capacity or operating in munificent industries tend to select intra-industry alliances; (2) firms selecting inter-industry alliances generally perform better than firms selecting intra-industry alliances; and (3) firms would make choices of alliance type that best correspond to the conditions they encounter.

Inter-Organizational Trust. A Joint Venture Conceptual Framework for Turst Development

STR
Diana Neely (University of South Florida)
Joint ventures (JVs) are becoming more prevalent, prompting interest in the strategic and organizational processes that contribute to their success. While these processes have been considered particularly under conditions of behavioral and environmental uncertainty, this paper extends the existing literature by conceptualizing the development of interorganizational trust in JVs. I begin by focusing on the two primary modes of trust, contractual and relational, then I explore how firms navigate the uncertainties inherent in the pre and post deal phases of joint projects. The logic of the proposed framework suggests that organizations develop varying levels of trust at different stages of the exchange process. I establish a foundation for evaluating the motivations for interorganizational trust at the pre and post-deal stages of JVs and then offer theoretical propositions for further studies. The goal is to contribute to the literature on JV success by highlighting the relevance of information processing in facilitating trust between exchange partners during the pre- and post-deal phases of a JV transaction.

Boards Don't Govern Alone: Why JV Success Requires Governance Bundles

STR
Elko Klijn (Old Dominion University); Rene Olie (Space for Business); Fabrice Lumineau (The University of Hong Kong); Niloofar Tabarhosseini (Old Dominion University)
Although research on joint venture (JV) governance has focused primarily on contractual and relational mechanisms, the role of JV boards and their interaction with other governance mechanisms remain poorly understood. We address this gap by conceptualizing JV governance as a configuration of contractual, relational, and board-based mechanisms and examining how these mechanisms jointly shape JV performance under varying levels of uncertainty. Using fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) on 152 joint ventures, we analyze how contract specificity, relational governance, board involvement, and board size combine to produce high performance. Our findings show that no single governance mechanism—including board governance—is sufficient for high JV performance. Instead, performance emerges from distinct governance configurations. We identify three high-performing configurations, all of which include strong relational governance. Board size functions as a critical structuring condition: small to medium-sized boards enable limited substitution between contractual governance and board involvement, whereas larger boards require the simultaneous presence of all governance mechanisms. Under high uncertainty, relational governance becomes a necessary condition and viable configurations narrow substantially. Overall, our study advances a configurational perspective on JV governance, clarifies the contingent role of boards, and refines understanding of complementarity and substitution among governance mechanisms in interorganizational collaboration.

Reputations for Knowledge Misappropriation and the Sharks Dilemma

STR
Hugo Leenders (New Mexico State University)
A common way for technology ventures to develop and commercialize their inventions is through R&D alliances with incumbent firms. However, in seeking such alliances, technology ventures face a “sharks dilemma”: the tension between access to valuable resources and the risk of knowledge misappropriation. We investigate how incumbents’ reputations for knowledge misappropriation – captured by their histories of patent infringement – influence partner selection. We find that ventures avoid incumbents with such reputations, particularly when they receive more media attention as defendants in patent infringement litigation, and when competition among incumbents is intense. By highlighting the role of incumbents’ past misappropriation behaviors, this study extends research on the sharks dilemma and demonstrates how character reputations shape R&D alliance formation between technology ventures and established firms.
