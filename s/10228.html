Session overview

Hierarchy is a fundamental and powerful feature in organizations. It determines how power, authority, and resources are distributed and plays important functional roles in task motivations, coordination, and conflict attenuation that directly determine an organization’s success (Halevy et al., 2011). It also directly shapes individuals’ experience at work through various forms, such as what it means to be “successful” at work, whose ideas exert greater impact over others’, and what entitlements some deserve relative to others (Magee & Galinsky, 2008). Despite its importance to organizations, the effects of organizational hierarchy on team effectiveness are largely context dependent (Anderson & Brown, 2010). Although the functionalist theory of hierarchy suggests that a clearer chain of command through hierarchy provides more clarity and structure in task coordination, other work suggests that hierarchy may increase disruptive team conflicts due to a lack of consensus on individuals’ positions on the hierarchy and an incongruence in the levels of influence and respect that individuals are conferred (Anicich et al., 2016; Kilduff et al., 2016). Therefore, the role of hierarchy in organizations depends not just on what it does to the structures and processes of work tasks but on how individuals confer and use it in evaluations of others and decisions about organizational change. The purpose of this symposium is twofold. First, we aim to deepen scholarly understandings in how recent technological and ideological change on a society level may intersect with individual beliefs and behaviors to influence whether and which individuals are granted more influence than others at an organizational level. Second, we aim to discuss insights about why individuals may support or resist change in the existing hierarchy of organizations and how it in turn influences their receptiveness to team membership change to shape its subsequent performance outcomes. Through a mix of experimental and field investigations, the papers in this symposium explore a range of important questions. This includes 1) What explains the prevalence of hierarchy in organizations, and how does it inform our understandings of change in organizational structures and designs? 2) How does organizations’ existing level of hierarchy shape their responses to membership change in the face of performance failures? 3) When and why does disagreement about hierarchy in organizational arrangements arise, and how does it shape leader emergence and work collaboration? 4) How does new technologies like Artificial Intelligence (AI) change the way status is conferred and challenge the existing social hierarchy in organizations?
