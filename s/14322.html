Dignity in Entrepreneurship: Designing Jobs with Autonomy in Mind

Session overview

Scholarly consensus exists that workplace dignity is important for organizations. While scholars have theorized about what the dignity construct entails and why it matters in a variety of ways, autonomy has always been identified as the primary element of the construct. Recently, scholars have noted the importance of studying job design in entrepreneurship. Specifically, the actions that a founder or founding team take at the onset of a new organization have lasting effects on what an organization ultimately becomes in terms of culture, strategy, decision making, structure, and other important factors. It follows that increasing scholarly understanding of the job design choices that founders make is an important endeavour. Responding to a recent call for research to examine what role individual factors play with respect to job design, this paper studies the relationship between individual factors and job design. Specifically, we investigate how individual factors, such as past work experience and gender, drive entrepreneurs to intentionally design autonomy into their new ventures. We empirically test the theorized model using general linear modelling (GLM) on a unique sample of 73 nascent entrepreneurs. Results show that nascent entrepreneurs who had a high level of input in organizing their own work in their past jobs are more likely to design autonomy in their future ventures. Further, females are more likely to design their ventures with autonomy in mind, which confirms existing findings that females are more motivated by autonomy in an entrepreneurial context.
