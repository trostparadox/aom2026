Cognitive Biases in Hybrid Intelligence: A Systematic Review of Human-AI Collaboration

TIM
Ricardo Limongi (Federal University of Goiás); Daiana Pimenta (Federal University of Goiás)
The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence from predictive tools to autonomous agents fundamentally transforms how humans and machines collaborate in organizational settings. While hybrid intelligence theory has established frameworks for understanding human-AI problem-solving, critical gaps remain regarding the cognitive biases that shape these collaborations. This systematic literature review synthesizes research across three converging streams: hybrid intelligence theory, AI agents’ evolution, and cognitive biases in human-AI collaboration. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm and extending Raisch and Fomina's (2025) hybrid problem-solving framework, we identify seven theoretical gaps at the intersection of these literatures. Our analysis reveals that automation bias, algorithm aversion, confirmation bias amplification, and expertise paradoxes operate distinctively in agentic AI contexts, yet remain undertheorized in management scholarship. We propose an integrative framework mapping how cognitive biases manifest across autonomous, sequential, and interactive hybrid problem-solving processes. This review contributes to management theory by establishing cognitive biases as a critical lens for understanding hybrid intelligence outcomes, offers practical implications for organizations deploying AI agents, and outlines a research agenda addressing the recursive dynamics between human cognition and increasingly autonomous AI systems.

How Does Algorithmic Assistance Impact Human Performance in Collaboration?

TIM
Jong Sig (Sik) Chung (University of Wisconsin-Madison)
Algorithmic assistance is increasingly deployed to assist human performance in knowledge-intensive work, yet its integration may inadvertently undermine the human collaborative processes essential for human performance in knowledge-intensive work. Focusing on the introduction of algorithms in human collaboration, this study argues that it will stifle collective discussion essential for developing their shared understanding. Therefore, it will lower human’s performance. Using a stacked cohort generalized Difference-in-Differences design and data about software development projects on GitHub, the study finds that although the adoption of Continuous Integration (CI) bot within a project—algorithms that assist evaluation— accelerate evaluation decisions, they also narrow the scope of collective discussions among developers during evaluation. Ultimately, this leads to a decline in developer’s performance in two essential tasks where shared understanding about the project is valuable: recognizing problem areas and exploring new improvements within the project. Plus, it increases total un-resolved problem areas within the project, possibly due to reduced developer’s performance. Lastly, these adverse effects are magnified when projects receive contributions from diverse knowledge domains. These findings challenge prevailing perspectives about human-algorithm augmentation, highlighting the need for a balanced approach to integrating algorithmic assistance.

Human Says “Yes”, AI Says “No”: How Does Human-AI Conflict Influence Compliance Behavior? (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Zixin Huang (Renmin University of China); Songbo Liu (Renmin University of China); Hengrui Chen (Renmin University of China); guojin xiong (Renmin University of China)
As Artificial Intelligence (AI) increasingly provides guidance in problem-solving and decision-making, individuals frequently encounter human-AI advise conflict when AI recommendations diverge from their own expectations. Utilizing Expectancy Violation Theory (EVT), we conduct interviews and an experiment to formulate and evaluate a moderated mediation model that elucidates how conflict variably influences adherence to AI suggestions compared to adherence to personal expectations. In two studies, we demonstrate that task familiarity negatively moderates the impact of conflict on positive violation valence, while positively moderating the effects of conflict on negative violation valence and perceived justifiability for oneself. The relationship between conflict and compliance is influenced by violation valence and perceived justifiability; specifically, positive violation valence enhances compliance with AI, while negative violation valence and self-justifiability augment compliance with personal expectations. By applying EVT to human-AI interaction, we redefine AI advise as an expectancy-laden signal, elucidate the causal relationship between conflict and varying compliance decisions, and provide a process-oriented perspective for analyzing compliance behavior in AI-integrated decision-making environments.

Organizational Technology Ladders: Remote Work and Generative AI Adoption

TIM
Gregor Schubert (University of California Los Angeles)
This study proposes that firms move along an ``organizational technology ladder'': adopting one technology transforms hiring and work processes and builds skills and organizational capital that change the cost of adopting subsequent technologies. I study how firms' adoption of remote work technology during the COVID-19 period shaped later uptake of generative AI. Using U.S. job-posting data and an instrumental-variables strategy based on predicted differences in labor-market pressure to offer remote work, I estimate that a 10 percentage point increase in remote hiring in 2021–-2022 increases the share of job postings mentioning generative AI in the 12 months ending Sep. 2024 by 0.4 percentage points across firms and 0.7 percentage points across occupations within firms. I provide evidence on mechanisms consistent with a technology-ladder channel: remote work adoption shifts hiring toward technical and managerial capabilities that predict faster conversion of generative AI exposure into adoption. Firms with return-to-office mandates-- interpreted as revealing low remote productivity--exhibit a substantially larger response of generative AI adoption to remote work, consistent with an organizational frictions channel. I formalize the technology-ladder mechanism using a task-based model of sequential technology investments where remote work changes the costs and benefits of generative AI automation.

Sociomaterial Entanglement and Affective Events: Human Staff’s Experiences in Hybrid Teams

TIM
Premilla D'Cruz; Ernesto Noronha
We present a multi-site, multi-city, multi-method study combining visual and qualitative techniques in Indian full-service, dine-in, family restaurants with human and robot waiters, capturing sociomaterial entanglement and its accompanying emotions. Engaging Pickering’s mangle of practice framework, we show how human staff accommodated robots’ limited mechanical and digital materialities through a process of tuning. Human staff exhibited several emotions including excitement, happiness, fear, annoyance, irritation, relief, protectiveness and pride. By focusing on emotions, our study addresses a long-standing much-recognized gap in the sociomaterial literature. We contribute by showing sociomaterial entanglement as an affective event which triggers an emotional episode, marked by emotional ambivalence, that impacts job performance. Human staff’s intentionality, linked to their work ethic, helped them effectively navigate their emotional ambivalence and ensure flawless customer service, thereby fulfilling organizational goals. We also add visual methods to the sociomateriality methods repertoire.
