Does China Intellectual Property Pilot and Demonstration City Influence Firm Innovation Performance

TIM
Jiayi Hu (University of Leeds); Kausik Chaudhuri (University of Leeds); Muhammad Ali Nasir (University of Leeds)
National innovation policies are designed to stimulate firm-level innovation; however, their micro-level impacts and underlying mechanisms differ across countries. This paper examines the impacts of China’s Intellectual Property Pilot and Demonstration City (IPPDC) policy on firms’ patenting behavior, using firm-level patent and R&D data from 2008 to 2022. Using a multi-period difference-in-difference estimator on a propensity-score-matched sample of Chinese manufacturing firms, we find that the IPPDC policy program significantly enhances firm-level innovation, primarily through increased city-level investments in science, technology, and education, which in turn stimulate corporate R&D expenditure. Significant heterogeneity exists on different sub-groups: positive impacts are pronounced among large firms but for small firms the effects are negative, firms in less competitive markets are more likely to increase innovation, and firms without separation of management and ownership are more likely to promote innovation activities. Regional differences also emerge with coastal firms only prioritize technological innovation, while inland firms also focus on non-technological patents. Our findings remain robust across various checks and reveal that the policy delivers both immediate and sustained long-term effects, underscoring its success in fostering innovation.

State-Orchestrated Innovation Ecosystem and Industrial Emergence

TIM
Ataullah Kiani (Wenzhou-Kean University)
Although emerging industries are increasingly founded on multi-actor innovation ecosystems, the role of state involvement in shaping these ecosystems remains under-researched. Drawing on a longitudinal, process-based analysis of the EV charging industry in China, this study examines how state-orchestrated innovation ecosystems influence coordination, legitimacy, and technological selection during industrial emergence. Using archival data supplemented by semi-structured interviews with key ecosystem participants, we identify three mechanisms through which state orchestration accelerates ecosystem development: (1) facilitating coordination among heterogeneous actors, (2) building collective legitimacy at the ecosystem level, and (3) guiding technological selection toward dominant designs. We further show that these mechanisms co-evolve over time, generating mutually reinforcing dynamics that stabilize industry structure and technological standards. Our findings contribute to theories of industrial emergence, innovation ecosystems, and institutional dynamics, and offer practical implications for managers and policymakers seeking to promote nascent industries in complex institutional contexts.

The First Domino to Fall: Green Innovation Spillovers of Environmental Public Interest Litigation

TIM
Xuemei Xie (School of Economics and Management, Tongji University); Liang Xu (Tongji University)
Litigation initiated by private interest organizations (e.g., shareholders, competitors, and non-practicing entities) can influence strategic decisions among peer firms. However, it overlooks a distinct category of lawsuits: lawsuits initiated by environmental public interest organizations (hereafter, EPIO litigation) to defend public interest. To fill this gap, we apply a staggered difference-in-differences method to a panel dataset of publicly listed firms in China from 2007 to 2022 to empirically examine the effect of EPIO litigation against sued firms on peer green innovation. We find that EPIO litigation has a positive effect on peer green innovation and that the relationship varies depending on contingent factors. Specifically, the positive effect is more pronounced when EPIO litigation is successful and when the lawsuit is initiated by environmental enforcement agencies rather than environmental non-governmental organizations (environmental NGOs). Furthermore, we find that EPIO litigation increases peer green innovation by amplifying peers’ perceived litigation risk. In doing so, we challenge the prevailing view that litigation spillover research is limited to private interest organizations and driven mainly by economic or competitive logic. Our study demonstrates that non-private interest litigation driven by institutional logic, namely, EPIO litigation, is a key judicial instrument for promoting industry-wide green innovation—mainly through coercive institutional pressure—offering important implications for environmental policymaking.

The Role of Industrial and Innovation Policy in Advancing Sustainable Technologies: 3D Printing (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Wendy Bradley; Julian Kolev (united states patent and trademark office)
What is the impact of mission-oriented industrial and innovation policy on the direction of technological development and innovation? In 2012 the U.S. government announced ‘America Makes’—the first U.S. Institute of Manufacturing Innovation—as part of a broader strategy to modernize American manufacturing and promote technological sovereignty and sustainability. Using a difference-in-differences design to track U.S. patents by government contractors, universities, and other organizations from 2006 to 2019, this paper finds large increases in annual patenting for 3D-printing technology compared to other patenting by the same organizations. Furthermore, we find greater increases for U.S.-government contractors versus other organizations after ‘America Makes’. The largest gains occurred in upstream portions of the value chain, and in 3D-printing inventions that were also listed under “green” patent subclasses. Surprisingly, universities including U.S.-contractor universities were unaffected by the policy. The results imply that mission-oriented interventions can impact not only the quantity of invention, but also the composition of organizational types and the direction of innovation within the technology. Our findings have implications for twin-transition policy, sustainability, technological sovereignty, and the concentration of patent ownership in sectors where innovation cycles are long and path dependent.

Too Much Support? The Role of Political Volatility in the Emergence of the Alternative Fuel Industry

TIM
Mariza Gabriela L. Mesquita (Louisiana State University); Joseph J. Cabral (Louisiana State University)
Public policies influence entrepreneurs’ opportunity evaluation and action. Drawing on institutional theory, this study investigates how distinct configurations of political support and policy volatility (i.e., the degree or frequency that support is changing) shape the emergence of new ventures. Using a sample of U.S. state-year policies from 2006 to 2023, including laws, regulations, and legal incentives for alternative fuels and sustainable transportation, data were analyzed using cluster analysis and regressions. As expected, higher levels of political support generally foster venture emergence, but this effect is nuanced by the frequency in which support is changing. Specifically, intermediate levels of political volatility spur more venture creation than both lower and higher levels of volatility, suggesting that too frequent introductions of support mechanisms may unintentionally dissuade entrepreneurship. In essence, it is possible to have “too much of a good thing” when the frequency of change creates the perception of instability or increased uncertainty.
