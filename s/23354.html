Delegation, Attention Allocation, and Innovation in Entrepreneurial Ventures

STR
Knarik Poghosyan (DIW Berlin); Alexander Kritikos; Theodor Lucian Vladasel
Despite growing recognition that organizational design can facilitate entrepreneurs' attention allocation to high-value activities that improve firm innovation, few studies have tested this theoretical insight. Guided by organizational structure and managerial frictions literature in innovation and entrepreneurship, we examine how and why shared decision rights affect innovation outcomes. In a representative survey of German entrepreneurs, delegation has a strong, robust, positive association with incremental innovation; evidence for alternative innovation outcomes or differential effects of delegation depth is limited. This relationship varies somewhat with incentives, employee, and founder ability, and is stronger in East Germany, where managerial frictions are larger. While delegation does not free up time for personal activities, it allows entrepreneurs to engage in a greater number of venture activities, especially crafting strategy, and perform them more intensely. Collectively, our findings imply delegation enhances innovation by facilitating entrepreneurs' attention allocation to critical managerial tasks, such as acquiring market and competitor insight or making growth and investment choices. By clarifying where the value of delegation resides and what activities founders prioritize, we highlight the importance of strategy in entrepreneurial ventures.

When and How Does Participative Management Improve Organizational Learning?

STR
Ekin Ilseven (Católica Lisbon School of Business & Economics)
Participative management, broadening who has influence over collective choices, has regained attention as firms confront faster change and more dispersed expertise. Its promise is better decisions and stronger execution, yet its benefits are uneven. A learning perspective suggests why: participation shapes organizational exploration not only through information aggregation in choice formation, but also through compliance in execution, thereby altering the experience stream from which organizations learn. We develop a stylized computational model of multi-agent reinforcement learning in which a committee of varying size selects a collective directive and members then decide whether to implement it or follow private preferences; we examine how these dynamics depend on individual exploration and task interdependence. When coordination demands are weak, broader participation and stronger compliance stabilize actions over time and across members, reducing organizational exploration and reproducing the classic exploration–exploitation trade-off between representation accuracy and current performance. Under strong interdependence, within-period variety in actions arising from noncompliance becomes costly and distorts feedback: accuracy peaks at intermediate participation, while performance is maximized at design extremes, either small committees with exploitative members or broad committees with exploratory members. We therefore reframe participative management as a contingent design lever that governs organizational learning, and we highlight that participation should be designed jointly with legitimacy-building and coordination practices rather than treated as a one-size-fits-all move toward inclusion.

Strategy for Multiunit Organizations

STR
Jorge Tamayo (Harvard Business School); Julian Jimenez
This paper examines how multiunit firms manage decision-making under varying levels of information asymmetry between central headquarters and individual units. We analyze the interplay between control—the extent to which firms enforce strategic discipline by penalizing unit deviations— misalignment costs, and profitability. Our findings highlight the trade-offs firms face in designing their strategy: while rigid control ensures alignment with corporate objectives, excessive rigidity can stifle managerial responsiveness to local conditions. We show that firms can enhance organizational agility by optimizing control, particularly by granting managers greater decision-making autonomy when uncertainty about local market conditions is high. We test the model’s empirical predictions using granular operational and personnel data from two large multiunit firms—a quick-service restaurant chain and a retail company—and find evidence consistent with the theory. These insights contribute to the broader understanding of strategy design in multiunit firms operating under heterogeneous environments.

Configurations and Competitive Advantage

STR
Danny Miller (HEC Montréal); Isabelle Le Breton-Miller (HEC Montréal)
The configuration literature has paid little attention to how firms can acquire and sustain economic rents in a competitive environment. At the same time, the strategy literature has underemphasized the competitive potential of configured organizational infrastructures, a key source of critical isolating mechanisms. By bridging insights from these literatures, we propose a model to distinguish the types of configurations that can garner and sustain different forms of economic rent. The model encompasses resource-based, positioning, and dynamic capability configurations, their organizational infrastructures, and the associated isolating mechanisms. We provide illustrative case examples of firms with a) functional configurations that underspecify sources of advantage, b) three strategic configurations that did sustain advantage, and c) a stagnant configuration of decline. From these we draw propositions, analytical discussion, and recommendations for further research.
