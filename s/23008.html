Hot Market, Cold Feet: Entrepreneurs’ Storytelling Strategies Between Hype and Stigma

SAP
Amanda Baum (Hamburg University of Technology); Pauline Charlotte Reinecke (Leuphana University of Lüneburg); Moritz Goeldner (Hamburg University of Technology)
Entrepreneurship increasingly engages with stigmatized societal issues, frequently using media channels such as social media to generate hype around them. In this context, new ventures encounter responses characterized by a tension between hype and stigma that entrepreneurs must actively navigate to build their ventures. Our case study of 30 entrepreneurs in the hyped German femtech market shows that interactions with investors often reactivate stigma, exposing entrepreneurs to discrepancies between public attention and backlash. We identify three storytelling strategies used by entrepreneurs during these interactions. Commercial storytelling leverages hype but conceals stigma by focusing on financial metrics to align ventures with investors’ expectations, thereby bypassing the hype-stigma tension and securing funding. Activist storytelling confronts stigma to fuel hype through emotional or provocative narratives, thereby amplifying the hype-stigma tension and sustaining public attention. Scientific storytelling strategically leverages hype while focusing on data generation, thereby minimizing the hype-stigma tension and enhancing credibility. We contribute to research on entrepreneurship by showing that entrepreneurs mobilize cultural resources in tension rather than in isolation; and by reframing stigma as an enduring tension to navigate rather than a condition to be resolved. We also believe this paper aligns closely with current calls in Strategy-as-Practice research to address societal challenges and to connect practice-based scholarship with other research streams, including entrepreneurship for social change (Jarzabkowski et al., 2025).

Resisting Change Through Technology Framing Work: An Ethnography of a Supercomputer

SAP
Daniel Gaeckle (University of Stuttgart); Christian Alexander Mahringer (University of Stuttgart); Birgit Elisabeth Renzl (University of Stuttgart)
Research on technological frames has focused on technology emergence and implementation, providing limited insights into how framing matters once technologies are fully embedded in organizational routines. We examine technological framing in such a settled context through a twelve-month ethnographic study of an organization whose routines rely on a Supercomputer. Although actors identified problems in routines and proposed changes to address them, the organization rarely implemented these changes. Our analysis shows that resistance to change arose from how actors framed the technology in everyday work. Our findings advance research on technological frames and organizational routines in three ways. First, we show that framing remains effortful in settled contexts because routine performances regularly surface problems that prompt change, which in turn trigger framing. Second, we unpack the technology framing work entailed: We find four framing moves (i.e., normalizing imperfections, anticipating ramifications, externalizing accountabilities, and downplaying anomalies) and show how they combine through reinforcement and contradiction. Third, we unpack the reciprocal relationship between technological framing and routines. Routine performances generate problems that prompt technology framing work, and this work can resist routine change.

Framing Strategy Through Material Practices and Artifacts: A Design Approach to Strategizing

SAP
Martina Legnani (Politecnico di Milano); Stefano Magistretti (Politecnico di Milano); Ileana Stigliani
Strategy as Practice (SAP) research emphasizes doing strategy by focusing on the social, material, and discursive practices through which strategizing unfolds. However, SAP overlooks what occurs at the moment of strategy emergence, a phase typically characterized by ambiguity as different stakeholders hold divergent understandings of the emerging strategy. In this phase, strategy framing becomes critical. While prior SAP studies have examined strategy framing mainly in discursive terms, they have overlooked its material dimension, despite the potential of artifacts to help actors navigate ambiguity. In this regard, this study aims to examine the material practices and artifacts involved in strategy framing during the emergence of a new strategic initiative. Based on the case of a new banking service creation design studio supporting a strategy framing process, our analysis identifies three phases through which material practices and artifacts evolve, progressively framing the emerging strategy. We conceptualize strategy framing as a three-step, design-led process, highlighting how strategy framing is a generative process rather than time-bounded, extending SAP framing research beyond a purely discursive view and contributing to the literature on design as a metaphor for strategy.

Read Between the Lines: The Role of Multimodality in Communicating Strategy on Social Media

SAP
Tiantian Qin (University of Leeds); Josh Morton (University of Leeds); Krsto Pandza
This study explores the role multimodality plays in communicating strategy on social media, through a strategy-as-practice perspective. We conducted qualitative netnography research by sampling a focused dataset of 1,000 strategically focused posts from 50 strategists, out of all 968 FTSE 100 company strategists who have a presence on LinkedIn. Our results conceptualise three roles multimodality affords: distributing understanding through multimodal choices, persuading with multimodal evidence and resonating with multimodal semiotics. This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, we extend the literature on strategic communication by showing how strategists integrate multimodal resources - textual, visual, video and interactive - to construct strategic meaning, perform identity work, and cultivate influence. Second, we advance the study of genres in strategy communication by identifying social media as a distinct genre of strategic communication that redistributes control of the narrative and enables new forms of relational engagement. Third, we expand the methodological repertoire of critical discourse analysis by demonstrating how digital and multimodal strategy communication on social media can be systematically collected and analysed at scale.

Discursive Practices of Resistance: Multimodal Irony and Resistance to Dominant Visions of the Future

SAP
Caroline Makoni (University of Liverpool); Carola Wolf (University of Liverpool); Joep Cornelissen (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
ABSTRACT Narratives actively constructed and disseminated by powerful incumbents to convey their commitment to social issues often naturalise corporate logics and marginalize alternatives, making resistance within organisational fields difficult. While counter- narratives emerge, they rarely disrupt mainstream discourse. If these narratives are so resilient, how can they be effectively disrupted? We investigate this through a qualitative, multimodal discourse analysis of how the environmental organisation Greenpeace resists Coca-Cola’s field-defining narrative about its commitment to addressing plastic pollution. Our findings show that Greenpeace constitutes and deploys multimodal irony, reordering familiar elements and mixing visual, verbal, and audio modalities to create clashes that lead audiences from recognition to surprise. Thus, resistance to dominant narratives is not only discursive but also multimodal, leveraging the interplay of visual, verbal, and audio modalities to exploit unique affordances, such as mimicry, contrast, and emotional resonance, that purely verbal critique cannot fully achieve. By leveraging multimodality, resistors can surface contradictions and open space for alternative narratives.
