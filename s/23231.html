Redistributing Decision Errors by AI: The Omission–Commission Trade-off in Hierarchical Organization

STR
Linhui Wu (Northwestern University); Moshe Barach (University of Minnesota); Shaker A. Zahra (University of Minnesota); Yining Luo (Zhejiang University); Niron Hashai (IDC Herzliya)
We study how Artificial Intelligence (AI) impacts the tradeoff between errors of omission and errors of commission in a hierarchy. We propose a theoretical framework that unpacks AI's influence into two opposing effects. While AI successfully mitigates errors of omission at the bottom of the hierarchy, it simultaneously increases the potential for errors of commission. We empirically test these effects by exploiting a natural field experiment within the radiology department of a major research hospital, using advanced textual analysis on a dataset of approximately 225,000 junior-drafted and senior-corrected CT (Computerized Tomography) scan reports. We find that AI significantly increases the number of distinct diagnostic topics identified by junior radiologists. These topics are incorporated into the final diagnostic reports of senior radiologists, implying that AI is enabling the location of diagnostically pertinent information that might have otherwise been missed, mitigating errors of omission. We also observe that AI decreases the semantic similarity between the analysis and recommendations contained in junior and senior reports; this rise in senior correction activity implies an increase in the need to mitigate commission errors. Thus, AI redistributes the costs of decision-making from the initial search stage to the final verification stage, increasing the burden of gatekeepers.

The Dawn of Human-Machine Coopetition in the Age of Agentic AI: Cooperation & Competition Paradox

STR
Giovanni Battista Dagnino (LUMSA University)
The emergence of agentic artificial intelligence systems marks a fundamental shift in human-machine interaction, moving beyond simple automation toward autonomous decision-making entities capable of pursuing goals with minimal human oversight. This paper introduces and develops the notion of human-machine coopetition as a key notion for understanding the simultaneous competitive and cooperative dynamics between humans and agentic AI systems in organizational contexts. Drawing on coopetition theory, organizational routines literature, and recent advances in AI capabilities, we argue that the relationship between humans and agentic AI exists along a dynamic continuum characterized by temporal flux and contextual contingencies. We develop a three-dimensional framework encompassing the competition-cooperation continuum, temporal dynamics, and conjoined routines through hybrid agency. We identify four strategic configurations firms may adopt and advance five interrelated propositions specifying how these configurations influence performance through distinct mechanisms, moderators, and boundary conditions. Illustrative evidence from professional services, healthcare, scientific research, and creative industries demonstrates how coopetitive dynamics manifest in practice. We then address critical governance challenges including algorithmic bias, transparency, human autonomy, and organizational justice. The paper concludes by articulating theoretical implications for coopetition theory, organizational routines, and strategic management, alongside practical guidance for managers and directions for future inquiry.

Demand for Managerial Roles: Firm’s Anticipatory Responses to Generative AI

STR
Matteo Devigili (INSEAD); E. Dogukan Yilmaz; Vibha Gaba (INSEAD); Henrich Greve (INSEAD)
The impact of technologies on reshaping organizational authority structures remains theoretically and empirically underexplored. This paper examines how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) alters the authority to direct, a core managerial function that entails integrating effort by enabling access to and use of organizational knowledge. We propose that GenAI reduces both the need for direction, by allowing subordinates to solve problems independently, and the scope of direction, by diminishing the informational advantage of managers. This produces a contraction in the demand for organizational hierarchies through an immediate reduction in demand for managerial roles, higher wages for lower-level jobs, and a sizable decrease in middle-management reporting structures. We test these predictions using a large-scale panel of job postings from U.S. public firms, exploiting the release of ChatGPT as a quasi-exogenous shock. Employing a difference-in-differences design, we show that firms more exposed to GenAI reduced managerial hiring by 19–27% relative to less exposed firms. Simultaneously, we find an upward shift in posted salaries for non-managerial roles, suggesting a reallocation of knowledge responsibilities to lower hierarchical levels. Also, we observe substantial declines in demand for manager-to-manager reporting ties and some tentative evidence of declines of manager-to-director ties. These patterns are consistent with the decrease in demand for middle managers as knowledge intermediaries who control information flow between organizational layers. Our study contributes to research on technology and organizational design by providing early empirical evidence that GenAI induces rapid, observable changes in demand for managerial skills in anticipation of de-layering in organizations. We also extend the literature on human-AI collaboration by moving beyond task-level productivity and skills effects to meso-level structural consequences.

The Curvilinear Effects of AI Augmentation and Automation on Firm Performance

STR
Dayuan Li (Central South University); Chen Huang; Yingzhu Fu (Central South University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing firm competition, yet the optimal deployment of augmentation (human–AI collaboration) and automation (AI substitution) remains elusive. This study examines their nonlinear effects on firm performance, theorizing inverted U-shaped relationships: moderate adoption boosts efficiency and innovation through balanced rationalities, but excess adoption leads to dependency, rigidity, and diminished adaptability. We further posit that AI interaction capability flattens the curve of augmentation, moderating both potential and threats, while steepening the curve of automation, amplifying both benefits and risks. Analyzing longitudinal data from Chinese high-tech firms with AI patents (2012–2023) via BERT-based text mining, topic modeling approach, and System generalized method of moments (System GMM) regression model, our results largely confirm these hypotheses. By introducing a dual-rationality framework, this research resolves prior inconsistencies in AI literature, offering fresh theoretical lenses and practical guidelines for calibrating AI strategies amid rapid technological shifts, while paving the way for future explorations of AI’s evolving organizational dynamics.

The Resilience Cost of Automation

STR
Dalbert Ma (London Business School)
Organizations increasingly pursue full automation in controlled operational environments, assuming predictable routines render human workers redundant. This paper identifies a hidden cost of such decisions: the loss of organizational slack that enables resilience. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm and system coupling theory, I argue that human presence provides adaptive capacity that full automation eliminates in pursuit of steady-state efficiency. Using granular operational data from autonomous trucking across Chinese ports—comparing fully automated and hybrid human-AI facilities deploying identical technology—I find strikingly divergent system dynamics. Hybrid operations experience larger initial disruptions following mechanical shocks but demonstrate rapid recovery to baseline. Fully automated operations experience smaller initial disruptions but show compounding delays consistent with cascading failure. The slack enabling recovery takes a counterintuitive form: human drivers cutting in front of autonomous vehicles, deviating from assigned routes, adjusting speeds unpredictably—behaviors that appear disruptive yet collectively prevent disruptions from cascading through the system. Within-port spatial analysis confirms that human presence specifically enables recovery. These findings reveal humans serve as system-level shock absorbers—a strategic role omitted from task-level frameworks and absent from efficiency-focused automation assessments that may systematically understate automation's true cost.
