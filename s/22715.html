Impact of Vertical Integration on ESG Violations: The Role of Integrative Capabilities

OSCM
Finn Petersen (Kelley School of Business, Indiana University); William Diebel (University of South Carolina)
Buying firms increasingly face reputational and financial costs from ESG violations occurring in their supply chains. Vertical integration is often assumed to represent a structural remedy, but its empirical effectiveness and boundary conditions are unclear. We analyze a panel of 3,061 U.S. publicly traded firms (2003--2020), combining LSEG ESG violations and capability data, a text-based measure of vertical integration from 10-Ks, FactSet supply-chain links, and Compustat controls. Using two-way fixed-effects models (with complementary 2SLS checks), we find that vertical integration is, on average, associated with fewer ESG violations: a one-unit increase in integration corresponds to an approximate 17\% reduction in violations. Crucially, this effect is substantially amplified by firm-level integrative capabilities. Firms one standard deviation above the mean on ESG evaluation capability see the integration effect roughly double, and firms high on ESG training capability see an even larger (ca. 2.7×) amplification. Firms lacking these capabilities show little or no benefit. For managers and policymakers, the results indicate that integration can reduce ESG risk exposure but works best when paired with credible evaluation and training infrastructures---otherwise it risks internalizing, not resolving, supply chain ESG risk.

Dispersion vs. Integration: How Does ESG Influence Corporate Supply Chain Network

OSCM
Bolin Qu (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Weiping Liu (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Yeyanran Ge (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
Prior research has mostly focused on the financial impact of non-market strategies, often overlooking how these approaches transform core operations. This study bridges non-market strategy and operations management by examining how corporate ESG performance influences vertical governance, specifically supply chain concentration. We challenge the traditional static view of bargaining power by introducing a bilateral dynamic bargaining mechanism, suggesting that ESG acts as a flexible strategic resource that allows firms to manage their dependence within supply chains. Analyzing Chinese listed firms from 2011 to 2022, we find that higher ESG performance generally leads to lower supply chain concentration, reflecting a risk-hedging approach through diversification. However, this effect depends on the capability and institutional context. Strong internal capabilities (such as digitalization), active external monitoring (analyst coverage), and developed regional markets can weaken or even reverse the dispersion trend, enabling firms to integrate the supply chain for greater efficiency. In contrast, firms in heavily polluting industries face stronger legitimacy pressures, which intensify the drive for defensive dispersion. These findings extend resource dependence theory by showing how non-market factors like ESG are translated into operational and governance changes within firms.

The Divergent Effect of Supplier ESG (Dis)Advantage on Supply Chain Resilience

OSCM
Weixi Sha (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Yi Liu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Daniel Chen (Baylor University)
Contemporary supply chains are marked by intensified interdependence between the supplier and customer and the growing salience of ESG concerns, making a supplier’s ESG performance consequential not only for itself but also for its customer’s supply chain resilience (SCR). Prior SCR research has largely overlooked the role of customer-supplier relationship in shaping SCR, especially the ESG-based interdependency. To address the gap, this research draws on resource dependence and social exchange theories to distinguish two forms of ESG incongruence between a focal customer and its supplier in a dyadic supply chain relationship: supplier ESG advantage and disadvantage relative to the customer, and theorizes that ESG incongruence triggers interdependency dynamics that lead to reciprocal or alienate behaviors, thereby divergently affecting the focal customer’s SCR. Using a 2017-2023 multi-source panel dataset, results suggest that, supplier ESG advantage enhances the focal customer’s SCR, whereas supplier ESG disadvantage undermines it. Further, the research identified supplier base competition and customer ecocentricity as critical contingent factors. By integrating ESG-based interdependency, this study extends the SCR literature beyond traditional operations elements, revealing a new relational perspective that underscores the interdependent and comparative nature of supply chain partners, and identifying boundary conditions rooted in competitive dynamics and external stakeholder connections.
