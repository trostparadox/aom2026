Honey, Who Shrunk the Standard Errors in my Moderated Multiple Regression?

RM
Arturs T. Kalnins (University of Iowa)
The testing of moderated relationships using interaction terms within Ordinary Least Squares/Moderated Multiple Regression (OLS/MMR) is hampered by the low statistical power of typical sample sizes to detect interaction effects relative to the linear effects of primary terms. Despite the low power, empirical surveys report frequent support for moderation hypotheses, raising questions about potential biases. This paper derives novel methodological theory to explain this seeming contradiction. Focusing on omitted quadratic terms, we demonstrate analytically that their exclusion can create error terms that are uncorrelated with, but not independent of, included variables such as interaction terms. We show how this non-independence will artificially shrink standard errors of interaction coefficients and generate excess type 1 errors. In contrast to the existing literature on quadratic/interaction confounds, this phenomenon persists even when an interaction’s primary terms are uncorrelated. We then use Monte Carlo simulations to quantify the bias induced by small, uncontrolled-for quadratic effects. We establish that a consistently excessive quantity of false interaction positives will result: between 10% and 17% of OLS/MMR analyses are likely to incorrectly identify interaction coefficients as p < 0.05, depending upon distributional assumptions, when there is no true effect. Finally, we demonstrate the efficacy of a particular form of robust standard errors to eliminate excess false interaction positives induced by this non-independence in large and small samples alike. Our findings offer theoretical insights and practical solutions to enhance OLS/MMR accuracy in testing moderation hypotheses.

Why Non-Normality is (Usually) not Your Regression Problem

RM
Mikko Rönkkö (University of Jyväskylä); Yvette Baurne (University of Jyväskylä)
Regression analysis relies on several assumptions, including normality. Although organiza- tional methodologists have written extensively on this topic, misconceptions persist regarding what must be normally distributed, why normality matters, and when its violations should lead to preoccupation. In this paper, we clarify these issues and argue that concerns about normality are often overstated relative to their actual consequences for regression analysis. As a result, com- monly recommended remedies—such as quantile regression or data Winsorization—are fre- quently unwarranted. Drawing on a review of methodological misconceptions in organizational research, a discussion of statistical theory, and large-scale Monte Carlo simulations, we derive practical guidelines for assessing and addressing non-normality in applied regression analyses.

Appealing to Theory Without Overstating Predictive Power: A Contingency Framework

RM
Yongheng Yao (Wilfrid Laurier University); Guangrui Guo (University of Windsor); Jia Lin Xie (University of Toronto)
Appealing to theory has been proposed as an important way to advance congruence research; however, growing concerns suggest that this practice, particularly when theorization is partial, may artificially inflate predictive power and, in turn, distort inferences about theoretical support. We address this concern in three steps. First, we argue that predictive power inflation does not stem from appealing to theory per se, but rather from insufficiently informed choices between two algebraically equivalent yet conceptually different forms of polynomial regression and response surface analysis (PRRSA): the classical and contemporary forms. We further distinguish constraint testing from theory testing as two fundamentally different research purposes and show that inflation arises when the classical form, originally developed for constraint testing, is inappropriately applied for theory testing. Such misapplication can inflate predictive power and increase the risk of Type II errors. Second, we examine the advantages of the contemporary form and explain why it is better aligned with theory testing. Third, we integrate these insights into a contingency framework that delineates the boundary conditions and appropriate uses of the classical and contemporary forms in congruence research. Empirical illustrations and Monte Carlo simulations support our arguments. Overall, the proposed framework provides practical guidance for improving theory–method alignment and strengthening inferential validity in congruence research.

Theory in the Second Moment: How Conditional Random-Effect Covariances Advance Management Theory

RM
Guangrui Guo (University of Windsor)
Management theories are inherently structured heterogeneity: systematic differences in how firms, teams, or employees respond to the same drivers, which are dynamically linked through history, trajectories, and transitions. Yet management research remains centered on first-moment (mean) relationships, while second moments (variances and covariances) are commonly treated as ancillary. We argue that many theories are incomplete if they stop at the first moment because key mechanisms of dynamic coupling and persistence are fundamentally second-moment claims. We introduce conditional random-effect covariances as theory-bearing estimands for these mechanisms and formalize effect coupling as a theoretical primitive to capture whether unit-specific mechanism strengths systematically co-move. We develop an effect-coupling typology to provide reusable hypothesis templates and reporting standards, and offer a workflow to support cumulative use across micro and macro domains. We apply this approach to examine the effect of CEO succession on firm performance, illustrating how the covariance structure reveals turnaround and continuity coupling signatures that mean-centric models cannot test directly. We broaden this framework to demonstrate its applicability across the management domains. We conclude with a set of reporting guidelines designed to help scholars rigorously theorize and test the second moment, thereby advancing a more nuanced, contingency-based understanding of management phenomena.
