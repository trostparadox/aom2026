When Incentives Activate Stereotypes: Performance Judgments of People with Disabilities

Friday, Jul 31 2026 · 11:30AM–12:30PM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 4
DEI

People

Authors: Musa Essa Tilburg University; Rene Bakker Tilburg University

Session overview

Despite increasing organizational efforts toward workplace inclusion, people with disabilities (PwD) continue to face systematic disadvantage in evaluation and selection contexts. We propose that this disadvantage is not constant but depends on whether evaluators’ own payoffs depend on judging PwD performance. Drawing on stereotype content theory and motivated reasoning, we argue that evaluators rely more heavily on negative stereotypes about PwD when (a) disability status is disclosed and (b) the evaluation task is incentive compatible—that is, when evaluators’ monetary outcomes depend on prediction accuracy. In such contexts, stereotypes provide a cognitively efficient heuristic that appears “instrumental” under high stakes, even when it leads to biased judgments. We plan to test these predictions in a two-stage experimental design. We manipulate whether disability status is disclosed and whether predictions are incentive compatible. We hypothesize that disclosure alone does not necessarily increase bias; rather, bias emerges most strongly when disclosure coincides with incentive compatibility. Specifically, we predict higher absolute prediction error—and more negative bias—toward PwD when disability is disclosed and evaluators’ payoffs depend on accuracy, compared to disclosure without incentives or incentives without disclosure. This research contributes to the literature on disability, stereotypes, and judgment by showing that bias against PwD intensifies precisely when evaluators believe they are being rational and payoff-maximizing. More broadly, it highlights how incentive structures can unintentionally amplify discrimination in performance evaluation contexts.
