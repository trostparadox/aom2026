Men’s Domesticity & Labour Regime: An Ethnography of Young Migrant Men Living on Construction Sites

CMS
Harish Shetty (XLRI Xavier School of Management)
While the relationship between women’s domestic lives and labour control mechanisms has long been recognised, studies on men have largely overlooked these dimensions, focusing almost exclusively on the workplace. Drawing on ethnographic data of young migrant men living on construction sites in India, this paper examines how labour regimes unfold in contexts where employers exercise control over both home and work life of the workers. The findings show, first, unlike the subjugation of women, where men dominate and exploit their gendered vulnerabilities, young men are organised through leveraging their gendered privileges. They are controlled through mechanisms including facilitation and exploitation of urban aspirations, the transfer of domestic patriarchal logics into the workplace, and the use of patriarchal networks, all enabled by their temporary living arrangements on construction sites. Second, the findings highlight the ambiguous ways in which men experience agency, where their gendered privilege becomes a source of both freedom and subjugation. This ultimately generate a self-perpetuating cycle that supplies a constant stream of young, cheap, and temporary labour to meet the demands of urban work. The study’s findings widen our theoretical understanding of men as gendered subjects, their domesticity, and their agency within the literature on labour regimes.

Decolonizing Critical Discourse Analysis Through the Negotiation of Intersectional Borders

CMS
Hellen Marquezini (University of Victoria)
This paper proposes the negotiation of intersectional borders as a decolonial approach to Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) anchored in an Afro-Latin American standpoint. We present a praxis for conducting CDA in research teams composed of authors with divergent intersectional positionalities and worldviews. Grounded in an experience of conducting a decolonial analysis of management textbooks, our approach centers the negotiation of intersectional borders as a core analytical strategy. Rather than simply acknowledging epistemic differences, we advocate for actively inhabiting the tensions they produce by (re)marking a standpoint; (re)situating knowledge production and challenging colonial reasoning. By treating the research team’s diverse positionalities as a form of creative friction zones, and by adopting an Afro-Latin American standpoint as an onto-epistemological anchor, we offer a reflexive analytical mode of meaning-making that resists epistemic erasure and hegemonic neutrality. This proposal contributes to the growing field of decolonial methodologies in organizational research by demonstrating how establishing a standpoint and negotiating intersectional borders can be mobilized as methodological tools to disrupt colonial reasoning in managerial knowledge.

Rethinking Reflexivity across Disciplinary Training, Elite Narratives, and Lived Experience

CMS
Tathagata Bhowmik (Masters' Union School of Business); Nimruji Jammulamadaka
This paper builds on Charmaz’s Constructivist Grounded Theory (CGT) to interrogate the conditions under which different aspects of subaltern participant identities are made visible in postcolonial qualitative research. While reflexivity has assumed an important position in qualitative inquiry, it remains largely confined to researchers’ social positions and often neglects the influence of disciplinary orientations and dominant cultural discourses curated by elites. We argue that participant identities are not simply discovered but co-constructed through the interplay of researchers’ positionalities, disciplinary frameworks, and historically sedimented narratives. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork with subaltern Baul folk performers and their upper-caste audiences in India, we analyze interview excerpts through distinct interpretive lenses to reveal three constructions of participant identity: conceptual (produced through theoretical categories), socially imposed (shaped by elite cultural narratives), and experienced (emerging from participants’ own articulations). Each construction privileges certain dimensions of lived subaltern experience while obscuring others, illustrating how methodological choices shape both visibility and erasure in qualitative research. By juxtaposing these identities, we move beyond additive models of intersectionality to foreground the fractured, split, and relational nature of subaltern subjecthood. This analysis underscores the need for a decolonial methodological praxis that critically interrogates the epistemic scaffolding of research and creates space for alternative ways of knowing.

Sadean Heterotopias: Desire, Ateleology, and the Dark Side of Entrepreneurial Hustling

CMS
R. Duncan M. Pelly (Fisk University); Ghazal Layeghi (Free University of Bozen-Bolzano); Andrea Caputo (University of Trento)
Dark entrepreneurial actions emerge when urgent and unorthodox behaviours occur within ambiguous and unregulated spaces. Based upon three years of firsthand observation, this study unveils Sadean heterotopic hustling to describe ways in which desire, spatial ambiguity, and hustling interact ateleologically to escalate harmful entrepreneurial practices. This is the unconventional tale of a multi-faceted entrepreneur who sequestered a vacant lot owned by a local school to sell drugs, prostitution, auto and truck repair services, lodging, and food. This layered account autoethnography focuses upon an entrepreneur whose actions mirror those of another proponent of dark entrepreneurial hustling: the Marquis de Sade. This nouveau-Sade fabulated a vacant lot into a heterotopic space rife with negative creativity and excessive hustling. The story begins with an individual hustling out of economic necessity; expanding into the creation of a separate space; and, culminating in an auto-destructive cesspool of hedonism rife with greed and unchained desire. The findings highlight the subversionary potential of entrepreneurial hustling within contested organizational spaces, and advances the understanding of the manner in which space, desire, and ateleology shape the dynamics of organizing and entrepreneurial action. The discussion explicates the entrepreneur’s behaviors, and concludes that, although his actions could be characterized as evil, he is a heroic villain of economic and regional development. This writing is significant because it introduces a counternarrative in entrepreneurship that focuses upon its potential cataclysmic and ateleological impacts. The manuscript serves as the foundation for future studies in entrepreneurship that prioritize destructive forms of hustling.

The Silences of History: Rethinking Practice Theories in Management and Organization Studies

CMS
Marcelo De Souza Bispo
I aim in this article to reconceptualize the socio-historical as a constitutive dimension of practice in Management and Organization Studies (MOS), building on authors often neglected in these debates. While practice-based research has illuminated how organizing unfolds through routines, interactions, and collective knowledgeable doings, it has often reduced history to scenery—flattening practices into presentist accounts and marginalizing questions of power, exclusion, and epistemic dependency. A parallel limitation can be observed in Organizational History, which, despite its advances in theorizing memory and uses of the past, frequently treats history as a resource mobilized by organizations rather than as a contested terrain of silences and struggles. Building on the Haitian anthropologist Michel-Rolph Trouillot and the Brazilian sociologist Alberto Guerreiro Ramos, I propose a socio-historical lens that deepens Practice Theories and broadens MOS. Trouillot’s notion of silences in the production of history shows how what is remembered and what is forgotten are effects of power, while Ramos’s sociological reduction highlights the need to re-ground analytical categories in concrete socio-historical realities rather than assuming their universality. Together, these perspectives provide a decolonial practice lens that reframes practices as historically sedimented accomplishments, shaped by struggles over legitimacy and voice. I illustrate the analytical potential of this lens by reinterpreting empirical studies in MOS, showing how silences, exclusions, and epistemic dependencies permeate organizational practices. By treating the socio-historical as constitutive of practice, I invite scholars to engage more reflexively with the histories that organize organizational life.
