Beyond Linear Paths: How Reputation Bursts Reshape Niche Enterprises’ Innovation

TIM
SHENGXI YANG (Sichuan University); Dehui Li
This study examines reputation burst among niche enterprises and their innovation effects. Unlike gradual market-driven reputation accumulation emphasized in existing theories, high-credibility external events can trigger discontinuous reputation leaps—abrupt, intense shifts generating widespread attention within short periods. We investigate how such bursts help niche enterprises overcome resource constraints and drive innovation. Analyzing 683 Chinese manufacturing niche leaders through text analysis and manual verification, we demonstrate that reputation burst significantly enhances innovation by restructuring slack resource management (absorptive and non-absorptive) and collaboration with digital-intensive and knowledge-intensive partners. Our findings advance the understanding of nonlinear reputation dynamics in high-information-asymmetry contexts and offer practical insights for reputation governance to small and medium-sized enterprises (SME) managers, policymakers and SME support organizations.

Build or Borrow? A Study on Technology Relatedness Driving the Development of Dominant Designs

TIM
Xiaoying Zhang (Central South University); Yunsheng Zhang (Central South University); Lingxiang Liao (Central South University)
In high-tech industries characterized by significant network externalities, why and how do firms choose to cooperatively develop dominant designs? Existing literature on dominant designs has primarily focused on market entry timing or exclusive competitive strategies, paying scant attention to how technology relatedness between a focal firm and its partners drives this strategic decision. Drawing on network externalities theory and the resource-based view, we construct a "technology-market" dual-dimensional analysis framework to investigate the mechanisms by which technology relatedness influences the cooperative development of dominant designs. Using a sample of 353 listed companies in China's ICT industry from 2000 to 2022, we empirically tested our theoretical hypotheses. We found that both core technology overlap and complementary technologies significantly enhance a firm's willingness to participate in the cooperative development of dominant designs, driven by increased demands for knowledge sharing and the internalization of indirect network externalities. The analysis of moderating effects reveals complex boundary conditions: directed learning ability significantly attenuates the positive relationship between core technology overlap and cooperative development, suggesting that strong internal absorptive capacity creates a "substitution effect" for external cooperation. Furthermore, user base size exerts an inverted U-shaped moderating effect on the relationship between complementary technologies and cooperative development, unveiling a dynamic logic wherein firms shift from seeking complementary synergy to pursuing ecosystem exclusivity as they cross the critical tipping point of network effects. Finally, we discuss the study's academic contributions and managerial implications regarding the shift from a competitive to a co-opetitive perspective, the deconstruction of technology relatedness, and the integration of network externalities theory.

Exploratory Organizations: Gaining Support Through Multi-Pitching

TIM
Loic Voisin (Université Savoie Mont Blanc - IREGE); Barthélemy Chollet (Grenoble Ecole de Management); Thomas Gillier (Grenoble Ecole de Management)
Pitching ideas is an essential part of innovators’ jobs in large corporations. They must convince decisions-makers to provide support, resources and validation to their projects, and research on pitching suggests that they do so essentially by reducing perceived uncertainty. This strategy, however, is counterproductive when the project is highly exploratory. A different approach is needed. In this paper, we study exploratory organizations, i.e. organizational subunits that are granted some autonomy to explore ideas irrelative to mainstream R&D formal processes and goals. Based on 15 months of field observations in a major French automotive manufacturer, we look at how the leaders of exploration units handle the challenge of promoting innovation with other tools than uncertainty reduction. We find that they create unique forms of pitching visits, meant to generate curiosity and eventually take key stakeholders to embrace uncertainty. We document these practices using notes from months of in-situ observations, discussions with several actors, and internal documents. We identify a series of practices that, instead of trying to affect decision-makers’ evaluations, focus more fundamentally on altering their evaluation criteria. This results in a switch from an economic mindset to an exploratory mindset. Those preliminary findings constitute a first step towards enlarging the theorizing of pitching beyond single ideas and extend it to the promotion of exploration activities at large.

Optimal Distinctiveness in Emerging Technology Fields: Pre-Market Discourse and Valuation

TIM
Jan-Marco Nepute (RWTH Aachen University); Torsten Oliver Salge (RWTH Aachen University)
Organizations need to distinguish themselves from their competitors in order to be successful - but at the same time, they must meet certain expectations to be seen as credible and legitimate. This is the core paradox of optimal distinctiveness (OD) theory. While OD is typically explored in stable markets, this study extends the theory to uncertain, emerging technology contexts (unstable markets) with unknown industry logics and before innovations materializes. We propose that in these “pre-market” environments, the relationship between distinctiveness and performance is not structurally predetermined but is communicatively constructed through technology discourse. Analyzing technology discourse surrounding hydrogen within around 110.000 earnings calls of the S&P 1500 over the last 25 years we find empirically that technology discourse distinctiveness exhibits no significant inverted U-shaped relationship with performance. However, the relationship changes significantly when moderating for technology discourse volume. High discourse volume flattens the inverted U-shaped curve, turning a illegitimacy discount into a neutral position. Furthermore, technology discourse ambiguity also moderates the relationship, where low ambiguity shifts the profile to neutralize the legitimacy penalty associated with high distinctiveness. We demonstrate that for emerging technologies, distinctiveness is a latent signal that yields value only when amplified by high-volume or low-ambiguity technology discourse. Thus, the “optimal” distinctiveness trade-off is not structurally determined but communicatively constructed through the strategic clarity and intensity of the organization’s narrative.

The Impact of Strategic Persistence on Innovation Quality: Moderating Role of Environmental Dynamism

TIM
Yiyun Luo (Zhejiang University); Aiqi Wu; Jingyi Lei (Zhejiang University); Xinyao Wu (Zhejiang University)
Purpose: This study examines how strategic persistence influences innovation quality in manufacturing firms and investigates the moderating roles of technological dynamism and economic policy uncertainty in this relationship. Design/methodology/approach: Drawing on organizational learning theory, we analyze 10,371 firm-year observations from Chinese listed manufacturing companies (2007-2019). Robustness checks include Tobit regression and heterogeneity analyses across firm characteristics. Findings: Strategic persistence positively affects innovation quality, with this relationship strengthened under both technological dynamism and economic policy uncertainty. The moderating effects are particularly pronounced for younger, smaller, and non-state-owned firms that rely on environmental stability to compensate for resource constraints. Mature, larger, and state-owned firms show consistent positive effects regardless of environmental conditions. Originality/value: This study challenges the conventional negative framing of strategic persistence as organizational rigidity, demonstrating instead that maintaining strategic focus enhances innovation quality through sustained organizational learning. The findings reveal that environmental dynamism counterintuitively strengthens rather than weakens the value of strategic persistence, particularly for resource-constrained firms, offering new insights for managing the tension between strategic flexibility and persistence in dynamic environments.
