Investigating How Overqualified Employees Influence Leader-Rated Performance

MOC
Chao Ma (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Zhou Jiang (RMIT University); SIJIA ZHAO
Perceived overqualification has emerged as a prevalent phenomenon in contemporary organizational management. Evidence suggests that employees who feel overqualified can leverage their capabilities to enhance work performance. However, despite their efforts, performance evaluations are typically conducted by supervisors. In this context, we argue that employees who feel overqualified may strategically navigate the dynamics of power within dyadic work relationships to poisitively influence how a leader rates their performance. Drawing upon the theoretical frameworks of power dependence and dominance complementarity, we argue that employees who feel overqualified are inclined towards a strategy of deceptive help-seeking from their leaders, particularly when driven by a covert competition motive. This deceptive help-seeking is anticipated to foster leaders’ sense of power, which leads them to rate the focal employees more favorably. We conducted a multi-source and multi-wave field study (N = 552) which provides support for our hypotheses. This research speaks to a nuanced relationship between perceived overqualification and performance evaluation, highlighting the role of power manipulation in shaping supervisor-rated performance outcomes.

Inspirational or Demotivational: Experimental Evidence on the Impacts of Role Models

MOC
Aurelia Di (UCLA Anderson School of Management)
Role models’ success levels may shape their effectiveness: Are more successful role models always more motivating, or can those with moderate success also be valuable? Using a randomized controlled trial with 1,920 students across five Chinese middle schools, this study varies the portrayed success level of the same role models and examines their impacts on academic performance and mental health. Two months after the intervention, students exposed to higher-achieving role models improved test scores by 0.07-0.18 standard deviations yet, on average, experienced no improvements in mental health. Conversely, those exposed to moderately achieving role models did not receive any test score gains but were 12.0 and 9.3 percentage points (=28.8% and 26.6%) less likely to report feeling depressed and stress, respectively, suggesting that there is no single "best" role model type. Given that role-model success is relative, I also examine whether treatment effects differ across students’ baseline achievement levels. Higher-achieving role models improve low-performing girls’ academic outcomes but increase their mental health issues, as these girls increased effort but found their improved performance still falling short of their elevated aspirations. This paper highlights a design trade-off: role models’ success levels should be chosen to match program goals, and evaluations should cover both performance and well-being of the audience.

CEO Locality and Corporate Extraregional Expansion: An Attentional Geo-Fences Perspective

MOC
Xiaoyang Deng (Wuhan University); Yongqing Ye (Wuhan University); Tao Wang (Wuhan University)
This study introduces the concept of attentional geo-fences (AGFs)—cognitive boundaries rooted in executives’ geographic origins that channel managerial attention toward localized spatial domains. Building on the attention-based view (ABV), we propose that CEOs with localized geographic backgrounds (i.e., born or raised in the firm’s home province) are less likely to pursue extraregional expansion due to path-dependent attentional inertia within their AGFs. We further contend that this effect is moderated by institutional factors (regional dialect diversity and marketization) and CEO characteristics (international experience and gender). Using a longitudinal sample of publicly listed Chinese firms and multi-destination logit models, we find robust support for our hypotheses. Furthermore, we develop multidimensional text-based measures to capture AGFs and prove its role as an underlying mechanism. Taken together, our findings extend the attention-based view by identifying the antecedent boundaries of attention and provide a cognitive micro-foundation for firms’ geographic expansion decisions.

Cognitive Frames and Dynamic Complex Search Amid the Federal Reserve’s Financial Crisis Response

MOC
Joosef Valli (Aalto University); Pooria Assadi (California State University-Sacramento)
The challenge of dynamically searching for solutions to complex problems lies in integrating multiple, interdependent choices into a coherent whole over time. While simulation research has advanced theory on dynamic complex search, we have little empirical accounts of how those mechanisms are enacted, particularly in high-stakes group deliberations. Drawing on qualitative analysis of the Federal Reserve’s monetary policy committee meeting transcripts, we analyze how three Reserve Bank presidents, who navigated multiple interdependent policy dimensions during the 2008-2009 financial crisis, employed technical-rationality and appropriateness cognitive frames to present, justify, and revise complex policy proposals to work out a coherent response. We trace the temporal evolution of proposals across meetings and map proposal language to subsequent Federal Reserve statements. Our findings reveal that integrative search processes in group settings (1) progress from abstract policy options to more detailed, concrete proposals; (2) foster convergence amongst cognitively complex decision-makers in both problem framing and proposal content; and (3) influence group-level outcomes by exposing all facets of the decision-making problem to the rest of the group, even as individual complex proposals are not fully adopted. In doing so, we advance understanding of how cognitive frames enable collective problem solving in dynamic, interdependent decision contexts with implications for bridging simulation-based models of complex search with sensemaking and framing perspectives in organization studies.

From Scarce Resources to Resourcing Spirals: A Theory of Mindful Resourcing

MOC
Ravi S. Kudesia (Temple University); Sara Jansen Perry (Baylor University); Cristina Rubino (California State University); Ari Malka (California State University-Northridge)
People in organizations often face demands in excess of their resources. Psychological theories of resources reflect this scarcity, emphasizing how people conserve or balance resources. While they acknowledge that interventions like mindfulness training can create resources, these theories struggle to explain how, as they assume that resources are necessarily scarce. In this study, we introduce a theory of mindful resourcing. Rather than reducing mindfulness to a scarce psychological resource that a person has, it reveals mindfulness to be a skillful practice that a person does to enhance their agency to create resources. Mindful resourcing theory shows how mindfulness skills form a foundation for resourcing—the ability to create and enact resources—and how resourcing reciprocally enhances mindfulness skills, such that the practices of mindfulness and resourcing intertwine to become mutually sustaining and supporting. We develop this theory across a three-phase research design that (1) introduces a conceptual model which sequences how these practices intertwine and (2) uses this sequencing to surface the otherwise implicit design structure of mindfulness trainings, before (3) abductively building theory on how people enact and develop competence in mindful resourcing from longitudinal qualitative data of 98 non-profit employees undergoing training. Our theory-building discovers the critical role of resourcing spirals—showing how employees create and enact resources by skillfully assembling spirals and recursively expanding those spirals into longer and more impactful sequences as they gain competence. Taken together, our study illuminates the understudied human activities by which people not only create and enact resources but learn to do so more mindfully.
