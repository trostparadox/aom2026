Who Gets to Learn? Adversity, Equity, and Wellbeing in Management Education

Pennsylvania Convention Center — 106B
MED

People

Moderator: Barjinder Singh Elon University

Session overview

Management education claims to develop all students, but whose flourishing is actually centered? This session examines the experiences of learners and educators who face structural, psychological, and existential barriers: students with disabilities navigating psychological safety dynamics, Afghan learners sustaining motivation when educational pathways are existentially blocked, underrepresented aspiring entrepreneurs asking who "futures literacy" is really designed for, and educators teaching management under the psychological weight of armed conflict. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, expectancy-value theory, and equity-centered frameworks, these papers make a compelling case for a more just, compassionate, and humanly honest management education.

From Psychological Safety to Collaborative Learning: Roles of Self-Efficacy and Disability Status

MED
Barjinder Singh (Elon University); Sarthak Singh (University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee); Ruhaannvir Singh (Apex Friendship High School)
Understanding the importance of psychological safety in determining student learning, our study examines the mechanisms that clarify how and when psychological safety influences collaborative learning in an educational setting. Grounded in Conservation of Resources (COR) and social cognitive theories, the present research proposes that the relationship between psychological safety and collaborative learning is mediated by self-efficacy, with student disability status as a moderator of the psychological safety – self-efficacy relationship. To test these propositions, we conducted a survey-based field study by collecting data from 564 undergraduate students at a large Midwestern US university. The results show that self-efficacy is the mechanism through which psychological safety promotes collaborative learning. Moreover, the study provides support for the proposed moderating effect of disability status, such that the positive influence of psychological safety on self-efficacy is greater for students with a disability than for students without any disability. By combining COR and social cognitive theories, the present study contributes to the understanding of the resource-based processes underlying collaborative learning. Furthermore, our findings offer practical guidance to educators and educational institutions aiming to create safer, more inclusive, and effective learning environments.

Beyond Stress Reduction: A Dual-Path Framework of Student Wellbeing in Management Education

MED
Ajoy Kumar Dey (IILM UNIVERSITY, GURUGRAM); Sachin Tyagi (Goa Institute of Management); Shreya Mishra (Birla Institute of Management Technology)
Student wellbeing has become a critical concern in management education, yet dominant approaches remain narrowly focused on regulating stress and mitigating psychological strain. While such regulation-centred strategies are necessary, they provide an incomplete explanation of how students sustain effective functioning and growth under persistent academic and career-related pressures. Drawing on interdisciplinary wellbeing and higher education research, this paper develops a dual-path wellbeing framework that conceptualises student wellbeing as a dynamic process unfolding through four phases: pressure, response, dual-path processes, and functional stability. The framework integrates a regulation pathway, which restores equilibrium by managing stress responses, and a development pathway, which builds engagement, meaning, psychological resources, and supportive relationships. By synthesising constructs and operationalisations from prior empirical studies, the paper demonstrates how similar pressures can lead to divergent wellbeing trajectories depending on the availability and interaction of these pathways. The proposed framework advances existing theory by reframing student wellbeing as sustained functional stability rather than symptom absence, and by explicitly aligning wellbeing processes with the pedagogical and institutional goals of management education. The framework offers a rigorous foundation for future empirical research and provides actionable guidance for designing integrated wellbeing strategies in business schools.

When All Hope Seems Lost: Sustaining Learning Motivation In The Absence Of Educational Pathways

MED
David Nawrath (Technical University of Munich); Hasina Jalal (University of Pittsburgh); Froozan Danish (University of Toronto); Abdul Qahar Sarwari (University of Malaya)
Although motivation research explains engagement through expectancy, value, and need satisfaction, it remains unclear how learners persist when education’s utility is stripped away and the costs of studying become psychologically, socially, and materially high. We ask: How do learners sustain learning motivation when formal educational pathways are blocked? Drawing on expectancy–value theory, self-determination theory, and hope theory, we conducted an inductive qualitative study of Afghan women pursuing business learning under educational prohibition. Across 63 interviews, 24 longitudinal diaries, and we triangulated with archival documents, we develop a process model of Spirals of Hope. We find that motivation turns on shifting “hope conditions” that make learning either costly or workable. Rupture shocks, visibility/expectation thresholds, and capacity losses precipitate a downward spiral characterized by withdrawal, rumination, and learner-identity threat. In contrast, feasibility windows, proof-ready tasks, and safe witnessing channels trigger an upward spiral in which women scale hope to near-term goals, enact micro-agency, produce countable traces of progress, secure low-risk legitimation, and stabilize commitment through continuity of learner identity. We discuss how this model extends motivation and psychological-capital perspectives by specifying mechanisms of persistence under foreclosed utility, and we outline implications for educators designing feasible, identity-affirming learning in uncertain environments.

Entrepreneurial Futures – Why and for Whom?

MED
Julia Voegele (MCI - The Entrepreneurial School); Stephanie Elizabeth Raible (University of Delaware)
This study contributes to innovative teaching and learning pedagogy by examining a Futures Literacy Lab (FLL) as a future-oriented educational intervention in entrepreneurship education. In times of uncertainty, entrepreneurship education equips learners to engage productively with ambiguity, experiment with new ideas, and develop innovative solutions, empowering them actively shape the future of the economy and society. This qualitative research explores how learners’ perceived future images relate to entrepreneurial intentions within a structured futures literacy learning environment. We recruited 48 participants, primarily from Europe, with a focus on groups underrepresented in entrepreneurship: women and young adults. Through pedagogical engagement with probable, possible, and desirable futures, the FLL enables participants to surface and critically reflect on their assumptions, perceived constraints, and aspirations regarding entrepreneurship. The findings show that future imaginaries are closely connected to entrepreneurial intentions, both by revealing barriers—such as limited resources, social expectations, and risk perceptions—and by uncovering latent possibilities for entrepreneurial and socially oriented action. Importantly, the intervention demonstrates how futures-oriented pedagogy can foster reflexivity, agency, and confidence in engaging with uncertain futures. By applying futures literacy as an innovative pedagogical approach in entrepreneurship education, this study contributes to management education research by rethinking entrepreneurship as a learning space for futures thinking, agency, and responsible leadership. The findings offer actionable insights for educators seeking to design impactful, future-oriented learning environments that encourage learners to envision and proactively shape their futures, while navigating uncertainty and contributing to sustainable innovation and societal transformation.

Teaching in War-Impacted Classrooms: Psychological Issues, Strategies, and Educators’ Experience

MED
Yevgen Bogodistov (MCI - The Entrepreneurial School); Amanda Budde-Sung (U.S. Air Force Academy)
Armed conflict profoundly disrupts higher education, intensifying the job stress experienced by faculty members. This study examines the work-related stressors faced by professors of management and management-adjacent subjects during times of war, with particular attention to issues of care, self-care, emotional exhaustion, and the deterioration of physical infrastructure. Drawing on the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model, we analyze how wartime conditions simultaneously amplify job demands while constraining key professional and personal resources. Based on a qualitative study, the findings challenge a purely defensive interpretation of resource conservation. Rather than distancing themselves from stressors to preserve depleted resources, many professors report increased involvement in student support, adaptive course design, and community-building efforts. The study extends the JD-R model by illustrating how prosocial motivation and ethical commitment shape faculty responses to extreme contextual stressors, with implications for educational strategy and faculty support during crises.
