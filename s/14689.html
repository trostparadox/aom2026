From Ideation to Action: Risk-Taking in the Entrepreneurial Process of Japanese Students

Session overview

Despite expanded entrepreneurship education, entrepreneurial engagement among Japanese university students remains substantially inactive compared to other developed countries. This study examines the psychological mechanisms that translate entrepreneurial intention into action, addressing the gap between latent interest and actual behavior. Focusing on Individual Entrepreneurial Orientation (IEO)—innovativeness, proactiveness, and risk-taking—we analyzed data from 1,344 students across over 30 universities. The results reveal that IEO dimensions influence the entrepreneurial process differently by stage. While innovativeness and proactiveness predict initial interest and short-term intentions, they are insufficient for execution. Crucially, risk-taking emerges as the essential bridge translating vague intentions into long-term commitment and nascent entrepreneurship. This highlights a specific challenge in the Japanese context, where high perceived costs of failure within traditional career paths inhibit the transition from thinking to doing. Practically, these findings suggest that education should shift from early-stage ideation to fostering psychological resilience and risk management. Theoretically, this study contributes to the intention-action transition literature by illustrating how culturally embedded career norms shape the psychological antecedents of nascent entrepreneurial behavior, demonstrating that risk-taking is the decisive factor for execution in risk-averse environments.
