Shame at Work: Daily Events, Stress, and the Moderating Role of Core Self-Evaluations

Friday, Jul 31 2026 · 8:30AM–9:30AM
Philadelphia Marriott Downtown — Poster Wall 9

People

Authors: Yeon Ka Kim San Jose State University; John Kammeyer-Mueller University of Minnesota

Session overview

This study proposes a model in which daily identity-threatening work events (e.g., performance failure, interpersonal conflict, or moral failings) shape employees’ stress experiences through shame, and how core self-evaluations (CSE) buffer individuals’ reactions to such events. Using an experience sampling design with 151 working adults, we test a multilevel model in which daily identity-threatening events predict within-person fluctuations in job stress via shame, with CSE moderating the event–shame relationship. Results indicate that shame mediates the relationship between identity-threatening events and stress. Consistent with our model, CSE further operates as a cross-level moderator: employees lower in trait CSE are more likely to translate identity-threatening events into heightened shame, whereas those higher in trait CSE exhibit attenuated shame responses to the same events. This study identifies shame as a critical affective mechanism linking daily identity-threatening events to stress and highlights CSE as an important moderator shaping employees’ vulnerability to identity-threatening experiences at work.
