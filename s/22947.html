Not All Employee Volunteering is Equal: The Benefits of Corporate vs. Personal Employee Volunteering

ODC
Florencio F. Portocarrero (London School of Economics and Political Science); Katerina Gonzalez (Suffolk University); Aaron Aujla (London School of Economics and Political Science)
Do corporate-sponsored volunteering initiatives provide greater benefits to organizations and their employees compared to when employees engage in personal volunteering? Drawing on organizational identity theory, we assess the relative impact of corporate versus personal volunteering on organizational outcomes (e.g., organizational attitudes, organizational behaviors) and personal outcomes (e.g., perceptions of impact, well-being). We test our hypotheses across four complementary studies: (1) an archival study using nationally representative U.S. Census data, (2) a field study of volunteers engaged in a social entrepreneurship program, (3) a multi-level meta-analysis using data from 87 independent samples with over 105,000 individuals, and (4) an organizational-level study of 509 aspiring B Corporations. Across the first three studies, we find that employee engagement in corporate-sponsored volunteering is more strongly associated with favorable organizational outcomes, compared to personal volunteering. Although we generally find similar effects for personal outcomes, the relationship is more nuanced and depends on the specific outcome. In the fourth study, at a different level of analysis, we show that the beneficial effects of corporate volunteering can extend beyond the individual level, finding that organizations’ corporate volunteering efforts are positively associated with retention. Overall, these findings highlight the unique potential of corporate volunteering to foster goodwill, organizational commitment, and personal growth among employees, offering both theoretical and practical implications for the design of corporate social impact initiatives and strategic use as organizational interventions.

Playful Work Design and Proactive Vitality : Energizing Creative Performance via Informal Learning

ODC
Muhammad Qamar Zia; Dr Muhammad Sufyan Ramish (Dow University of health sciences)
With growing concern about informal workplace learning in the hospitality sector, this study aims to empirically investigate the drivers and consequences of informal workplace learning. Building on the Job Demands-Resource theory, the data of 389 frontline employees and their supervisors from different regions of Pakistan were used to examine the proposed hypotheses. The findings revealed that both proactive approaches—playful work design (PWD) and proactive vitality management (PVM)—are significant predictors of informal workplace learning, which in turn fosters creative performance. The study also identified informal workplace learning as an effective mediator between these drivers and their outcomes. Moreover, the results exhibited that high work pressure is a job demand which diminishes participation in informal learning, even when job resources are available. This study is among the pioneers in the hospitality context, providing evidence on the mechanisms of informal workplace learning between drivers and outcomes, while making a novel contribution by examining the moderating role of work pressure.

Intrapreneurial Sensemaking: How Employees React to Positively Perceived Change

ODC
Marika Platz (University of Münster); Julia Backmann (University of Münster); Matthias Felix Sinnemann (University of Münster); Felix Hoch (University College Dublin); Johannes Hueby (University of Münster); Johann Nils Foege
This study examines how employees make sense of and sustain positively appraised organizational change over time. We do so through an in-depth, longitudinal study of six organizations piloting a worktime reduction without a salary cut, drawing on 92 interviews conducted across three waves. We trace how employees engaged in what we term intrapreneurial sensemaking, i.e., an employeeled process in which sensemaking goes beyond shared understanding towards agentic change realization. Rather than merely interpreting a new initiative, employees used ongoing interpretation and enactment to shape whether and how the change could be sustained in practice. While prior sensemaking research has largely focused on responses to crisis or disruption, we examine sensemaking in the context of demanding yet positive change. We show how opportunity sustains sensemaking over time. Specifically, employees initially engaged in cognitive and task crafting to redefine their roles, routines, and performance expectations, and subsequently expanded their sensemaking to include interdependencies with colleagues through relational and collective crafting. Through this scaling from individual to collective enactment, sensemaking became a mechanism for embedding new coordination practices that stabilize change. We further show that management played a pivotal role early on by framing the change positively and providing enabling conditions, but largely receded thereafter, as intrapreneurial sensemaking shifted the change from a top-down direction to a peer-driven, bottom-up endeavor.

Reflection and Performance at Work: The Roles of Unlearning and Employee Thriving

ODC
Sunyoung Park (Louisiana State University); Eun-Jee Kim (Kyonggi University)
The purpose of this study is to examine the relationships among critical reflection, unlearning, thriving at work, and work performance. Responses from 368 employees were collected and analyzed using structural equation modeling to test the proposed hypotheses and assess the relationships among the key constructs. The findings revealed that critical reflection has a significant positive effect on employees’ experiences of thriving at work, unlearning, and work performance. Furthermore, both unlearning and thriving at work directly enhance employees’ work performance and serve as mediators in the relationship between critical reflection and work performance. Overall, this study advances research on critical reflection by positioning it as a key antecedent of unlearning, thriving at work, and performance, and by clarifying the cognitive and psychological pathways through which critical reflection enhances employee performance.

Sustained Change Engagement: A Process of Energy Expression and Energy Restitution

ODC
Jill W. Paine (IE Business School); Inger G. Stensaker (Norwegian School of Economics)
How can organizations sustain employee engagement when change is no longer episodic but ongoing? We address this question by challenging a pervasive assumption in change research: that effective transformation requires continuous engagement and uninterrupted effort. We reconceptualize sustained change engagement as a dynamic process of energy expression and energy restitution, in which recovery is not disengagement but a generative phase that enables future engagement. Building a multilevel process model, we theorize how individual-level energy cycles aggregate into organizational capacity for change over time. We identify construal shifting as a central mechanism through which engagement oscillates between execution-focused action and higher-level reflection, learning, and meaning-making. Our theory explains why organizations with similar resources and change architectures may diverge in their ability to sustain transformation and why persistent pressure for effort can undermine long-term capacity. By foregrounding human energy as a temporal and renewable resource, this paper advances change theory and offers a new lens for understanding engagement, capacity, and endurance in contexts of continuous change.
