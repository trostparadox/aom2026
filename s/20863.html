Civil Society Organizations and the Moralization of Markets Across Countries

Authors: Gregory Jackson King's College London; Sumrin Kalia Freie Universität Berlin

Session overview

Civil society has become a central arena in which markets are governed, contested, and moralized. Across countries, civil society organizations (CSOs) pressure firms in markedly different ways: some collaborate with business to reform practices and embed social standards; others mobilize protest, boycott, or legal challenge; still others politicize markets by framing corporations as symbols of moral corruption or elite domination. Movements such as #MeToo, Black Lives Matter, climate activism, and populist anti-corporate mobilization illustrate that civil society does not merely constrain or enable markets, but actively reshapes how economic power is justified and contested. Yet existing research lacks a comparative framework explaining why similar forms of activism emerge as reformist in some countries and confrontational or populist in others. This paper advances a configurational theory of civil society–market relations. We conceptualize civil society as a heterogeneous field structured by the interaction of two foundational dimensions: patterns of network embeddedness (brokerage versus closure) and normative orientation (inclusive versus exclusive norms). Crossing these dimensions yields four ideal-typical configurations of civic engagement—universalist, communitarian, particularist, and populist—each associated with distinct ways of pressuring and engaging markets. We argue that pro- and anti-business orientations are not inherent organizational traits, but patterned outcomes of how CSOs are structurally and normatively situated within national civil societies. Empirically, the paper draws on World Values Survey (Wave 7) data to map civil society configurations across countries, using attitudes toward market institutions, economic values, business ethics, and repertoires of activism. The analysis reveals systematic cross-national variation in how civil society moralizes and contests markets.
