Tocqueville's Contributions to Understanding Social Theory and Corporate Citizenship

MH
Robert S. D'Intino (Rowan University)
Alexis de Tocqueville’s distinctive ideas on democracy are presented emphasizing how his ideas impacted and elucidated traditional social theory constructs, and how this knowledge can contribute to understanding corporate citizenship. Tocqueville provides an enlightening perspective on the foundational social theories of individualism and communitarianism, theories employed as a scaffold to increase our understanding of democratic civic participation and corporate citizenship policies and activities. Tocqueville greatly admired how democratic society advanced personal and community liberty. However, he was concerned that the new individualism he observed in America contained the potential to reduce the indispensable vitality of civil society, democratic governance, and competitive markets. Furthermore, he thought that a loss of community identity and cohesion could result in democracy's eventual destruction, replaced by political and economic tyranny. The social theories of individualism and communitarianism are described, explained, and contrasted to produce a theoretical foundation for Tocqueville's observations and conclusions about American democratic citizenship. He navigated a balanced approach between opposing ideological views on achieving and sustaining individual liberty and sustain a functioning civil society. Tocqueville's writings about individual democratic participation challenges transitions to explain and appreciate the apparent contradictions within corporate citizenship's strategic goals. Global corporations have recently expressed inclusive statements concerning their responsibilities to the people and localities where they conduct business. Corporate citizenship is a construct comprising divergent purposes pursuing business profits and owner wealth while simultaneously benefiting society and the planet. Referencing Tocqueville’s concerns about democracy's viability to survive its internal contradictions, a unique perspective is presented on the emergence of corporate citizenship and the ways corporations can achieve business profits and social purposes. Limitations in applying his ideas are discussed, concluding with Tocqueville's conceptual contributions to contemporary social theory and corporate citizenship.

ManagementThought OverTime: Historical Review of Theoretical Foundations and Contemporary Implications

MH
Minakshi Middha (The Research Beacon); Saed Adnan Mustafa
: Management thought has evolved through a rich and complex intellectual trajectory shaped by changing economic, social, and organizational conditions. Despite this legacy, contemporary management research often privileges novelty over historical grounding, resulting in theoretical fragmentation and limited engagement with foundational ideas. This paper provides a historical integrative review of management thought, tracing its development from classical foundations through behavioral, systems, contingency, and strategic perspectives, and examining their contemporary implications. Drawing on seminal works and recent advances in management and organizational history scholarship, the review adopts a historical–integrative methodology that combines chronological synthesis with thematic analysis. Rather than treating management theories as discrete or superseded paradigms, the paper conceptualizes management thought as a cumulative and context-dependent repertoire of ideas that are continuously reinterpreted in response to emerging challenges. The review demonstrates how core managerial concerns such as efficiency, authority, motivation, adaptation, and strategic direction persist across time, albeit in evolving forms. Building on this analysis, the paper proposes an integrative historical framework that links major periods of management thought with dominant managerial logics and contemporary organizational applications, including digital transformation, governance, and sustainability. The paper contributes to management history scholarship by clarifying theoretical continuities and discontinuities, demonstrating the relevance of historical analysis for contemporary theory building, and outlining a future research agenda that positions management history as a central resource for understanding and addressing modern organizational challenges. Keywords: Management history; Management thought; Organizational theory; Historical review; Contemporary implications

A Swindle-Based Management Model: Evidence from Accounting Practices in French Railways (1883–1914)

MH
Mohamed Ali Dakkam (Lorraine University); Cédric Poivret (Gustave Eiffel University)
This study analyses the accounting-based management model implemented in the railway sector following the 1883 conventions between French railway companies and the government. This pivotal period in the construction of the third French railway network was marked by economic crisis and political tensions. A swindle-based management model emerged during this period, shaped by the interaction between a government lacking the financial resources to build the new railway network and railway companies willing to participate in the project while shifting the “unpredictable” portion of construction costs onto the State Our study draws on Flichy’s socio-technical framework, which distinguishes between a framework of operation (accounting knowledge and techniques) and a framework of use (actual practices, diversions, and strategies), in order to understand how the French government of the time and railway companies used accounting as a tool of political and legal negotiation. The analysis shows that railway companies relied on accounting to justify managerial choices, conceal certain costs, and steer political debate. Moreover, railway accounting became a means of legitimizing public policies and reflected the power relations between railway companies and the State. Far from playing the role of a management or control tool often attributed to it in the literature, accounting primarily served strategic and political purposes.

The 1828 Hicksite Schism: An Organizational Ecology Comparison

MH
Jay J. Janney (University of Dayton); Della Stanley-Green (University of Dayton); Terry Lee Amburgey (University of Toronto)
Within Indiana Yearly Meeting we examine the 1828 Hicksite schism, which led to the formation of a new Yearly Meeting. Using Institutional Theory, we test the survival rates of 238 monthly meetings associated with the Hicksite split (we compare three survival rate models, and employ a Weibull distribution). For Orthodox, they appear to engage more in mimetic isomorphism with other protestant denominations, while Hicksite appeared to engage in more resource partitioning.. Of those who remain within Indiana Yearly Meeting there are both more survivals and closures, leading to a smaller survival rate. We find two different institutional approaches at work. We did not find retention of the meetinghouse to be statistically significant. We observed an intriguing phenomena, a variant of institutional drag where some monthly meetings were laid down, then were revised, enjoying greater survival rates.
