Climate-Induced Racial Pay Gaps: How do Firms Adapt to Climate Change Through Pay Reallocation

STR
Luis Ballesteros (Boston University); Xia Li (London Business School); Leandro Nardi (HEC Paris)
This study examines how organizations adapt to climate-related disasters through within-firm pay allocation. We geolocate large formal business establishments in Brazil and severe floods, storms, and landslides affecting them from 2003 to 2017. Using Callaway–Sant’Anna’s staggered difference-in-differences design, we find that within-firm pay gaps between white and nonwhite employees increase by about 22% following climate disasters. These effects grow over time and scale systematically with disaster intensity. We explore potential mechanisms and find evidence consistent with a central role for managerial decisions made under constrained firm resources and reduced local labor supply in the aftermath of disasters. Average compensation rises by roughly 5%, driven primarily by increases for white employees, thereby suggesting that firms pay a post-disaster premium but allocate it unequally across racial groups. We find no evidence that the widening pay gap reflects changes in skill composition, nor that it is driven by risk reallocation through shifts toward more precarious labor contracts.

Red Zodiac Years, Green Investor Signals: A Dynamic Upper Echelons Model of Firm Climate Innovation

STR
Siqi Luo; Sicheng Luo (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Eric W. K. Tsang (The University of Texas at Dallas)
How do firms respond to attentive green investors’ drive for climate mitigation innovation (CMI)? We theorize that the answer depends on a corporate leader’s time varying cultural psychology. Integrating upper echelons, institutional, and cultural psychology perspectives, we propose that the Chinese zodiac year superstition—widely believed to bring misfortune but thought to be offset by fortune-enhancing prosocial acts—alters how board chairs translate green investor attention (GIA) into CMI. Using a panel dataset of Chinese listed firms from 2008 to 2023, we find that GIA is positively associated with CMI on average. However, when the chair is in their zodiac year, the GIA-CMI association weakens, contrary to the competing positive-moderation hypothesis. We also find this moderating effect is heterogeneous, becoming non significant for chairs with academic backgrounds and for globally oriented boards, but stronger when prior firm performance is inferior and in regions with stronger Taoism based religious environments. The results advance a dynamic, state contingent view of upper echelons theory, challenge the assumption that investor attention is often catalytic, and connect informal institutions to the micro foundations of green innovation. Practically, they imply that engagement timing, de risking instruments, and board composition shape the productivity of green capital in driving climate technologies.

Grounds for Adaptation: Resource Allocation following Climate-Induced Supply Disruptions

STR
May-Anne Very (IESE Business School)
Climate change poses fundamental threats to firms' supply chains, yet we know little about how climate disruptions shape core strategic decisions. This paper examines how firms allocate resources when climate shocks affect their sourcing regions. Studying the U.S. coffee industry from 2004-2019, where geographic provenance constitutes a primary source of differentiation, I construct a novel dataset linking Nielsen data covering 14,778 products from 887 firms across more than 30 coffee-producing countries with climate event records. I exploit within-firm variation in climate shock exposure across portfolio countries to identify how climate-induced supply disruptions shape product introduction decisions. The findings provide evidence consistent with strategic adaptation through portfolio-level resource reallocation. The mechanisms underlying these patterns reflect the interplay between relationship-specific investments, resource constraints, and institutional governance structures. This paper contributes to emerging research on firm-level climate adaptation by examining how climate shocks shape firms' core strategic choices. It advances resource allocation theory by demonstrating how supply-side disruptions external to firm boundaries drive resource redeployment across portfolios of interfirm relationships.

Pricing Carbon, Transforming Energy: How Market-Based Mechanism Revalues Energy Technologies

STR
Narae Lee
We examine how cap-and-trade programs influence industrial facilities’ adoption of combined heat and power (CHP) systems, costly and architecturally disruptive technology. We theorize that carbon pricing embeds continuous marginal costs into firms’ core operations, transforming CHP from an economically unattractive efficiency technology into a strategically valuable investment. Using a facility-level panel of more than 4,700 U.S. industrial plants from 2011 to 2023 and exploiting the introduction of California’s cap-and-trade program, we employ difference-in-differences and matching designs to identify causal effects. We find that regulated facilities significantly increase CHP capacity following cap-and-trade implementation. This effect is stronger for facilities with higher emissions intensity, limited relocation flexibility, and greater exposure to climate-induced electricity grid disruptions. Consistent with the proposed mechanism, increased CHP capacity is associated with lower stationary greenhouse gas emissions and greater use of waste-to-energy recovery. Our findings advance research on non-market strategy, climate risk, and innovation by showing how market-based regulation can induce fundamental operational reconfiguration by revaluing capital-intensive efficiency technologies.

Silent Yet Sincere: CEO Humility and Climate-Risk Hushing

STR
Yuanhao Ciaran Li (Indiana University Bloomington)
This study examines why some firms with high exposure to physical climate hazards under-communicate these risks in their investor disclosures, focusing on the role of CEO personality. We build on upper echelons theory and research on strategic silence to propose that CEO humility can paradoxically increase "climate-risk hushing," the gap between a firm’s climate risk exposure and its climate risk discussion. We test our framework using an archival panel of U.S. publicly listed firms from 2009 to 2019, combining a text-based measure of CEO humility with novel measures of firm-specific climate risk exposure and climate-risk disclosure. Our analyses show that firms led by more humble CEOs exhibit significantly larger exposure–talk gaps, supporting the baseline hypothesis. However, this effect is mitigated and even reversed when the CEO has prior sustainability-related experience, and it is amplified when the firm’s environmental performance deteriorates. These findings reveal a nuanced micro-foundation of climate risk governance: humility can curb risk disclosure in the absence of domain expertise or under adverse performance conditions.
