Filling Governance Voids: How Institutional Custodians Coordinate Amid Regulatory Collapse

OMT
Keiondre McAllister (University of Oregon); Alan D. Meyer (University of Oregon)
This study advances a mechanism-based theory of how institutional custodians fill "governance voids" following the collapse of centralized authority. While institutional research often explores stable systems, we focus on systemic ruptures—periods of discontinuous, multi-level change where established logics are destabilized and regulatory infrastructures fragment. When formal bureaucratic authority fails, how do field inhabitants maintain coordination and coherence? Using U.S. intercollegiate athletics as a paradigmatic case, we investigate how institutional custodians—actors tasked with safeguarding traditions, identities, and values—navigate the fragmentation of the NCAA's regulatory infrastructure. Through grounded theory analysis of Faculty Athletic Representatives (FARs), we identify four transformational mechanisms through which custodians substitute inhabited governance for bureaucratic authority: (1) Story-Based Sensemaking, (2) Relational Coordination Through Dialogic Governance, (3) Structural Capability Building, and (4) Normative Boundary Work. Our findings demonstrate that custodians do not merely enact or maintain existing institutions; they actively construct compensatory governance infrastructure—architecting interpretive, relational, and moral scaffolds that render a chaotic field navigable when foundational rules have collapsed. This shifts coordination from enforcing hierarchical decrees to fostering inhabited mechanisms grounded in narrative reasoning, interpersonal trust, distributed capabilities, and defended values.

Firm Resilience in the Wake of Collective Violence: The Double-Edged Effect of Firm-Community Align

OMT
Romain Boulongne; Desiree F. Pacheco; Pascual Berrone (IESE Business School)
This study examines how community alignment—the socio-demographic and cultural alignment between a firm and its local community—affects firm resilience in volatile contexts. Drawing on data from the 2005 French riots, we find that community alignment mitigates the negative impact of collective violence intensity on short-term resilience by securing support from community stakeholders. Yet, the very alignment with community stakeholders that secures short-term gains constrains strategic adaptability, as external stakeholder perceptions become adversely affected, ultimately decreasing long-term resilience. These findings provide evidence of a paradox: deeper alignment with the local community offers short-term benefits while creating vulnerabilities over the long run. Our results highlight the complex temporal trade-offs firms face when balancing local integration with the need for sustained adaptability in socially volatile environments.

Governing Mega-Science: Socio-Symbolic Work in Transnational Multi-Stakeholder Collaboration

OMT
Weiting Zheng (UNSW Sydney); Patricia Margaret Kelly (University of Canberra); Mary-Anne Williams (UNSW Sydney); Soniya Rijal (The University of Sydney)
How do large, transnational multi-stakeholder collaborations progress when no actor can command compliance and stakeholder interests diverge? We address this puzzle with a longitudinal qualitative study of the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) Observatory, a multi-decade, “mega-science” collaboration spanning scientists, state funders, and host country communities across more than 16 countries. Anchored in a socio-symbolic work perspective, we analyze several decision junctures where cooperation could have plausibly fractured. Using multi-source archives and elite interviews, our preliminary findings show that collaboration is advanced and maintained through various forms of socio-symbolic work enacted across key collaborating parties (scientific leaders and funding agency representatives), traveling through forums and artifacts to produce workable settlements that carry forward. We aim to develop a process model of collaboration maintenance that shifts scholarly attention from collaboration “enablers” to “micro-processes” at decision junctures, and clarifies coalitional agency in which leadership shifts across principals and episodes, ultimately leading to escalated commitment and governance formalization. The study contributes to research on governance of multi-stakeholder collaboration and offers implications for organizing and sustaining grand-challenge collaborations.

From Shareholder to Stakeholder Governance: Government Mediation in the Diffusion of Share Buybacks

OMT
Chien-Hao Chen (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Jiwook Jung (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
The present study examines how governments shape the domestic diffusion of globally circulating shareholder-value practices, focusing on share buybacks. Using panel data on all publicly listed firms in Taiwan from 2001 to 2023, we show that the state does not merely transmit external pressures but actively mediates diffusion by translating buybacks into locally legitimate purposes and by supplying a countervailing, stakeholder-oriented governance logic. In Taiwan’s developmental policy regime, buybacks were institutionalized as a dual-purpose practice—either to support capital structure and shareholders’ equity or to transfer shares to employees—while regulatory design and implicit signaling disproportionately favor the employee-transfer form. To test our argument, we examine whether firms more embedded in the developmental regime—especially technology-intensive firms and those supported by innovation policy—are more likely to adopt employee-transfer buybacks. Leveraging a 2010 R&D tax-credit policy change as an exogenous shock, additional analyses indicate that government’ implicit support increases firms’ R&D investment, which in turn promotes the use of buybacks as a human-capital management tool. By showing how states steer which interpretation of a diffused practice becomes dominant, the study advances research on global diffusion and stakeholder governance.

Typology of the Purpose Orientation of Hybrid Firms

OMT
Diya Yang (York University); Preet S Aulakh (York University)
Hybrid firms pursue social purposes while striving to be financially viable. In the face of competing demands, hybrid firms selectively couple elements of commercial and social logics into internally consistent configurations. Current research on hybrid organizational structures and processes has predominantly attributed differences among hybrid firms to institutional logics, while largely neglecting the different types of agency shown by these firms. We develop a firm-level framework of purpose orientations that links how hybrid firms interpret social purpose to coherent bundles of organizational choices under competing institutional logics. We theorize three ideal-typical orientations, Provider, Steward, and Architect, differentiated along four frames. The framework clarifies why stakeholder evaluations diverge, why decoupling can become tempting when purpose interpretations are poorly understood, and how heterogeneity among hybrids can generate not only competition but also complementarity across firms addressing complex social issues.
