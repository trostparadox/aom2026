How Firms’ Attributions of Own versus Peer Failures Shape Cross-Border Acquisition Decisions

MOC
Jian Du (Zhejiang University); Guixiong Chang (Zhejiang University); Jie Lu; Huilin Shao (Zhejiang University)
Research on organizational learning from failure has examined how failures shape subsequent strategic behavior, often without distinguishing their sources. We theorize that own and peer cross-border acquisition failures exert opposing effects on host-country acquisition propensity and that firms integrate these effects when both are observed. Drawing on attribution theory, we argue that self-serving bias leads firms to attribute their own failures to external conditions, reducing subsequent acquisition propensity, whereas the fundamental attribution error leads firms to attribute peers’ failures to firm-specific deficiencies, increasing acquisition propensity. Crucially, we theorize that when both types of failure are present, attributional integration consistent with the covariation and discounting principles reallocates causal weight toward external explanations, rendering own failures more deterrent while reducing the encouraging influence of peer failures. Using data on more than 4,000 cross-border acquisitions by U.S. firms between 2010 and 2024, we find robust support for these predictions.

Ethical Organizational Anthropomorphism: Validity, Frequency and Consistency

MOC
Felix Martin; Davide Secchi (Paris School of Business)
Organizational anthropomorphism (OA) is the tendency to ascribe human characteristics to an organization. It has an instrumental value in channeling sentiments, visions, views, and perceptions to others. However, OA can be misleading due to its visceral nature and its association with biased forms of identification. This paper presents a theoretical framework (organized in 9 theoretical propositions) to understand the ethical nature of OA claims. In so doing, the paper utilizes organizational identity orientations to conceptualize the effects of OA on the preservation or disruption of the independent (ethical) self. Ethical OA is defined as the moral judgement shared—through anthropomorphizing—by individuals about an organization. The combination of identity orientations, their “dark” forms, self-continuity, self-esteem, self-verification, and self-enhancement lay a path to understand when ethical OA is consistent, frequent and, above all, can be intended as valid.

How Model-Driven Learning Leads to Dichotomous Thinking and Divergence in Interpretations

MOC
Seoul Lee (Northwestern University); Hyejin Youn (Seoul National University)
We present a computational model of interpretation that explains both the emergence of dichotomous thinking at the individual level and divergence in interpretations within homogeneous groups. In our framework, at each round, individuals use their mental models to interpret information and then update those mental models based on two sources (data-driven vs. model-driven). Our methodological contribution is the introduction of the learning component into models of interpretation, which allows us to examine how the source of learning shapes the structure of mental models and interpretive outcomes. We show that when individuals update their mental models based on their own interpretations (model-driven) rather than on raw information (data-driven), they come to develop bimodal mental models over time which force them to view the world through black-and-white categories. This result explains a paradox at the collective level, where even cohesive groups that share a homogeneous mental model often diverge in specific interpretations due to its bimodal structure. Taken together, our analyses suggest that seemingly puzzling interpretive behaviors are natural consequences of individuals’ everyday information-processing activities.

Cultural Distance and Incongruence Between Implicit Followership Theories and Follower Perceptions

MOC
Christine L. Bell (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Jun Yang (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
Leadership research has long emphasized the relationship between leaders and followers (Sy, 2010). As scholars increasingly recognize followers’ central role in organizations (Matshoba-Ramuedzisi et al., 2022) interest in followership theories has grown, including work on implicit followership theories (IFTs), defined as "individuals' personal assumptions about the traits and behaviors that characterize followers" (Sy, 2010, p. 74). This study examines IFTs from a cultural perspective by investigating whether cultural distance between the leader and follower predicts congruence between the leader’s ideal follower prototype and their perceptions of their actual follower. To analyze congruence, the paper introduces the Directional and Nondirectional Difference (DNDD) framework (Bednall & Zhang, 2020) to the IFTs literature as a robust methodological approach to examining cognitive (in)congruence. Drawing on a dyadic survey of 147 leader–follower pairs, cultural distance was examined using the Kogut-Singh index, and tested as an antecedent to leader prototype congruence. Results showed that cultural distance did not significantly affect prototype congruence. However, post hoc analysis revealed that cognitive incongruence is influenced by specific divergences in power distance and collectivism. This study contributes to managerial cognition by assessing alignment between leaders’ ideal and actual follower schemas and by applying a rigorous methodological approach to map cognitive schema (in)congruence.

My Work or Not My Work: The Paradox of Contribution in AI-Mediated Creative Work

MOC
Andrea Tunarosa (University of Tampa)
As generative artificial intelligence (AI) becomes embedded in creative and knowledge-intensive work, workers increasingly deliver outcomes for which they remain accountable yet did not fully produce. This shift disrupts a core assumption in theories of work identification—that individuals can locate themselves as contributors to work outputs. Drawing on paradox theory, I conceptualize this condition as a paradox of contribution, in which workers must simultaneously claim ownership over outcomes and relinquish authorship over their production. Both demands are necessary and legitimate, yet fully enacting one undermines the other, rendering the tension persistent rather than transitional. In this theory piece, I develop a process model showing that work identification under AI mediation depends on contribution construals, defined as the interpretive frames through which workers understand how they relate to their work and why that contribution warrants identification. The model delineates four ideal-typical construals that reflect distinct modes of paradox engagement and give rise to divergent identity outcomes. In doing so, the paper extends theories of work identification, advances research on technology and work, and introduces the paradox of contribution as a novel, micro- level identity tension in AI-mediated work. Keywords: work identification, paradox of contribution, contribution construal
