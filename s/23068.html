Equity as an Emergent Outcome of Multi-Level Organizational Design in Adolescent Mental Health Care

HCM
Elisabetta Trinchero (Bocconi University); Oriana Ciani (Bocconi University); Erin Ferenchick (Columbia University); Viviana Mangiaterra (Bocconi University)
Adolescent mental health care increasingly relies on complex collaborations across health, social, educational, and community organizations. Integrated care is widely promoted as a response to fragmentation and inequity, yet little is known about how integration is organizationally designed to produce equity. This study examines how equity is generated through integrated care design by analyzing five promising practices of adolescent mental health and psychosocial support in Italy, selected from a systematic mapping of 51 initiatives. Using a comparative qualitative case study design, we draw on Valentijn et al.’s (2013) integrated care framework as an analytical scaffold to examine how organizations design access pathways, coordinate professional and inter-organizational arrangements, and align these practices with system-level governance. Our findings show that integration alone does not ensure equity. Rather, equity emerges as an organizational property produced through the alignment of four key mechanisms—access design, youth participation, digital governance, and institutional anchoring—across service delivery, organizational coordination, and system-level governance. Normative values such as trust and participation facilitate engagement but need formal governance and institutional embedding. By conceptualizing equity as an emergent outcome of multi-level organizational design, this study advances healthcare management, demonstrating how integrated care can be deliberately designed to promote fairness.

Patient Experiences with Discrimination or Bias and Therapeutic Connections: An Exploratory Study

HCM
Cheryl Rathert (Saint Louis University); Isabella Cipolla-Pruitt (Saint Louis University); Derick R. Simmons (Saint Louis University); Jessica N. Mittler (Virginia Commonwealth University)
Health disparities based on race, sex, and other characteristics have been persistent, even though reducing them has been a focus in the U.S. for more than two decades. Some research has examined the extent to which care provider discrimination and implicit and/or explicit bias accounts for some disparities. Patients who have experienced discrimination in the past have less trust in the health care system and may even avoid care. Recent research has demonstrated the importance of therapeutic connections (TCs) between patients and care providers for positive patient experiences and outcomes. We propose that TCs may ameliorate past experiences of discrimination or bias such that recent TCs may reduce patient dissatisfaction with care invoked by previous care encounters characterized by discrimination or bias. In this exploratory study, we used patient survey data (n=522) to examine the extent to which patients experienced discrimination or bias, and tested the mediating role of recent TCs on the relationship between past bias and current patient satisfaction. We found that experiencing bias in the past was negatively related to patient satisfaction, but experiencing a TC at the most recent care encounter mediated this relation, i.e., TC appeared to reduce the negative effects of previous bias. In addition, we analyzed two open-ended questions from the survey for patients who had experienced bias in the past. The experience of feeling validated or not by their provider(s) was the most common theme. We discuss ideas patients shared for actions that make them feel connected and validated.

Disparities in Care Team Response to Patient Portal Messages: Impacts of Content and Writing Style

HCM
Mitchell Tang (Columbia University in the City of New York); Lisa Rotenstein; Ariel Dora Stern; Rebecca Mishuris; Michael Barnett (Brown University)
Patient portal messages are an increasingly central channel for patient-clinician communication, but there are inequities in how responsive care teams are to messages from socioeconomically disadvantaged patients. Prior studies have found that, even within the same clinic, socioeconomically disadvantaged patients are less likely to receive timely responses to their messages and are also less likely to receive responses from physicians (and more likely to receive responses from nurses). The reasons driving these disparities are not well understood. Using natural language processing, we analyze the text of patient portal messages from a large academic health system to assess the roles of two potential mechanisms: differences in the underlying request of the message (i.e., message content) and differences in the way the messages are written (i.e., message writing style). We find that, while the category of message request is a significant predictor of care team response, it cannot explain observed differences across demographic groups. On the other hand, the way the message is written – including message characteristics such as length and formality – accounts for nearly half of the observed differences in care team response for some patient groups. Our findings identify an important potential mechanism underlying disparities in care team response, highlighting avenues for mitigating them and deepening our understanding of care disparities more broadly.

Hidden Burdens of Racial/Ethnic Concordance: An Empirical Investigation in Home Visiting Programs

HCM
Minjeong Kang (University of Minnesota Twin Cities); Rachna Shah (University of Minnesota); Laurel Davis (University of Minnesota Twin Cities); Virginia Zawistowski; Rebecca J. Shlafer (University of Minnesota)
In provider-routed service operations, matching providers and customers is a critical decision, particularly in high-contact, repeat-service contexts where customer retention depends on sustained relationships. Management research increasingly recognizes homophily—the tendency to connect with similar others—as a key dimension of provider-customer fit, yet its role in non-reciprocal service settings where participation is voluntary remains unexplored. We examine this gap in the context of home visiting programs—public interventions providing maternal and child health support to vulnerable families. Drawing on homophily theory, we examine whether racial/ethnic concordance—matching provider and beneficiary race/ethnicity—improves retention. Analyzing a novel dataset of 99,318 home visits involving 5,430 beneficiaries and 88 agencies in Minnesota, we find concordance reduces beneficiary discontinuation likelihood. However, this effect is contingent on operational strategy: retention benefit of concordance is more pronounced in agencies with lower operational focus, suggesting operational focus can substitute for concordance. Furthermore, we demonstrate that universal concordance is operationally infeasible, creating caseload imbalances between providers exceeding 300%. Quantifying system-wide capacity, we show that while cross-agency resource pooling can improve concordance by 30%, the maximum achievable rate is capped at 60.7% due to workforce constraints. Given operational focus can substitute for racial concordance, we identify two pathways for managers and policymakers: deepening operational excellence, which avoids ethical concerns of race-based matching, or expanding concordant care through cross-agency coordination and workforce development.

The Role of Collective Impact in a Cross-Sector Partnership for Black Men's Cardiovascular Health

HCM
Daniel Gilmore (The Ohio State University); Daniel M. Walker (The Ohio State University); Ramona G. Olvera (The Ohio State University); Jennifer Garner (University of Michigan); Carrie Baker; Tanikka Price; Diana Briggs; John Gregory; Mysheika W. Roberts; MakedavC. Porter; Angela Dawson; Joe Mazzola; Chantel Morgan; Loletia Davis (The Ohio State University); Cameron Stauffer (The Ohio State University); William J. Hicks II; Timiya S. Nolan (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Joshua J. Joseph (The Ohio State University)
Effectively delivering interventions that promote health, particularly among minority populations, may benefit from the collective resources found in community-academic-partnerships. However, these partnerships do not typically involve government and industry partners that may be necessary given the complexity of population health problems. In this case study, we examine Black Impact, an Academic-Community-Government-Industry partnership to deliver a 24-week lifestyle intervention co-created with and for Black men in a large midwestern metropolitan area. By mapping insights gathered from 17 in-depth qualitative interviews with representatives of Black Impact partner organizations to the Collective Impact Framework, we explored the origin of each partner’s involvement and their experience supporting implementation. All five Collective Impact constructs—a common agenda, backbone support, mutually reinforcing activities, shared measurement, and continuous communication—were relevant to Black Impact’s successful implementation. Our findings can support scaling and expansion of future collaborations seeking to address inequities across the spectrum of chronic disease.
