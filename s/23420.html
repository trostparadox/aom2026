Green Guardians or Mission Drift: Executive Political Appointments and Corporate Carbon Emissions

PNP
Jiaqi Qin (Tongji University); Wei Xiong (Tongji University); Hucheng Xu (Tongji University)
Does the institutional co-optation of private entrepreneurs transform them into “green guardians” of public values, or does it induce a “mission drift” toward high-carbon expansion? Integrating public value theory with the public value failure (PVF) framework, this study re-conceptualizes executive political appointments (PAs) as a “public value contract” and investigates their environmental consequences. Utilizing a staggered Difference-in-Differences (DID) design on a sample of Chinese listed firms (2010 to 2022), we find that PAs are associated with a significant increase in corporate carbon emissions (CEs), supporting the PVF hypothesis over the normative stewardship view. Further, we unpack this failure through two mechanisms rooted in PVF criteria. Internally, an offensive “resource crowding-out” effect. While PAs facilitate the capture of government subsidies, these political rents substitute for, rather than supplement, substantive green innovation and other sustainable investment. Externally, we identify a systemic “high-carbon lock-in” within the supply chain. Politically connected elites leverage their status to insulate their networks from green institutional pressures, entrenching reliance on low-cost, high-emission suppliers.

Fostering Cross-Sector Partnerships for Large System Change: The Role of Science-Based NGOs

PNP
Haiying Lin (Hainan University); Lihe Lin (Hainan University)
Grand challenges (GCs) such as rural decline require transformations that are broad in scope and deep in impact, known as Large System Change (LSC). While cross-sector partnerships (CSPs) are recognized as critical mechanisms for tackling GCs, it remains unclear how individual, resource-constrained organizations—specifically science-based NGOs (SNGOs)—can strategically initiate and sustain these collaborations to achieve systemic impact. This paper proposes a Sequential Leveraging Framework based on a longitudinal study of Smart Fisheries, a SNGO in China. We identify nine types of CSPs categorized by breadth (technical to ecosystemic) and depth (transactional to transformational). We argue that SNGOs catalyze LSC by strategically sequencing these partnerships, leveraging early technical trust to enable institutional co-creation.

Experiments in Public Administration Research: Contributions, Challenges, and the Road Ahead

PNP
Ricardo Correa Gomes (FGV EAESP); Gustavo Tavares (Insper); Gustavo Mirapalheta (FGV EBAPE)
Experiments are essential for testing theory and evaluating policy interventions, with significant potential to guide decision-making in the public sector. This study provides a primer on experimental and quasi-experimental methods and reviews their use in public administration research by analysing 1,143 articles through bibliometric and regression techniques. It focuses on laboratory, field, survey-based, and quasi/natural experiments. The findings reveal rapid growth in experimental research, particularly survey-based experiments, which now dominate the field. Key theoretical clusters, themes, and references are identified, and statistical analyses investigate the link between experiment type and citation counts. The study concludes with recommendations for advancing experimental research in public administration.

Damage-Resistance-Control: Safety Risk Assessment and Mechanism Analysis of Coastal Megacities

PNP
Kangning Zhang (Ocean University of China); Keman Liu (Ocean University of China); Gang wang (Ocean University of China)
As urbanization progresses, urban risks are gradually evolving from individual risks to complex ones. Coastal megacities face more significant risk management challenges than other megacities because of their maritime and land-based risks, large urban areas, high population densities, and resource concentrations. This study develops a macro-level safety risk assessment system for coastal megacities based on Chinese case studies, analyzes the characteristics and mechanisms of risk evolution, and aims to provide essential support for urban planning and safety management. First, from an urban safety perspective, we innovatively proposed the Damage-Resistance-Control (DRC) model, establishing a three-tiered indicator system for evaluating coastal megacity safety risks. Second, based on indicator differentiation and redundancy, we conducted a screening of the assessment system. Third, we quantitatively measured the risk distribution characteristics of seven coastal megacities in China and analyzed the mechanisms underlying risk evolution. Research reveals three types of safety risks in coastal megacities and regional variations. Furthermore, we observe that coastal megacities face compound risks and amplification effects, which simultaneously exacerbate urban vulnerability. Meanwhile, risk governance actions exhibit rigidity and lag. Based on these findings, we propose policy recommendations for local governments to reference in practice.

When Does AI Become Disruptive Innovation? Monitoring Technologies for Optimizing Public Facilities

PNP
Aliaksei Kazlou (Linköping University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) and digital technologies are increasingly adopted in public-sector organizations to improve efficiency, yet their potential to constitute disruptive innovations in non-market settings remains underexplored. This article examines whether and when simple AI-based technologies become disruptive in municipal public services, and how they reshape governance relationships and democratic value creation. Focusing on Swedish municipalities, we analyze the introduction of AI-enabled camera monitoring in public sports facilities. Using longitudinal facility-level data, we estimate regression models to assess the association between AI-based monitoring and utilization across multiple facilities. The results show that AI monitoring is associated with improved utilization efficiency. The magnitude of the effect varies substantially across facilities, suggesting that local governance contexts intensity mediate outcomes. Theoretically, the study extends disruptive innovation theory to municipal governance. We argue that disruption in the public sector can manifest through shifts in institutional logics—from trust-based, relational governance toward data-driven and algorithmic control. While AI-based monitoring can function as a creative accumulation, it becomes disruptive when it substitutes established governance competences. The findings highlight the dual nature of AI-driven innovation in the public sector, generating efficiency gains while introducing risks related to excessive monitoring, trust erosion, and democratic legitimacy.
