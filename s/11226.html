Session overview

Micro-businesses constitute the economic backbone of emerging economies, yet their performance outcomes often diverge from expectations even when entrepreneurs possess adequate financial knowledge. The study draws on human capital theory, prospect theory and dual-process decision-making theory to explore risk-taking propensity as a behavioral mediator and financial satisfaction as an emotional moderator on the relationship between complex pathways linking financial literacy and performance. Using a mixed-methods approach, the research analyzed quantitative data from 415 micro-enterprises with Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) and thematic semi-structured interviews with 15 entrepreneurs. Findings show that financial literacy (financial knowledge, financial attitudes, and financial behaviors) does not completely, positively and significantly translate into performance, with risk-taking propensity partially mediating this effect, while financial satisfaction enhances both direct and indirect relationships. The study challenges the assumption that financial knowledge alone leads to better performance, revealing that entrepreneurs require a willingness to take risks and emotional satisfaction to effectively leverage their financial literacy. Economic uncertainty, informal financial norms and survival-driven decision-making emerged as themes firming the dynamics. Overall, the research advances a more nuanced understanding, offers practical implications for entrepreneurship training, microfinance design, and policy interventions targeting micro-enterprise resilience in emerging markets.
