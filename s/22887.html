Exploring the Antecedents that Differentiate Work and Entrepreneurial Passion

OB
Olivia Aronson (University of Richmond); Marina Astakhova; Alexander Hamrick (University of Richmond); Charles Murnieks (University of Missouri-Kansas City)
The literature on antecedents of passion has proceeded in a somewhat haphazard manner, resulting in a landscape that lacks coherence and integration. While the range of studied passion predictors has been diverse and included biological, psychological, and social factors, existing research primarily employs a fragmented approach, restricting investigations to only one or two predictors at a time, limiting our comprehensive understanding of passion as a complex construct shaped by the simultaneous interplay of multiple influences. Further, despite a growing need to differentiate between passion domains, comparative studies on the formation of domain-specific passion remain scarce, limiting our understanding of unique influences that shape passion in different contexts. Using fuzzy set methodology, we analyze how various antecedents may combine differently to predict passion across two of the most frequently studied domains in the management literature, work and entrepreneurship, and explore which combinations are particularly conducive of harmonious versus obsessive passion. Grounded in the biopsychosocial framework, we examine interrelations among eight biological, psychological, and social predictors. Our findings reveal that passion arises from complex configurations of predictors which vary based on the passion domain and type. By capturing the interdependencies among these predictors, we elucidate the distinct formation of domain-specific passion.

The Introvert Paradox: When Work Passion Is Misperceived and Adaptively Regulated

OB
Yiming Huang (The Ohio State University); Timothy A. Judge (The Ohio State University); Hanho Lee (The Ohio State University); Kai Krautter (Harvard University)
Personality traits, particularly extraversion, are widely used in organizations as explicit or implicit criteria for evaluating employees. Yet growing evidence suggests that extraversion does not reliably predict long-term performance or potential. Building on this discrepancy, we propose the introvert paradox and argue that introverted individuals are systematically disadvantaged during organizational gatekeeping processes despite showing no corresponding deficits in longer-term career outcomes. Drawing on the Brunswikian lens model (Brunswik, 1943, 1952), we argue that this paradox arises because evaluators rely on and reward perceived work passion, a highly valued but unobservable attribute inferred from visible expressive cues that extraverted individuals are more likely to display. Across two studies, we test these arguments. Study 1 uses large-scale longitudinal data (N = 1,218) and shows that introversion predicts lower selection success but is unrelated to career success. Study 2, a multi-source field study of leader–follower dyads (N = 98), demonstrates a moderated mediation process in which introversion is indirectly associated with lower promotability assessments via leaders’ perceptions of work passion, and this indirect effect is contingent on individuals’ ability to adaptive extraversion regulation, defined as the ability to flexibly regulate the expression of extraverted behavior in response to situational demands. Together, these findings highlight a systematic evaluative bias against introverted individuals and clarify when and why such disadvantages arise.

Are Halos One-Size-Fits-All?: Perspective-Sensitive Evaluation in Multirater Personality Assessment

OB
Andrew Perossa; Brian S. Connelly (University of Toronto)
Multirater personality assessments routinely reveal discrepancies between self- and other-ratings. These discrepancies are typically attributed to self-enhancement bias or differential information access. We propose a complementary explanation: self- and other-raters apply fundamentally different evaluative standards when rating personality. Drawing on cognitive theories of person perception, we argue that evaluation is egocentrically biased—self-raters evaluate traits relevant to intrapersonal fitness (emotional stability), whereas other-raters evaluate traits relevant to interpersonal fitness (prosociality). We test this idea in two multirater samples: management students rated by personal acquaintances (N-targets = 669; N-informants = 2,059) and managers rated by workplace colleagues (N-targets = 6,026; N-informants = 70,363). Using bifactor models applied to residualized scores to isolate evaluative bias from specific trait perceptions, we find that (1) other-raters show stronger overall evaluation than self-raters in both samples, and (2) the content of evaluation differs by perspective in personal but not professional relationships—self-raters' evaluation centered on emotional stability whereas informants' evaluation centered on prosociality in Sample 1, but both perspectives centered on prosociality in Sample 2. Critically, evaluation asymmetry between perspectives predicted lower self–other agreement at the facet level (r = -.61, p < .001). We discuss implications for interpreting personality ratings in organizational settings and for the design of multirater feedback systems.

Open to Openness to Experience: The Bottom-Up Construction of Leader Identity

OB
Jasmine Huijie Shi (Michigan State University); Philip DeOrtentiis (Michigan State University); Russell Eric Johnson (Michigan State University)
The flow of influence lies at the core of leader–follower relationships, shaping how individuals define themselves interpersonally as leaders or followers. Although prior research has fruitfully centered on leaders’ roles in shaping follower identity, this one-sided account remains incomplete if influence flowing from followers to leaders is not examined. Drawing on leader identity theory, we conceptualize follower-expressed humility as a grant of leader identity and follower taking charge as a claim of leader identity, which respectively activate and inhibit leader identity and ultimately translate into more or less vision communication. As a theoretically important yet often understudied trait in leadership research, leaders’ openness to experience is hypothesized to function as an influence gatekeeper, signaling receptivity to follower input and moderating the effects of follower behaviors on leader identity. Findings from a 10-day experience sampling method (ESM) study of 121 leaders largely support this framework, with the exception of the effect of follower taking charge on leader identity. Theoretical implications for followership and leader identity, as well as practical implications for leader development and training, are discussed.

Investigating Network Dynamics as the Missing Links Between Big Five and Workplace Outcomes

OB
Nicholas Rosemarino (Teachers College of Columbia University); James D. Westaby (Teachers College of Columbia University)
Personality traits have long been recognized as robust predictors of employee outcomes; however little is known about the social mechanisms through which these effects unfold. This study examined whether network dynamics mediate the relationships between Big Five personality traits and three meta-analytically demonstrated employee outcomes: job satisfaction, burnout, and turnover intentions. Drawing on Dynamic Network Theory, we employed a three-wave time-lagged design with 594 full-time U.S. employees to test whether network motivation and network resistance ties account for personality's influence on workplace well-being. Results demonstrated that personality traits predicted network dynamics in theoretically coherent patterns, and network ties subsequently predicted the employee outcomes. Model comparisons revealed that a partial mediation model fit best, with network dynamics accounting for 14% to 27% of personality-outcome relationships, except for extraversion and job satisfaction where 61% was explained by network ties. However, personality traits retained substantial direct effects, indicating that dispositional characteristics operate through both relational and non-relational channels. Additionally, extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness primarily predicted network motivation, whereas agreeableness and neuroticism primarily predicted network resistance. These findings demonstrate that personality and network dynamics function as complementary but differentially potent pathways to workplace outcomes.
