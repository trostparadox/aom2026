Profiles of (Un)Sustainable Global Careers: Health, Happiness, and Productivity

CAR
Rodrigo Mello (University of Vaasa); Samu Kemppinen (University of Vaasa); Vesa Suutari (University of Vaasa); Arnold Bakker (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
Research on sustainable careers has often treated health, happiness, and productivity as separate outcomes, offering limited insight into how these dimensions combine within individuals’ careers and how gains in one may coincide with costs in another. This limitation is especially salient in global career contexts, where high-density work promises strong developmental returns but also elevates risks for well-being. Drawing on sustainable career theory and a person-centred lens, we examine how health, happiness, and productivity cluster together among global workers and which factors steer individuals into more or less sustainable patterns. Using survey data from 325 expatriates and other globally mobile employees, we conducted a latent profile analysis that yielded four distinct career profiles, comprising one broadly sustainable and three comparatively unsustainable configurations. We then investigated how agency-related resources, job and work-environment characteristics, macro-level home–host country distance, and temporal factors relate to membership in these profiles. The results extend sustainable career theorizing by introducing a taxonomy that captures the joint configuration of health, happiness, and productivity, and by highlighting multilevel antecedents that channel individuals into different sustainability patterns.

A Formal Model of Sustainable Employability

CAR
Elizabeth Maria Beekman (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Jesse Fenneman
Research on sustainable employability has grown in relevance amid aging workforces, extended retirement, and labour shortages. Despite increasing attention, the concept remains fragmented, with inconsistent definitions and operationalizations that limit theory development, empirical comparability, and evidence-based practice. This paper addresses these challenges by proposing an integrative, formal model of sustainable employability that synthesizes perspectives from work and organizational psychology, labour economics, and sociology. The model conceptualizes sustainable employability as a dynamic process emerging from the interaction of multiple individual, organizational, and labour-market factors, rather than a static construct. Drawing on a utility-based framework, it accounts for motivational, affective, and instrumental drivers of continued employment, highlighting that sustained work engagement can occur under diverse conditions. By clarifying both what sustainable employability entails and how it functions as a system, the model provides a foundation for theory development, empirical research, and actionable organizational and policy interventions.

Rethinking the Measurement of Sustainable Employability

CAR
Elizabeth Maria Beekman (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
Research on sustainable employability (SE) has expanded rapidly, yet persistent conceptual and methodological problems limit the validity of existing measures. This paper identifies three core issues. First, dominant definitions require predicting long-term or lifelong career trajectories, creating a temporal paradox: short-term assessments misclassify transitional stages, while long-term measures are unreliable. Second, SE measures are validated through correlations with related self-reports rather than observable longitudinal outcomes, possibly resulting in circular validation that cannot establish whether employability is truly sustained. Third, SE is commonly treated as a static construct, despite being shaped by dynamic, interdependent processes. I argue that SE is better understood as an emergent outcome pattern rather than a psychometric entity and propose three directions for future research: prioritising longitudinal outcome-based validation, incorporating objective indicators of sustained employability, and adopting dynamic, process-oriented modelling approaches. These recommendations aim to strengthen the methodological foundations and practical relevance of SE research.

Navigating the Life-Centric Turn: Ikigai, Trust, and Career Sustainability

CAR
Ruhong Zhang (Waseda University); Mami Taniguchi (Waseda University)
The post-pandemic "Great Re-evaluation" has fundamentally shifted employee priorities toward a "life-centric" worldview, yet the resulting physical and emotional detachment poses a severe threat to career sustainability. While current career theories emphasize human agency (e.g., Protean Career Orientation) as the primary driver of success, this agency-centric perspective overlooks a critical vulnerability: in a disconnected world, solitary agency consumes resources without replenishing them. Without a deep-seated source of relational meaning, high agency leads to isolation and burnout. To resolve this theoretical tension, we integrate Self-Determination Theory and Conservation of Resources theory to propose the Meaning-Agency Activation Model. Grounded in the high-context Japanese economy, we identify Ikigai (a relational meaning resource) as the "Volitional Fuel" that must be channeled by Protean Career Orientation (the "Unified Engine"). Crucially, we posit that this synergy is conditional upon Perceived Organizational Support, acting as a "Cultural Valve" that signals Shinrai (trust) and grants the social permission to act. Without this valve, the agency engine "idles"—consuming fuel without generating momentum. This paper contributes by tracing the evolution of career theory from individualistic self-management to relational meaning-making and offering a concrete longitudinal roadmap for empirically validating Ikigai in organizational settings.
