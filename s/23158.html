And the Winner Was: How Organisations Manage Predictive Models Through Cycles of Ground Truthing

CTO
Lorna Anne Downie (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Melissa Mazmanian (University of California Irvine)
Organizations increasingly rely on predictive models for high-stakes decisions, yet we know little about how they manage the consequences of these models over time. In this paper, we examine the organisational work involved in detecting and addressing issues of alignment between modelled and empirical ground truth. Drawing on a four-year ethnographic study of a national Olympic committee and three of its national sport federations, we analyse the use of predictive models for Olympic selection. We theorise ground truthing as a cyclical sociotechnical practice through which predictive models are assessed and revised. Ground truthing comprises phases of reflecting, resetting, and running, anchored by a single, fixed, and universally recognised empirical moment. By articulating this cycle, we show how organisations can stabilise predictive models for high-stakes decision-making while creating structured opportunities to recalibrate their relationship to a changing empirical world. We further show that efforts to detect and address issues of alignment between modelled and empirical ground truth creates space for additional forms of organisational work in managing predictive models. More specifically, we theorise model stewarding as a construct that captures the work of interrogating the values embedded in a predictive regime, assessing the ramifications and lived experience of that predictive regime, and revising predictive models to promote a desired world. In doing so, we show how organisations can manage not only predictive accuracy but also attend to the values and world-making effects of predictive models over time.

The Relativity of Time in Digital Platforms: Algorithmic Temporality Control

CTO
Marcelo Catunda Bradaschia (FGV EAESP)
Digital platforms have introduced control mechanisms that govern labor through temporal parameters embedded in algorithmic systems. This paper develops the concept of Algorithmic Temporality Control to describe how platforms couple performance evaluation with task allocation, converting temporal compliance into differential access to earning opportunities. Drawing on labor process theory and studies of platform work, the analysis identifies four mechanisms through which algorithms exercise temporal control: responsiveness, intensification, discipline, and fragmentation. These mechanisms operate through digital intermediation, behavioral surveillance, algorithmic opacity, and independent contractor classification. The framework addresses gaps in existing research by connecting classical analyses of industrial time-discipline to contemporary algorithmic management. Propositions for empirical testing and implications for regulatory intervention are presented.

Beyond Progressive Encapsulation: The Biological Boundary of Professional Authority Amid GenAI

CTO
Na Liu (KU Leuven); Wenyu Liao (Northwestern University)
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is fundamentally altering professional jurisdictions through a process of Progressive Encapsulation (PE)—the internalization of expert routines and evaluative authority into algorithmic "black boxes." While current literature focuses on task-level automation, this study explores the theoretical and material limits of this process by investigating the "Biological Boundary" of embodied and relational labor. We argue that while GenAI can successfully encapsulate the informational coordinates of a profession, it remains fundamentally blind to the physical validation and emotional scaffolding required in high-stakes service work. Drawing on a qualitative study of professional fitness coaching—an empirical site where sports science meets physical palpation—we utilize semi-structured interviews and participatory observations to theorize the friction between digital encapsulation and human embodiment. Our findings identify three stages of relational negotiation: from Algorithmic Scaffolding to Embodied Integration: while programmatic knowledge is rapidly internalized by AI, the "immaterial dimensions" of labor (tactile intuition, longitudinal trust, and sensory feedback) form a durable strategic moat for practitioners. We contribute to the existing literature on the impact of GenAI by introducing the concept of "Biological De-encapsulation," a mechanism through which practitioners reclaim authority by "kneading" AI outputs into the client’s physical reality. We theorize the emergence of the "Cognitive Mediator," a professional role that moves beyond the authorship of plans to the orchestration of embodied intelligence. Ultimately, we argue that the future of professional authority in the GenAI era is not anchored in informational superiority, but in the unique human capacity for biological validation.

Transparent Marketplace Governance: Levers of Strategic Information Sharing in Gig Economy Platforms

CTO
Christian Bruck (WU Vienna University of Economics and Business); Werner Helmut Hoffmann (Vienna University of Economics and Business)
The rise of gig economy platforms, such as Fiverr, Upwork, Lyft, or Uber, has redefined the fabrics of several labor-intensive industries. These platforms create value for demand and supply side users through flexibility and efficiency, which results from platform governance. Popular gig economy platforms are known for being hardly transparent when governing users. While recent advances argued that transparency builds trust beneficial for a platforms’ competitive positioning, gig economy platforms face a strategic dilemma: how much to tell users about what’s going on behind the scenes. In this paper, we argue that there are two distinct ways to be transparent: event transparency (sharing what happened, e.g., ratings, reasons for blocking, payment rules) and process transparency (sharing how decisions are made; e.g., how tasks are allocated, how algorithms evaluate performance). We show that competitive positioning through transparency is not just about ‘sharing more.’ It’s a strategic choice about which kind of information to reveal depending on regulation, competition, and the nature of transactions. Our paper adds to a better understanding of the role of transparency in marketplace governance and provides guidance for putting transparent marketplace governance into action.
