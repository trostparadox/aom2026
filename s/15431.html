Session overview

As artificial intelligence reshapes the future of work, leaders are challenged to consider not only organizational systems but also the existential foundations of human meaning (George & Dane, 2020). Although AI enhances efficiency, mechanistic optimization has contributed to alienation, invisibility, and burnout (Kellogg et al., 2020; Seligman, 2023). Well-Being AI is emerging across domains as a catalyst for human and societal flourishing (Mittelstadt, 2023). In healthcare, compassion-sensitive systems reduce clinician burnout and support dignity in care (Islam et al., 2024; Jennings et al., 2022). In corporate settings, sociotechnical systems detect inequities, lessen overload strain, and enhance belonging (Aguinis & Glavas, 2019; Lee et al., 2023). In education, reflective AI supports self-awareness, ethical learning, and leadership growth (George & Dane, 2020). In public sectors, wellbeing analytics and participatory platforms strengthen civic trust (Mittelstadt, 2023). Planetary wellbeing applications integrate long-term ecological stewardship into decisions (Benn et al., 2021). Across all sectors, technology must evolve to serve humanity—not the reverse (Floridi & Cowls, 2019). This symposium introduces Well-Being AI—an emerging paradigm that integrates ethical intelligence, compassion, consciousness, and human dignity to restore relational depth and purpose in organizational life (Williams & Sharma, 2023). Grounded in the Awakened Compassion–Wisdom–Care (ACWC) model of integral leadership, which emphasizes inner transformation as the basis of compassionate organizational systems (Singh Sengupta, 2025), Well-Being AI calls for principled leadership that fosters learning cultures and shared purpose (Singh Sengupta & Kahemei, 2023). Consciousness-centered leadership supports ethical presence, trust, and well-being in technology-enabled collaboration (Floridi & Cowls, 2019; Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2022). This symposium also aligns with the United Nations Agenda 2030 – Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) by highlighting how Well-Being AI can reduce burnout, psychological strain, and feelings of invisibility caused by algorithmic systems, while strengthening dignity, psychological safety, and mental well-being at work. This symposium explores how Well-Being AI can strengthen psychological safety (Edmondson, 2019), increase fairness in decision-making (Lee et al., 2023), and foster greater meaning and connection at work (Aguinis & Glavas, 2019). Therefore, the purpose of this Panel Symposium is to engage a group of panelists in a formal, moderated, interactive discussion on how Well-Being AI—aligned with the global priority of mental health and well-being under the United Nations SDG 3—can be designed and governed to restore dignity, reduce human suffering, and sustain meaning in AI-enabled workplaces through the following guiding questions: 1. How can AI help people feel more seen and valued — instead of invisible or replaceable? 2. Which ethical lines must leaders draw now — before AI advances further into human decisions? 3. What leadership and design choices allow AI to support genuine dignity and compassion in healthcare? 4. In what situations is AI currently helping reduce human suffering, and which leadership and design choices are making those efforts successful? 5. How do we sustain meaning at work when machines take over identity-shaping tasks? Learning Outcomes & Contribution to Division This symposium advances scholarship and practice by equipping participants with the leadership mindsets and practical tools required to ensure that AI strengthens rather than undermines human wellbeing at work. Attendees will learn to clearly distinguish Well-Being AI from efficiency-driven automation models and gain an applied understanding of how ACWC-based leadership can ethically guide technology integration toward compassion, dignity, and shared purpose. Through dialogue and experiential co-design, participants will develop the capability to identify and mitigate psychological and ethical harms associated with algorithmic systems, while learning how to build cultures grounded in psychological safety, reflective learning, and relational trust. By the end of the session, leaders will be prepared with actionable strategies to enhance meaning, belonging, and connection in AI-enabled workplaces — ensuring that intelligent technologies elevate human potential and flourishing. Reversing ‘Reluctance to Lead’ With AI: How Technology Could Help Us Rethink How We Identify and Develop Future Leaders Allison Keene The idea of the reluctant leader has become more mainstream as younger generations actively disengage from climbing the corporate ladder. Only recently emerging as a field of study, reluctance to lead (RTL) has tremendous implications for the future of work, requiring employers to rethink past processes on how we identify and develop future talent. With the help of well-being AI, companies can completely rethink their approach to succession planning and developing the next generation of leaders in two ways. First, by creating systems that use specific markers to pre-identify potential leaders, not by stereotypical traits such as personality type, for example, but by analyzing data on performance and behaviors as well as offering unique assessments to measure emotional intelligence, thus expanding the pool of potential leaders. Second, AI can offer a myriad of development options to help managers and employees alike pinpoint areas of weakness for further development. For example, a high performer with reluctance to lead due to interpersonal skills can complete simulations that mirror issues that would occur in the workplace, including how to provide critical feedback to a team member or coaching on conflict resolution. By expanding the leadership playing field to provide individual coaching support to potential leaders, companies can ensure continuity in their leadership structures for years to come. In her presentation Dr. Keene will address the issues related to wellbeing AI and the future of work. AI with Heart: A Joy-Centered Framework for Ethical AI Development & Implementation Desmond Upton Patton Artificial Intelligence (AI) equally offers possibilities for social cohesion and for further division amongst communities. This paper presents findings from Integrating Emotional Stories Online (IESO), a pseudo-social media platform developed by computer scientists, linguists, and social work researchers, with the main goals of fostering community and analyzing expressions of grief and distress that occur digitally. We explore the pedagogical potential of AI to facilitate and amplify joy as a tool for managing complex emotions, fostering more nuanced and compassionate interactions online. We recruited 125 Black Harlem residents and students from a large New York City university, employing a mixed-methods approach to examine 949 posts of grief and distress. Our analysis yielded four themes that include: interpersonal grief, institutional systemic losses, and everyday stressors. The fourth and final theme, Joy, included gratitude for overcoming challenges, relational connectedness, and reflections on personal achievements. On IESO, joy emerged as crucial for emotional healing in the face of interpersonal and systemic losses. Thus, this paper aims to tackle 3 main questions regarding AI development and implementation that involve ethicists, developers, and researchers: 1. How does joy currently shape AI development/use? 2. How could joy shape generative AI tools and create pathways for connection online? 3. How should joy shape AI development/use? In other words, can we train LLMs to empower marginalized communities, rather than perpetuate marginalization? By centering joy as a communicative force within digital interactions, the findings from IESO raise the question of how AI can be leveraged, not only to detect harm or distress, but to cultivate collective well-being. Our findings recognize that AI can potentially support a shift on social media from an arena of polarization to one of joyful engagement. Human Touch, Compassion, and Consciousness in AI Sunita Singh Sengupta AI is more than a technological disruption—it is a profound test of what it means to be human at work. Beyond algorithms lies the question of how we weave compassion, dignity, and consciousness into the very fabric of digital transformation. Algorithmic systems can amplify bias, fragment workplaces, and displace workers, yet they also hold potential to augment human capacities and foster inclusivity when guided by compassion and ethical consciousness (Cath et al., 2018; Susskind & Susskind, 2022; Singh-Sengupta & Kahmei, 2023; HR Daily Advisor, 2025; TIME, 2024). Mindful and conscious engagement with AI emphasizes intentionality, value-based reflection, and accountability in how intelligent systems are adopted within organizations (Vallor, 2016; Coeckelbergh, 2020). Instead of allowing automation to override awareness, leaders are encouraged to remain attentive to the emotional, relational, and ethical dimensions embedded in digital exchanges (Kabat-Zinn, 2005). From a consciousness-based perspective, AI is most beneficial when it extends—rather than replaces—uniquely human capacities such as empathy, intuition, and moral discernment (Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, 1969; 1972). The Awakened Compassion–Wisdom–Care (ACWC) model reinforces this view by centering inner transformation as the basis of ethical leadership behavior, ensuring compassion, insight, and responsible decision-making shape how technology is designed and governed (Singh Sengupta, 2025). When aligned with such higher-order awareness, AI becomes a collaborative partner — strengthening ethical presence, enhancing psychological safety, and cultivating organizational cultures rooted in unity, meaningful purpose, and collective well-being (Floridi et al., 2018). In her deliberation Prof. Sengupta will focus on Compassion–Wisdom–Care (CWC) model, which frames leadership as serving people, purpose, and planet (Singh Sengupta, 2025). By combining AI’s optimization capabilities with leaders’ moral sensibilities and planetary consciousness, organizations can foster a future where digital innovation accelerates sustainability transitions and supports human and ecological flourishing. Abstract for the Focus of Discussion Governance for Well Being AI: How the United Nations Is Shaping a Future of Human Flourishing Artificial intelligence is rapidly transforming organizational, educational, and civic life, yet its expansion raises profound questions about human well being, safety, and dignity. The United Nations has constructed a robust global governance foundation that directly supports the development of AI systems designed to enhance human flourishing. Core UN governance mechanisms—including the Secretary General’s High Level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence (United Nations, 2024), the 2025 UN General Assembly Resolution establishing the Global Dialogue on AI Governance and the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI (United Nations News, 2025; United Nations Office at Geneva, 2025), and the UN System White Paper on AI Governance (United Nations Chief Executives Board, 2023)— collectively articulate a powerful mandate for AI that benefits all people, protects human rights, and strengthens equitable human development. Across these initiatives, the UN asserts that AI must enhance well being and dignity, protect individuals from psychological and social harms, promote inclusion and equity, and reinforce rather than erode trust, autonomy, and collective safety (United Nations University Centre for Policy Research, 2023–2025; World Economic Forum, 2025). These priorities align directly with emerging scholarly and practitioner calls for Well Being AI—an approach centered not only on reducing harm but on cultivating positive relational, emotional, and communal outcomes. Importantly, this governance architecture also provides a concrete enabling environment for future research and design work on well being aligned, dignity centered, and human aligned AI systems. The UN’s emphasis on safeguarding psychological well being, affirming human rights, and ensuring equitable benefit (United Nations Chief Executives Board, 2023) creates the normative conditions under which researchers can systematically investigate how AI might strengthen relational connection, support resilient communities, and combat the deleterious effects of taking humans out of the loop. By grounding future studies in these global governance principles, scholars can move beyond deficit based models of risk and harm to empirically explore the mechanisms through which AI can enhance emotional flourishing, contextual understanding, cultural affirmation, and prosocial digital behaviors. In this way, UN governance not only legitimizes but actively guides the next generation of research into AI systems that uplift human potential, support vulnerable populations, and advance a global vision of well being in an increasingly AI shaped world. How can emerging Well Being AI research capitalize on and advance the United Nations’ existing commitments under SDG 3—particularly its mandate to promote mental health and well being—to develop AI systems that measurably support psychological resilience, reduce harm, and strengthen global mental health outcomes. Abstract for Setting the Tone by the Moderator Artificial Intelligence can alleviate human suffering not only through the technical capabilities of the system, but also through leadership and designs choices needed to address the values of equity, dignity, and care. Evidence from research continues to indicate the potential of compassionate sociotechnical systems to address the problem of burnout, fairness, and meaning in the workplace, especially for groups of people considered to be on the fringes of the workplace (Kellogg et al., 2020; Noble, 2018). For instance, in the healthcare industry, compassion-driven AI works to assist professionals in the healthcare industry by rebalancing the intellectual workload of professionals while maintaining human judgment (Jennings et al., 2022; Islam et al., 2024). Additionally, AI in the workplace can reveal inequalities in the compensation, evaluation, and workload of people when leaders position the system as a mirror and not just an arbiter of decision-making (Aguinis & Glavas, 2019; Lee et al., 2023). From a diversity, equity, and inclusion point of view, AI has the potential to reduce suffering when led by transparency, participation, and psychological safety instead of or in addition to more emphasis on efficiency. The Moderator will introduce the symposium theme and the Discussant will initiate deliberations on how Well-Being AI—aligned with the global priority of mental health and well-being under the United Nations SDG 3—can be designed and governed to restore dignity, reduce human suffering, and sustain meaning in AI-enabled workplaces.
