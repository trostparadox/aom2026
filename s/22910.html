Moral Memory Entrepreneurship: Collective Remembering and Forgetting in the Digital Creator Economy

OMT
Kenny Ching
Digital entrepreneurship increasingly unfolds in spaces with little formal regulation, raising new ethical questions about how norms of responsibility emerge and endure. This paper examines how a digital creator community reconstructed its moral order following controversy. Using a longitudinal digital ethnography of ninety public artefacts, the study identifies a four-phase process—exposure, amplification, repair, and reconstruction—through which a collective re-made its moral past. We conceptualise this as moral memory entrepreneurship, the entrepreneurial reconstruction of collective memory to define acceptable conduct in post-institutional contexts. The findings extend theories of moral entrepreneurship and collective memory by showing how rivals, audiences, and algorithms serve as mnemonic agents. The paper concludes by proposing the principle of responsible remembering—balancing the moral duties to remember and to forget—as central to ethical governance in the digital age.

Sustaining Distributed Leadership in Collaborative Governance

OMT
Derrick Boakye Boadu (Florida International University)
Governments routinely collaborate with community groups and organizations. Yet, effective collaboration still needs leadership. The literature suggests that such leadership can be particularly effective if related behaviors and functions are distributed among members and rotated across initiatives or time. However, distributed leadership is often considered to grow naturally out of social interactions, giving little guidance to groups on how to activate and sustain such practices. This paper examines distributed leadership in a cross-sector, multi-organizational anti-displacement collaboration in Miami, fighting the impact of climate gentrification. The paper is based on a case study identifying social mechanisms through the reconstruction of narrative episodes. It illustrates how instrumental mechanisms can activate distributed leadership, but value-based mechanisms are needed to sustain it: Distributed leadership can result from a group’s efforts to engage in pressure buffering but is unlikely to be sustained without the group completing the process of identity formation through internalization and construction.

The Influence of Green Public Procurement on Firm Responsible Innovation

OMT
Han Sun (East China Normal University); Yanling Lian (East China Normal University); Chengcheng Xia (East China Normal University)
The study systematically investigates the influence mechanism of Green Public Procurement (GPP), a pivotal policy tool, on firms’ responsible innovation. Drawing upon institutional theory, we posit that the acquisition of legitimization benefits derived from GPP positively promotes firms’ responsible innovation efforts. Furthermore, as crucial complementary formal institutional arrangements, both regional digital financial inclusion and green finance strengthen the positive effect of GPP through a complementary moderating mechanism. Conversely, we contend that negative media attention and public environmental concern, operating as pressures stemming from informal institutions, weaken GPP’s incentive for responsible innovation via a substitutive moderating mechanism. Empirical validation of these hypotheses is established using a sample of Chinese A-share listed companies spanning the period from 2015 to 2022. This study enriches the literature on the economic consequences of GPP and elaborates on the drivers of responsible innovation.

Anti-Corruption Reforms and Retrenchment in International Sport Federations: The Case of FIFA.

OMT
Roman Gibel (University of Zurich); Florian Ueberbacher (Montpellier Business School); Andreas Georg Scherer (University of Zurich)
We examine how and why anti-corruption governance reforms are gradually retrenched over time, drawing on a longitudinal qualitative case study of FIFA from 2016 to 2024. Building on institutional theory, the study conceptualizes retrenchment as a multi-phase process shaped by declining external pressure and the reassertion of entrenched clientelist governance logics. The analysis shows that intense external pressure initially prompted the adoption of anti-corruption reforms that suppressed, but did not eliminate, clientelism, resulting in structurally vulnerable reform designs. As external scrutiny waned, clientelist practices re-emerged, producing a policy–practice decoupling in which formal reforms were maintained primarily as symbolic mechanisms to sustain legitimacy. With further erosion of external pressure, internal political and functional dynamics led to the reversal of reforms at both the practice and policy levels through a process of regressive recoupling. The study contributes to research on corruption, institutional change, and retrenchment by theorizing reform decay as a temporally unfolding, institutionally embedded process rather than a discrete policy reversal.

Logic (In)Compatibility, Scrutiny, and Strategic Responses of Corporate Foundations (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Ruiqian Xu (East China University of Science and Technology); Shipeng Yan (The University of Hong Kong)
We examine how corporate foundations (CFs) strategically navigate the institutional environment of charity, challenging prevailing predictions that CFs publicize their prosocial efforts to gain favorable evaluations. Extending research on institutional compatibility and strategic responses to institutional scrutiny, we argue that certain CFs engage in transactional philanthropy, a covert practice incompatible with the logic of charity and feasible under low institutional scrutiny. Using data linking CFs to their parent firms in China, we theorize and find that CFs initiated by private firms, firms in less developed markets, and firms from business-to-business industries embed a stronger transactional orientation than their counterparts and thus are more likely to underreport philanthropic activities. Moreover, when institutional scrutiny constrains CFs’ discretion over reporting, those with stronger transactional orientations reduce their donations. Our findings contribute to the literature on institutional compatibility and scrutiny responses and offer insights into how CFs incorporate business and charitable objectives.
