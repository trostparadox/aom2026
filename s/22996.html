Downstream Industry Exposure to U.S. Trade Protection and Japanese Subsidiary Employment Worldwide

IM
Marie-Ann Betschinger (HEC Montréal); Sergey Gelman (Concordia University); Kyoji Fukao
This study examines whether and under what conditions downstream industry exposure to trade protection in a protectionist country affects employment in foreign subsidiaries of third-country MNEs across their global networks. We consider two mechanisms: locational arbitrage, which allocates employment in more attractive subsidiaries, and global transmission, through which conditions in the protectionist country propagate abroad. Using global data from 2009- 2023 on Japanese foreign subsidiaries and U.S. trade protection exposure, we find that transmission effects dominate: U.S. downstream industry trade protection exposure reduces employment in subsidiaries in the same industries outside the U.S., especially for intermediate-goods producers and firms with larger U.S. footprints. Evidence of arbitrage appears only for subsidiaries with pre-existing U.S. sales links. Our findings advance research on trade protectionism and foreign subsidiary growth.

Facing Trade Decoupling: Firm Strategic Pathways in the Face of Geopolitical Trade Disruption

IM
Luis Miguel Bolivar (EAFIT University); Elisa Garcia-Marcano (EAFIT University)
This study examines how emerging-market firms confront sudden and severe decoupling by analyzing the collapse of Colombia–Venezuela trade relations, which abruptly halted nearly 10% of Colombia’s total trade. Using an embedded multiple-case design and semi-structured interviews with 22 Colombian firms, we explore how decoupling affected firms and how they responded. Our analysis shows that firms with high dependence on the decoupled market rely on defensive dynamic capabilities rather than proactive reconfiguration. We identify distinct strategic pathways such as survival strategies and reduced exposure, defensive strategies and safe engagement, and persistence strategies and adaptive transformation. These findings extend decoupling research beyond great-power contexts and demonstrate that resilience in emerging markets is a non-linear, path-dependent process shaped by institutional volatility and asymmetric interdependence for international firms.

US-China Trade War and Third-Country Firm Brokerage Behavior in GVC: Evidence from Vietnamese Firms

IM
Runqiong Wang (Renmin University of China)
The US-China Trade War has significantly reshaped firm global value chain (GVC) activities. Existing studies on the firm impacts of the Trade War primarily focus on its direct effects on firms located in China or the United States, while overlooking the responses of third-country firms?those located outside of China and the U.S. Drawing from network literature and resource dependence theory (RDT), this study examines third-country firm GVC brokerage behavior, defined as the transfer of resources from Chinese suppliers to U.S. buyers and vice versa, and theorizes how the Trade War has reshaped the resource dependence dynamics inherent in this triadic structure, thereby encouraging firms to engage in brokerage activities. Using Vietnamese firms engaged in GVCs as the sample, this research adopts the DID approach and finds that the Trade War has significantly increased firm brokerage behavior. Prior ties with Chinese or U.S. GVC partners and financial slack demonstrate firms' capacity to identify and capitalize on brokerage opportunities, thereby strengthening the above relationship. This study aims to contribute to the IB literature by offering a ?third country? perspective on firm responses to bilateral geopolitical tensions. It also enriches the literature on the antecedents of firm brokerage by addressing a previously neglected determinant: bilateral geopolitical tensions. Additionally, it extends the analytical scope of RDT from specific dyads to the resource dependence dynamics embedded within networks, revealing interactions among various dyads.

Trade Decoupling Shocks: A Theory of Firm Resilience in a Fragmented World

IM
Luis Miguel Bolivar (EAFIT University)
As global integration yields to politically-driven fragmentation, MNEs face "trade decoupling shocks" defined as acute, state-driven ruptures in commercial equilibrium. This article challenges current dyadic and supply-side biases by proposing an integrative framework centered on decoupling resilience. Drawing on dynamic capabilities and institutional theory, we conceptualize this higher-order capability through three dimensions: operational, market, and institutional. Using a process model and typology, we theorize how MNEs navigate legitimacy traps via strategic duality and institutional arbitrage. We advance a generalizable theory of geoeconomic rupture tackling, emphasizing firm agency and demand-side resilience in an increasingly bifurcated global economy.

Geopolitical Resource Dependence: Configurations of U.S. Imports Under China Rivalry

IM
Tarek Ghani (Washington University in St. Louis); Harald Puhr (Not Associated); Anne Jamison (Copenhagen Business School); Witold Jerzy Jerzy Henisz (University of Pennsylvania)
We develop a geopolitical resource dependence theory (GRDT) to explain how U.S. firms reconfigure cross-border supply chains as source countries shift their geopolitical alignment. Building on resource dependence theory, GRDT treats firms’ reliance on foreign suppliers as shaped jointly by interstate alignments and alliances, the structure of firms’ dependence across countries and products, and their capacity to implement changes in sourcing. We test GRDT using a 2007–2023 panel of more than 10 million firm–country–product–year observations on approximately 137,000 U.S. importers, constructed using U.S. customs records. High-dimensional fixed-effects models show that U.S. importers increase sourcing from exporting countries after their perceived geopolitical alignment shifts away from China, with this response attenuated for imports from formal U.S. security allies. The mean year-on-year decrease in China alignment observed in our data is associated with a .44% increase in imports the following year. Firm responses to exporter’s geopolitical alignment are stronger for large and diversified firms, and weaker when the scope for adjustment is low due to highly concentrated sourcing, complex and knowledge-intensive supply chains, or a limited number of viable exporter countries. The study extends resource dependence theory into the geopolitical domain and informs debates on decoupling, friend-shoring, and organizational adaptation under geoeconomic fragmentation.
