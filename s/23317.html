Competition for Critical Resources and a Firm's Product Innovation and Product Deployment Strategy

STR
Valerie Isakova (North Carolina State University)
Understanding the relationship between competition for critical resources on the one hand and product innovation and product deployment strategy on the other hand, is crucial for strategy research. This study examines how supply-side competitive pressures, such as rivals’ relative strength in expanding their geographic coverage, affect a firm’s decision to innovate by discontinuing maturing products and introducing new ones and adjust the number of products it deploys in stores across the geographic areas where this firm has established its presence. The study theorizes and finds that a firm responds to such competitive pressures by renewing its product portfolio and increasing the number of products it deploys in stores. This study contributes to strategy literature by highlighting the under-researched yet critical role of supply-side competitive pressures.

Automation Adoption in Manufacturing: An Analysis Through the Technology-Organization-Environment

STR
Yan Pan (The University of Hong Kong)
The current technological revolution, characterized by digitalization, algorithms, automation, robotics, artificial intelligence, and machine learning, is reshaping the nature of work and employment. The study examines the factors influencing automation adoption in manufacturing using the Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE) framework and the resource-based view (RBV). The empirical investigation identifies labor intensity and intelligence intensity as significant drivers of automation adoption. Market demand moderates the relationship between intelligence intensity and automation adoption negatively, while positively moderating the relationship between technology effectiveness and automation adoption. The findings highlight the need for context-specific automation strategies that consider both organizational capabilities and market conditions. The study contributes to TOE frameworks by providing empirical evidence on the specific factors that drive automation adoption in manufacturing.

The Persistence, Co-Evolution, and Performance Effects of Dynamic Capabilities in Digital MNEs

STR
Jianqing Lin (University College London)
Dynamic capability theory is crucial for explaining digital multinational enterprise (MNE) performance, yet research remains largely static, leaving the inter-temporal co-evolution of capabilities and their impact on evolutionary fitness under-theorized. Addressing this gap, we develop and test a longitudinal law of motion for sensing, seizing, and transforming capabilities, examining their persistence, sequenced co-evolution, and incremental effect on firm performance beyond the inertia of prior outcomes. Using a novel natural language processing (NLP) approach on 10-K filings, we construct a panel of 108 US-based digital MNEs with 1435 firm-year observations and employ dynamic panel (System GMM) models to trace their capability dynamics. Our findings reveal that dynamic capabilities are durable stocks exhibiting a novel hierarchy of persistence: sensing is most inert, transforming occupies a middle ground, and seizing adjusts most rapidly. Crucially, while the absolute level of capabilities does not improve performance beyond inertia, we find that an increase in their joint stock is a significant predictor of subsequent performance improvements. Based on the empirical test of the co-evolution of dynamic capabilities, this study contributes to that capability improvement, not just possession, is the key mechanism for breaking performance inertia and driving evolutionary fitness for digital MNEs.

A Temporal View of International Scaling in Digital Firms

STR
Gianluigi Giustiziero; Jing Sun (The University of New Mexico)
This paper develops a temporal framework to explain how heterogeneity in digital resource properties shapes international scaling in digital firms. Prior research often treats digital resources as a homogeneous “black box,” implicitly equating digitalization with speed and simultaneity. We move beyond this view by distinguishing between digital resources that primarily enable replication and scale and those that support adaptation and contextual learning. We argue that these resource properties map onto distinct temporal logics of internationalization. Scalable resources tend to privilege early entry, rapid pacing, simultaneous or leapfrogging expansion, and clock-time–driven growth. Adaptable resources, in contrast, align international expansion with event-time processes, fostering delayed entry, moderated pacing, sequential market entry, and iterative learning to build institutional fit and legitimacy. Integrating temporal perspectives from organizational theory with international business research, we theorize how scalability and adaptability jointly shape three temporal dimensions of cross-border expansion: timing, pacing, and sequencing. The framework identifies four international scaling trajectories: Balanced Global Scaling, Hyperscaling, Adaptive Localization, and Constrained Scaling, each reflecting a distinct configuration of digital resources and temporal outcomes. For scholars, the study refines the distinction between scaling and internationalization and advances a capability-contingent view of digital globalization. For managers, it clarifies how to align digital resource strategies with temporal logics, informing when to prioritize speed and when to invest in localized adaptation for sustainable international growth.

Orchestrating Adaptation: How Technology-Based MNEs Achieve Ambidextrous Business Model Innovation

STR
Shashank M P (Indian Institute of Management Kozhikode); Nandakumar Mankavil Kovil Veettil (Indian Institute of Management Kozhikode)
In this study, we discover the process of business model innovation (BMI) in technology-driven multinational enterprises (MNEs). These companies often operate in very turbulent and complex environments. Considering the technology-intensive nature of businesses, there is a long-term commitment to a set of resources and capabilities, leading to path dependency. Based on the theoretical lens of dynamic capabilities (DCs) and resource orchestration (ROT), we undertake a detailed case study of a leading global machine tool conglomerate to understand how companies pursue contextually ambidextrous (explorative and exploitative) business model innovation (ABMI) and succeed in overcoming these challenges to sustain their performance. In-depth interviews were conducted with 8 top management executives and 22 functional leaders of the group. A qualitative interpretivist research approach using the GIOIA methodology divulges a five-stage process model. We add to the existing research by showing how transformative organizational enablers and customer-engagement practices lead to reframing capabilities and resources in constant interaction with the environment. This interaction produces ambidextrous business model innovation, which is recursive in nature and enables technology-driven firms to achieve tangible performance benefits.
