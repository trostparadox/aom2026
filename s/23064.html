From Behavioral Failure to Biological Control: Obesity as an Institutional Blind Spot

HCM
Karina Protti De Mattos (FGV EAESP); Tania Veludo-de-Oliveira
Despite decades of research and public health interventions, obesity prevalence has continued to rise globally, suggesting a growing misalignment between dominant behavioral theories and real-world consumption environments. While psychology and consumer research have largely conceptualized obesity as a problem of individual self-regulation, the widespread adoption of pharmacological appetite suppressants such as GLP-1 receptor agonists signals a fundamental shift in how obesity is managed in practice. This pharmaceutical turn raises critical questions about the adequacy of individual-level theories under structurally obesogenic conditions. In this study, we argue that obesity represents not only a behavioral crisis but also a theoretical and institutional blind spot within psychology and marketing research. We conduct a comprehensive bibliometric review of 77 years of obesity research at the intersection of psychology and marketing, analyzing metadata from 6,474 publications from 1946 to 2023. Using performance analysis, science mapping, and analysis of the theoretical frameworks, we show that the literature remains overwhelmingly anchored in micro-level explanations centered on individual responsibility, alongside a systematic neglect of meso- and macro-level organizational, market, and governance factors. Marketing perspectives are marginal, and structural features of food environments, such as incentive systems, market design, and organizational practices, are rarely integrated into dominant theoretical models. We argue that the contemporary reliance on pharmacological solutions does not correct earlier distortions in how obesity was framed; rather, it completes them by shifting responsibility from consumers’ behavior to consumers’ biology. We conclude by proposing a future research agenda that repositions obesity as a market and institutionally produced phenomenon, calling for greater attention to responsibility allocation, structural accountability, and governance in health-related consumption.

Where You Sit Shapes What You See: Leadership Hierarchy and Variation in Patient Safety Culture

HCM
Pejmon Noghrehchi (The Ohio State University)
Patient safety culture is widely recognized as a key determinant of safe and high-quality health care delivery, yet efforts to improve organizational safety culture have produced mixed results across health care settings. This study explored variation in perceptions of patient safety culture across the leadership hierarchy, using an integrative framework that distinguishes among the enabling, enacting, and elaborating domains of safety culture. The cross-sectional study used pooled data from the 2022 and 2024 Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture 2.0. We estimated 12 hierarchical linear regression models with the 10 dimensions of patient safety culture and two summary scores as outcomes. Samples ranged from n = 245,654 to 411,557 respondents nested within 616 to 623 hospitals. Results indicated a clear leadership gradient for most enabling and elaborating domains of safety culture, with increasingly positive perceptions reported at higher leadership levels. There were mixed results within the enacting domain, with insignificant differences in communication openness and in reporting patient safety events between supervisors and senior leaders. These findings underscore the domain-specific nature of leadership-related variation in patient safety culture. Our findings offer additional insight for the development, implementation, and evaluation of patient safety improvement interventions and propose directions for future research.

Leadership Leap: Lived Experiences of Nurses Moving from Clinical Practice to Management

HCM
Wendy L Sarver (Cleveland State University); Michele Heath (Cleveland State University); Heather Marie Rice (Cleveland State University)
Nurses transitioning from bedside practice to leadership roles often encounter significant challenges related to limited formal preparation, inadequate onboarding, and insufficient organizational support. Unlike new graduate nurses, who benefit from structured residency programs, experienced clinicians are frequently expected to assume leadership responsibilities with minimal guidance, increasing vulnerability to role strain and attrition. This study examined the lived experiences of nurses transitioning from bedside roles into leadership positions and explored how these experiences align with and extend Duchscher’s Transition Shock Theory (TST). Semi-structured interviews and focus groups were conducted with a total of 19 mid-career nurse leaders employed across two large hospital systems. Data were collected via Zoom, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis supported by Intellectus qualitative software. Participants described pronounced transition shock characterized by inadequate onboarding, emotional distress, role ambiguity, and reliance on informal support networks. Experiences aligned with the TST phases of Doing, Being, and Knowing, reflected in themes such as insufficient formal onboarding and educational support, work life balance challenges, adapting to organizational complexity, culture gatekeeping through incivility and bullying, and navigating organizational complexity. Mentorship, experiential learning, and reflective leadership practices emerged as critical mechanisms for mitigating transition shock over time. Findings indicate that experienced nurses transitioning into leadership roles experience transition shock comparable to that of new graduate nurses. This study extends TST by demonstrating that transition shock is not limited to early career stages but recurs during key professional role transitions. Healthcare organizations should implement structured, longitudinal onboarding and mentorship programs tailored to nurse leaders to enhance role satisfaction, retention, and organizational stability.

Reconstructing Professional Legitimacy Under Institutional Complexity: Evidence from Global Health

HCM
Swetketu Patnaik (Anglia Ruskin University); Maureen Ayikoru; Leenkesh Ramlagun
The global health system has undergone profound institutional reconfiguration, marked by the rise of public-private partnerships (PPPs), philanthropic foundations, and new governance arrangements. While prior research has documented these structural changes, we know far less about how professionals working within this fragmented system experience and respond to institutional complexity, and how their responses cumulatively transform professional logics. Drawing on interviews with 25 global health professionals across multilateral organizations, PPPs, and international NGOs, we examine how everyday coping practices shape professional legitimacy and authority. Our analysis identifies three mechanisms through which professional logics evolve endogenously: elastic legitimation, whereby professionals gain credibility by acknowledging uncertainty rather than projecting mastery; moral tension work, through which professionals privately critique institutional arrangements while publicly complying with them; and constraint-as-authority, where professionals derive credibility from demonstrating awareness of institutional limitations. These mechanisms reveal how professional logics change not through deliberate institutional entrepreneurship but through the accumulated effects of routine professional practice. By theorizing professional logic evolution as an emergent, practice-driven process, this study contributes to institutional theory, research on professions, and global health governance. More broadly, it demonstrates the value of phenomenon-based, abductive theorizing for explaining surprising patterns of professional authority under conditions of institutional complexity.

Masculinity Contest Culture in Medicine: Implications for Physician Outcomes

HCM
Katrina Monton (Rutgers University)
Physician attrition has increased in recent years, drawing attention to the broader culture of medicine and its contribution to outcomes such as poor mental health, burnout, mistreatment, and dissatisfaction. These challenges appear particularly salient for women, who experience higher attrition rates than men. This study applies the masculinity contest culture (MCC) framework to medicine for the first time to examine how organizational norms shape physician experiences. MCC is characterized by four norms: show no weakness, strength and stamina, put work first, and dog-eat-dog competition (Berdahl et al., 2018). Using a cross-sectional survey of U.S. physicians (n = 215), we assessed relationships between MCC and physician outcomes including well-being, mental health symptoms, workplace incivility, burnout, turnover intentions, career satisfaction, and job performance. Higher MCC was associated with poorer well-being, greater mental health symptoms, increased incivility, burnout, and stronger turnover intentions. Gender moderated several relationships: MCC showed a stronger negative association with well-being among women, and it was negatively associated with career satisfaction and self-rated performance for women but not men. Exploratory analyses further indicated that MCC was positively associated with turnover intentions among physicians with caregiving responsibilities. These findings offer new insight into how entrenched cultural norms contribute to physician attrition and disproportionately harm women.
