Contesting the Future of Work: How Collective Agency Reshapes Technological Outcomes

OMT
José Luis Gallegos-Quezada (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Tal Simons (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University); Rebecca (Bex) Hewett (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
Research on technology and work emphasizes worker agency at the point of use, yet the most consequential effects of new technologies—such as job displacement—are typically shaped upstream, where workers have limited influence. We shift attention from individual engagement with technologies in use to how workers, as collective actors, intervene in the governance of technology. Drawing on a longitudinal study of dockworker unions at the Port of Rotterdam, we examine how workers assessed terminal automation across two analytically distinct levels. At the operational level, automation was experienced as affording improvements in safety and reduced physical strain. At the strategic level, it was understood as enabling work reorganization and employment reduction. Although excluded from initial design and deployment decisions, workers shaped organizational outcomes through what we term affordance realignment—a process through which collective actors constrain, condition, and repurpose intended technological affordances via mobilization and collective bargaining. The study advances a governance-centered account of affordances and clarifies how collective agency reshapes technological outcomes under conditions of power asymmetry. Keywords: affordances; technological change, worker agency, automation, job displacement.

Freedom in Practice: How Platform Workers Divergently Enact Work Autonomy

OMT
Dan O'Sullivan (UCL School of Management)
Workers on online labour platforms face work environments characterized by contradictory affordances and constraints – how do they make sense of and navigate this situation? Prior research struggles to answer this question, having largely focused on workers in traditional organizations, where social cues guide them toward more uniform and unambiguous experiences of autonomy and control. In contrast, platform workers work alone. This qualitative study of 94 platform workers shows that, in the absence of social guides, their experiences of autonomy diverge drastically, reflecting four distinct ways of navigating the contradictions they face. This variation was explained by differences in workers’ perceived degree of dependence on platform work and their appraisals of whether or not it fulfilled their expectations for autonomy – which, together, anchored their interpretations of and responses to their work environment. These findings recast autonomy as an enacted condition and challenge prevailing assumptions that workers in the same jobs have similar experiences of autonomy, shaped primarily by social and structural factors. They also shed light on how workers navigate contradictory affordances and constraints in the absence of social guides, and offer novel insights into experiences of autonomy and control in platform work.

From Manufacturing Consent to Materializing Matters: Performing Control in Liquid Organizing

OMT
Simon Altmejd (McGill University)
Organizational control can be seen as the translation of what matters for individuals into actions that support what matters for the organization. But in postbureaucratic workplaces, what indeed matters for individuals and collectives is increasingly ambiguous and contested, suggesting that new perspectives on organizational control may be needed. I answer this call in two steps. First, I reflect on the contingencies of modern workplaces by developing the concept of liquid organizing, characterized by eroding hierarchies, dissolving narratives, and extensive digitalization. Then, I reframe organizational control through a performative lens, as unfolding in communicative events through which abstract entities, material artefacts, and human actors are dynamically made to matter - or made insignificant - to each other. This framework provides two affordances that advance research on control in postbureaucratic workplaces. First, it allows to better capture the emergence of organizational control in the absence of managerial hierarchies. And second, it reveals the effect of knowledge on the unfolding of control mechanisms. I illustrate this framework through the empirical case study of Liberated Trucker Inc, a prototypical liquid organizing context.

From Organizational Participation to Civic Participation: A Spillover Perspective

OMT
Miheer Prafulla Agnihotri (Rotterdam School of Management); Rebecca (Bex) Hewett (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
Research demonstrates that organizational participation has benefits at both the employee and organizational levels. Yet evidence of broader societal benefits of organizational participation remains mixed. Building on Pateman’s (1970) spillover hypothesis, we argue that this empirical ambivalence stems from limited theorization of how, and under what conditions, behaviors cultivated at work extend beyond organizational boundaries. Integrating research on organizational democracy, organizational design, organizational citizenship, institutional theory, and civic engagement, we develop a conceptual model explaining how exposure to systemic participation at the workplace fosters prosocial and community-oriented behavioral orientations among organizational actors that can spill over beyond the organization to shape social outcomes through civic participation. We theorize the mechanisms underlying this spillover and identify three moderating conditions—existential, instrumental, and environmental—that influence when such a behavioral spillover occurs.
