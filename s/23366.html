When Are Hiring and M&A Substitutes? Factor Market Arbitrage Between Labor and M&A Markets

STR
Heejung Byun (The Pennsylvania State University); Koungjin Lim (Kennesaw State University)
The build-or-buy decision remains a cornerstone of corporate strategy, yet research has largely overlooked how relative competitive conditions in factor markets shape this choice. We introduce a theory of factor market arbitrage to argue that firms do not merely substitute between modes but actively reallocate demand toward whichever market offers more favorable conditions. We test this using 157 terminated M&A deals to investigate the reverse pathway where hiring substitutes for failed acquisition attempts. Our results show that firms exploit the competition spread: when M&A markets are relatively more competitive than labor markets, firms systematically pivot toward hiring. Furthermore, we find this arbitrage effect is strongest for firms lacking advantages in either market, suggesting that the ability to exploit cross-market differentials is a distinct source of competitive advantage. This study bridges strategic human capital and corporate strategy by demonstrating that mode substitutability is bidirectional and factor market-contingent.

Recovery Process: Evidence of Re-Adaptation After a Failed Post-M&A Integration

STR
Luca Pistilli (University College Dublin); Alessio Cozzolino (University College Dublin); Gianmario Verona (Bocconi University)
Most acquisitions stumble during post-merger integration, yet little is known about how firms recover once integration has failed. Using an inductive longitudinal case study of Amgen Italy’s 2008–2015 turnaround after its troubled merger with Dompe?Biotech, we trace a post-failure recovery process. We identify three meta-processes—socialization (rebuilding interpersonal ties), disambiguation (clarifying decision rights and information flows), and alignment (realigning structures and values)—operationalised through six workstreams. These mutually reinforcing mechanisms corresponded with a marked improvement in employee satisfaction and with target-relative performance. We contribute by offering a theoretically grounded definition of post-integration failure, theorising recovery as a distinct strategic option alongside divestiture, and specifying process-centric levers that restore trust, clarity, and shared identity. The study refines post-merger integration theory by showing how managers can convert a faltering integration into a functioning organisation without altering ownership boundaries. Beyond M&A, the model illuminates how processual levers can regenerate morale and productivity in organizations facing relational and structural drift.

Private Equity, Corporate Acquirers, and Product Innovation in Technology Acquisitions (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Sathya Vijayakumar (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania)
Private equity has become an increasingly active player in technology acquisitions in recent years, yet most prior scholarship has focused on the effects of corporate acquirer ownership on performance and innovation outcomes. As a result, research provides little guidance on how tech firms should choose between the two acquirer types. To remedy this gap, I construct a panel data set of acquisitions in the chemical, biopharmaceutical, and medical device industries between 1990 and 2019, tracked yearly through 2022. Then, I examine how private equity and corporate acquirers differentially affect product innovation at acquired technology targets using USPTO trademark and hand-collected new product introduction data. My results illuminate the opportunities and tradeoffs facing managers at technology companies in choosing between private equity and corporate acquirers.

The Double-Edged Sword of M&As: Balancing Similarity for Innovation Success

STR
Jildou Bouma (University of Groningen); Marvin Hanisch (University of Groningen)
Mergers and acquisitions represent a double-edged sword for innovation because they can expand firms’ knowledge bases while simultaneously complicating how that knowledge is processed and applied. In this paper, we examine this tension through the lens of information processing theory. Although information processing and organizational similarity literature clearly distinguish between the integration and interpretation of technological knowledge, prior studies have given primacy to the former. By omitting the interpretive dimension, these studies only partially explain post-merger innovation because they overlook how firms make sense of and apply knowledge in new contexts. To address this, we theorize about both technological similarity, which determines what knowledge is integrated, and cognitive frame similarity, which shapes how knowledge is interpreted, as important boundary conditions for post-M&A innovation. Using unique data on 261 biopharmaceutical M&A deals and 6,509 innovations derived from 15,841 corresponding clinical trials, we find that technological similarity exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with innovation, whereas cognitive frame similarity follows a U-shaped pattern. Overall, our study offers a novel sociocognitive explanation for heterogeneous post-M&A innovation outcomes.

Career Similarity, Work Structure, And Post-Acquisition Employee Outcomes In Technology Acquisitions

STR
Junyan Lu (National University of Singapore); Weiyi Ng (National University of Singapore); Lisa Tang (National University of Singapore)
Acquisitions represent involuntary career transitions that fundamentally alter career trajectories, yet little is known about how acquired employees’ (AEs’) prior career profiles shape their post-acquisition outcomes. We address this gap through the concept of career similarity: the extent to which an AE’s prior professional affiliations (firms worked in) and job experiences (functional roles held) resemble those of matched incumbent employees at the acquiring firm. Combining these two dimensions, we distinguish four similarity profiles and theorize that their effects depend critically on the nature of work—relationship-dependent in business roles versus knowledge-intensive in technical roles. Matching data on 2,114 technology acquisitions by S&P 500 IT firms (1995– 2020) with 8.6 million LinkedIn resumes, we operationalize affiliation and experience similarity using cosine similarity measures and estimate deal-stratified survival models of turnover and promotion. Our analyses reveal a striking pattern: career similarity leads to opposite outcomes across role types. For business AEs, similarity—especially in prior affiliations—is associated with elevated turnover and diminished promotion prospects. For technical AEs, similarity—especially in job experience—is associated with greater retention and improved promotion likelihood. These patterns are further magnified when both dimensions are present. Our findings advance research on corporate strategy, careers, and strategic human capital by showing how ex-ante career similarity shapes post-acquisition mobility, with implications for AEs navigating career disruptions as well as for managers seeking value creation from their acquired human capital.
