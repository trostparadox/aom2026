Supporting the Integration of Migrants: Civil Society Organisations and the Logic of Domopolitics

CMS
Marco Distinto (The Open University); Cinzia Priola; Alexandra Bristow (The Open University)
This paper examines the work of intermediary organisations supporting migrant integration in Italy. It does so by exploring the role of domopolitics, the concept of governing a nation as if it were a home, in shaping the work of civil society organisations (CSOs) supporting forced migrant integration. The paper draws on an ethnographic study of two Italian refugee reception centres implementing a national programme to support the resettlement of forced migrants within host communities and broader society. By observing the everyday organisational life of these refugee centres, we adopt a governmentality perspective to investigate the dynamics of power/knowledge within the relationships between CSOs, migrants and local communities. Our aim is to understand how domopolitics influences the practices of CSO workers, specifically analysing how it shapes their approach to migrant integration. The findings reveal that, despite CSO workers endorsing a multicultural view of integration, a covert form of assimilation emerges as a reaction to feelings of perceived powerlessness in the face of a conservative political landscape and unreceptive local community. Not considering themselves capable of transforming the local community and promote a culture of inclusion, CSO workers were pushed to adopt micro-practices of integration targeting migrants’ everyday behaviours and shaping their subjectivities in line with the logic of domopolitics and the idea of good guests.

Expatriation, Power and Agency: A Study of Indian Corporate Expatriates in the Netherlands

CMS
Dulini Fernando (Aston University); Mehdi Boussebaa (University of Glasgow); Nidhi S. Bisht; Mustafa B Ozturk
This study examines how Indian corporate expatriates adjust to new organisational settings in the Netherlands. Drawing on a discursive understanding of West–Rest power relations, we show how highly-skilled Indian expatriates often experience devaluation within their host organisations, and how the strategies they employ to negotiate a sense of fit are shaped by internalised hierarchies relative to their Western colleagues. We advance theoretical understandings in expatriate adjustment research by outlining the influence of global power asymmetries and career and life aspirations on the agency exercised during adjustment. We offer practical implications for multinational organisations seeking to support talent from the Global South.

Bollywood Narratives and the Ethics of National Identity

CMS
Vijayta Doshi (Indian Institute of Management Udaipur); Ajnesh Prasad (Audencia Business School)
This article centers on Bollywood film narratives around national identity and their ethical implications. Using the social identity theory and reviewing Bollywood movies, we illuminate how Bollywood films, through the cultural narratives propagate, construct and represent Indian national identity. We show that Bollywood mobilizes three particular narratives—(1) the dangerous Muslim, (2) the oppressive colonizer, and, (3) the bad foreigner—to antithetically define Indianness. These identity narratives communicate messages related to ‘who one is not’ in an attempt to represent ‘who one is.’ By considering the ethical implications of the conceptions of national identity discourses conveyed through Bollywood, we broaden our extant understanding of social identity theory, particularly national identity literature, in management and organization studies.

Organizational Responses to a Doomsday Narrative

CMS
Mabel C. Sanchez (California State University-Los Angeles); John Richard Parsons (University of Nevada-Reno); Anna Stevenson
The anti-immigration sentiment in the U.S. provides fertile grounds to explore organizational change through the theories of affect, sensationalism, and communication constitutes organization, and how narratives materialize into organizational practices. Since the inception of the U.S.-Mexico border, ordinary U.S. citizens have mobilized in attempts to control immigration. Through a historical and ethnographic analysis, we demonstrate the extent to which sensationalist narratives can affect organizational change via the emotional responses they elicit in individuals and communities. We show that current rhetoric describing the violence and danger of border crossers has resulted in U.S. citizen responses becoming increasingly militarized. These changes reflect the government response and indicate the militias are reflections of broader policy changes and narrative influences. The militias, as exemplars of organizational change, represent an invaluable location to examine theoretical understandings of narrative and organizational change.

A Critical Realist Framework for Theorizing Geopolitical (Re)Configuration in Global Production

CMS
Sina Mirzaye Shirkoohi (Laval University); Muhammad Mohiuddin (Laval University)
Research on geopolitical supply chain (re)configuration has largely focused on documenting the correlation between 'shocks' and 'outcomes. We argue this event-driven, positivist approach is theoretically incomplete, as it fails to explain the deep generative mechanisms that explain how and why firms reconfigure. Without a 'depth ontology,' our theories remain stuck describing what while failing to explain why. To resolve this, we adopt a 'blending and merging' review strategy. We 'transfer' the philosophy of Critical Realism to 'problematize' the existing literature and 'set out a new conceptualization.' The result is a new, conjunctural model that explains reconfiguration as the emergent outcome of three interacting, real-domain mechanisms: (M1) the logic of financialization, (M2) the logic of neo-nationalism, and (M3) the logic of digital mediation. We derive a new, mechanism-first research agenda.
