Emotion Regulation in Episodic Interventions to Strategy: The Case of Organizational Foresight

MOC
Alex Fergnani (Asian Institute of Management); Gerard P. Hodgkinson (The University of Manchester)
Episodic interventions to the strategy of an organization with consultant-facilitators can trigger significant emotional responses. Yet little is known on how the emotions of all parties involved therein should be regulated. This is particularly evident in organizational foresight processes as these elicit more pronounced emotional responses than other client-consultant relationships because they challenge deeply held views about the future and the strategic identity of the organization. Hence, we discuss organizational foresight to illustrate how the successful regulation of emotions can be achieved in episodic interventions to strategy. Specifically, we suggest that adopting an explicit and relational psychological contracting process contributes to emotion regulation in organizational foresight interventions. We outline the content areas of such a contracting process and explain why it is useful for the emotion regulation of participants’ and consultant-facilitators in three different situations. Finally, we discuss the implications of a relational psychological contract for future research (by providing a set of research propositions and suggestions), and for practice in episodic interventions to organizational strategy more broadly.

The Future Seems Wide Open With Possibilities: Landscape Thinking in the Midst of Turbulence

MOC
Wanzheng Zhao (Beijing Forestry University); Wenwen Liu (Beijing Forestry University)
Organizations today operate in environments characterized by unpredictable, uncertainty, and tightly coupled interdependencies, making adaptive action and strategic flexibility increasingly essential. Addressing complex problems characterized by high interdependence requires a fundamental cognitive shift. Drawing on evolutionary biology, this study develops a theory of landscape thinking—an adaptive mindset that enables organizations to explore multidimensional possibility spaces. We articulate three foundational constructs of landscape thinking: (a) multivariable and diverse possibilities, which shape the structure and ruggedness of the landscape; (b) initial divergence in exploration paths, which reflects how early positions and search heuristics generate distinct adaptive trajectories; and (c) dynamic and adjacent possibilities, which highlight how new opportunities emerge through ongoing interaction with the environment. To illustrate how these constructs jointly influence organizational adaptation, we employ computational modeling based on NK model and simulate both incremental fine-tuning and irregular oscillation mechanism. The simulations reveal how variable interdependencies, initial positions, and dynamic neighborhood updates shape adaptive outcomes under conditions of turbulence. This framework advances research on organizational adaptation by clarifying the foundations of the landscape metaphor and demonstrating how landscape thinking can guide organizations in responding to complex, evolving environments.

Crafting Simplicity: How Managers Can Design and Evaluate Heuristics for Decisions Under Uncertainty

MOC
Niklas Ungar (University of St. Gallen); Johann Peter Murmann (University of St. Gallen)
Research on ecological rationality demonstrates that simple heuristics can match or exceed the accuracy of complex decision methods in uncertain environments. Yet despite repeated empirical validation, heuristics continue to carry a negative connotation in the managerial discourse and remain mostly absent from managerial education. We argue that two factors hinder adoption: the lack of effective methodologies for designing heuristics for specific contexts, and insufficient research comparing the performance of multiple heuristics. We address these gaps by developing a replicable methodology for the design and evaluation of heuristics in the managerial domain. We test our approach in the context of university admissions using application data and job-market outcomes over ten years from 629 accepted and rejected candidates to a master's in management program. We design and compare over 20 fast and frugal heuristics – including take-the-best, fast and frugal trees, tallying, and satisficing. We benchmark these heu-ristics against the program's resource-intensive admission process and against large language models. We find that neither the expert-driven process nor the large language models outperform simple heuristics in predicting graduate success. We thus contribute to research on ecological rationality by providing a systematic methodology for designing and comparing multiple heuristics for a specific managerial task environment.

Investor Cognition and the Evaluation of Immigrant-Founded Ventures

MOC
Ozan Kirtac (London School of Economics and Political Science)
Entrepreneurial ventures depend on external evaluators to assess opportunities amidst uncertainty, relying on sparse cues from founders' backgrounds and narratives. While entrepreneurial cognition has focused on how entrepreneurs process opportunities, less attention has been paid to how investors evaluate them, particularly in the context of immigrant entrepreneurs whose ventures often present unfamiliar cues. This study examines how 51 investors in Turkey evaluate immigrant-founded ventures, focusing on how they prioritize credibility, competence, and risk in their decision-making. Adopting a socially situated cognitive framework, the research reveals that investors’ judgments are shaped by social, cultural, and institutional contexts, not just objective venture attributes. Using thematic analysis, enhanced with LLMs like LLaMA 3 and RoBERTa, the study identifies key themes such as reputational defensibility and social acceptance as critical in evaluating immigrant ventures. The research contributes to the investor cognition literature by emphasizing how reputational concerns and legitimacy impact funding decisions, offering insights for both investors and immigrant entrepreneurs navigating complex evaluation processes.
