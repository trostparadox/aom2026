Promote or Obstruct? The Role of State Participation in Cross-Border Multi-Stage Alliance Formation

IM
Yuan Tian (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Xu Jiang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Carl Fey (BI Norwegian Business School); Chenyi Shi (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
This study examines how state participation affects the formation of cross-border alliances in overseas markets, which include two stages—alliance announcement and alliance establishment. Drawing on the institutional logics perspective, we explore the different roles that state participation plays in the two stages. Based on data from 1,094 cross-border alliances announced by Chinese listed companies from 2015 to 2022, we find that state participation promotes the announcement of cross-border alliances in the first stage while inhibiting the establishment of cross-border alliances in the second stage. We also examine how the process from alliance announcement to alliance establishment be affected by country-level contextual factors, including economic distance, national cultural distance and bilateral political relations. Our findings provide new empirical evidence for research on firms’ alliance formation strategies.

Populism and Multinational Establishment Decisions

IM
Xingcan Zhang (The University of Auckland); Yuman Xiong (Hanyang University); Seong-jin Choi (Hanyang University)
Populist regimes often portray multinational enterprises (MNEs) as foreign threats to national sovereignty, reshaping the institutional context for international investment. Drawing on new institutional economics, we examine how populist hostility influences MNEs’ choice between cross-border acquisition and greenfield investment. We develop a contingency view in which acquisitions are the preferred establishment mode under populist institutional pressure, but as host-country intellectual property rights (IPR) protection and firm-level research and development (R&D) intensity increase, this preference weakens and can even be reversed in favor of greenfield investment. We test our hypotheses on a firm-level dataset of nearly 8,000 Chinese foreign direct investment (FDI) projects in 65 countries from 2007 to 2023. Logistic regressions support the proposed relationships, and the results remain robust to alternative measures and an instrumental-variable approach.

Protectionism and Hierarchical Global Value Chains: Incomplete Contract Perspective

IM
Xingcan Zhang (The University of Auckland); Luyao ZHANG (Ningbo University of Finance and Economics); Seong-jin Choi (Hanyang University)
Using a global panel from 1998 to 2022, we demonstrate that host-country protectionism serves as a contracting shock, reallocating residual control within Global Value Chains (GVCs) in accordance with the theory of incomplete contracts. This reallocation shifts the efficient governance boundary and reduces the lead multinational enterprises’ (MNEs’) financial performance. Leveraging a staggered Difference-in-Differences (DiD) design around 200 national elections across 54 countries and more than 13,000 observations, we find significant post-shock declines in performance for treated GVCs relative to matched controls. We further show that these effects depend on ownership and bilateral relations. Home-country state ownership attenuates losses when bilateral relations are positive, while non-home foreign ownership buffers losses when bilateral relations are negative. Overall, the results connect property rights to GVC governance by identifying who controls the GVC’s contractual white spaces and under what conditions in an era of rising protectionism.

Private Gains, Public Goods: Why Labor and Environmental Upgrading Diverge in Global Value Chains

IM
Shubham Singh (Rutgers University); Ram Mudambi (Temple University)
This study examines how private and public governance shape social and environmental upgrading in GVCs. We focus on two dominant forms of governance, MNE buyer–led private ordering and state-imposed regulation, and develop a framework that links their effectiveness to the internalization of externalities through cost-offset opportunities and buyer-provided incentives. Using a unique dataset of over 2,000 suppliers from emerging economies in Asia, we analyze how these mechanisms differentially influence labor and environmental upgrading. Our findings reveal a sharp asymmetry across domains. Both private ordering and labor regulatory stringency are positively associated with supplier labor upgrading. In contrast, while private ordering also supports environmental upgrading, greater environmental regulatory stringency is associated with lower levels of supplier environmental upgrading. We show that these effects operate through a buyer–regulation intermediation mechanism, whereby regulatory stringency reshapes MNE buyers’ sourcing strategies, which in turn condition suppliers’ incentives and capacity to invest in upgrading. In labor domains, buyer responses reinforce upgrading incentives by aligning compliance with productivity gains. In environmental domains, stricter regulation is associated with lower sourcing responses that weaken suppliers’ ability to undertake costly environmental investments. This mechanism helps in reconciling the mixed findings in the GVC governance literature on GVC upgrading.
