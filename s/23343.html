Sustaining Environmental Performance in Crisis: Board Gender Diversity and Cultural Constraints

ONE
Anjali Chaudhry (Dominican University); Sui Sui (Toronto Metropolitan University); Vikkram Singh; Aranthy Sabratnam (Toronto Metropolitan University)
Existing management literature has acknowledged the intricate interplay between board gender diversity (BGD), national culture, and environmental performance (EP). However, the COVID-19 pandemic offers an unprecedented context to reexamine these relationships. This study contributes to the discourse by investigating how specific cultural traits may impede EP during crises and how BGD mitigates these adverse effects. Drawing on a sample of 261 international pharmaceutical companies and employing Heckman's two-stage model, the findings demonstrate a positive influence of BGD on EP improvements during the pandemic. Moreover, cultural dimensions such as high individualism and short-term orientation are associated with slower progress in EP during this period. Notably, firms with greater BGD effectively buffer these negative cultural impacts, mitigating their detrimental influence on EP. These findings illuminate the intersection between national culture, board composition, and environmental performance, offering practical insights for fostering resilient and sustainable corporate governance in global crises.

The Role of Board Governance in Corporate Environmental Performance: Cross-National Evidence

ONE
Sumit Kumar Maji (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Apalak Khatua (XLRI Xavier School of Management)
Environmental responsibility has become an important topic in corporate governance research. While prior studies have examined the relationship between board governance and firms’ environmental performance, findings remain mixed and vary across settings. Hence, using firm-level data from multiple national contexts, we re-examine the relationship between board governance mechanisms and various dimensions of environmental performance. Our findings suggest that governance mechanisms are more strongly associated with disclosure-oriented environmental outcomes in more developed institutional environments, and this is not the case in environments where enforcement constraints are less pronounced. In short, this study contributes to research on corporate governance and sustainability by moving beyond uniform assumptions about board effectiveness. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of considering both organizational governance practices and broader institutional contexts when evaluating corporate environmental performance.

CSR as Sustainability Governance: Institutional Context and PRI Adoption in Global Finance

ONE
Steve Michael Loren
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) has emerged as an important mechanism of sustainability governance within global financial markets. This study examines the Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI) as a transnational governance initiative shaping the diffusion of CSR among financial actors. Using multi-level theorizing, we analyze how institutional mechanisms—diffusion, isomorphism, and decoupling—condition the adoption of the PRI across heterogeneous macro-institutional contexts. Empirical analyses show that asset owners in liberal market economies adopt the PRI earlier than those in coordinated market economies, while legal origin further differentiates adoption timing. Asset owners located in jurisdictions characterized by stronger property rights protections are significantly more likely to be early adopters. In contrast, macro-institutional factors do not significantly shape adoption timing among asset managers or service providers, highlighting the importance of organizational position within the financial hierarchy. Building on these findings, we theorize that faster adoption in liberal market economies is more likely to involve symbolic commitment, reflecting tensions between shareholder primacy and broader stakeholder objectives, whereas later adoption in coordinated market economies is more likely to support substantive implementation. By distinguishing adoption speed from governance effectiveness, this study demonstrates how institutional context conditions the operation of voluntary sustainability initiatives. The findings contribute to understanding CSR as a mode of sustainability governance and clarify the institutional foundations through which financial actors shape environmental and social outcomes.

How Does Board Prevention Focus Influence Corporate Philanthropy: Evidence from China

ONE
zhao liming (Wenzhou University); Chenli Yin
Integrating upper echelons and regulatory focus theories, we examine how the psychological composition of the board—specifically board prevention focus —shapes corporate philanthropy. While prior research emphasizes leadership traits, the collective regulatory orientation of the board remains a black box. Using a sample of Chinese A-share listed firms from 2007 to 2019, we theorize and find an inverted U-shaped relationship between board prevention focus and philanthropic engagement. We argue that while moderate prevention focus encourages philanthropy as a risk-mitigation strategy, excessive prevention focus stifles resource allocation toward non-core activities. Furthermore, we find that this curvilinear relationship is moderated by external and internal pressures: the presence of interlocking directors and regional NGOs amplifies the positive effects of prevention focus, whereas strong corporate environmental commitment acts as a substitute, diminishing them. This study advances the micro-foundations of corporate governance by highlighting how collective psychological traits and distinct contextual forces jointly configure firm-level prosocial behavior.

Policy Support, Governance, and Sustainable Aviation Fuel Commercialization

ONE
Olivia Guarinello (Georgia Institute of Technology); Jordan Smith (Georgia Institute of Technology); Riley Chodak (Georgia Institute of Technology); Daniel Matisoff (Georgia Institute of Technology)
Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) has emerged as a central pathway for decarbonizing aviation, yet commercialization remains uneven. Despite a rapid increase in announced SAF production facilities worldwide, many projects experience delays, downsizing, or cancellation before reaching operation. This divergence between announcement and implementation raises important questions about the institutional conditions under which organizational sustainability commitments translate into realized investments. This study examines how governance capacity, policy support, and innovation infrastructure shape the likelihood and scale of SAF commercialization. Using a panel dataset of 170 SAF plant announcements and operational outcomes across 35 countries from 2024 to 2025, compiled from Argus Media SAF Capacity Maps and supplemented with country-level indicators of governance quality, economic development, environmental policy orientation, intellectual property protection, and SAF-specific policy support; the analysis evaluates plant-level outcomes through logistic and negative binomial regression models. Drawing on institutional theory, the study conceptualizes SAF commercialization as an organizational process embedded within national governance contexts. Results show that stronger governance quality and the presence of SAF blending mandates significantly increase the probability that announced plants become operational by 2025. While results for production capacity are less conclusive, observed patterns suggest that governance and economic resources may influence the scale of investment. The findings contribute to organizational and management scholarship by demonstrating that policy instruments are most effective when embedded in credible and capable institutional environments. More broadly, the study highlights governance capacity as a critical condition for translating sustainability ambitions into organizational action in emerging clean technologies.
