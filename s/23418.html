Global Experience to Local Impact: How and When TMT Overseas Experience Drives Firm ESG Performance

ONE
Hui Lu (Nanjing University of Science and Technology); Zhiyuan Ma (Nanjing University of Science and Technology); Zheng Jiayi (Nanjing University of Science and Technology)
Developing countries face significant environmental and social challenges, necessitating the integration of ESG practices into corporate strategies. Based on upper echelon theory and the attention-based view, we propose that top management team (TMT) members with overseas experience positively influence their firms’ ESG performance, with TMT’s ESG attention mediating this relationship. We also examine the moderating role of female executive participation and the overlap of female and overseas experience executive participation in enhancing the relationship between TMT overseas experience and ESG attention. Using data from SynTao Green Finance, CSMAR, and Wind databases, covering 260 listed Chinese firms from 2015 to 2023, we test our hypotheses with 2080 balanced panel data points. Our findings reveal significant theoretical contributions and practical implications, highlighting the importance of the collective TMT effort in prompting effective ESG strategies in developing countries.

Performance Feedback and Its Impacts on Green Innovation: A BTOF Perspective

ONE
Saha Iqbal HOSSAIN (The University of New Mexico); Ajith Venugopal (Texas A&M International University); Ned Kock (Texas A&M International University)
Despite growing pressure to address climate change, firms vary widely in their commitment to green innovation. Why do some firms intensify green innovation while others retreat under similar environmental pressures? Drawing on the Behavioral Theory of the Firm (BTOF), this study argues that green innovation reflects managerial responses to performance feedback rather than uniform sustainability pressures. We distinguish between historical and peer-based aspiration comparisons across environmental and financial performance and examine the moderating role of industry dynamism. Using panel data from 117 U.S. S&P 1500 firms (2010–2024), we find that environmental underperformance relative to aspirations stimulates green innovation through problemistic search, whereas environmental overperformance induces satisficing and innovation extraction. In contrast, financial underperformance constrains green innovation, while financial outperformance yields modest gains. Industry dynamism further conditions these effects. Overall, the study extends BTOF to environmental sustainability and reframes green innovation as aspiration-driven and context dependent.

How the Chief Sustainability Officer Role Structure Affects Sustainability Performance

ONE
Vanessa Bröker (University of Münster); Sarah Lassek (University of Münster); Verena Rieger (University of Münster); David Lohmar (University of Münster)
Firms increasingly appoint Chief Sustainability Officers (CSOs) to their top management teams to address growing sustainability demands, yet evidence on when and how CSOs influence sustainability performance remains mixed. Drawing on the attention-based view, this study examines how CSO presence and role structure shape organizational attention to sustainability and, in turn, sustainability performance. We theorize that CSO presence is positively associated with environmental and social performance and that these relationships depend on whether CSOs hold a dedicated sustainability role or combine sustainability responsibilities with other functional roles. We further argue that industry ESG controversies heighten the salience of sustainability issues, thereby conditioning the effects of CSO role structure. Using panel data from S&P 1500 firms between 2002 and 2024, we find that CSO presence is positively related to social performance but not environmental performance. Moreover, CSOs with multiple functional responsibilities exhibit a stronger association with social performance than CSOs with a dedicated sustainability role. We also find that the effectiveness of CSO role structures varies with industry context, with CSOs in dedicated sustainability roles positively influencing social performance in industries with high ESG controversies. These findings advance understanding of how executive role structure shapes organizational attention and sustainability outcomes.

When Do Chief Sustainability Officers Matter? A Configurational Re-examination of UET

ONE
Taiwo Abraham (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); MAHSHAD GHAZALI (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); Gabriel Owusu; Xuan Wang (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley)
Despite increasing appointments of Chief Sustainability Officers (CSOs), many remain underutilized, and prior research yields contradictory findings on what makes CSOs effective. We argue this fragmentation stems from variance-based approaches that treat executive attributes as independent predictors with additive effects. Drawing on upper echelons, social network, and institutional theories, we develop a configurational embeddedness perspective positing that CSO effectiveness emerges from combinations of network centrality and institutional legitimacy. Using fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis on 243 CSOs from U.S. publicly traded firms, we identify four configurations sufficient for high ESG performance: the Institutional Insider, Focused Strategist, CEO-Proximate Insider, and Fully Embedded Integrator. Insider rank appears in all pathways, functioning as a quasi-necessary foundation upon which different network positioning strategies build. These findings demonstrate equifinality in sustainability governance design and provide diagnostic guidance for organizations configuring CSO roles to enable meaningful strategic influence.
