Explaining Digital Trust in Leadership Through Dual Compensatory Neural Pathways

NEUB
Julia Shipitsyna-Buchholz (BA School of Business and Finance)
Digital leadership increasingly unfolds in technology-mediated environments where traditional mechanisms of trust formation based on physical presence and rich social cues are constrained. While trust remains a critical driver of leadership effectiveness, psychological safety, and organizational performance, dominant trust theories are largely grounded in face-to-face interaction contexts. This paper explains trust in digital leadership by proposing that sensory deprivation in technology-mediated environments creates a bifurcated neurobiological challenge simultaneously activating heightened threat sensitivity and elevated cognitive load that requires two complementary compensatory pathways rather than a single mechanism. Adopting a theory-bridging conceptual perspective and a dual-systems analytical lens, this paper synthesizes interdisciplinary research from neuroscience, organizational behavior, and digital leadership studies. Drawing on neuroscientific evidence on trust-related neural systems including the amygdala, prefrontal cortex, mirror neuron systems, and neurochemical modulation the analysis proposes that organizations can restore trust in digital contexts through two distinct but complementary pathways: (1) Pathway A: Cognitive Compensation, wherein explicit communication strategies (emotional transparency, clear intentions, behavioral predictability, process transparency) reduce cognitive load and activate prefrontal cortex safety signals; and (2) Pathway B: Affective Restoration, wherein high-fidelity technological communication (high-quality video, immersive VR, biometric synchronization) partially restores sensory channels and reactivates mirror neuron systems and limbic engagement. The paper articulates how these pathways produce synergistic effects when simultaneously activated, providing functional redundancy, mutual amplification, and neurochemical reinforcement. Contributions This paper makes four primary contributions. First, it proposes an original dual compensatory pathways framework that explains digital trust as a qualitatively distinct neurocognitive process requiring two complementary neural mechanisms rather than viewing it as a weakened form of face-to-face trust. Second, it advances organizational neuroscience by contextualizing neural mechanisms within a specific, testable framework applicable to digital leadership and organizational contexts. Third, it reframes the digital trust challenge as a bifurcated rather than unitary neurobiological problem, offering a more nuanced explanation of why single-pathway interventions (communication alone or technology alone) produce incomplete trust restoration. Fourth, it advances four theoretically grounded propositions derived from the framework the Dual-Suppression Effect, the Threshold Effect, the Substitution Asymmetry, and the Trust Durability Hypothesis that are empirically testable and provide actionable insights for the design of trust-supportive digital work environments and leadership development. Keywords: Trust; Digital leadership; Neuroleadership; Neuromanagement; Organizational neuroscience; Neural mechanisms; Remote work; Remote teams; Psychological safety

Depolarization Through Dopamine

NEUB
Murad A. Mithani (Rutgers University-Camden); Ipek Kocoglu (Kean University)
Side payments—such as policy concessions and personal privileges—are widely used to reduce polarization. However, organizational research often presents an overly simplified view of their attributes. A side payment works whenever it is exchanged, and exchange implies that the side payment is proportional, certain, and temporally invariant to compromise. Drawing on neuroscience research linking motivation and rewards, we examine the assumptions surrounding side payments’ proportionality, certainty, and temporality for depolarization. We propose that the effectiveness of side payments depends on the nature of polarization. Polarization around issues is comprehensively addressed by marginally disproportional, certain, and advance side payments; around ideology partially by disproportional, uncertain, and delayed side payments; and around identity more narrowly by highly disproportional, marginally uncertain, and concurrent side payments. Our study advances a theory of side payments that explains the neural underpinnings of intraorganizational compromises. It elucidates the conditions and attributes that optimize the use of material and symbolic rewards for depolarization.

The Biological Tax of Teamwork: A Neuro-Socio-Psychological Framework of Homophily and Heterophily

NEUB
Vaneet Kaur (Kent State University); Hossam Zeitoun (University of Warwick)
In the volatile environment of modern organizations, the drive for internal harmony often masks a hidden neurobiological paradox that limits collective achievement. In social psychology, the long-standing “efficiency-cohesion” axiom posits that how well group members work together often depends on how cohesive they are. In this paper, we provide a novel explanation for why and when this axiom can break down, by synthesizing social identity theory with nascent findings in social neuroscience. By differentiating team coordination from cooperation, we reveal how the mechanisms traditionally heralded as pillars of success—such as high homophily and seamless alignment—can paradoxically become the architects of catastrophic failure. We propose a Neuro-Socio-Psychological Framework and introduce the concept of “neural lock-in”—a state where intense collective synchrony (alignment of emotions, biological states, and behaviors) acts as a physiological barrier to dissonant information. We propose that the “cognitive friction” of diverse teams can enhance resilience by habituating groups to the requirements for navigating volatile environments. Central to this framework is the “biological tax” —the hidden cost incurred when team members must exert significant effort to stay “in sync.” This interdisciplinary perspective shows that finite neural resources can crucially limit cooperation in organizations. Understanding and managing these neural resources fundamental to collective success in a volatile world.

Social Interactions as a Swich for Bayesian Cognition of Entrepreneurs: A Neurobiological Framework

NEUB
Jiaxun Liu (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Kevin Au (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); King Hin Wong (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Irene HO (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
Decision-making under Knightian uncertainty is a challenge for entrepreneurs. While a Bayesian perspective, wherein entrepreneurs act as scientists updating beliefs, offers a robust theoretical framework, a gap persists in understanding the mechanisms that trigger entrepreneurs, who often use intuition, into the Bayesian cognitive process. We integrate social and decision neuroscience to advance a theory of communicative rationality, proposing communication as the catalyst for triggering Bayesian decision-making. Accordingly, communication serves as a sense-making instrument, forcing the externalization of implicit heuristics and gut feelings into a structured, rational format of delivering. This process activates specific neural pathways: the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC) for Bayesian reasoning, the Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) for Bayesian inference through conflict monitoring, and the brain's reward pathways for Bayesian learning via reinforcement. We delineate a three-stage process, articulation, reconciliation, and iteration, whereby communication mitigates endogenous uncertainty and transforms subjective priors into more rational, evidence-based decisions. Our insights contribute to the microfoundations of entrepreneurial action by revealing how social interaction serves as the neurological switchboard for engaging rational Bayesian cognition, thereby providing a practical pathway for implementing Bayesian entrepreneurship.

Physiological Arousal and Verbal Behaviors During Conflicts in Teams: An Exploratory Field Study

NEUB
Lara Carminati (University of Twente); Desirée Van Dun (Politecnico di Milano); Maurizio Mauri (Politecnico di Milano - School of Management)
Although conflicts are an inherent part of team dynamics, there has been limited exploration of how these phenomena unfold at the behavioral and physiological micro-levels. This study examines the relationships and patterns between observed verbal behaviors and within-person physiological arousal during conflict situations in regular meetings. We collected data from 7 agile teams, comprising 14 meetings and 58 individuals, triangulating video observations, experts’ ratings, and skin conductance responses. We observed 44 naturally occurring (micro-) conflicts and identified two primary pathways whereby conflicts unfold, namely, escalatory and de-escalatory. Escalatory conflict processes consisted of negative relations-oriented behaviors, frequent arousal peaks, and high-intensity emotional expressions. In contrast, de-escalatory conflict processes were characterized by task- and change-oriented behaviors, fewer and weaker physiological responses, and low-intensity emotional manifestations. Strikingly, the arousal levels of individuals directly involved in the conflict and those not involved did not differ significantly, implying that conflicts are pervasive and felt by all team members, even ‘bystanders’, likely through emotional contagion. Interestingly, compared to low-performing individuals, high-performing individuals showed heightened alertness during conflicts, with more frequent and stronger arousal responses. As such, this study employs neuroscientific methods to extend the literature on conflict by uncovering conflict patterns that would otherwise remain unnoticed.
