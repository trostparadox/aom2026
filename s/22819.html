Proactive Personality and Career Success: A Trait Activation Theory Perspective

CAR
Surendra Babu Talluri (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore); Varun Sharma
Proactive personality reliably predicts career success (objective outcomes such as salary growth and promotions, and subjective outcomes such as career satisfaction). Yet prior work emphasizes job- or organization-specific mechanisms and pays less attention to trait-relevant career behaviors. Drawing on trait activation theory, we propose that proactive career behaviors serve as the key pathway linking proactive personality to career success. We argue that growth mindset and self-efficacy are individual-level activation cues that strengthen the effect of proactive personality on these behaviors. Across two studies, structural path analysis (Study 1) supports proactive career behaviors as an intermediary mechanism, and cross-lagged panel analysis (Study 2) shows that growth mindset and self-efficacy accentuate this trait-to-behavior link over time. Overall, the findings clarify when and how proactive individuals translate their traits into career success, extending the scope of trait activation and careers research.

Rethinking Mindset in Careers: Meta-Mindset as a Meta-Competency

CAR
Peter A. Heslin (UNSW Sydney); Lauren A. Keating (emlyon Business School)
Mindsets refer to implicit beliefs about the extent to which the personal characteristics that shape behavior can be changed. Decades of theorizing and empirical research have focused on how holding a growth mindset tends to serve individuals better in their careers than a fixed mindset. Scholarship on the role of mindsets in career interests, development, and outcomes almost always culminates in calls to encourage individuals with a fixed mindset to adopt a growth mindset. In this conceptual paper, we advance three critiques of current careers scholarship on mindsets: its tendency to dichotomize mindsets as either fixed or growth, its ambiguity about whether mindsets are treated as stable traits or malleable states, and its neglect of the potential downsides of resolutely promoting growth mindsets. We discuss the relevance for the vocational behavior literature of the recent advent of mixed mindsets, the dual-process model of mindsets, and compensatory growth mindset messaging strategies for averting harm resulting from growth mindset interventions. We thereby reframe mindsets from a simplistic, panacea-like individual difference to a dynamic, contextually situated mental framework that individuals can—via the meta-mindset career meta-competency—reflect upon and deliberately nudge to shape how they engage with and experience their evolving careers. We conclude with implications for research, as well as for organizations, career counselors, and individuals engaged in their careers.

Making Chance Matter: How Behavioral Configurations Enable Career Serendipity

CAR
Mariko Hiraiwa (Kyoto University); Tomoki Sekiguchi (Kyoto University)
Career research has increasingly emphasized chance and uncertainty, with serendipity providing a lens for understanding how unexpected cues lead to career-relevant gains. While prior work emphasizes noticing and meaning-making, little is known about the behavioral preparedness through which individuals act on chance cues and develop them into career-relevant gains over time. Using an inductive mixed-methods design in Japan, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 69 MBA alumni and students to identify the behavioral and motivational components involved in leveraging chance. We then operationalized these components as survey measures and used latent profile analysis to derive configurations of preparedness associated with career serendipity. Profiles reflecting action-oriented configurations—proactive cue engagement, bounded-risk commitments under uncertainty, sustained effort, and intrinsic engagement—reported higher career serendipity than weak or unbalanced profiles did. Preparedness is dual-temporal: over-time, values-based self-guidance provides direction, whereas present-oriented enactment supports real-time judgment and active engagement with emerging cues as situations unfold. Future work selves help prioritize the chance cues worth pursuing. These findings help explain why similar chance events lead to different career outcomes, depending on individuals’ behavioral preparedness.
