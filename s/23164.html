Beyond Layered Modular Architectures: How Deep Neural Networks Change Digital Innovation

CTO
Julian Lehmann (Arizona State University); Aron Lindberg (Stevens Institute of Technology); Stefan Seidel (University of Cologne); Hilmar koch
Neural networks are increasingly embedded at the core of digital innovations, yet prior research has largely conceptualized them as tools that assist innovation rather than as elements that fundamentally reshape its outcomes. In this paper, we develop a theory of how firms create and appropriate value when neural networks are infused into products and services. Building on the layered modular view of digital innovation, we conceptualize neural networks as assuming two distinct architectural roles: as functional components that perform tasks within systems and as generative interfaces that mediate interactions across components, applications, and ecosystems. We integrate these roles into a 2×2 framework that distinguishes configurations in which neural networks operate as components, interfaces, or both simultaneously. The framework explains how each configuration gives rise to different mechanisms of value creation, organizational requirements, and competitive dynamics. We show that neural networks as components enable technology epiphanies by redefining what products do, while neural networks as interfaces enable architectural innovation by reconfiguring how system elements connect. When neural networks function as both components and interfaces, digital innovation becomes radical: system functionality and structure become probabilistic, adaptive, and continuously evolving. We develop a set of theoretical propositions that link these configurations to distinct strategic orientations and organizational archetypes. Across configurations, we show that competitive advantage shifts from problem solving toward problem framing, data construction, and the governance of probabilistic behavior. Governance correspondingly moves from stabilizing interfaces to shaping boundary conditions and intent. By theorizing neural networks as constitutive elements of digital innovation, this paper advances research on AI, digital architecture, and organizing, and provides a foundation for studying innovation in environments where uncertainty is a generative force rather than a residual to be eliminated.

Relational, Stratified, and Emergent: A Sociomaterial Analysis of Boundary Management in Remote Work

CTO
Nitzan Navick (Rutgers University); Jennifer Gibbs (University of California Santa Barbara)
As remote work becomes a sustained organizational reality, managing work–nonwork boundaries remains a central challenge. Although prior research shows that remote and distributed work blur work–nonwork boundaries, this literature often treats remote work as a homogeneous condition and frames boundary management as an individual, rather than a relational and organizational practice. Drawing on sociomateriality and boundary theory, this study reconceptualizes boundary management as a relational, emergent, and stratified set of practices shaped by the entanglement of digital communication technologies, organizational norms, team structures, and remote work configurations. Using a longitudinal qualitative case study of a healthcare IT organization, the study analyzes seven years of Slack communication using a digital forensics approach. The findings suggest that boundary management in remote work is not an individual act, but a relational and communicative process shaped by audience, role, and organizational context. Boundaries emerge through ongoing interaction in Slack, where availability and disengagement are negotiated rather than declared, making boundary management an emergent sociomaterial practice rather than a fixed rule. These practices are stratified by role, function, and remote work configuration with higher-status actors able to assert boundaries more directly while others rely on softened or conditional signals to remain visible and legitimate.

Stabilizing Unfaithful Appropriation in Rigid Digital Technologies

CTO
Anass MAWADIA (Poitiers University)
This paper examines how bricolage contributes to the stabilization of unfaithful appropriation in rigid digital technologies, addressing an important gap in information systems research that has largely emphasized flexible or malleable technologies. Drawing on a qualitative study of ERP implementations across multiple small subsidiaries in an international group, the analysis conceptualizes ERP implementation as a privileged site of sociotechnical appropriation where deviations from intended use are negotiated, enacted, and progressively stabilized. Using Actor–Network Theory as a process-oriented analytical lens, the study identifies recurrent bricolage practices, including workarounds, iterative adaptations, resource recombination, detours, and the creative re-use of existing artefacts. Rather than remaining temporary or local, these practices are translated into durable material inscriptions that circulate across organizational sites, enabling rigid technologies to function across heterogeneous contexts. The findings extend appropriation theory by reconceptualizing unfaithful appropriation as a cumulative, collective, and translocal process, and by demonstrating the relevance of bricolage for highly structured digital systems. For practitioners, the study challenges compliance-oriented views of ERP projects, suggesting that bricolage should be recognized not as a failure of control but as a legitimate mechanism for stabilizing appropriation while preserving the benefits of integration and standardization

Building Trust in Banking Chatbots: Competence, Warmth, and the Asymmetric Pathways to Adoption

CTO
Sadia Malik (University of Sargodha); Man Young (University of Westminster)
Despite widespread investment in conversational AI, customer adoption of banking chatbots remains uneven. This study advances the existing literature by conceptualizing trust as a functionally differentiated mechanism through which anthropomorphic design cues shape adoption in high-risk service contexts. Drawing on the distinction between epistemic trust (confidence in accuracy and reliability) and relational trust (perceptions of warmth and benevolence), the study examines how perceived competence and perceived warmth influence customers’ intention to use banking chatbot avatars. Using survey data collected from banking customers, the study tests a theoretically grounded model employing confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modelling. Trust is modelled as a second-order construct comprising epistemic and relational dimensions. The results reveal that perceived competence and perceived warmth both positively influence trust, but with asymmetric effects. Competence exerts a stronger effect on trust and influences intention to use exclusively through trust, indicating full mediation. Warmth, by contrast, has a weaker effect on trust and retains a small but significant direct effect on intention to use, indicating partial mediation. Trust itself emerges as the strongest predictor of intention to use. These findings demonstrate that competence activates epistemic trust as a threshold condition for chatbot adoption in banking, while warmth activates relational trust as a reinforcing mechanism that enhances engagement once trust has been established. The study contributes to anthropomorphism and AI adoption research by moving beyond binary debates about human-likeness and offering a risk-calibrated account of how specific anthropomorphic cues matter in high-stakes service environments. Practically, the findings suggest that banks should prioritize signals of reliability and competence in chatbot design, using warmth strategically to support engagement rather than as a substitute for functional trust.

Deal Portfolio Complexity and Sales Performance: The Roles of CRM and Generative AI

CTO
Ankur Jaiswal (McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin); Huseyin Tanriverdi (The University of Texas at Austin)
Salespeople increasingly manage portfolios of concurrent opportunities that vary in products, customers, and selling processes. While such deal portfolio complexity can expand value creation, it can also hinder execution by generating cognitive, relational, and procedural burdens. Prior research offers limited insight into how different dimensions of complexity affect sales performance and how digital technologies shape these effects across sales stages. Drawing on complexity science and information systems research, we examine how product, customer, and process complexity influence sales performance across the sales funnel, and how customer relationship management (CRM) systems and generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) moderate these relationships. Using granular agent- and deal-level data from a large CRM platform serving U.S. insurance agencies, we show that complexity has dimension-specific and stage-contingent effects: product and customer complexity enhance early-stage qualification, while process complexity facilitates early advancement but impedes later-stage resolution. We further demonstrate that CRM and GenAI shape these effects through distinct mechanisms. CRM tames complexity by deterministically structuring interactions and information flows, preventing the emergence of ad-hoc dynamics and consistently improving performance across stages. In contrast, GenAI provides probabilistic augmentation that tames complexity only when interactions remain structured; when tasks involve customer heterogeneity or late-stage execution, GenAI fosters ad-hoc information flows and escalating verification costs that weaken performance. These findings show that digital sales technologies with overlapping goals are not interchangeable and that effective complexity management depends on aligning technological architectures with task demands and sales stages.
