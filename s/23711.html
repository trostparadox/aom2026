Mom, It Is Not Fair: Distributive Justice, Role Conflict, and Turnover Intentions

DEI
Matthew Aplin-Houtz (University of Louisiana at Monroe); Gordon Bruce Schmidt (University of Louisiana at Monroe); Abagail Ezell; Eliana Pines
Working mothers face persistent challenges balancing paid work with disproportionate caregiving and household responsibilities, contributing to elevated turnover intentions (TOI) and labor market exit. Despite extensive research on work–family conflict (WFC), less is known about how fairness perceptions across work and home domains shape whether conflict translates into withdrawal intentions. Drawing on organizational justice theory and organizational role theory, this cross-sectional study examines how WFC and family–work conflict (FWC) relate to TOI among working mothers and whether distributive justice at work and at home condition these relationships. Survey data were collected from 274 full-time employed mothers in the United States with at least one child under 18. Results indicate that WFC was positively associated with TOI, whereas FWC did not demonstrate a unique direct effect when modeled concurrently. Distributive justice at work was negatively associated with TOI but did not moderate conflict effects. In contrast, distributive justice at home significantly moderated both conflict pathways. When household labor and caregiving were perceived as unfair, the relationships between WFC, FWC, and TOI were stronger; when fairness at home was higher, these relationships were weaker. These findings underscore the importance of cross-domain fairness perceptions in shaping employment-related withdrawal and demonstrate that justice perceptions formed in the home have consequential implications for work outcomes among working mothers.
