Disclosure Is Not Just About Me: The Organizational Stakes of Individual Disability Disclosure

CMS
Rachel Le Marois (emlyon Business School)
Research often stresses the different factors leading people to disclose or not their invisible stigmas, yet the collective dimension of the stakes of disclosure is under researched. To demonstrate how states and organizations influence individual disclosure, this article explores the case of disability disclosure in French workplaces. Drawing on interviews, participant observation and archival data, this study examines how (quasi-)governmental agencies, organizations and disability managers have collective stakes in the disclosure of individual (invisible) disability. This article uncovers the concrete pressures that motivate these actors to push for workers' disability disclosure, pressures which may not align with the interests or concerns of disabled workers. The findings shed new light on concealable stigma disclosure, framing it as a form of collective activity referred to as disclosure work, in line with sociological discussions on collective and equipped work.

The Double Burden of Doing Good: How Employee Resource Groups Reproduce Professional Inequalities

CMS
Katharina Hug (Leuphana University of Lüneburg); Stefanie Gustafsson (University of Bath); Katharina Chudzikowski (University of Bath)
Research on professions documents the persistence of inequality, yet little is known about how inequality is reproduced within collective spaces intended to achieve organizational change. This paper examines how an employee resource group (ERG) focused on climate action became a site where professional inequalities were reproduced rather than transformed. Drawing on Bourdieu’s concepts of doxa, illusio, and symbolic violence, we analyze 47 interviews with professionals involved in a climate-focused ERG in a global consulting firm. We trace the group’s evolution from grassroots activism to institutionalized organizational initiative and show how professional field structures shaped whose voices were legitimized, whose labor was valued, and who bore the costs of participation. Our findings reveal how ERG participation generated a ‘double burden’ for marginalized professionals, who were required to sustain both professional performance and unpaid activist labor. We contribute to research on professional inequality, marginalized workers, and ERGs by theorizing ERGs as structurally ambivalent spaces that simultaneously enable voice while reproducing inequalities.

Embracing Selective Assimilation: The Case of Tribal Actors Overcoming Marginalization

CMS
Wilson Odhiambo Odek (University of Southampton)
This paper responds to the call for the enrichment of identity work in the global south, especially where social categorization and tribal identities often limit the extent to which individuals can scale social and economic mobility. Through 27 semi-structured interviews with actors in Kenya, East Africa, the paper provides insights through which actors manage to scale social and economic mobility despite years of colonization, marginalization, trivialization, and tribal violence. Three distinct themes emerge where tribal expectations and roles are faced with a growing youthful and resilient positivity. Nonetheless, these expectations and roles act as a straitjacket constraining social economic mobility, while tribal identities and their salience appear to have a hierarchical permeation through powerful politicians who have marginalized non-powerful tribes. Consequently, this paper contributes to a better understanding of how marginalized actors engage in identity work through selective assimilation to allow them scale social and economic mobility. This selective assimilation is undertaken as a belief-signalling identity performance to take advantage of the benefits accruing from appearing as a member of powerful in-groups. Further, given that the country is just over 60 years removed from colonial rule, there appears to be a transience of tribal identification evidenced by a youthful demographic that is increasingly indifferent to tribal expectations and roles, tribal violence, and marginalization. These insights may prove beneficial for organizations seeking to create identification with tribal actors in tribal contexts.

When Work Wounds: Moral Injury, Violence, and the Suppression of Women’s Ethical Agency

CMS
Ali NAWAZ Khan (Macau Millennium College); Mohsin Ali Soomro (Hubei Engineering University); Farrukh Moin
This study investigates how women employed in high-risk environments experience moral injury and how such experiences shape their ethical voice at work. Drawing on vulnerability theory and conservation of resources theory, we develop a model in which exposure to community violence, fear of terror, and gendered microaggressions predict moral injury, which in turn constrains moral agency expression. Using a two-study design involving female healthcare workers in conflict-affected regions and frontline employees in multinational service chains in Pakistan, data were analyzed using structural equation modeling. Findings indicate that both external and workplace threats significantly intensify moral injury, thereby suppressing ethical voice and work engagement. Cognitive reappraisal moderates this relationship, attenuating the negative effects of moral injury in resource-rich urban contexts but not in severely depleted, high-violence settings. These findings advance research on employee well-being and ethics by identifying moral injury as a critical psychological mechanism eroding engagement and trust. The study underscores the need for trauma-informed, gender-sensitive HR practices to restore moral agency and sustain ethical climates in precarious work environments.
