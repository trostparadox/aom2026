Boundary Conditions for Government Contracting Performance: Evidence from Necessary Condition Analys

PNP
Jiahuan Lu (Syracuse University)
Contracting out is a dominant mode of public service delivery, making governments’ performance increasingly dependent on how well contracting is managed. Yet most research on contracting performance relies on regression-based approaches that identify average associations, potentially overlooking “must-have” conditions that limit what performance levels are feasible. We apply Necessary Condition Analysis (NCA) to identify boundary conditions for contracting performance, using children looked-after services in England as our empirical setting. Results show that market competition is the most consistent constraint, functioning as a bottleneck for poor outcomes, particularly high service costs and poor service quality. In contrast, achieving high service quality requires a broader set of enabling conditions, including sufficiently strong competition, lower fiscal stress, and a less liberal governing orientation. These findings extend contracting research by distinguishing necessity for success from necessity for failure and offer practical implications for contracting management.

From Mission Language to Borrower Mix: Performance Systems and Inclusion in Social Enterprises

PNP
Mahinda Wijesiri (Independent Researcher)
Social enterprises must reconcile social aims with financial accountability, yet performance systems routinely privilege outcomes that are easily measured. Drawing on the attention-based view and multitask incentive theory, this study examines how formal metrics reconfigure organizational priorities, with particular focus on gender inclusion. We develop a framework linking measurement regimes to the allocation of managerial effort across competing objectives, distinguishing between formal commitments and practical inclusion. The analysis identifies an underlying mechanism and patterns that are consequential for the governance of social enterprises. The findings advance understanding of how accountability structures shape the translation of stated priorities into organizational practice.

How Do Nonprofit Organizations Perform in Changing Landscapes?

PNP
Paulin GOHOUNGODJI (HEC Montréal); Nabil Amara (Laval University); Danilo Dantas (HEC Montréal)
Previous studies on museums’ performance tend to focus mostly and separately on the market and custodial performance. This study advances knowledge by simultaneously looking into both high and low-performing market and custodial museums and by focusing on factors increasing or impeding custodial and market performances in museums. To achieve this, we use data from 250 Canadian museums to identify factors affecting to move up from low to high performance. The analysis of the data reveals four museums’ profiles: 1) Low-performing museums, 2) Market performing museums, 3) Custodial performing museums, and 4) Custodial and market performing museums. Multinomial Logit model and binary logit models are then used to investigate factors that explain why museums are any of these performance profiles rather than another. Overall, although we found that many factors influence markets and custodial performance, two major sets of results emerge from this study: firstly, high custodial and market performing museums differ from those in the low performance in many aspects: they use technological resources, have large seizing and infrastructural capabilities, receive adequate financial resources and develop co-creation strategy. Secondly, infrastructural capacity, co-creation with visitors and partners, and the host of artist-produced works are not essential for achieving custodial performance while public museums are more likely to achieve this performance compared to private museums. In addition, more aged museums are more likely to be market-performing museums rather than low-performing ones. Finally, the paper discusses implications for the achievement of performance by policymakers and museums managers willing to increase custodial and market performance.

Regional Sustainability Gap Index (RSGI):A Measure of Regional Sustainability Through Human Capital

PNP
Robert Jerald Ogburn (Central Washington University); William Provaznik (Central Washington University); Coco Wu (Tennessee Technological University)
Regional governments rely heavily on aggregate indicators, such as GDP, employment, and household income, to evaluate their economic development policies. While these measures often signal success at the state or metropolitan level, many subregions experience declining purchasing power, workforce out-migration, and reduced long-run opportunity. This divergence leads to policy misdiagnosis by ignoring a region’s relative context and obscuring the sustainability of regional wage structures. Drawing on theories of expectations, human capital reproduction, and regional divergence, we introduce the Regional Sustainability Gap Index (RSGI). The RSGI is a wage-based diagnostic that distinguishes a region’s current position relative to its economic core from its trajectory over time. Using county-level data from Washington State as an illustrative case, we demonstrate how regions can appear prosperous under conventional metrics while steadily losing their relative wage position in ways that, according to established theory, signal a heightened risk of long-run workforce erosion.

Economic Development Incentives: Multicultural Consumer Markets and Black Entrepreneurial Success

PNP
Onome Ighoavodha (Johnson County Community College); dt ogilvie (Rochester Institute of Technology)
This exploratory study on the Opportunity Zones (OZs) program of the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) informs whether the program’s spatially-targeted tax incentives increase economic development in heterogeneous urban neighborhoods. The OZ program leverages tax breaks to incentivize private investment in distressed neighborhoods (Marcin, 2024; Gale et al., 2024b). However, research has shown the program’s primary beneficiaries are real estate developers and affluent investors; the program also suffers from little transparency and community accountability (Kaye, 2021; Nemergut, 2021; Keo, 2025). With Houston, Texas as a case study, this study examines 24 census tracts (12 OZ designated vs. 12 comparison non-designated tracts) using a Difference-in-Difference design to provide evidence on the program’s impact on job creation, business formation, and Black-owned business. Results from ACS PUMS, CBP, and LEHD data find that OZs had a small, positive impact on jobs but had no significant impact on total business activity. There were positive but not statistically significant effects for Black-owned businesses, which is unlikely attributable to the OZ program. This study’s findings are consistent with previous authors’ claims that spatial tax incentives alone are not sufficient to organically incentivize development in multicultural markets and need to be complemented with intentional ecosystem building and race-conscious policies (Kaye & Greenlee, 2024; Clausing, 2025; Corinth et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2024).
