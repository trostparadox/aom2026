What Keeps Public Servants Employed? Understanding Sustainable Employability in the Public Sector

PNP
Meytal Masuri (University of Haifa); Zehavit Levitats (Bar-Ilan University); Dana Rachel Vashdi (University of Haifa)
Purpose – To define the concept of public servants' sustainable employability (PSSE) and explore its constituent components. Design/methodology—A qualitative grounded theory approach was used to gain an understanding of PSSE and its unique components. We conducted semi-structured interviews with 31 current public-sector employees and with 18 participants who were previously employed in the public sector. Findings – PSSE, an individual's ability to remain employed within public organizations over time, is shaped by specific resources and demands. The enabling resources, which motivate employability in the public sector over time, include self-sacrifice, motivation to work, and job crafting. The hindering demands, which undermine employability over time, include insufficient organizational support, political constraints on pay and promotion, and low work ability. It seems that employees who are motivated by these resources and who are not highly affected by these demands are sustainably employed in the public sector. Originality/value – This work challenges the idea that sustainable employability is universal and extends the Job Demands-Resources model by suggesting that resources and demands not only shape health and well-being but also determine public servants’ ability to remain employed over time. The findings may provide policymakers and managers with a new perspective on how to improve retention in the public sector.

Invisible Work, Visible Value: How Office Housework Sustains Women’s Careers in Public Healthcare

PNP
Lin Jun (Taiyuan Normal University); Wenjing Cai (University of Science and Technology of China); Jialu Yang (University of Science and Technology of China)
This research examines how gendered invisible work shapes women’s career sustainability in public healthcare institutions. Drawing on conservation of resources theory and job crafting perspectives, we propose that office housework, often seen as routine and low-promotability, generates relational energy that enhances career sustainability when women’s relational job crafting is high. Using a sequential mixed-methods design, Study 1 (interviews with 25 women across public healthcare institutions) explores how women interpret and navigate the relational dynamics of office housework. Study 2 (a survey of 205 women in a large public hospital) tests the proposed mediation model, showing that relational energy mediates the relationship between office housework and career sustainability, and relational job crafting strengthens this indirect effect. The findings reframe invisible work as a relational resource process and highlight the need for public organizations to recognize, redistribute, and revalue gendered relational labor to build more equitable and sustainable public service careers.

How Public Service Contexts Shape Teachers’ Innovative Work Behaviour: A Qualitative Study

PNP
Furkan Khan; Kathan Shukla (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
Literature consistently links student outcomes to various teacher-level factors. According to school improvement research, teachers' innovative practices have a significant impact on both student learning and non-academic outcomes. However, there is little research available on the factors that influence the innovative behaviour of teachers, especially within the critical context of Indian government teachers operating in highly resource-constrained environments. Accordingly, this study explored the factors that influence teachers' innovative behaviour in government schools. We examined the meaning-making of teachers' innovative behaviour and the factors that support such behaviour. We conducted this study in the two north Indian states and used purposive sampling to identify the participants. In this study, in total, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 50 participants. We utilised the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model and employed qualitative content analysis to identify the factors impacting teachers' innovative behaviour. The findings reveal that, in addition to common factors, there are several unique education system-specific factors, including teacher-student relationships and engagement, in-house professional learning communities, and parental involvement. All these factors influenced teachers' innovative behaviour.

Customer Service in Public Sector: Role of Organizational Pride and Gender Audit Participation

PNP
Dynah A. Basuil (Asian Institute of Management)
Within the New Public Management (NPM) movement that treats citizens as customers, this study contributes to the growing literature examining antecedents to customer-oriented behaviors of public service employees. Relying on social identity theory, we hypothesized employees who feel pride in being part of the organization are more likely to behave in customer service manner towards citizens. We further hypothesized that this relationship intensifies when public service employees are involved in activities, in this case gender audit, that mainstream socially impactful values. We found support for our hypotheses in the data we collected from a local government unit (barangay) in the Philippines. We also suggest ways for public managers to provide an enabling environment where employees can experience pride so the overall goal of effectively serving the diverse needs and demands of citizens can be supported.

Beyond the Relevance Gap: Sector-Conditioned Mechanisms Shaping the Use of Management Research

PNP
Lei Liu (University of Exeter)
Management scholars continue to debate why academic research fails to be applied in practice. While much prior work has examined this “relevance gap” from the perspective of scholars and research systems, we shift our attention to the sectoral context. Focusing on third-sector organizations, we inductively theorize how sector-specific conditions shape organizational members’ engagement with academic management knowledge. Drawing on 25 semi-structured interviews with leaders and frontline staff in organizations devoted to social services and community development, we develop a multilevel model that explains how sector-conditioned mechanisms shape how people construct the relevance of academic management knowledge and engage in learning it. We show how sector-level legitimacy and resource scarcity, under third sector service intensity, direct people’s attention toward immediate delivery demands and generate moral burden, thereby constraining learning pathways. These conditions make people privilege service-proximate learning and rely heavily on experience-based, interpersonal learning channels. Engagement with academic management knowledge is further impeded by cognitive friction, including identity-protective pride and academic language barriers. We further demonstrate the individual- and organizational-level contingencies that relax these constraints. By integrating the institutional perspective with the cognitive mechanisms uniquely shown in the third sector, this study advances a sector-based, practitioner-centered theory of management knowledge relevance.
