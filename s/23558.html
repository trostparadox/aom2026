AI Disclosure and Anti-AI Narratives: Market Evaluation in Crowdfunding

TIM
Mana Heshmati (University of Washington); Ming Zhu Wang (University of Washington)
Generative artificial intelligence (AI) can raise entrepreneurial productivity, yet market response also depends on how products are evaluated, particularly in creative domains where human craft is central. Prior work emphasizes AI’s internal benefits but offers limited evidence on how market evaluation changes when AI use is revealed. As platforms increasingly require AI-use disclosures, entrepreneurs may face consequences of making AI statements about whether and how AI was used in the product’s creation. We examine this phenomenon using Kickstarter’s AI-disclosure policy, which requires creators to disclose and describe their use of AI tools. Analyzing 42,745 crowdfunding campaigns launched after the policy’s introduction, we compare projects that disclose AI use or explicitly adopt anti-AI narratives to those that remain silent about AI. Relative to silent projects, AI-disclosing campaigns attract 33% fewer backers and are 11.9 percentage points less likely to reach their funding goals, whereas anti-AI narratives are associated with 29% more backers and a 3.9 percentage-point higher success rate. Among AI-disclosing projects, framing AI as assisting rather than replacing human work attenuates, but does not eliminate, the disclosure penalty. The results highlight an AI transparency dilemma and show that visible AI statements function as a consequential dimension of positioning in creative markets: disclosure intended to build trust can instead reduce market support.

How Does Firm's Innovation Input Influence the Disclosure of Responsible Innovation?

TIM
Min Huang; LI WEN (Renmin University of China)
Responsible innovation (RI) plays a crucial role for companies seeking to align technological progress with societal and ethical standards. However, its disclosure presents a strategic challenge due to the conflict between gaining legitimacy and maintaining a competitive edge. Building on optimal distinctiveness (OD) theory, this research explores how innovation input impacts firms' responsible innovation disclosure (RID) and how the competitive environment—the level of peer RID and competitor density—moderates this relationship. Using data from 22,647 firm-year observations of Chinese A-share listed companies (2008–2019), we uncover an inverted U-shaped connection between innovation input and RID. This suggests that companies initially increase disclosure to build legitimacy with higher investment but eventually reduce it to protect proprietary advantages and reduce spillover risks. Additionally, we find that a higher level of peer RID and competitor density smooth out this relationship, as companies adjust their disclosure strategies to align with industry norms or to mitigate competitive risks. This study adds to the RI and OD literature by combining both internal and external factors influencing disclosure decisions and offering practical guidance for companies balancing stakeholder expectations and competitive challenges.

Obfuscation as Protection: Firm’s Strategic Behavior in Patent Disclosure Quality

TIM
Ning Wang (Tsinghua University); Xibao Li (Tsinghua University)
The disclosure requirement of the patent system creates an inherent tension between protecting intellectual property and disseminating knowledge. This study advances a novel perspective by showing that firms strategically manipulate patent disclosure text to navigate this tension. We find that patents occupying more central positions within a firm’s knowledge network exhibit more obfuscated disclosures, driven by stronger protection incentives. This effect is weakened when alternative protection mechanisms are available: higher technological interdependence raises imitation difficulty and reduces the need for obfuscation. By contrast, competitive intensity strengthens this relationship, as dense technological domains amplify firms’ incentives to shield valuable proprietary knowledge. Together, these findings highlight disclosure quality as a strategic choice in patenting and offer new insights into how the patent system balances knowledge diffusion and innovation incentives.

Patent Visibility and the Diffusion of "Trapped Knowledge"

TIM
Randol Yao (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
Valuable knowledge developed in one part of the world may remain trapped locally due to frictions in how knowledge is exposed and shared globally. This paper examines increasing the visibility of foreign innovations--by granting them US patents--"untraps" knowledge and facilitates global diffusion. Using a difference-in-differences design, complemented by examiner leniency as an instrument, I find that US grants of home country patents significantly increase both the likelihood and intensity of forward citations, including marked increases from third countries. Using a novel measure of an invention's "trappedness," I show that knowledge from historically more trapped countries and sectors sees larger diffusion benefits after the US grants. These findings highlight the central role of the US as a platform of global knowledge diffusion, turning overlooked ideas into globally relevant innovations.

When Transparency Raises the Bar: AI Disclosure and Post-Trial Evaluation

TIM
Sicong LI (The University of Hong Kong)
The rapid diffusion of generative AI creates a strategic communication dilemma: transparency about AI use can signal innovation or invite skepticism about authenticity and creative effort. We study how audiences interpret such disclosure in creative markets using a large dataset of indie games released on Steam after the platform introduced a mandatory AI-disclosure policy in January 2024. We conceptualize platform-mandated disclosure as a governance lever that shapes innovation evaluation during early AI diffusion, when interpretive institutions remain unsettled. Using matching and weighting frameworks, we estimate average differences associated with disclosure on three stages of audience response: initial visibility, engagement intensity, and post-trial evaluative sentiment. Across specifications, we find a consistent market penalty. AI-disclosed titles are about as likely to be sampled, show mixed and often imprecise differences in review intensity, and are evaluated less favorably once experienced. The penalty is attenuated at larger production scale, consistent with diagnosticity dilution whereby extensive conventional content makes the AI cue less diagnostic of overall quality. The study links platform governance to audience-side valuation of AI adoption, identifies when transparency raises evaluative standards, and shows how production scale provides contextual buffering.
