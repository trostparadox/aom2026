More Need, Less Help: Disasters Create A Supply-Demand Paradox In Prosocial Organizational Behavior

OB
Polly Kang; David P. Daniels (National University of Singapore)
A prevailing view in the organizational and social sciences is that when more people need help, more people will step up to provide it (“more need, more help”). However, we theorize and demonstrate that the opposite phenomenon (“more need, less help”) can also occur under certain conditions. We propose that although traumatic shocks (large-scale negative exogenous events) will often increase help-seeking behavior, they can also decrease help-giving behavior – because such high-stakes situations can induce distress not only among help-seekers but also among help-givers, who may find it harder to provide high-effort help to others as a result. We test this view by analyzing data from volunteer crisis counselors at a large nonprofit organization. By empirically comparing disaster days (days when plausibly random major disasters happen) to non-disaster days, we demonstrate that major disasters indeed increase help-seeking behavior while simultaneously decreasing help-giving behavior in a high-stakes situation. This juxtaposition produces a supply-demand paradox in prosocial organizational behavior – specifically, “more need, less help.” We further show that when disasters lead to more fatalities, the effects of disasters on both help-seeking behavior and help-giving behavior are amplified. In placebo tests, as expected, we find no “effects” of major disasters during pre-disaster days, boosting confidence in our causal inference approach. Our findings help uncover a crucial boundary condition: although the prevailing “more need, more help” assumption likely holds in low-stakes situations (consistent with prior research), this assumption can break down when distressing high-stakes situations undermine high-effort prosocial behavior – leading to “more need, less help.”

Helping Under the Spotlight: High Performers’ Envy Attributions, Relational Identification and OCB

OB
Elizabeth Ogbodo (Toulouse I Capitole University); Assâad El Akremi (Toulouse School of Management); Christian Troester (Kühne Logistics University); Ismaël Rafai
High-performing employees (HPEs) contribute disproportionate value to organizations not only through exceptional task performance but also through discretionary helping behaviors toward coworkers. Yet prior research offers mixed evidence regarding HPEs’ engagement in organizational citizenship behavior directed at individuals (OCB-I), portraying them alternately as highly productive “all-stars” or isolated “lone wolves.” We argue that this inconsistency reflects differences in how HPEs interpret coworkers’ reactions to their success, particularly envy, and how these interpretations are shaped by relational context. Drawing on the envy-attribution model and Emotions as Social Information (EASI) theory, we develop a moderated mediation framework in which HPEs’ perceptions of coworkers’ envy, whether benign or malicious, explain when high-performance status translates into OCB-I, and how relational identification shapes these interpretations. Across two vignette experiments (N = 837), a two-wave field study (N = 233), and a laboratory experiment (N = 197), we find convergent evidence that perceiving benign envy increases OCB-I, whereas perceiving malicious envy decreases it. Relational identification selectively reduces perceptions of malicious envy and buffers its negative indirect effect on OCB-I but does not significantly shape perceptions of benign envy or its positive behavioral consequences.

The Power of Help: How Helping Behaviors Build Power in Gig Networks

OB
Liat Eldor; Hagay Volvovsky (Tel Aviv University); Maayan Shoshana Sacher (Tel Aviv University); Joseph Lee (University of Pennsylvania)
Helping behaviors among gig platform workers present a puzzle. Gig platforms are an environment designed to minimize interdependence, isolate workers, and reward only individual task performance in ways that pit workers against one another. Yet gig drivers provide help to peers–offering roadside assistance, sharing information, and tips on how to increase earnings–despite clear costs in time, effort, and competitive disadvantage. Integrating interviews with gig drivers in India and the United States and a decade-long analysis of 2.9 million online interactions among U.S. ride-hailing drivers, we show that helping generates power. Qualitative evidence reveals that helpers become objects of dependence, obligation, and deference. Quantitative analyses demonstrate that helping predicts subsequent increases in network centrality—a structural source of power—net of individual and time fixed effects, prior centrality, and activity levels. These findings reconceptualize helping as a network-building practice that actively generates power, extend social network theory by showing how discretionary prosocial behavior produces structural advantage, and challenge the assumption that helping requires formal organizational rewards.

Proactive Helping, Helper Gender and Status: An Observer-Centric Perspective

OB
Daoming Sun (Wuhan University); liling Liu (Lyon College); Yidong Tu (Wuhan University); Sai Lan (emlyon business school)
Proactive helping represents a distinctive form of prosocial behavior in the workplace which is defined as offering assistance without being asked. It simultaneously conveys prosocial concern and unsolicited initiative, which has led to mixed findings regarding its consequences for helpers. To reconcile these inconsistencies, we adopt an observer-centric perspective to understand the mixed findings between proactive helping and the helper’s status. According to social role theory, we propose that helper gender serves as a key boundary condition shaping observers’ motivational attributions, which in turn determine whether proactive helping enhances or undermines helpers’ status. Across a multi-source field study with matched reports from helpers, coworkers, and supervisors, and a scenario-based experiment, we find support for the hypotheses. These findings clarify when and why proactive helping helps or hurts helpers, advance an observer-centered account of social evaluation, and shifting the prevailing views on the gendered consequences of prosocial behavior.

Exploring the Longitudinal Impact of Leaders’ Wait-to-See Responses on Follower Proactivity

OB
Yueyu Xiong (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
Employees’ continuous engagement in proactive behavior is valuable for organizational survival and improvement. However, sustaining employee proactivity remains a critical challenge for many organizations. In proactivity dynamics, leaders’ responses assume crucial importance: while followers initiate proactive behaviors by themselves, implementing these changes typically necessitates leaders’ support and resources. Focusing on leaders’ wait-to-see responses, this study highlights the significance of leaders’ ambiguous response in followers’ proactivity dynamics yet is largely overlooked in existing research. This study employed a longitudinal design with random-intercept cross-lagged panel modeling (RI-CLPM) to separate between-person effects from within-person effects. Mediation analyses demonstrated that followers’ perceived wait-to-see responses from leaders discourage followers’ future proactive engagement via diminished role breadth self-efficacy (RBSE). This study enhances understanding of proactivity dynamics and provides practical implications for managing employee proactivity.
