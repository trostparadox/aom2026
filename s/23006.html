Analyzing Social Practices

SAP
Rebecca Bednarek (Victoria University of Wellington); Paula Jarzabkowski (The University of Queensland); A. Paul Spee (The University of Queensland)
Qualitative studies grounded in practice theory often generate rich accounts of local activity, yet struggle to explain how these activities constitute larger social phenomena. This paper responds to growing calls for methodological tools that enable researchers to move beyond micro-isolationism and theorize broader patterns without reverting to abstract, higher-order explanations. We develop an analytical framework based on a site ontology, developed by practice theorists Theodore Schatzki, to show how localized doings and sayings can be used to build explanations of larger social phenomena. The paper distils three advanced analytical moves from empirical exemplars: site-crafting, which defines the unit of analysis as a nexus of practice; site-connecting, which traces how dispersed practices are connected across time and space; and site-turning, which layers and juxtaposes multiple sites to deepen explanation. Together, these moves provide qualitative researchers with an analytical framework for theorizing how large-scale social phenomena are enacted through situated localized activities.

Transcending the Single-Practice Paradigm: A Model of Multi-Practice Diffusion

SAP
Andrew Sarta (York University); Angela Aristidou (UCL and Stanford DEL/HAI)
When government policies mandate multiple new practices within a sector, these practices often diffuse unevenly to create differences where some organizations generate societal benefits and superior business outcomes. Despite this, practice diffusion theory is limited in explaining how multiple practices diffuse, as most studies focus on single practice diffusion. To overcome this limitation, we propose a multi-practice approach to diffusion. Our article develops this approach through a longitudinal field study in which a government mandate initiated the diffusion of 14 new practices to 50 healthcare organizations in Ontario, Canada, from 2019 to 2021. By allowing multiple practices to emanate from multiple sources, our empirical study reveals a previously unacknowledged mechanism of new practice diffusion, the generation mechanism, which arises unexpectedly from local community stakeholders.

Telemedicine as Sociomaterial Practice: Digital Apartheid in Brazilian Public Health

SAP
ALESSANDRO PEREIRA RAMOS (Federal University of Santa Catarina); Artur Rocha Silva (Federal University of Santa Catarina); Eduardo Guedes Villar (Federal Institute of Education, Science and Technology of Santa Catarina); Rosalia Aldraci Barbosa Lavarda (Federal University of Santa Catarina)
This study investigates the insertion of telemedicine in the Brazilian Public Health System (SUS), utilizing the case of the health care system in Joinville, south region of Brazil, through the lenses of Strategy as Practice (SAP) and sociomateriality. By analyzing management reports and municipal documents, we demonstrate how technological artifacts—specifically Unified Electronic Health Records and Business Intelligence systems—transcend their roles as support tools to become constitutive elements of organizational strategy. The findings reveal that these artifacts exert material agency, disciplining the medical gaze and reconfiguring power dynamics within public health management. However, this digitalization produces a "digital apartheid," where algorithmic efficiency privileges connected users while marginalizing vulnerable populations lacking digital literacy or devices. This phenomenon challenges the constitutional principles of universality and equity. We conclude that public health strategy is a sociomaterial practice where technology co-produces organizational reality, necessitating sociotechnical governance to mitigate exclusion.

Instigating Serendipity: Analogous Mapping of an Artistic Practice to Strategic Management

SAP
Stefan Meisiek (The University of Sydney); Robert D Austin (Ivey School of Business)
Within strategic management, serendipity emerges as a critical yet underexplored phenomenon. Recent scholarship emphasizes that serendipity is not mere luck; rather, it involves a process of recognizing and acting upon unexpected cues. Yet, this proves to be a one-sided account of serendipity when comparing it to the literature on creative art practices. Some artists and designers cultivate their ability to respond to serendipity, while others instigate serendipity to harness chance for their making processes. To extend the theory of serendipity for strategic management, we are analogically mapping the artistic instigating of serendipity to the literature on multimodal strategic practices. This indicates how strategists can beneficially instigate serendipity by giving agency to accidents for discovery and innovation within the boundaries of avoiding risk and dangerous outcomes.

Artificial Intelligence in Strategy-as-Practice: Rethinking Tools and Roles

SAP
Tien Dung (Jack) Le (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Jane Kirsten Le (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management)
Many organizations now position themselves as AI frontrunners, yet strategizing often unfolds in the absence of clear plans, stable roles, or shared direction. This study examines how strategizing persisted during a non-profit organization–wide rollout of generative AI tools. Although the initiative was framed as transformational, internal actors navigated technological opacity and organizational ambiguity without centralized coordination or agreed evaluative criteria. Adopting a practice-theoretical lens, we show how sociomaterial tools enabled continued engagement and action while failing to stabilize strategic direction. Rather than anchoring coordination or producing convergence, these tools supported participation, sustained momentum, and deferred closure. We conceptualize this condition as strategic persistence without convergence, advancing understanding of emergent strategy as a sociomaterial accomplishment that can endure even when coherence, alignment, and institutionalization remain suspended.
