Session overview

This symposium examines the persistent gap between the ideal of integrated care and the reality of how care and services are experienced and delivered in practice. Across health and social service settings, integration has become a dominant aspiration--invoked to promise continuity, coordination, and person-centeredness for people with complex needs. Yet despite widespread organizational reforms, partnerships, and innovations undertaken in the name of integration, fragmentation remains a defining feature of many patients’ and clients’ lived experiences. Rather than treating integration as an achieved organizational state, the studies in this symposium interrogate how integration is imagined, enacted, and experienced---and why it so often falls short of its ideals. Collectively, the papers show that the distance between integrated ideals and integrated reality becomes visible when integration is examined as a subjective, lived phenomenon rather than a structural or programmatic one. Across contexts, including cancer care, care for people experiencing homelessness, and chronic disease management in federally qualified health centers, formal arrangements that appear integrated on paper do not necessarily translate into experiences of coherence, inclusion, or continuity. Patients and clients may encounter care that is nominally coordinated yet experientially fragmented, revealing that integration is not simply something organizations declare or design, but something people must feel and recognize in order for it to exist in any meaningful sense.
