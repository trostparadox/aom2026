Session overview

Learning is essential to the successful launch and growth of a new product or venture (Agarwal, Camuffo, Gambardella, Sonka, & Valentine, 2025; Allen, 2025; Moeen, Agarwal, & Shah, 2020; Nickerson & Zenger, 2004; Peterson & Wu, 2021), particularly for entrepreneurs and innovators who, by nature of their pursuits, frequently develop solutions to complex problems in the face of highly uncertain environments (Packard, Clark, & Klein, 2017). One common learning approach often prescribed to entrepreneurs is learning by experimenting, a pseudo-scientific approach to entrepreneurship in which entrepreneurs develop causal theories about potential futures (Felin & Zenger, 2017), run quasi-scientific experiments to test those theories and learn about their environments (Camuffo et al., 2020, 2024; Gans, Stern, & Wu, 2019), and sometimes pivot based on the outcomes of those theories (Pillai, Goldfarb, & Kirsch, 2019; Valentine, Novelli, & Agarwal, 2024). In the end, such work suggests that entrepreneurs gain considerable advantages by taking a learning approach to the market. Yet learning does not always do what we think it does. Research in entrepreneurship and strategy points to potential flaws and limitations in both the agents doing the learning and the procedural approaches that entrepreneurs take to learning itself. On the one hand, management scholars have long acknowledged the cognitive limitations of human decision makers, particularly the limitations of human perception, memory, and computational power (Simon, 1947, 1955, 1956), cognitive objectivity and ability to accurately process external stimuli (Gavetti, Levinthal, & Rivkin, 2005; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974), and the ability to accurately conceptualize the set of problems and (potentially future) solutions themselves (Gavetti & Levinthal, 2000; Felin, Kauffman, Koppl, & Longo, 2014). On the other hand, recent work points to potential flaws and limitations in the learning processes themselves (Argyris, 1977; 1982; Agarwal, Camuffo, Gambardella, Sonka, & Valentine, 2025), including the limitations of trying to learn customer preferences with low-quality early products (Peterson, 2025) or high-quality novel products (Allen, 2025). Both streams of research highlight the important fact that, despite the observed importance of entrepreneurial learning, firms may encounter learning traps, which are situations in which they believe their actions have revealed market information, but that information is wrong or misleading in its representation of actual or possible market characteristics and dynamics. The purpose of this symposium is to explore four potential learning traps. First, LaMarr & Valentine examine what they call experimental gravity, a demand-based trap which arises from the structural aspects of demand such as heterogeneous and potentially overlapping preferences of a prospective customer base (Adner, 2002). Peterson examines what he calls the mediocrity trap, a supply-side trap which arises when entrepreneurs attempt to learn from market feedback generated by the introduction of low-quality products. Allen examines a potential social learning trap that arises from the nature of the diffusion of innovation, particularly the challenge of predicting the total demand for a novel product before that product has fully diffused. Lastly, Agarwal and colleagues examine the implications of using entrepreneurial approaches that prescribe certain actions based on ontological assumptions that may not fit the entrepreneur’s actual environment, offering a perspective on learning traps related faulty assumptions about the world.
