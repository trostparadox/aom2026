Government Contracting and Venture Capital Exit Outcomes

ENT
Douglas Cumming (Stevens Institute of Technology); David Jay (Florida Atlantic University); Masim Suleymanov (University of Houston)
While prior research highlights the role of government customers in shaping firm performance and information quality, little is known about how these dynamics influence VC-backed companies. Using a large sample of VC investments, we show that portfolio companies receiving government contracts after VC investment are more likely to achieve successful exits via initial public offerings (IPOs) or acquisitions and are less likely to be liquidated. The effect is moderated by suppliers’ bargaining power with government agencies: firms with weaker bargaining power benefit more in IPO exits, while those with stronger bargaining power see greater improvements in acquisition and survival outcomes.

Government Promotion, Talent Misallocation, and the Fad Trap in Chinese Entrepreneurship

ENT
Song Wang (Zhejiang University); Liaodan Zhang
This study explores how government promotion creating a resource-munificent context unintentionally triggers an entrepreneurial fad. We argue that such promotional policies disproportionately incentivize less capable individuals to launch new ventures yet conversely amplifies financing, innovation, and survival among capable individuals. Exploiting the rollout of China’s Mass Entrepreneurship and Innovation policy in June 2015 as a quasi-natural experiment, we causally identify that government promotion increases entry among less talented individuals but concentrates performance gains among talented individuals. Difference-in-differences estimates corroborate these results. By demonstrating how resource munificence misallocates talent and triggers an unintended fad, we highlight the complexity of government promotion: policies designed to stimulate entrepreneurship may reshape rather than simply enlarge the entrepreneurial field in emerging economies.

How Government Contracting Shapes Venture Capital Staging and Syndication

ENT
Masim Suleymanov (University of Houston); Douglas Cumming (Stevens Institute of Technology)
This paper studies how private firms’ government contracting activity shapes the staging and syndication decisions of their venture capital investors. Government contracts simultaneously improve firms’ information quality and expose them to political risk, generating opposing incentives for venture capital investors. Using a comprehensive sample of U.S. venture capital financing rounds matched with federal procurement data, we show that investments in government contractors exhibit more frequent staging and greater syndication intensity overall. These effects are concentrated in follow-on financing rounds and are driven by contracts awarded through competitive bidding, consistent with venture capitalists responding to heightened political risk as informational frictions diminish. In contrast, government contracting is associated with less staging and syndication in initial investments, particularly when contracts are awarded through non-competitive bidding, where signaling benefits are strongest and political risk is lower. The results are robust to alternative specifications, propensity score matching, and instrumental-variable approaches. Taken together, the findings highlight how information production and political risk jointly shape the structure of venture capital investments.

The Entrepreneurial State? Promotion Incentives, Bureaucratic Risk Preferences and Government VC

ENT
Dilan Yang (University of Oxford); Yiran Xu (University of Oxford)
We study a core mechanism underlying state entrepreneurship: how bureaucratic promotion incentives shape public officials’ willingness to bear risk. We exploit a sharp institutional feature of the cadre system in China — city mayors become effectively ineligible for further promotion once they reach age 58 — to identify the causal effect of promotion incentives in a regression discontinuity design. We proxy risk preferences using revealed behaviour in government VC investments, focusing on investments that are typically riskier: early-stage deals, deals without private co-investors, and investments in new local industries. We find officials with promotion incentives undertake substantially more government VC investments, with the increase concentrated in these high-risk categories. These effects are robust to balance tests, permutation placebos, and alternative specifications. We then examine longer-run consequences using patent outcomes of government VC-backed firms over the subsequent five years. On average, greater risk-taking does not yield robust improvements in innovation. We further document heterogeneity in treatment effects. Longer-tenured mayors respond less strongly to promotion incentives and generate higher-quality innovation, while less capable mayors respond by taking greater risks, which worsens the average innovation performance of government VC–backed firms.

Too Much of a Good Thing? Government Venture Capital and Early-Stage Venture Success

ENT
Haoqi Sun (Peking University); Hong Zhang (Soochow University); shuo xu (Peking University); Shihua Chen (Peking University); Boqun Zeng (Peking University)
Does government venture capital promote or inhibit the listing of new ventures? Prior research has generated mixed views and findings on this important question. To address this puzzle, we propose that incentive alignment and incentive distortion between government venture capital and portfolio firms exert differential effects on venture success. Using a panel dataset of Chinese venture capital investments, we show that government venture capital is positively related to early-stage venture success, but exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship as the intensity of government participation increases. Furthermore, we find that venture capital experience weakens the negative impact of government venture capital on venture success and flattens the inverted U-shaped relationship between government venture capital and venture success. These findings reconcile prior inconsistencies regarding the effects of government venture capital and provide a novel analytical framework for the government venture capital literature.
