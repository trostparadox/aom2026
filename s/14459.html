Session overview

Psychological ownership—the feeling that an idea is 'mine'—is central to creative work, fueling commitment, effort, and the motivation to champion ideas through implementation. Yet ownership is a double-edged sword: it can also trigger territorial behavior, reluctance to collaborate, and defensiveness toward feedback. Despite recognition of ownership's dual nature, research has primarily treated it as a static, individual-level construct, overlooking the social and temporal dynamics that shape how ownership develops and manifests. This symposium addresses this gap by presenting four papers that integrate relational, temporal, technological, and observer perspectives in the study of psychological ownership. Specifically, this symposium asks one central question: How do social interactions and temporal dynamics shape the development, expression, and consequences of psychological ownership across the creative process—from ideation to championing to credit attribution? The four papers examine this question using complementary approaches and methods, including a historical case study of the Lennon-McCartney partnership revealing how ownership claims shift as creative relationships evolve, a three-wave field study investigating how ownership shapes feedback-seeking from high-status versus lower-status advisors, a field experiment demonstrating that beginning ideation independently before incorporating AI increases ownership and championing intentions, and a study of third-party observers examining when idea similarity becomes perceived as 'idea theft.' The symposium will conclude with a discussion led by Dr. Markus Baer, who will integrate the papers, highlight boundary conditions and mechanisms, and identify future directions for research on psychological ownership in creative contexts. Keywords: psychological ownership, idea ownership, creativity, collaboration, feedback-seeking, artificial intelligence
