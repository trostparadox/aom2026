Becoming the Entrepreneurial Self Through Others: Tensions in Identity Construction

ENT
Tiantian Li (University of Stuttgart)
Where people work profoundly shapes their attitudes and behaviors. In particular, open creative labs, such as coworking spaces expose founders to diverse resources and social interactions, shaping both entrepreneurial actions and their self-conception as entrepreneurs. Yet, the mechanisms through which these spaces shape founders’ self-conception remain relatively unexplained. Through a two-year qualitative study of first-time founders within coworking spaces, we find that entrepreneurial identity construction is deeply intertwined with their interactions with the resources (physical, social, financial, and knowledge-related) and tensions (structural, cooperative, interpersonal, and value-based) within coworking spaces. Specifically, we found that founders who actively utilized the social resources of coworking spaces came to more readily embrace an entrepreneurial identity. In contrast, founders who experienced value-based tensions within these spaces question their identity as entrepreneurs, and in some cases, drop out of coworking spaces. Based on these findings, we theorize a process model detailing the dynamic interplay between resources, tensions and entrepreneurial identity construction in coworking spaces. Our study highlights the positive impact of open creative labs and their dark side, providing an integrated understanding of how open creative spaces shape an individual’s entrepreneurial identity development.

Coworking Spaces in Marginalized Communities: An Inclusive Ecosystem Perspective

ENT
Farzana Chowdhury; Stephanie A. Fernhaber (Butler University); Ronia Hawash (Butler University); C. Daniel Graham Sr.
We explore how coworking spaces can be a contributing factor in an entrepreneurial ecosystem. While the entrepreneurial ecosystem literature has focused on market-based structures, competition, and resource flows, there is limited understanding of how coworking spaces contribute to entrepreneurial activity in marginalized communities. Based on community logic theory and qualitative data from an in-depth case study of a community-centered coworking space in the U.S. Midwest, we find that intentional design of space, relational leadership, and community culture help create relational infrastructure in which trust, psychological safety, and shared purpose shape both entrepreneurial action and community-level transformation. These findings offer insights into designing inclusive, place-based ecosystems that support entrepreneurship while strengthening community well-being rather than relying solely on competitive intensity.

Learning in the Margins: How Informal Interactions Shape Entrepreneurship in a Diverse Incubation

ENT
Dugassa Tessema Tessema Gerba (Aalto University); Patrick D. Shulist (Aalto University)
This study explores how informal, day to day interactions within a cross-cultural incubation space shape entrepreneurial practice. Drawing on in-depth qualitative data from an incubator in Ethiopia, a country marked by rich diversity and deep social complexity, we trace how participants developed greater cognitive openness, adapted their behaviours, and built relational solidarity. These intertwined processes facilitate more inclusive collaboration and enhance entrepreneurs’ ability to identify opportunities and navigate complex socio-cultural landscapes. Our findings show that sustained informal intercultural interactions within an incubator help entrepreneurs move beyond entrenched assumptions, learn effectively through experimentation, and build trust across ethnic and regional divides. This trust, in turn, supports collaborative ventures, opens pathways to broader markets, and encourages innovative business models. Grounded in practice theory, the study advances our understanding of entrepreneurship as a socially embedded and culturally shaped practice. We offer practical recommendations for incubator design and policy aimed at fostering inclusive entrepreneurial ecosystems in ethnically diverse emerging economies. Key words: Informal interactions, incubation, entrepreneurial practice, Ethiopia

Spaces of Entrepreneurial Well-Being: A Study of a Wellness-Focused Incubator Program

ENT
Hanna Kling (University of Jyväskylä); Monika E. Von Bonsdorff (University of Jyväskylä); Miia Sainio (University of Jyväskylä); Marke Kivijärvi (University of Jyväskylä)
Research on employee well-being inadequately informs how entrepreneurs’ well-being can be advanced, as entrepreneurs face unique stressors and operate in distinct organizational contexts. Drawing on Lefebvre’s spatial triad framework, we investigate how incubator programs function as spaces for constructing entrepreneurial well-being. We followed eight entrepreneurs over one year in a wellness-focused Finnish incubator program. We analyzed how entrepreneurs constructed their well-being through physical arrangements, social interactions, and subjective experiences within the incubator environment. Our findings reveal that even with limited participation in formal activities, the incubator fostered well-being through its environment, interactions, and visible wellness cues. Using Lefebvre’s spatial triad, we demonstrate that entrepreneurial well-being emerged collectively within the incubator’s “lived space” rather than from individual effort alone. These findings reframe entrepreneurial well-being as spatially and socially constructed, suggesting that support organizations can foster well-being without intensive interventions. The study advances entrepreneurial well-being research by demonstrating how support is spatially constructed in organizational environments, indicating that incubators should create spaces that normalize well-being alongside traditional business development activities.

When Complementary Assets Matter: Contextual Contingencies in Business Incubator Performance

ENT
Luiz Guerrazzi; Jorge Miguel Lopo Gonçalves Andraz; Dinis Caetano; Vanessa Vasconcelos Scazziota (University of Evora)
This study examines how complementary assets shape entrepreneurial survival in business incubation, extending complementary assets theory by theorizing institutional context as a boundary condition of asset effectiveness in early venture development. Drawing on data from 161 business incubators operating in Brazil and Portugal, we analyze how three categories of complementary assets—infrastructure, formalization, and legitimation services—relate to the three-year post-incubation survival of entrepreneurial ventures. Using cluster analysis and non-parametric tests, we find that specialized complementary assets consistently outperform generic infrastructure provision in supporting entrepreneurial survival. However, asset effectiveness is institutionally contingent. In weaker institutional environments, infrastructure assets play a compensatory role by alleviating market failures and resource constraints faced by nascent entrepreneurs. In contrast, in more developed institutional settings, formalization services exhibit stronger performance associations, reflecting higher regulatory complexity and managerial demands during venture scaling. These findings contribute to entrepreneurship research by clarifying why incubation outcomes vary across contexts and by distinguishing between universally valuable and context-dependent forms of entrepreneurial support. More broadly, the study advances complementary assets theory by empirically demonstrating how institutional environments condition the value of assets critical to new venture survival. The results offer analytically generalizable insights into how incubation models should be aligned with institutional conditions to effectively support entrepreneurial ventures.
