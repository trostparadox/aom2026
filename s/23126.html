Behind the Wall: An Integrative Review of Defensiveness at Work

CM
Jeffrey Yip (Simon Fraser University); Dayna Herbert Walker (University of Colorado-Colorado Springs)
Defensiveness is a pervasive challenge in organizational life. It emerges when leaders resist feedback, when teams deflect accountability, and when organizations develop routines that insulate them from change. While management research treats defensiveness as a negative response, psychological research reveals a more complex picture, one in which defensiveness serves protective and adaptive roles for individuals. This paper integrates insights from both disciplines to offer a more dynamic perspective on the mechanisms of defensiveness in organizations. Based on an integrative review of 323 peer-reviewed articles, we address four primary questions: What is defensiveness? What triggers defensiveness? How does defensiveness unfold in organizations? What are effective ways to manage defensiveness? Our research uncovers a core paradox: defensiveness may offer short-term relief to individuals, but it creates dysfunction in the systems it aims to protect. Understanding this paradox is key to breaking the cycle.

Rumination or Reappraisal? Employees’ Differentiated Responses to Customer Anger Expressions

CM
Jiyu Li; Chen-Yuan Wang (East China Normal University)
The existing literature presents conflicting perspectives on the impact of customer anger expressions on employee behavior. This study, grounded in the cognitive appraisal theory of stress and coping, proposes differentiated coping strategies among employees in response to customer anger. Using a situational experimental study (Study 1) and a three-phase time-lagged survey (Study 2), the findings reveal that customer anger expressions positively interact with employees' cognitive reappraisal, enhancing their problem-solving pondering. When employees exhibit higher cognitive reappraisal, they engage more significantly in problem-solving. Conversely, customer anger expressions also interact positively with expressive suppression, leading to increased affective rumination; employees with higher expressive suppression experience greater affective rumination. Moreover, problem-solving pondering mediates the relationship between customer anger expressions and proactive customer service performance, while affective rumination mediates the relationship between customer anger expressions and service sabotage. These findings provide a nuanced understanding of the differentiated effects of customer anger expressions on employee behavior, offering valuable insights for employee management and organizational customer service strategies.

How Leader Coping with Team Territorial Infringement Shapes Employee Emotions and Work Disengagement

CM
Xingwen Chen (Fudan University); Zhiying Shi (Fudan University); Zhenguo Cheng (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Zheng Zhu
Teams establish and defend their own territories within organizations, yet interteam interactions frequently blur these boundaries. What happens when employees perceive that their team’s territory has been infringed? Drawing on the appraisal theory of emotion, we propose that leader’s copying strategies play a critical role in shaping employees’ emotional and behavioral responses when a team’s territory is infringed. Specifically, we posit that when leaders respond to such infringement with avoidance, employees are more likely to experience anger toward the leader, whereas leader confrontational coping is more likely to elicit admiration. These emotional reactions will consequently influence follower’s disengagement to the work. Results from a field survey and a scenario experiment support most of our hypotheses. Our research uncovers the importance of leader’s coping strategies in managing territorial dynamics and provides important implication for future research and practice.

How Does Leader Anger in Response to Employee Transgressions Influence Leader Effectiveness?

CM
Matthew Lupoli (Monash University); Bo Shao (Deakin University); Nathan Eva (Monash University)
To denounce and disincentivize ethical transgressions, leaders may respond with anger toward transgressors. How will leader anger expressions in these contexts influence their perceived leader effectiveness? In four studies, we show that the impact of leader anger on leader effectiveness hinges on the punishment of transgressors and the guilt those transgressors display. In Studies 1 and 2, we present causal evidence that transgressor punishment and guilt, respectively, decrease the effectiveness of leader anger. In Study 3, we identify a mechanism: leaders who express anger in the presence of punishment and/or guilt are seen as less benevolent, which, in turn, drives leader effectiveness downward. Finally, in Study 4, we document a boundary condition by showing that punishment causes angry leaders to be viewed as less effective only when that anger display is strong. Collectively, these results underscore the importance of a nuanced consideration of the role of anger in leadership.
