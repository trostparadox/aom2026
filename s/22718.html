When Digitalization Manufactures Distance: Rethinking Supply Chain Governance

OSCM
Piyush Shah (Florida Gulf Coast University); Irita Mishra (University of Wisconsin-River Falls); Senali Amarasuriya (Middle Tennessee State University)
Core supply chain design levers such as centralization, formalization, and standardization have historically been enacted through direct buyer-supplier interaction. Digital technologies in this context have largely been assumed to function as neutral supports for these governance structures by reducing geographic and informational distance without altering the underlying logic of coordination or control. However, we argue that digitalization can also generate new forms of separation by reshaping how interorganizational interaction is organized. We introduce the concept of digitally induced distance and distinguish three forms: buyer-access distance, time-compression distance, and capability distance. We theorize how these distances condition the effectiveness of centralization, formalization, and standardization in shaping coordination, scalability, and participation outcomes. By reframing digital technologies as sources of structural distance rather than purely coordination-enhancing tools, this paper contributes to research on organizational design and supply chain governance and explains why formally sound governance arrangements may yield uneven outcomes under digitalization.

Corporate Strategy Integration and Operational Efficiency: Power Consumption in Multi-Unit Firms

OSCM
Serghei Musaji (University of New Brunswick); Daniel Andrews (Georgia State University); Deepa Goradia (Georgia State University)
While operations management (OM) research has extensively examined integration across supply chains and inter-organizational networks, far less is known about how integration functions with corporate hierarchies, where ownership and authority coexist with coordination frictions. We investigate how corporate structure enables improvements in resource utilization efficiency in multi-unit firms. We conceptualize corporate strategy integration as a parent-driven capability that allows corporate parents to detect cross-unit performance patterns, codify routines, and orchestrate portfolio-wide implementation. We theorize that three first-order structural characteristics—portfolio size, organizational distance, and unit-level TMT interlocks—jointly determine how effectively corporate parents convert integration opportunities into operational efficiency gains. Using monthly power consumption data (2012-2016) for 215 Canadian business units of multi-unit firms, we find that units embedded in larger portfolios consume less power. These efficiency gains are strongest when units are organizationally proximate to the parent and diminish when units maintain extensive internal and external TMT interlocks. Our study advances OM research by demonstrating how corporate hierarchies uniquely shape integration mechanisms. We show that structural characteristics are critical yet often overlooked levers that can facilitate or hinder operational efficiency across business units.

From Disclosure to Value Creation: Environmental Reporting, Supply Chain Governance, and Performance

OSCM
Deepak Sardana (RMIT University); Narain Gupta (Birmingham City University); Krish Saha
This study examines whether environmental reporting functions merely as a transparency device or whether it constitutes a strategically meaningful governance capability that shapes firm performance. Drawing on the resource-based view and signalling theory and using secondary data from the S&P Global ESG Scores for 2022 and objective firm-level revenue for 2023, we analyse a global sample of 994 firms. Environmental reporting is captured through disclosure coverage and third-party assurance, while SCM is measured using multiple indicators of supply chain governance, risk management, and transparency. Mediation and moderated mediation analyses show that the financial value of environmental reporting is realised through supply chain management and conditioned by institutional context.

Managing Paradoxical Tensions in Biodiversity Governance: A Multi-Stakeholder Study of Food Supply

OSCM
Phuong Phan (Aalto University); Katri Kauppi; Seongtae Kim (Aalto University)
Biodiversity loss is emerging as a critical concern for food and beverage (F&B) companies, yet sustainable supply chain research still treats it largely as a secondary dimension of environmental performance. This study examines how F&B firms navigate biodiversity-related paradoxical tensions that arise from the expectations of multiple salient stakeholders. Drawing on paradox theory and the stakeholder salience framework, we ask: (1) What kind of paradoxical tensions do companies in the food supply chains face resulting from different stakeholders’ expectations regarding biodiversity conservation? and (2) How do companies manage these tensions? We conduct a qualitative multi-case, multi-stakeholder study of the Finnish F&B sector, combining 26 semi-structured interviews with managers in focal F&B firms, government officials, NGOs and experts, and major retail and foodservice customers, complemented by policy and corporate documents. Using Gioia-informed analysis, we identify a configuration of five interrelated paradoxes at the company level, and then derive four management strategies showing that firms do not use a single approach but can adopt different strategies across paradoxes. The study contributes to the literature by conceptualizing biodiversity governance as a multi-actor paradox system, as well as by theorizing biodiversity as a distinct and structurally challenging object of supply chain governance rather than a generic environmental topic.

An Autopoietic Systems Perspective on the Use of Power in Coopetition

OSCM
Anni Rajala (University of Vaasa); Annika Laine-Kronberg (Åbo Akademi University)
This study adopts an autopoietic system perspective to explore power in coopetitive business relationships. That perspective recognizes both multilevel and dynamic activity. The current research investigates the use of power tactics over time and considers coercive or non-coercive activities within a system that simultaneously influence, and are influenced by, a coopetitive business relationship. The empirical contribution derives from a qualitative case study of a coopetitive relationship between two multinational firms, one a buyer and the other a supplier. The study contributes to existing research on the systemic nature of business relationships by introducing the value of the autopoietic system perspective, which reveals activities, including the development of competing products and power tactics, to be recursive and connected from a multilevel and dynamic perspective. The findings of the study also complement existing research on power in business relationships by showing how the use of coercive and non-coercive power tactics at different levels relates to the dynamics of the relationship. This study shows that the use of power tactics by one party in a business relationship can drive the other toward developing a new competitive product. That shift will affect the future power tactics applied in the relationship and its very nature. Moreover, we contribute to existing research on coopetition, by increasing knowledge about the relatedness of power tactics on multiple levels and the development of the business relationship concerning cooperation and competition.
