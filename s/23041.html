Embodied Experiences, Archetypal Femininity, and the Un-Taming of the Wild Woman Driver

CMS
Cat Spellman (Durham University Business School); Oonagh Hamilton
Driving work has long been constructed as a masculine domain, shaped by assumptions that privilege physical endurance, emotional stoicism, and risk tolerance. While existing research has documented the structural barriers women face in such occupations, it has tended to foreground constraint, adaptation, and deficit, offering limited insight into the symbolic, embodied, and imaginative dimensions of women’s agency. This article addresses this gap by developing archetypes as feminist theoretical tools for understanding identity, embodiment, and agency in masculinised work. Drawing on qualitative, semi-structured interviews with women working across a range of driving roles in the UK, we combine Jungian archetype theory with embodiment theory to explore how women make sense of, inhabit, and reconfigure gendered work. We focus on the Wild Woman archetype as a symbolic figure through which participants articulated instinct, intuition, bodily knowledge, and resistance to domestication within male-dominated occupational spaces. Our findings show how women’s experiences of driving are shaped not only by structural inequality, but also by embodied memory, intuitive expertise, emotional labour, and the reclamation of space. By grounding archetypal analysis in lived, corporeal experience, we demonstrate how archetypes can illuminate forms of agency that are affective, imaginative, and symbolic, yet materially situated. The article contributes to feminist organisational scholarship by extending theories of identity work and embodiment, and by offering archetypes as a novel lens for interpreting women’s experiences in masculinised labour beyond narratives of coping or conformity.

Autonomy Assumed, Inequality (Re-)Produced? Rethinking Cooperative Governance Relationally

CMS
Jonas Friedrich (University of St. Gallen); Chris Steyaert (University of St. Gallen)
The cooperative governance literature has centered on constituting rights and decision-making rules. We argue that certain basic assumptions—such as the autonomous, rational, liberal subject, often drawn from neoliberal theorizing and some strands of democratic theory, have constrained the theoretical development of governance. By generating alternative assumptions, we propose that the understanding and practice of cooperative governance can be reimagined to address deep-seated inequalities, particularly those related to diversity and ecological concerns. To this end, we develop the concept of “feminist-based decentering,” which draws on three feminist frameworks and their alternative assumptions: the ethics of care, intersectionality, and new materialism. Meta-theoretically, decentering cooperative governance enables a rereading of the cooperative subject as vulnerable, power-sensitive, and interdependent. In doing so, we support a new research agenda that promotes processes and practices that complement existing governance frameworks, fostering the cooperative’s potential to build inclusive and sustainable communities and societies.

What the Fach!? Feminist Disruptions in the Gendering of Voices

CMS
Richard Longman (The Open University)
In the musical world, the Fach system is usually presented as a practical taxonomy for protecting singing voices and organizing casting. This article treats Fach instead as an organizational technology of listening: a classificatory regime that allocates professional futures by making vocal sound administratively legible. Drawing on feminist theory, queer musicology, and critical organization studies, I embrace écriture feminine to create brief, deliberately implausible scenes that place canonical operatic roles alongside interventions by women and queer composers. This demonstrates how classificatory listening falters and makes its governance work audible. Across these scenes, I trace how Fach operates through recurring mechanisms that redistribute responsibility for vocal risk onto singers while stabilising gendered and racialised norms of employability. The article contributes a vocabulary for analysing organizational audibility and demonstrates how writing-as-method can surface the limits of classification when voices exceed its terms.

Power Maintenance Through Feminism Appropriation in the Academy

CMS
Natalia A. Vershinina (Audencia Business School); Sophie Alkhaled; Amal Abdellatif
This paper theorizes feminism appropriation as a distinct organizational practice through which institutions and actors deploy feminist rhetoric to accumulate symbolic capital while evacuating feminism of its redistributive potential. Drawing on collaborative autoethnography across three career stages and racialized positionalities in UK and European contexts, we extend Ahmed's (2012) concept of non-performativity by showing that feminist language functions not merely as hollow speech but as productive infrastructure for power maintenance—what we term the feminist façade. Through intersectional analysis of critical incidents, we identify feminism appropriation as operating through two interlocking tactics: procedural weaponization, where transparency rituals legitimate targeted exclusions while extracting emotional labor, and equalist capture, which depoliticizes feminist expertise while enabling opportunistic acquisition of EDI roles once they become promotable. We demonstrate how neoliberal audit culture, academic precarity, and promotion criteria emphasizing measurable service create structural conditions that incentivize appropriation. The contribution is threefold: we theorize feminism appropriation as recognizable organizational practice producing the feminist façade as governance infrastructure; we center affective violence—shame, rage, institutional gaslighting, strategic silence—as organizationally consequential mechanisms through which power is maintained; and we articulate what feminist accountability requires beyond visibility metrics: binding consequences for decision rights, authorship equity, and epistemic recognition, while acknowledging the political resistance such redistribution will face.

(Un)Selving Encounters: Compassioning on City Streets as a Prelude to Feminist Relational Activism

CMS
Katie Beavan (New York University); Line Revsbaek (Aalborg University)
Pragmatism is a living philosophy that continually reshapes its tenets in response to changing social conditions and challenges. Feminism is a responsive philosophy oriented toward engaging, challenging, and transforming enduring inequalities. This paper is a creative experiment in moving through everyday affective landscapes with feminist–pragmatist philosophy. Oriented toward a feminist–pragmatist relational activism, we diffract into and through unselving encounters in public passing on city streets and in workplaces the idea and practice of compassioning as articulated in Buddhist teachings, educating ourselves toward a different kind of relationality. We explore compassioning as a prelude to feminist relational activism: the cultivation of affective and relational capacities without which power-with practices cannot be sustained or received. Drawing on unselving moments in public encounters, we show how professional, feminist, academic, and racialized selves are undone and reassembled in the flow of lived interactions, revealing compassioning as a situated, challenging relational practice. Our everyday inquiry engages the shadows of our time, including the rise of authoritarianism, the resurgence of patriarchy, the crisis of masculinity, and the ongoing whitewashing of colonial histories, treating performed awareness as an empirical field. Feminist– pragmatism emerges here as a living, responsive philosophy that enables us to trace how practices of compassioning prefigure relational activism and open into Anzaldúa’s new mestiza consciousness. In doing so, we seek to aid the renewing of the feminist project through a re-founded relationality among us humans.
