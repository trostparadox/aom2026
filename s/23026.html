Local and Non-lLocal Intuition: Review of Literature, Practical Implications and Integration

MSR
Alex Fergnani (Asian Institute of Management)
This article examines two contrasting scholarly perspectives on intuition: the local view and the non-local view. The local view, dominant in cognitive sciences, psychology, and management studies, conceptualizes intuition as a learned, pattern-based process. In contrast, the non-local view, developed within consciousness studies and reflected in several spiritual traditions, posits that intuition is the ability of a body-brain system to receive non-local signals that transcend conventional spatiotemporal constraints via quantum entanglement. Being similarly experienced, these two forms of intuition are often conflated. Building on these perspectives and to address this conflation, the paper pursues four objectives: (1) to review, synthesize, and integrate the literature on both forms of intuition, outlining differences and similarities; (2) to derive distinct practical implications from each form of intuition for managerial decision-making; (3) to propose an integrative model of intuitive reasoning that delineates antecedents, processes, outcomes, and moderators for both local and non-local intuition, as well as their interaction; and (4) to outline promising research directions for advancing intuition studies in management.

Enfleshed Creative Spirituality: An Embodied Framework for Teaching the Entrepreneurial Imagination

MSR
Wendelin Merlin Küpers; Olimpia Rosales (Tecnologico de Monterrey); Felipe Ignacio Symmes (EGADE Business School)
Building on Merleau-Ponty’s (1995, 2010, 2012) notion of the “flesh” (la chair) as the elemental, intercorporeal tissue that connects all beings, coupled with recent alternative discussions on the entrepreneurial imagination and spirituality in entrepreneurship, we introduce the concept of “enfleshed creative spirituality” as a novel theoretical foundation for understanding and cultivating the entrepreneurial imagination in entrepreneurship education. We define this concept as an imaginative force and mediating practice that fosters entrepreneurship from three sources: the integration of individual bodies, interpersonal embodiment, and ecological corporeality. Each of these sources is grounded in and enacted through an all-embedding, connective flesh as part of a lived spirituality. We argue that this embodied-spiritual integration nurtures three capacities that are essential for releasing the entrepreneurial imagination in more responsive, relational, and generative ways in entrepreneurship education: sensuous perception, affective resonance, and intuitive insight. We then propose pedagogical practices such as art and yoga to illustrate how enfleshed creative spirituality (broadening the scope of spirituality through the notion of embodied imagination) can innovate and enrich entrepreneurship education by fostering a capacity for inquiry and rehearsal as a means to envision and enact alternative desirable futures.

“God Made Me Do It.”: The Role of Affective Piety in Religious Entrepreneurship

MSR
R. Duncan M. Pelly (Fisk University); Oscar Javier Montiel (Universidad Autonoma de Ciudad Juarez); Irery Lizbeth Melchor Durán (Universidad Panamericana); Rosa Canales
This article explores stories of God speaking directly to three entrepreneurs who changed their lives and helped their communities. Although religion in entrepreneurship has been extensively explored, the emphasis remains upon the entrepreneur as the agent. Our research introduces stories whereby God provides direct instruction to entrepreneurs in a way that refusal is not possible. This process by which entrepreneurs receive God’s direct mandates is called affective piety and is a well-known concept in medievalist studies. Through a narrative case design blended with techniques found in sacred biographies and hagiographies, our research explores how affective piety can open the black box of a type of entrepreneurship where God, not the person, is the agent. This manuscript is of use to scholars of affective piety, as well as those of entrepreneurship.

Social Capital, Embeddedness and Entrepreneurial Support System: A Study on the Jain Community

MSR
Prajapat Gajendra Kanaram (Indian Institute of Technology Bombay); Ashish Pandey (Indian Institute of Technology Bombay); Varadraj Bapat (Indian Institute of Technology Bombay)
This qualitative study investigates a religious community-based entrepreneurial support system. Drawing on observations, field visits, secondary sources, and thirty-five semistructured, in-depth interviews with members of the Jain Community, our findings offer a nuanced understanding of how family (Parivar), temple (mandir), and community organisations, along with community orientation, support entrepreneurship. The study provides new insights into how religious communities facilitate access to resources, networks, and spiritual mentorship that contribute to entrepreneurial support. It contributes to social capital by adding a transcendental dimension to social capital, and to the entrepreneurial support system literature and the ongoing conversation on the “theological turn in entrepreneurship” by highlighting a socially embedded, value-based community entrepreneurial support system. Finally, it contributes to the ongoing conversation on the MSR field by bringing religion and spirituality in entrepreneurial support system context.

A Leap of Faith? Understanding the Development of a New World Winery and Wine Market

MSR
Miriam Isabella Cavalcanti Junqueira (Letourneau University); Allan Fernando Discua Cruz (Lancaster University)
This study examines how faith-driven entrepreneurs leading a vineyard in East Texas cultivate a new regional wine market. In a context where viticulture is culturally unfamiliar and ecologically uncertain, there is limited understanding as to how entrepreneurs who aim to integrate their religious values can inform a long-term investment in land, relationships, and community. Using ethnographic methods, including in-situ observation and interviews, this research draws on stewardship, trust, and identity theories to explain how a new world winery and a wine market is built through product quality, relational marketing, and values-based storytelling. The founders' ongoing expansion into hospitality and community engagement reflects faith-informed stewardship in practice, as they integrate Christian values into their business approach. Findings suggest that stewardship, influenced by religious values, functions as a commitment to landscape preservation and as a generative force that facilitates innovation, local learning, and identity formation. These findings contribute to our understanding of how trust, identity, and values coalesce to shape new market norms, offering implications for rural enterprise formation, faith-integrated strategy, and the creation of a place-based identity. This study extends entrepreneurship theory by positioning stewardship within ventures guided by values, faith, and a sense of purpose. It demonstrates how entrepreneurs in culturally hesitant markets align enterprise development with ethical commitments, reinforcing regional identity.
