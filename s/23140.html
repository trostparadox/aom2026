“I See You as a Person, First”: Repurposing Alienation-Based Anxiety Through Person-Centered Rituals

MOC
Alaina Segura (Harvard University); Hilary Marja Hoyt (University of Oxford); Jordan Nye (University of Michigan); Nathan Meikle (University of Kansas); Julia Lee Cunningham (University of Michigan)
Workplace alienation—the disquieting phenomenon of being estranged from the products, purpose, and social meaning of one’s labor—produces anxiety that is both individually felt and collectively consequential. Yet scholarship has devoted more attention to documenting alienation’s harms than to identifying how workers might find relief within the very groups that alienating systems fragment. Drawing on field observations and interviews with current and former employees at a fast-casual restaurant chain, we investigate how brief, scripted group rituals shape the experience of alienation-based anxiety at work. We find that alienation produces three threats to personhood (eclipsing individuality, diminishing self-efficacy, severing connectedness). Person-centered rituals, which direct collective attention toward each member’s humanity, address these threats through a process we call “repurposing anxiety.” Physical gathering initially heightens anxiety, but scripted exchanges of positive regard prompt workers to recognize shared humanity: they realize each other’s personhood beyond role, see contributions acknowledged, and discover relatedness. These realizations produce a release of anxiety, generating shared efficacy, elevated energy, and altered patterns of relating that extend into the workday. We conclude by discussing how person-centered rituals contribute to group relations theory, scholarship on ritual, and broader organizational research examining how employees psychologically and relationally manage oppressive work contexts.

Prioritization as Adaptation: How Demands Compatibility Shapes Team Responses to Multiple Demands

MOC
Hansong Fan (Carlos III University of Madrid); Pedro Marques Quinteiro (Lusofona University); Ramón Rico (Carlos III University of Madrid)
Extant research on team adaptation systematically studies how single demands trigger adaptation, overlooking the occurrence of multiple demands that are often concurring. Drawing on a stimulus-based perspective and team regulation theory, we examine how demands compatibility (i.e. the extent to which team resources can be simultaneously allocated across demands) shapes team adaptation. Across a field and a laboratory study, we find that the effect of demands compatibility on performance depends on demand novelty: incompatible but routine demands can be addressed efficiently, whereas incompatible and novel demands undermine performance through coordination flux. Both studies reveal that team mental model (TMM) updating during operations attenuates the performance differences between teams facing compatible and incompatible demands. Across both studies, our results unpack how compatibility of adaptive demands shaped team performance through compelling teams to adjust cognitively and behaviorally, and how this effect is bounded by demand novelty. In doing so, this research extends our knowledge on team adaptation and regulation in dynamic environments.

The Logic of Capitalism and Meanings of Work: How Marketization Destroys and Creates Meaningfulness

MOC
Xi Chen (University of Guelph); Alim J. Beveridge; Ping Ping Fu (Nottingham University Business School China)
Capitalism propels economic development, improvements in living standards and the adoption of post-materialist values. Yet, the market-centric institutions of capitalism are also strongly linked to materialist and extrinsic values, which generate limited motivation for work effort. Thus, it may have conflicting effects on the meaning individuals ascribe to their work. To resolve the conundrum, we utilize the gradual reform in China’s transitioning economy to understand how the degree of marketization relates to the meanings people ascribe to work. With a survey with a national sample of 1000 Chinese adults, we find that marketization is positively associated with the money and family orientations to work and negatively associated with the society orientation. Whereas the money orientation was negatively related to meaningfulness, the family and society orientations had positive effects. The findings highlight the loss of meaning associated with marketization, reveal the agency of individuals in meaning construction, and contribute to understanding societal influences on meaning of work.

Leader Interpersonal Emotion Management And Workplace Loneliness Amid Societal Crises

MOC
Liuba Belkin (Lehigh University); Michele Williams (University of Iowa); William J. Becker (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Sarah Tuskey (Miami Dade College)
Many employees report heightened emotional strain and a growing sense of loneliness amid a volatile social landscape marked by recurring local and global crises and always-on media connectivity that amplifies and carries these events into daily work. Against this backdrop, leaders’ attention to and management of employee emotions— i.e., interpersonal emotion management (IEM), may be especially consequential for how connected or isolated employees feel at work. In this multi-study investigation, we examine how two leader IEM strategies, reappraisal encouragement and suppression encouragement, relate to employee loneliness through two psychological pathways: emotional exhaustion and organizational identification. Drawing on four different employee samples (N = 1,850) collected in the United States and the United Kingdom at the onset of three distinct crises, we find that reappraisal encouragement is consistently associated with lower exhaustion, stronger identification, and less loneliness, whereas associations for suppression encouragement are more context dependent. Our paper reframes employee workplace loneliness as a dynamic leader-influenced experience, underscoring the importance of subtle leader behaviors in times of disruption and heightened uncertainty and positioning organizational identification as a relational resource.

To Improve Creativity, Don’t Lower Stress—Optimize It

MOC
Olivia Jurkiewicz; Christopher Oveis
People are often tasked with being creative in high-stress situations, and a mixed literature has not yet converged on the role of stress in impacting creativity. Here, we offer new theorizing regarding which aspects of the stress response are likely to help versus harm creativity and provide a model for how to capitalize on stressful situations. We test two interventions that leverage recent advances in the emotion regulation literature: task-is-positive reappraisal, which reframes task demands as positive, and stress-is-beneficial reappraisal, which reframes the stress response as beneficial. Participants developed and delivered their own creative start-up pitch to evaluators in a context that elicited real stress and provided an ecologically valid measure of creativity. Consistent with our theorizing, we found that greater physiological stress was positively associated with creativity, and that the stress-is-beneficial intervention—which preserved the physiological stress response and reframed it as beneficial—improved creativity relative to the control. In contrast, the task-is-positive intervention reduced physiological stress, but did not improve creativity relative to the control. These findings suggest that to improve creativity under pressure, employees should view their stress response as helpful. Additionally, this work gives practitioners guidance on which interventions work best depending on their goals.
