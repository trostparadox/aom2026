Dignity as a Spiritual Practice: Moral Survival in Contemporary Organisations

MSR
Aparna Mira Venkatesan (Independent Researcher)
Contemporary organisations often claim to be meritocratic, inclusive and morally neutral, yet many workers face humiliation, are excluded, and experience their morality erased in daily organisational life. Existing research on workplace spirituality and meaningful work assumes the presence of recognition, agency or voluntary engagement; thereby limiting theorisation of how individuals maintain moral selfhood when dignity is neither acknowledged nor institutionally safeguarded. Through 18 in-depth narrative interviews with Dalit professionals from three generational cohorts within Indian ICT organisations, this study reconceptualises dignity as a spiritual practice enacted under constraint. Rather than framing spirituality as flourishing or wellbeing, the analysis shows that dignity is sustained through moral–spiritual labour: the disciplined ethical effort required to preserve moral coherence in organisational contexts that deny moral worth. The study finds that dignity is practised through distinct, non-linear moral repertoires, including endurance, composure, and refusal across generations. By shifting workplace spirituality from flourishing to moral survival, the study extends MSR scholarship beyond wellbeing frameworks and offers a decolonial intervention that theorises spirituality from contexts of degradation rather than recognition. Dignity, it argues, is not an organisational outcome but an ethical burden unevenly borne by workers.

Kindness as a Unifying Theme in Multiple Moral Traditions Navigating Multilevel Tensions at Work

MSR
Sheila Hanson (University of North Dakota); Phoenix Van Wagoner (California State University-Fullerton)
Kindness serves as a unifying force in a world marked by divisions—globally from east to west and north to south, within organizations between leaders and employees, and amongst colleagues. Although moral traditions, popular media, and personal convictions encourage individuals to act kindly, our findings reveal a paradox: enacting kindness in the workplace often gives rise to tensions. Drawing on moral traditions that elevate kindness as a shared ethical commitment, and on interviews with 142 organizational leaders, we examine how kindness is understood and enacted at work. Through our qualitative analysis, we demonstrate how kindness operates as a compelling force with common elements and nuanced features across moral traditions, while also being complex and pluralistic. When enacting kindness at work, this plurality of kindness contributes to tensions at the personal, relational, and organizational levels. Rather than resolving ethical difficulty, kindness often requires judgment, balance, and ongoing negotiation. By uncovering and unpacking these tensions, we integrate leaders’ lived experiences into a more comprehensive and realistic understanding of kindness at work. In doing so, we offer theoretical and practical insights that explain why kindness is widely valued yet difficult to enact in organizational life.

Holding the Future Open: Collective Hope, Meaning-Making, and Agency Under Sustained Pressure

MSR
Maria Fernandez-Muiños
This study examines how collective hope is constructed and sustained under conditions of sustained pressure and uncertainty. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative study of a Spanish professional football team during the pre-season period, the paper explores how organizational members collectively navigate uncertainty when future outcomes are fragile and success cannot be guaranteed. Rather than conceptualizing hope as an individual psychological state or motivational resource, the study advances a process-oriented understanding of hope as a collective accomplishment grounded in shared meaning-making, moral commitment, and relational practices. The findings show that collective hope operates through a transformation of how time is experienced. Through shared practices of effort, reflection, and mutual support, participants reclaimed the present as meaningful and inhabitable despite uncertainty, enabling coordinated action without reliance on control or optimism. Hope and collective agency emerged as mutually constitutive, sustained through ongoing practices rather than stable capacities or institutional change. By integrating insights on hope, temporality, and agency, this study contributes to research on management, spirituality, and religion by illuminating how collectives sustain meaningful organizing and action under pressure.

Meaning At and Meaning In Work : 4 Empirical Studies in 4 French Industries

MSR
Richard Jackson Major (IGENSIA - RH IGENSIA Education Group); CHRISTOPHE BONNET (Grenoble Alpes University); Nathalie AYET (Grenoble Alpes University); Véronique Blanc-Brude (Grenoble Alpes University); Tran Vu Aurelie Viet Ha (Conservatoire national des Arts et Métiers); Michel Seccia
Building on Pratt and Ashforth’s (2003) conceptualization of Meaning at Work and Meaning in Work, this qualitative paper analyzes four empirical settings and distinctive work environments within the French industry, ranging from heavy metallurgy to microelectronics cleanrooms, in-cluding accounting firms and embedded electronics. Extending the empirical research of Kar and Elangovan (2019), these settings enable deepening and extending the understanding of the two facets of meaning: meaning at work, referring to the work environment, relationships, and workplace community, and meaning in work, which refers to the occupation, activities, and re-sponsibilities of individuals, as well as to the purpose and finality of work. The theoretical framework combines a literature review with a documentary content analysis examining schol-arly discourse on meaning, structured around three themes: crisis of meaning, quest for meaning, and construction of meaning. These themes are then examined considering the dual conceptual-ization of meaning at work and meaning in work (Bonnet et al., 2025a; Commeiras et al., 2022; Morin, 2008; Pratt & Ashforth, 2003), leading to a dimensional conceptualization. The cross-sectional perspective encompasses four distinctive work environments, illustrating diverse mani-festations and dynamics, to apprehend distinctions in meaning as experienced by employees. The empirical results provide several insights, as the dynamics between meaning at work, ori-ented toward collectives and relationships, and meaning in work, oriented toward individuals, responsibilities, and work purpose, vary across environments and populations. In metallurgy, employees’ position upstream or downstream in the production chain determines whether mean-ing is perceived primarily as collective (meaning at work) or individual and purpose-oriented (meaning in work). We identify characteristic features that could define a propensity to perceive one or the other facet of meaning. Among young accountants in training, relative levels of com-petence and autonomy influence their perception of meaning. In a technological company, con-tinuously evolving and transforming, components of meaning At work - trust in employees and a responsibility-oriented culture - foster the expression of individual singularity, including intui-tion, thereby enabling the development of meaning In work. Finally, in the microelectronics industry, a clinical study of work analyzes the loss of meaning experienced by cleanrooms pro-duction technicians facing the despatialization of their supervision activities. Meaning is recon-structed through a subjective reappropriation of gestures, tools, and systems, showing that indi-viduals create and recreate meaning dynamically when confronted with transformations of their work and their trade. Though these short sections only just provide a condensed rendition of each research project as they relate to meaning at/in work, the four contrasting empirical set-tings and organizational situations concretely illuminate conceptualizations of meaning at/in work and managerial and HR implications. Work environments that generate both meaning at work and meaning in work have the potential of providing employees with reasons for higher levels of both engagement and fulfillment at work.

Linking Spiritual Wellbeing with Flow Experience at Work: A Conceptual Analysis

MSR
Siddharth Garg (Management Development Institute Gurgaon); Sumita Rai Singh (Management Development Institute Gurgaon)
The aim of this paper is to discuss how employees with high levels of spiritual wellbeing can more easily enter their flow state and engage with their job through the mediating role of positive emotions. We position our work in the burgeoning literature on spirituality and performance in organizations (Karakas, 2010; Van der Walt & De Klerk, 2014; Rocha & Pinheiro, 2021). Using Fredrickson’s Broaden and Build Theory (Fredrickson, 1998; Fredrickson, 2001), we argue positive emotions build both cognitive resources and resilience which in build the employee’s ‘flow channel’. This flow channel acts as a psychological buffer against anxiety arising from job-related tasks above the employee’s current skill level. Thus, flow channel allows the employee to take on more challenging job-related tasks and enter the flow state. We conclude our paper by highlighting future directions for research and takeaways for practionners.
