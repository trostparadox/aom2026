Establishing a Robust Collectivistic Confucian Culture Career Ecosystem: Harmonized Academic Careers

CAR
Trang Vu Gardner (University of Southampton Business School); Yehuda Baruch (University of Southampton)
With significant growth in the relevance and size of the academic labor market, we aim to explore the global academic career landscape in the understudied geo-location and culture of Confucianism. Using the theoretical lens of the Career Ecosystem Theory, we explore the ways in which academics navigate their contemporary careers, and the roles of other career actors in academics’ career trajectories. Employing an inductive interpretivist methodology, we conducted interviews with 41 academics to explore how they perceive their academic careers. Our model depicts the role and impact of collectivistic Confucian culture on academic careers. Our findings suggest that, in Confucian work culture, the focus for career development is significantly associated with fostering the harmonization of a complex web of work- and non-work-related relationships between academics and various actors. Fostering harmonization was identified as a key factor for establishing a robust career ecosystem. This expands the Career Ecosystem Theory through a global perspective and introduces the construct of harmonization as a significant culture-based factor for developing sustainable careers. We also offer managerial implications to academics and universities and suggest a future research agenda.

Understanding Ambiguity in Academic Promotion Criteria: Organizational View of Career Script Signals

CAR
Adam Keeley (Maynooth University); Olga Ryazanova (Maynooth University); Peter McNamara (Maynooth University)
The expectations that have to be met by academics to achieve career advancement remain unclear, causing confusion and uncertainty for those seeking promotion. The diversity of requirements for delivering outcomes in areas of research, teaching and service can be overwhelming. Meanwhile, career management literature tends to offer advice that does not match the complexity of the promotion phenomenon. We explore promotion policies in 163 universities across six English-speaking countries, using the conceptual tools of signaling theory and career scripts literature to understand the organizational logic behind the choice of policy format and contents. Our analysis of 359 individual rank promotion documents finds that institutions signal eight categories of performance with 41 individual criteria. Despite the much-discussed trend for quantification of academic performance, we found that institutions overwhelmingly use ambiguous qualitative metrics to assess individuals for promotion. We discuss strategic and sector-specific reasons for this ambiguity and argue that the lack of clarity, while being challenging for individuals, seeks to protect academic freedom, respect the diversity of disciplinary norms, and support positive change towards societal impact creation.

Success Misalignment in Academia: When Institutional Metrics Diverge from Scholars’ Values

CAR
Lora Louise Broady; Bettina Maisch (HM Hochschule München University of Applied Sciences); Barbara Wolf (University of Munich); Leslie Bailey Streissguth (Le Moyne College); Kate Woods (King's College London)
Academic careers are increasingly evaluated through narrow institutional performance metrics such as publications, grants, and prestige, even as growing evidence suggests that many scholars define success in broader, more human-centered terms. This study examines the misalignment between Traditional Performance Metrics (TPM) and Holistic Flourishing Measures (HFM) in contemporary academic careers. Using a mixed-methods survey of 117 academics across career stages, disciplines, and national contexts, we investigate (1) how academics prioritize different success criteria, (2) the extent to which personal definitions of success diverge from perceived institutional success cultures, and (3) how this misalignment relates to overall career satisfaction. Results show that academics overwhelmingly prioritize holistic success dimensions, such as purpose, autonomy, meaningful relationships, well-being, and societal contribution, yet perceive their institutions as placing greater emphasis on TPM. Qualitative findings reveal identity strain, emotional dissonance, and adaptive coping strategies in response to this gap (Erikson, 1963). By reframing dissatisfaction as a structural misalignment between institutional success logics and scholars’ values rather than an individual deficit, the findings provide a foundation for narrative career approaches, such as Entrepreneurial Life Design, that help scholars navigate competing success logics (Savickas, 2005; Savickas, 2013). The study contributes to research on careers and institutional expectations by theorizing success misalignment as a central mechanism shaping academic career experiences and by opening new directions for institutional redesign and values-aligned career development.

Taming the Untamed: Social Support, Practices and Commitment Among Expatriate Academics

CAR
Alexei Koveshnikov (Aalto University); Ingmar Bjorkman (Hanken School of Economics); Perttu Kahari (Aalto University School of Business)
The internationalization of higher education has increased universities’ reliance on international academic staff, yet integrating and retaining expatriate academics remains challenging. While social support is known to facilitate expatriate adjustment, less is understood about how distinct sources of support and organizational practices shape expatriate academics’ organizational commitment and through which psychological mechanisms these effects occur. Drawing on anxiety and uncertainty management theory and organizational self-enhancement theory, this study examines the roles of supervisor support, coworker support, decision-making involvement, and equality-enhancing organizational practices, as well as the mediating effects of perceived ethnocentrism and perceived overqualification. Using survey data from 496 expatriate academics across 18 Nordic universities, the findings reveal differentiated effects. Supervisor support relates directly to organizational commitment, whereas coworker support influences commitment indirectly by reducing perceptions of overqualification. Equality-enhancing organizational practices emerge as the strongest predictor of commitment, while decision-making involvement plays a limited role. The study highlights the importance of inclusive practices and peer relationships in fostering expatriate academics’ commitment.

When the Script Ends: Career Transitions of French University Presidents

CAR
Sebastien Point (EM Strasbourg Business School); Lisa Thomas (KEDGE Business School)
The expansion of the academic labor market, in scope and relevance, underscores the growing importance of the career lifespan for identifying career paths and opportunities for leaders in the sector. We study the career trajectories of past and present presidents of French Universities to identify the future career outcomes and how their human capital may be effectively leveraged. Our career life-span perspective draws on Career Ecosystem Theory (CET), and Career Construction Theory (CCT) as we explore different career actors – individual leaders, their institutions, and the national system, affording a unique perspective that enhances the existing literature. We studied the career trajectories of University Presidents, using a mixed methodology, including 39 in-depth interviews: 21 with current University Presidents and 18 with former presidents. Our findings indicate a set of career pathways available following a leadership role in the French Higher Education sector. Boundary-crossing was typical, moving to a different sector, generally the public sector, or to retirement. However, the conflicts and tensions within the choices made suggest there is an extent to boundarylessness for those transitioning from senior positions. While this challenges the boundaryless career theory suggesting a need for theory refinement and modification, it also extends theory within CET in terms of extent of agency. We notice that at the stage of reaching the top, there is a need to move from ‘following the script’ to either ‘writing one`s own script’ or having a new script. We offer several managerial implications for various stakeholders to better support career transitions.
