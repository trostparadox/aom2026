From Road to Sky: How EV Battery Fire Incidents Spur Breakthroughs in Electric Aircraft Batteries

TIM
Xinran Wang (Baruch College, CUNY); Shavin Malhotra (University of Waterloo)
We examine how established technologies can influence breakthrough innovation in related, emerging industries. We use a technological paradigm perspective to theorize how exogenous external events can reshape technological lock-ins. Focusing on battery technology in the more established electric vehicle (EV) and the emerging electric aircraft (EA) industries, we explore EV and EA battery trajectories to illustrate the interplay between technological lock-ins and path creation in EA battery innovation. We then explain how external shocks in the EV industry influence breakthroughs in EA battery innovation. We argue that EV battery fire incidents will enhance breakthrough EA battery innovation by encouraging deviation from established EV paths. We also identify mechanisms that will hinder or strengthen breakthroughs in EA battery innovation by reinforcing or weakening the lock-in of the technological paradigm. By examining these dynamics, this study offers new insights into cross-industry innovation, the role of external shocks in path creation, and the impact of policy on breakthrough innovation.

Learning-Commitment Dilemma: Path Dependence in the Multi-Segment Electric Vehicle Industry

TIM
I-Chen Wang (Suffolk University); Hao-Yi Chen (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University); Kenzen Chen (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University); Russell Seidle (Suffolk University)
The study addresses the learning-commitment paradox, showing how early technological commitments simultaneously enable and constrain firms’ capacity to learn in nascent multi-segment industries. Extant research has rarely examined how firms learn across market segments in nascent industries, leaving unexplored how commitments in one segment shape options in others. Using a panel dataset of global automakers in the hybrid, plug-in hybrid, and battery electric vehicle segments from 2010–2023, the study combines cluster and lag-sequential analyses to trace firms’ product portfolio adjustments. The findings reveal strong path dependence at the cluster level but rich reconfiguration within clusters, where multi-model portfolios become experimental platforms that facilitate lateral movement across technological segments. Theoretically, the study reconceptualizes commitment as experimental infrastructure and articulates multi-segment learning as a mechanism to resolve the learning-commitment paradox. Managerially, it advises firms and policymakers to avoid early single-technology bets and instead design broad portfolios that preserve learning options while deepening commitment.

Strategic Diffusion of Electric Vehicle Technologies: Capability, Competition, and Institutions

TIM
Siddhartha Vivekanand Komati (Cornell University); Arnab Choudhury (Columbia Business School)
The global transition toward electric vehicles (EVs) represents one of the most consequential technological shifts facing incumbent firms in the automobile industry. Despite facing similar technological opportunities, competitive pressures, and regulatory mandates, firms have responded to this transition in markedly heterogeneous ways - differing not only in when they adopt EV technologies, but also in which technological domains they pursue and where they choose to commercialize them. Existing research in diffusion, innovation, and strategy explains parts of this variation but typically treats adoption as a binary event and examines underlying mechanisms in isolation. This paper develops a multi-stage theory of strategic diffusion to explain heterogeneity in firms’ EV strategies. We conceptualize adoption as a sequential process involving two interrelated stages: technology-domain entry, in which firms select among alternative EV technological domains, and geographic rollout, in which firms commercialize adopted technologies across markets with heterogeneous institutional environments. The framework in this paper clarifies how capability, competition, and institutions interact to produce patterned heterogeneity in incumbent firms’ responses to emerging technologies. Beyond the EV context, the theory offers a generalizable lens for studying multi-stage adoption processes in complex technological and institutional environments.

Technological Windows and Catch-Up Dynamics in the Automotive Transition from ICE Vehicles to EVs

TIM
Pengfei Chang (University College London)
This paper revisits “windows of opportunity” in industrial catch-up by integrating a narrative of Europe’s transition to electric vehicles (EVs) with a firm-level panel for 2014–2024. It distinguishes technological, policy, and demand windows and trace how their interaction structures competition between leaders and challengers. Building on this context, three hypotheses are developed about when challengers erode leader's market-share advantage during a technological window. Three results stand out. First, the opening of the technological window does not immediately advantage challengers: leaders initially defend or even extend their lead. Second, consistent with a competency trap, erosion accelerates when the EV leader is also the leader in Internal Combustion Engine vehicle segment: multicategory leadership divides attention and binds commitments, shrinking gaps once the window opens. Third, category size is state-dependent: in normal times a smaller EV niche speeds catch-up, but during the window a larger, more salient category amplifies challenger gains. The paper’s novelty is twofold. Conceptually, its recast windows as phase-structured: a temporary incumbent moat followed by a challenger surge as the window matures. Empirically, this study shows that the effect of EV category size reverses once the technological window opens: smaller categories speed challenger catch-up in normal times, whereas larger categories accelerate it during the window.. Linking narrative evidence to within-firm identification, the study refines catch-up theory and offers guidance on timing, focus, and orchestrating complementary assets in disruptive transitions.

Turning Away to Stand Out: Green Technology Divestiture and Firm Performance

TIM
Zhaoxing Hao; Yang Ye (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Wei Yu (National University of Singapore)
The critical role of green technologies in fostering sustainable development is widely recognized. Although prior research has extensively examined green technology acquisition, the phenomenon of green technology divestiture has received little attention. To address this gap, we develop a framework that integrate insights from optimal distinctiveness theory and expectancy violation theory to examine how green technology divestiture affects post-divestiture performance. Contrary to the intuitive view that divestiture represents a passive response, we argue that divesting green technologies enables firms to strategically reconfigure both technological and moral dimensions of optimal distinctiveness, thereby enhancing firm performance. Our findings show that green technology innovation and corporate social responsibility are key mediating mechanisms linking green technology divestiture to performance. Drawing on data from Chinese A-share listed firms, our empirical results consistently support these arguments. Furthermore, we reveal a nuanced moderating role of corporate stigma: it significantly strengthens the positive link between green technology divestiture and subsequent green technology innovation, yet paradoxically weakens its positive effect on subsequent corporate social responsibility. Overall, this study advances theoretical and practical insights by urging firms to proactively manage their green technology portfolios with ethical considerations in mind, while also offering a novel perspective on how corporate stigma shapes the strategic allocation of resources.
