The Deterrent Effect of Reduced Employer-friendly Labor Market Frictions on Employee Misconduct

STR
Eunkwang Seo (Oklahoma State University); Myeongho David Park (Rowan University); Christopher P. Dinkel (Oklahoma State University)
This study examines how reductions in employer-friendly labor market frictions affect employee misconduct within organizations. Focusing on physician overbilling in Medicare—a serious societal issue—we conduct a difference-in-differences analysis using a comprehensive physician-year panel (2013–2022). The results show that reduced labor market frictions, driven by state-level restrictions on the enforcement of non-compete agreements, significantly decrease physicians’ potential overbilling. We further find that this effect is weaker among highly compensated and highly collaborative physicians, consistent with our proposed mechanisms. These findings provide novel insights into the societal implications of labor market frictions and employee mobility, as well as the determinants of organizational misconduct.

Team Composition Responses to Employee Mobility Threats

STR
Deepak Nayak (The Ohio State University); Vivek Tandon (Temple University)
How do firms respond when employees’ mobility barriers weaken? While strategic human capital research has emphasized retention mechanisms that prevent departures, we propose that firms also insure against the disruption-induced departures by increasing compositional redundancy – structuring teams so that members possess overlapping knowledge portfolios. We test this proposition using a quasi-natural experiment: the American Competitiveness in the Twenty-First Century Act (AC21) of 2000, which increased job mobility for H-1B visa holders. Analyzing 31,769 patent-teams in the U.S. electronics industry (1996–2005), we find that teams with greater exposure to the mobility shock exhibit significantly higher knowledge similarity among members post-treatment. This effect is attenuated when potentially mobile inventors have extensive prior collaborative relationships with teammates, suggesting that relational embeddedness substitutes for compositional redundancy as an alternative insurance mechanism. Our findings advance strategic human capital theory by identifying a novel firm response to mobility threats that operates through proactive team restructuring rather than retention or replacement, revealing how organizations can cultivate resilience against human capital risks they cannot prevent.

Dancing with Shackles: Non-Compete Agreements, Employees’ Human Capital Investments, and Innovation

STR
Qianzhi Wei (Tsinghua University); Zhenzhen Xie (Sun Yat-Sen University)
This study examines how non-compete agreements (NCAs) shape firms’ innovation structure by influencing employees’ human capital investment decisions. Although prior research has documented heterogeneous effects of NCAs on innovation, how mobility constraints translate into systematic shifts in innovation structure through micro-level mechanisms remains insufficiently understood. Drawing on human capital theory, we argue that NCAs reduce the external investment risks associated with the non-transferability of firm-specific human capital, thereby encouraging employees to reallocate their investments away from general skills and toward firm-specific knowledge, which in turn reshapes firms’ innovation portfolios. Exploiting cross-state variation in NCA enforceability in the United States and using a difference-in-differences design with firm- and inventor-level data, we find that stronger NCA enforceability is associated with a shift from exploratory to exploitative innovation and increases inventors’ firm-specific human capital. Further analyses reveal substantial heterogeneity across firms, industry contexts, and inventors with different career characteristics. Overall, this study highlights employees’ strategic human capital investment responses as a key micro-level mechanism linking NCAs to firms’ innovation structure and advances human capital theory by identifying a novel risk-based antecedent of firm-specific human capital.

Building Capabilities Beyond Firm Boundaries: How Private Equity Implements Strategy Through Talent

STR
Elaine Pak (University of Pennsylvania)
While strategy research traditionally views active corporate owners as governance actors who mitigate agency costs, recent work suggests they also possess distinct operational knowledge and capabilities. I investigate how owners transfer these capabilities to firms they control by examining employee mobility within private equity portfolios. Using a dataset of career histories and work activities for over nine million employees across 12,775 private equity-owned firms and a difference-in-differences design, I find that post-acquisition, target firms significantly increase hiring from the owner’s portfolio. These common-owner employees (i.e., workers from other firms owned by the same owner) disproportionately occupy managerial, finance, and operations roles and drive a convergence in organizational activities between the focal target firm and the owner’s broader portfolio. Notably, this convergence centers on activities related to strategic renewal rather than cost management and monitoring. Together, these findings indicate that private equity owners implement distinct operating models by redeploying skilled talent across portfolios. This suggests that ownership boundaries constitute an important locus of organizational capabilities, and that competitive advantage resides not only within firms but also within corporate owners.

Can Inventors Take It With Them?: Forced Mobility and the Context Dependence of Innovative Performa

STR
James Spencer White (University of Maryland College Park); Justin Frake (University of Michigan); Evan Penniman Starr (University of Maryland College Park)
Is inventor performance portable, or is it specific to the organizational context? Prior research documents that inventor mobility benefits innovation through knowledge transfer and recombinant search. However, much of this research examines voluntary moves, which reflect favorable selection on two dimensions: who moves (high-quality inventors) and where they go (well-matched positions). We address this identification challenge by using mass layoffs as a research design to observe mobility in the absence of optimal matching. Using employment histories linked to USPTO records (13 million inventor-years) and a stacked difference-in-differences design, we find that displaced inventors suffer 32-36\% declines in patenting that persist 6+ years. These persistent effects challenge the assumption that inventive capability is fully portable. Consistent with context-specific human capital, we find that effects are mitigated when inventors land at technologically similar firms, but exacerbated for experienced inventors who lose deeper context-specific expertise. These findings suggest that match quality acts as a critical boundary condition for recombinant innovation: while voluntary movers may benefit from distant search, inventors forced into new contexts struggle to recombine knowledge effectively.
