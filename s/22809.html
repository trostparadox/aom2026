Strategic Ambidexterity in Transition: Renault and the Stop-Start Path to EVs (1972-2020)

MH
Stanislas Kihm (ISTEC); Adrien Jean-Guy PASSANT
Electric vehicles (EVs) are now central to twenty-first-century automotive strategy, yet their adoption has been gradual. For decades, electrification has competed with hybrids, hydrogen/fuel cells, and improved combustion engines, without a dominant path. Situated within the context of business history and the ‘companies in transition’ framework, this article examines Renault. This major European carmaker was not ‘born green,’ through a historical case study. The firm began experimenting with electromobility in the 1970s, but it was not until the mid-2000s that it launched a large-scale program for mainstream consumers. Using archives and interviews, we trace Renault’s discontinuous engagement with EVs over five decades. We use counterfactual reasoning to explore alternative paths that could have accelerated the transition. By demonstrating how established firms influence energy transitions, this study makes an empirical contribution to understanding the trajectories of electromobility. It conceptually clarifies the role of organizational ambidexterity and ‘perpetually emerging technologies’ in contexts of technological and institutional uncertainty.

Innovation in a State Sector Conglomerate:The Rise of a Chinese Technology Giant

MH
Kang Mo; Andrew Walder (Stanford University)
China’s leading technology companies are almost exclusively private firms founded by entrepreneurs outside the existing state sector. A notable exception is Hikvision Technologies, which was founded as a start-up inside a state-owned military-industrial conglomerate. It outpaced private-sector competitors to become by far the world’s largest provider of video surveillance equipment. Like its private sector rivals, the firm was innovative in technology and product design, but its success depended crucially on fashioning innovative organizational and ownership structures to circumvent the political and bureaucratic limitations of its setting. Its founders created structures that insulated its core scientific and commercial businesses, eventually providing equity incentives for core employees that mimic the private sector. Hikvision’s history shows how innovative corporate structures can overcome the inherent limitations state ownership, but it also suggests why such examples are still rare.

The Historical Evolution of Lights-Out Manufacturing

MH
Saheli Nath (University of Central Oklahoma)
This paper examines the historical evolution of lights-out manufacturing using the lens of technological S-curves and punctuated equilibrium theory. It analyzes how successive technological discontinuities shaped management approaches to automation and organizational transformation from the 1950s to the present. The study finds that lights-out manufacturing historically evolved through five distinct S-curve transitions. Each transition represented a technological discontinuity, which challenged the taken-for-granted norms of existing management paradigms. The transitions appeared to follow punctuated equilibrium patterns, with periods of incremental improvement interrupted by periods of relatively rapid organizational adaptation. This work provides the first comprehensive historical analysis of lights-out manufacturing, demonstrating how technological innovations historically challenged management assumptions about automation feasibility and organizational readiness, offering strategic insights for automation in today’s business landscape.
