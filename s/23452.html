Collaborative Research with Universities and Different Types of Impact: Evidence from Engineering

TIM
Viktor Ström (University of Gothenburg); Maureen McKelvey (University of Gothenburg); Bastian Rake (University of Gothenburg)
University-industry collaboration is increasingly emphasized as a mechanism for translating scientific research into technological and economic value. Yet how collaborative research generates technological impact and who captures that value remains insufficiently understood. This study examines the technological impact of academic engagement—collaborative research between universities and firms—compared with purely academic research, and decomposes that impact across three distinct pathways: individual transfer (where researchers participate in both scientific publication and patenting), organizational absorption (where colleagues within collaborating organizations develop related technologies), and knowledge spillovers (where external actors benefit from published findings). Analyzing 5,143 publications by Swedish electrical engineering professors (2000–2018) and their subsequent patent citations, we find that academic engagement generates substantially greater technological impact than purely academic research. However, the distribution of this impact raises important questions about appropriability. Knowledge spillovers dominate technological outcomes for both collaborative and academic-only research, accounting for over 60% of patent citations to collaborative publications. The “appropriable” pathways—individual and organizational— together represent less than 40% of the technological value generated through academic engagement. Regression analyses confirm that academic engagement significantly increases impact through all three pathways. These findings illuminate a fundamental tension: while university-industry collaboration produces technologically valuable science, most of that value diffuses to actors outside the collaboration, including competitors and foreign firms. The results carry implications for firm strategy regarding complementary appropriation mechanisms, for universities regarding expectations for industry partnerships, and for policymakers seeking to strengthen regional competitiveness through collaborative research investments.

Personal Data Protection System and Buyer–Supplier Collaborative Innovation

TIM
Qiuyi Zhu (Guangzhou University); Jiawen Chen (Guangzhou University); Linlin Liu (South China University of Technology)
Personal data protection systems are increasingly adopted voluntarily by firms to safeguard the privacy of the firms and their consumers. However, it is still unclear how a buyer’s adoption of personal data protection systems can have on collaborative innovation with its suppliers. This study addresses this issue by proposing a theoretical framework grounded in resource dependence theory. It suggests that a buyer’s adoption of personal data protection systems increase its suppliers’ dependence of market knowledge, thus motivating suppliers’ commitment in collaborative innovation to alleviate such dependence. This study finds that a firm’s adoption of personal data protection systems increases the firm’s collaborative innovation with its suppliers, and market competition reinforces such relationships.

Reliance on Scientific Ideas in Patenting

TIM
Sam Arts (KU Leuven); Nicola Melluso (LUISS)
Scientific ideas drive firm innovation, yet measuring firms’ reliance on science remains a challenge. Prior research has often relied on patent-to-paper citations, whose primary purpose is to delineate the legal scope of a patent rather than to document all scientific ideas on which it builds. We develop a novel text-based approach that identifies when patents reuse specific ideas from the scientific literature. We validate this method, including using firm R&D lab–level survey data, and show that patents frequently build on prior scientific ideas without citing the original source paper, particularly when the paper is older or less similar in content. Finally, we demonstrate that reliance on scientific ideas positively correlates with the private value of patents, even after controlling for traditional patent-to-paper citations and within the subset of patents without such citations. Together, these results suggest that traditional citation-based measures underestimate the role of science and illustrate the complementary value of our textbased approach and open dataset for studying how scientific knowledge contributes to firm innovation.

Scientist–Inventor Collaboration and the Transition of Science into Technological Applications

TIM
Kyung-Baek (Toni) Min (Kookmin University); Seungryul (Ryan) Shin (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology); Seongwoo Jang (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology); Ye Roo Park (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology)
This study examines how collaboration between academic scientists and industry co-inventors shapes the technological impact of patented inventions. Using a large patent-paper pair dataset and a topical cluster fixed effects design based on text embeddings, collaborative patents are compared to closely matched non-collaborative cases that draw on similar scientific foundations. To further address potential self-selection into collaboration, the analyses incorporate a selection correction that models collaboration propensity using local, technology specific patent litigation exposure in inventors’ regions. The results show that collaborative patents receive more forward citations on average. Among collaborative teams, greater technological similarity between the scientist and co-inventors reduces impact, while geographic proximity alone shows no consistent effect. Prior scientific familiarity moderates these relationships by attenuating the penalty from high technological similarity and by making co location more beneficial. Together, the findings specify when collaboration helps convert scientific insight into influential technologies and highlight the importance of complementary expertise and existing intellectual linkages even when accounting for non-random selection into collaboration configurations.

TTO Role Orientations and Technology Transfer Outcomes: Evidence from Brazil

TIM
Breno Valente Fontes Araújo (Instituto Federal de Educação, Ciência e Tecnologia de Minas Gerais - IFMG); Ana Cláudia Azevedo (Federal University of Viçosa); Rafael Morais Pereira (University of Sao Paulo); Adriana Faria (Federal University of Viçosa)
Universities play a central role in innovation ecosystems, with technology transfer offices (TTOs) serving as key intermediaries that facilitate interactions between academia and external stakeholders. Despite extensive research on TTOs, gaps remain in understanding how their strategic orientation influences early- and late-stage technology transfer (TT) outcomes, particularly in emerging economies. Adopting a process-oriented perspective, this study examines how TTO roles, categorized into internal and external interfaces, impact early- and late-stage TT outcomes in Brazil. Using robust regression analyses on data from the 2019-2023 FORTEC Innovation Survey, we find that a predominant internal interface orientation significantly enhances early-stage outcomes (e.g., invention disclosures) but shows no statistically significant impact on late-stage outcomes. Conversely, TTOs with a stronger external interface focus demonstrate superior performance in late-stage outcomes, particularly licensing agreements. These findings advance theory on the organizational design of TTOs and boundary-spanning activities, showing how distinct role orientations differentially shape TT effectiveness. In practice, the study offers insights for university administrators and policymakers on strategically balancing TTO roles, fostering ecosystem-oriented strategies to maximize innovation impact in emerging economies.
