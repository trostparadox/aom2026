Session overview

As work becomes more global, fluid, and technology-mediated, traditional assumptions about newcomer socialization are increasingly called into question. These assumptions are rooted in models of organizational entry that presume stable, co-located workplaces with clear hierarchies and readily available insider support. Instead, many begin work while navigating cross-border migration and separation from home-based networks, joining teams that convey different philosophies about learning from mistakes, encountering compensation systems that disrupt seniority-based expectations, or onboarding into gig platforms with limited access to human mentors or peer networks. These shifts make newcomer adjustment more complex and heighten its dependence on the cues, resources, and support systems available during the organizational entry process. Building on this reality, this symposium highlights a central message: newcomer socialization must be reconceptualized as a context-sensitive process that extends beyond formal onboarding and beyond the organizational boundaries. Contemporary newcomers are socialized through a mix of emotional experiences, social signals, structural arrangements, and technology-enabled guidance, often in environments where information is incomplete, support is uneven, and expectations are ambiguous. Understanding socialization in this landscape requires moving past “one-size-fits-all” models and examining how newcomers interpret and respond to the conditions they encounter early on. The symposium brings together perspectives that jointly illuminate how newcomers learn, connect, and perform when traditional socialization pathways are disrupted or redesigned. It highlights that adjustment is not simply about reducing uncertainty, but also about how newcomers make meaning of challenges, whether the local environment encourages learning versus avoidance, and how inclusion and legitimacy are communicated, intentionally or unintentionally, through everyday practices and systems. In doing so, the symposium advances the field toward a more integrated view that connects micro-level psychological processes with meso-level social environments and macro-level labor market trends and conditions. A second contribution is the symposium’s focus on boundary conditions, when and for whom certain cues and resources facilitate adjustment. This emphasis is especially timely as organizations seek scalable and equitable approaches to socialization for diverse newcomer populations. It also surfaces practical questions: which signals encourage proactive learning rather than withdrawal? How can organizations and platforms establish credible sources of guidance and support in dispersed, fast-moving contexts? And how might well-intended practices produce unintended consequences for newcomers’ belonging and development? Overall, this symposium provides a timely forum for advancing theory and informing practice in an evolving world of work. By reframing socialization as an adaptive process shaped by shifting organizational contexts, novel work arrangements, and increasingly heterogeneous newcomers, the symposium moves beyond static models of entry to illuminate how learning, belonging, and performance unfold over time. In doing so, it sets a generative agenda for future research and organizational design – one that better supports newcomer learning, integration, well-being, and sustained performance.
