Contesting Theory Performativity: On the Rise of Sustainable Finance

OMT
Rachelle BELINGA (California State University Northridge); Jean-Pascal Gond; Mette Morsing (University of Oxford)
Performativity studies aim to explain how theory relates to organizational practice, in ways that might bring the theory into being. Prior research that recognizes the risk that theory performativity might generate discontent tends to overlook actors’ deliberate attempts to undo performativity. By drawing on social activism literature, the current article examines one such set of actors, namely, epistemic change agents for sustainable finance who contest mainstream financial economics, as established performative theories. On the basis of 58 interviews with sustainable finance experts, observations, and secondary data, we determine that, despite their ambivalence toward financial economics theories, these change agents resource a sociomaterial assemblage of human and nonhuman entities to challenge and compete with financial economics. In addition to conceptualizing how discontented actors, through the transformation of assemblages, contest theory performativity, this study clarifies how change agents, by expressing distinct, nuanced types of discontent, collectively contribute to epistemic change. In establishing that sustainable finance knowledge production comprises a sociomaterial contestation of mainstream finance, the results inform sustainable finance scholarship in organization studies too.

May I Have Your Divided Attention? Exploring the Determinants of Peripheral Coalition Effectiveness

OMT
Jack Auslander (IESE Business School); Massimo Maoret (IESE Business School)
Organizations face increasing pressure to attend to a variety of financial and non-financial issues. Extant theory on intraorganizational political coalitions explains how dominant coalitions resolve such pressures by narrowing the organization’s focus of attention. Yet, peripheral coalitions – those lacking the authority to set the strategic agenda – may still advocate for their own goals or interests. We develop theory on peripheral coalitions, investigating the determinants and scope conditions of their effectiveness. We argue that peripheral coalitions with heterogeneous compositions of professional logics possess greater bargaining power relative to the dominant coalition, which in turn enhances their effectiveness. However, we theorize this effect is contingent on the stability of the peripheral coalition and organizational resource munificence. We test these predictions by investigating ESG coalitions – a nascent peripheral coalition within for-profit firms – among S&P 1500 firms from 2015 to 2023. Consistent with our theorizing, we find that ESG coalitions combining senior managers from environmental (e.g., Chief Environmental Officer) and social backgrounds (e.g., Chief Inclusion Officer), outperform homogeneous ESG coalitions on ESG performance. However, this relationship is attenuated when ESG coalitions increase in size, have unequal distributions of formal authority, or operate under unrestricted slack conditions, all of which may increase intra-coalitional conflict.

Heterogeneous ESG Disclosure Responses to COVID-19 Pandemic: Community Logic and Family Firms

OMT
Pramendra Singh Tank (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); William Ocasio; Sumit Chakraborty (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
Firms are embedded in multiple institutional logics, some of which guide decision making while others remain latent until situational cues increase their accessibility. We examine the COVID-19 pandemic as a societal disruption that increased the accessibility of community logic by exposing firms’ interdependence with workers, customers, and communities. Heightened accessibility redirected managerial attention toward community-oriented considerations, increasing the likelihood that these considerations were activated and expressed through ESG disclosure. We argue that this translation from accessibility to activation varies systematically across organizational forms. This heterogeneity is evident in family firms, where community logic interacts with a family logic characterized by competing control- and legitimacy-oriented priorities. Using a difference-in-differences design on a cross-country sample of firms from 43 countries, we find that ESG disclosure increased following COVID-19 and that this increase was larger for family firms, particularly in countries where family businesses enjoy greater societal legitimacy.

Social Positionality in the Latter Stages of Institutional Change: ESG Investing in the USA

OMT
Jared Peifer (Franklin & Marshall College)
The importance of peripheral organizational actors in the initial stages of institutional change has been fruitfully addressed. To the extent that peripheral, i.e., low status, actors are acknowledged in the latter stages, they are frequently assumed to be dominated by central, i.e., high status, actors. Is that the entire story? What role might social positionality, and particularly peripheral organizational actors, play in the latter stages of the institutional change? Around 2019, the increased popularity of ESG investing changed the investment field in the US. I interview representatives from these existing ESG pioneer organizations. Through analysis of my interview data, I inductively build theory on the role of social positionality in the latter stages of institutional change by demonstrating the possibility of 1) peripheral actors’ perception of intra-field harmony with central actors, 2) strengthened peripheral organizations and 3) a field-level bifurcation of diluted and authentic field practices. I end with my contributions to the literature on institutional change.

Commitment Over Conformity: Public Stakeholder Heterogeneity and Voluntary Certification Portfolios

OMT
Yunsung Lee (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology); Sangchan Park (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology)
This study examines how public stakeholder heterogeneity—the dispersion of a firm’s resource dependence across multiple public stakeholders whose evaluative criteria partially overlap yet remain procedurally uncoordinated—shapes firms’ voluntary certification portfolios in sustainability markets. Although these stakeholders share a normative commitment to sustainability, they operationalize it through divergent valuation procedures, so that the same corporate action may be validated by some stakeholders and discounted by others. We argue that under such conditions, the firm’s central challenge shifts from achieving episodic conformity to sustaining a coherent identity claim that remains legible and justifiable across heterogeneous public stakeholders. Accordingly, we contend that coherent identity claims—built through renewal and focused expansion—are easier to screen, compare, and defend across heterogeneous stakeholders, whereas category-spanning expansion may enhance distinctiveness at the cost of interpretive ambiguity. Using a longitudinal panel of Korean government-initiated environmental technology certifications (2000-2023) and qualitative field interviews, we find that greater public stakeholder heterogeneity drives firms toward coherent certification portfolios rather than broad category spanning. By disaggregating “the public sector” into a configuration of heterogeneous stakeholders, we illuminate how firms sustain legible commitment in sustainability markets.
