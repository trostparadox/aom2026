Busy Boards and Firm Financial Distress

STR
Dr Aitzaz Ahsan Alias Sarang (Institute of Business Administration); Asad Ali Rind (South Champagne Business School); Irfan Haider Shakri (RMIT University); Qazi Ghulam Mustafa Qureshi
Since the global financial crisis of 2008, board members have been expected to actively engage in risk management; however, many directors serve on multiple boards and are overburdened. This study examines the relationship between busy directors and firms’ financial distress risk (FDR). Using a US sample from 1999 to 2024, we find that busy directors increase FDR. This suggests that they are less effective in monitoring, which leads to excessive risk in the form of higher financial distress. This relationship persists across diverse specifications in which we adopt different measures for financial distress and busy directors, use additional control variables, and incorporate firm fixed effects. Our main results remain consistent when subjected to robustness analysis using generalized method of moments (GMM), two-stage least squares (2SLS), entropy balancing, and propensity score matching (PSM). Furthermore, while channel analysis indicates that CEO entrenchment and the number of analysts following a firm do not significantly change the core relationship between board busyness and financial distress, the effect of busy boards on FDR persists only under low competition. This effect disappears when competition (i.e., external monitoring) is high, suggesting that strong external monitoring mitigates the negative impact of director busyness. Moreover, busy directors increase FDR when firms operate under high financial constraints. In contrast, this relationship becomes insignificant under low financial constraints. Finally, the additional analysis results show that the joint effect of a busy board and FDR lowers financial performance. Our findings suggest important implications for investors and policymakers. Keywords: Board composition; Board of directors; Busy board; Financial distress risk; Multiple directors

Strategic Issue Processing in Boards: The Co-Evolution of Attention and Consensus

STR
José Eduardo Valladares Teixeira (Ninth of July University); Fernando A R Serra (Ninth of July University); Kent Miller (Michigan State University); Isabel Cristina Scafuto; Silvio Luis De Vasconcellos (ESPM)
Boards of directors are expected to process complex strategic issues and reach shared understandings that guide organizational action, yet we know little about how board-level attention mechanisms shape this process over time. We address this gap through a longitudinal qualitative study of an advisory board in a large Brazilian family business group, analyzing minutes and transcripts from 36 meetings spanning November 2012 to February 2016. By tracing 46 strategic issue episodes across meetings, we develop a process model that links attention mechanisms and communication practices to consensus trajectories in board deliberation. Our analysis reveals that strategic issue processing evolved through four phases as attention mechanisms shifted from meeting structures and issue sponsorship toward performance monitoring under mounting external pressure. This shift had consequences for consensus: achievement rates declined from 53% in the early sensemaking phase to 33% during crisis management, and issues followed distinct trajectories (rapid agreement, gradual building, or extended debate) depending on issue type, phase, and the communication practices employed. We contribute to attention-based theory by showing that attention mechanisms in boards are not fixed design features but evolve in response to internal development and external conditions, with implications for which issues can be processed effectively. We also advance understanding of cognitive diversity in boards by identifying interpretive space as a critical moderator: diversity supports consensus when attention arrangements allow sufficient time and formats for sensemaking, but rising pressure compresses this space and increases interpretive fragmentation.

What Triggers Director Departure? Power and Subgroup Perspectives

STR
Taekjin Shin (San Diego State University); Jihae You (The University of Memphis); Yun Chung (University of Idaho)
Integrating the approach-inhibition theory of power, group faultline theory, and power mental models, we examine how individual directors’ power and their subgroup’s power independently and jointly influence directors’ decisions to voluntarily leave the board. Analyzing S&P 500 board data, we find that directors with a low level of power are more likely to exit. We also demonstrate that subgroup power characteristics (i.e., subgroup power level, subgroup size, and the inclusion of the CEO) affect director departure directly and moderate the relationship between individual director power and director departure. The findings highlight power as a significant antecedent of director departure and offer deeper insights into power transfer between individual and subgroup levels.

Connecting the Top: How the Board–Top Management Team Interface Develops

STR
Katrina Nelson (WU Vienna University of Economics and Business); Patricia Klarner (Vienna University of Economics and Business)
In order to fulfill their governance roles of monitoring and advising top executives, boards of directors need interfaces to the top management team (TMT). Yet, how such interfaces are developed—and how they differ for new versus established CEOs—remains insufficiently understood. We adopt a multi-case study of six publicly listed firms operating in the two-tier corporate governance systems of German-speaking countries. This context, characterized by formal separation between supervisory and executive roles, foregrounds the interactional mechanisms through which boards and TMTs build interfaces. Our findings highlight the CEO’s pivotal role in initiating the interface. We identify seven behaviorally oriented components through which the interface is developed and sustained and show how these components vary depending on whether the CEO is newly appointed or has a more advanced tenure. This study advances research on the board–TMT interface by elucidating its informational and behavioral foundations, contributes to corporate governance research by explaining how boards develop interfaces to the TMT, and extends CEO succession research by revealing systematic differences in how new and established CEOs engage in interface construction.

How Do Dominant Coalitions Drive Board Refreshment in WISEs?

STR
Matthias Staessens (Montpellier Business School); Johan Bruneel; Tiago Ratinho; Wim Van Lent
We offer a fresh perspective on board refreshment as a response to routine performance appraisals. We theorize that performance shortfalls trigger refreshment and that both its magnitude and direction depend on the alignment between the shortfall’s domain (social vs. financial) and the board’s dominant coalition (social- vs. market-oriented). We test these ideas using a unique panel dataset of the population of Flemish work-integration social enterprises (WISE) from 2009-2018. Our results show that: first, performance shortfalls increase refreshment, with financial shortfalls exerting stronger effects than social shortfalls. Second, alignment leads to reinforcement of the dominant coalition; misalignment yields protective, largely cosmetic adjustments that preserve the status quo. Third, reinforcement is asymmetric: social-dominant boards tend to reweight via selective entries, whereas market-dominant boards reweight via selective exits. This study reframes refreshment as patterned modes of board self-reconfiguration and provides insight as to how WISE boards appraise and respond to multidimensional performance in practice.
