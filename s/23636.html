Beyond the Algorithm: Rethinking Diagnostic Equity in the Age of AI and Quantum Computing

MC
Natasha Dawn Harris (Marymount University); Aaron Scott Jaeger (Marymount University)
Modern healthcare is undergoing a profound transformation driven by Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the impending quantum computing era, with significant implications for diagnostic equity. This paper critically examines the organizational and ethical vulnerabilities exposed by the premature deployment of AI-powered diagnostic systems, exemplified by the SKIN-lens case at EFG University Hospital. Evidence from the SKIN-lens implementation, which achieved a 92% aggregate accuracy but exhibited significant underperformance and diagnostic inconsistencies for patients with darker skin tones due to non-representative training data, underscores a systemic failure. This failure is not merely technical; it reflects a broader pattern in which innovation timelines outpace institutional safeguards, resulting in delays in care for marginalized populations and an erosion of public trust. Adopting a systems-wide, quantum-level perspective reveals how such algorithmic bias quietly entrenches structural health inequities, particularly when rigorous demographic audits, fairness evaluations, and stakeholder input are absent. Drawing upon interdisciplinary insights from ethics, organizational development, digital transformation strategy, and complexity science, this work argues that diagnostic inequities are predictable outcomes of systems designed without intentional safeguards for inclusion. The convergence of technical design flaws, organizational readiness gaps (such as the absence of bias audits and ethical reviews), and macro-level regulatory blind spots creates compounded harm, disproportionately affecting vulnerable patient groups. Looking ahead to the quantum era, the potential to scale bias at unprecedented speed demands proactive, equity-centered governance, integrated from the earliest stages of innovation. This paper proposes a comprehensive, four-pillar framework spanning leadership, technical design, patient engagement, and regulatory infrastructure to embed anticipatory, justice-oriented innovation. Ultimately, cultivating a culture of transparency, continuous learning, and shared responsibility is essential for ensuring that future healthcare technologies serve as vehicles for distributive justice, rather than amplifying existing disparities.

Paws Versus Pixels: How Do Non-Human Influencers Form Consumers’ Attitude

MC
SUVODIP SEN (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
As brands shifts from human to non-human social media influencers (SMI), a critical question emerges: which non-human SMIs are better and in which context? This study develops the first direct comparison between petfluencers (PETIF) and virtual Influencers (VIF), revealing a competitive mediation framework that drives their efficacy. Across three experiments, this paper identifies a distinct relationship deficit from virtual influencers: VIFs generate significantly lower purchase intention than pets because they lack the sincerity warmth and natural biology which is required to form parasocial interaction (PSI), which is the essential precursor to brand personality. However, we also uncover a simultaneous "novelty effect." While VIFs suffer a relational penalty, they leverage their uniqueness, novelty, etc. to allure that drives purchase intention. Furthermore, this paper demonstrates that propensity to anthropomorphize (PTA) acts as a critical boundary condition. Contrary to expectations, low-anthropomorphizers respond more favorably to VIFs, evaluating them as refined technological constructs rather than judging them against biological standards. These findings challenge the view that VIFs are merely inferior endorsers, positioning them instead as "high-risk, high-reward" agents that require strategic targeting to overcome their relational deficits.

Blueprints for Resilience: Lessons from Successful Black Men to Support the Next Generation

MC
Justice Smith (Western Michigan University)
Black men in the United States experience persistent structural inequities, racialized stressors, and systemic barriers that shape educational, professional, and psychosocial outcomes. Although resilience among Black men has been examined across psychology, education, public health, and sociology, the literature remains fragmented, conceptually inconsistent, and often limited to individual-level adaptation. This integrative literature review synthesizes interdisciplinary research published between 1990 and 2025 to critically examine how resilience among Black men is conceptualized, measured, and operationalized. Drawing on stress process theory, ecological systems theory, and organizational resilience frameworks, this review identifies key mechanisms operating at individual, relational, cultural, and structural levels. Findings highlight strengths in culturally grounded coping, relational support, and identity-based resilience, while revealing significant gaps related to longitudinal designs, intersectional analyses, and organizational and institutional contexts. The review reconceptualizes Black male resilience as a dynamic, multilevel system shaped by the interaction of coping strategies, social networks, cultural identity, and structural conditions. Implications for research, practice, and organizations are discussed, emphasizing the need for holistic, culturally responsive, and structurally informed interventions that support well-being, equity, and leadership development among Black men.

Indian Cultural Heritage in the Global Fashion Market: From H&M to Prada

MC
Jyoti Bachani (Saint Mary's College of California)
This study examines the ethical, economic, and cultural implications of global fashion brands appropriating indigenous Indian craft traditions, using the H&M–Sabyasachi Wanderlust collection and Prada’s Kolhapuri-inspired sandals as illustrative cases. H&M’s collaboration with Sabyasachi sold out within minutes, leveraging the designer’s haute couture aesthetic rooted in Sanganeri and Kalamkari traditions, yet excluded local artisans entirely. Similarly, Prada’s sandals replicated centuries-old Kolhapuri designs without crediting the makers, highlighting persistent gaps in legal and ethical protections for traditional crafts, even when protected under Geographical Indications (GI). These instances reveal a structural imbalance: global brands capture disproportionate economic benefits while the artisans, whose design vocabularies constitute cultural capital, remain marginalized. Grassroots advocates, such as Laila Tyabji and Dastkar, emphasize that mass-market industrial reproduction erodes artisanal livelihoods, threatening centuries-old practices and local economies. The cases raise broader questions about responsible business practices, cultural intellectual property, and the role of consumers, governments, and NGOs in ensuring ethical engagement. They underscore the need for new norms, policies, and business models that go beyond symbolic acknowledgment to include fair compensation, supply-chain inclusion, and long-term sustainability of indigenous traditions. The study also interrogates the societal purpose of business, arguing that profit-maximizing models, particularly in fast fashion, risk replicating colonial dynamics by exploiting vulnerable communities. Ultimately, it calls for a reimagining of global fashion practices that balances aesthetic inspiration with cultural respect, equitable economic participation, and the preservation of living craft traditions.
