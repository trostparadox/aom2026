A Theory of Rehabilitative Entrepreneuring

ENT
Oluwaseun Kolade (Sheffield Hallam University)
This paper proposes a new theory of rehabilitative entrepreneuring that conceptualises entrepreneurial engagement as a process through which individuals with criminal histories can undergo moral, psychological, and social rehabilitation. Moving beyond instrumental accounts that frame entrepreneurship primarily as a post-offending employment pathway, the paper theorises rehabilitative entrepreneuring as a value-creating practice that reconfigures individuals’ relationships to responsibility, consequence, and identity. Drawing on entrepreneurship theory, criminological psychology, and identity transformation literatures, the paper advances a conceptual framework centred on three interrelated mechanisms: value creation, value capture, and ownership and stakeholding. Criminal conduct is reframed as a form of value destruction characterised by the externalisation of costs, short temporal horizons, and detachment from consequence, while entrepreneuring is theorised as a practice context through which responsibility, accountability, and future-oriented agency can be re-internalised. The paper contributes to entrepreneurship and organisation theory by extending process-based accounts of entrepreneuring into the domain of rehabilitation and desistance, and to criminology by offering a value-oriented, agency-centred perspective on behavioural change. It concludes by outlining implications for the design of rehabilitative interventions, prison-based enterprise initiatives, and future research on identity transformation through value-creating practice.

Between the Republic and the Empire : Public and Private Equity Investors’ Relationships in France

ENT
Jonathan LABBE (Université de Lorraine); Karine Cécile Revet
This research focuses on the study of relationships between public and private equity investors in France. In this regard, we need to apprehend the formal or informal nature of interactions that can sometimes take place within traditional innovation networks (Djellal & Gallouj, 2018). For this, our article mobilizes a public-private partnerships approach (PPPs) and the resource-based view theory. These perspectives emphasize the complementary role of disciplinary and incentive mechanisms as well as the exchange of specific resources as levers for value creation. Moreover, these orientations crossed with the perspective of a hybrid form of co-investment allow us to build a coherent and explanatory framework of the mixed syndication phenomenon. Our methodology is based on a qualitative approach with an interpretative aim, which includes twenty-seven semi-structured interviews. These data were subjected to a thematic content analysis using Nvivo software. The results suggest that the relationships between public and private Venture capitalists (VCs) of a formal or informal nature, more specifically in a syndication context, at a national or regional level, are representative of an "economico-cognitive" (Farrugia, 2014, page 6) approach to networking and innovation. Moreover, the phenomenon of mixed syndication reveals a context of hybridization of public and private actors that would allow the private VCs to benefit from the distribution of wealth when the company develops its innovation. We can also identify a process related to a quest for legitimacy on the part of the public actor characterized by its controlling role within the public-private partnership (Beuve and Saussier, 2019). Finally, our study has some limitations. One example is the measurement of the effects of relationships on "visible" or "invisible" innovation (Djellal & Gallouj, 2018, page 90).

Between You and Me: Common Investors as Intermediaries in R&D Partner Selection

ENT
Jieun Lee (Rochester Institute of Technology); Joel Andrus (University of Arkansas); Michael Deane Howard (Iowa State University)
This study examines the role of dedicated common investors as information intermediaries in R&D partner selection and its impact on innovation. While R&D collaborations are a central form of strategic entrepreneurship, many fail due to difficulties in partner selection. Prior research highlights the value of intermediaries in reducing uncertainty, but focuses primarily on venture capitalists with strong, technology-specific expertise. We shift attention to dedicated institutional investors in mature, technology-focused industries. Drawing on entrepreneurship and social networks literature, we theorize that common dedicated ownership creates indirect information ties that reduce search costs, thereby increasing the likelihood of collaboration. However, because dedicated investors lack deep technological expertise, their intermediation relies on weak, strategy-level information, creating a paradox in entrepreneurial outcomes. We argue that such intermediation biases firms toward convenient rather than optimal partners, constraining innovation novelty. Analyzing over 1.2M dyad-year observations between 717 U.S. technology firms (2002-2006) and tracking outcomes through 2016, we find that common dedicated ownership facilitates R&D collaboration formation but reduces subsequent innovation performance. This effect is strengthened by transient ownership and weakened by greater dedicated ownership concentration. We identify a partner-selection trap in which owner-driven intermediation can ease collaboration at the expense of innovation quality.

From Outsider to Insider: How Digital Footprints Shape Legitimacy in Ext. Succession of Fam. Firms

ENT
Christian Roland Neusser (University of Reading)
This study investigates how external successors in German family-owned manufacturing companies construct legitimacy through digital self-representation during early leadership transitions. Building on legitimacy theory, digital impression management, and SEW, we explore how non-family leaders navigate identity-sensitive environments where legitimacy is traditionally ascribed through kinship. Using a qualitative design, we analyse 28 interviews and digital footprints from eight German Mittelstand firms. We identify four symbolic strategies (Continuity Signalling, Innovation Framing, Value Anchoring, and Visibility Avoidance) and show how successors transition between these strategies in response to relational cues and contextual pressures. Our framework links each strategy to a dominant legitimacy logic (cognitive, pragmatic, moral) and outlines the conditions under which symbolic alignment is more or less effective. The study contributes a process model of digital legitimacy construction, reframes SEW as a communicative resource, and positions impression management as a form of symbolic governance. Practical implications are offered for boards, advisors, and successors.

More Than a Split: Equity Distribution as a Process of Belief Updating in Light of Self-Concept

ENT
Mohammad Hawily (University of Bologna); Denis A. Gregoire (HEC Montréal); Paola Giuri (University of Bologna)
Although strategic entrepreneurship literature acknowledges the consequences of equity allocations for new ventures’ subsequent journey and the challenges of negotiating them, academic understanding of how entrepreneurial teams navigate these negotiations remains limited and under-theorized. To address this gap, we conduct an inductive study of cofounders’ equity allocation practices. Our analysis reveals interactive dynamics between Bayesian belief updating and self-concept considerations, which shape negotiation exchanges and drive mutually acceptable adjustments to equity shares. These findings advance theory on the dynamic processes of equity allocation, highlight the role of Bayesian reasoning in entrepreneurship, and demonstrate how self-concept influences decision-making in nascent ventures.
