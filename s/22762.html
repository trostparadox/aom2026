Balancing Role Paradoxes: Developing a Paradox Mindset Among Women Leaders in the Military

OB
Stefanie Acksteiner (Universität der Bundeswehr München); Hendrik Huettermann (University of the Bundeswehr Munich); Alexandra Jussli (Bundeswehr University Munich)
Women leaders in male-dominated environments often face an identity-related belonging paradox rooted in conflicting role expectations to be both agentic (e.g., assertive, dominant) and communal (e.g., caring, nurturing). Although a paradox mindset – approaching such tensions as a both-and challenge – has been proposed as a promising way to work productively with these contradictions, we still lack a clear understanding of how leaders develop this mindset, and which individual and contextual conditions facilitate or hinder that development. Drawing on an inductive, qualitative design using the Gioia methodology, this study analyzes twenty-six in-depth interviews with women leaders in the German military. Our findings identify key individual resources and contextual factors that inform a qualitatively grounded, integrative framework of paradox mindset development. The framework explicates three interrelated micro-processes – intuiting (initial tension awareness), interpreting (sensemaking and structuring of competing demands), and developing (behavioral adaptation and competence building) – through which women leaders learn to balance agentic and communal expectations in everyday leadership practice. By specifying these developmental micro-processes and their enabling conditions, the study contributes to paradox mindset research and enriches the women leaders literature by foregrounding women’s active role in navigating role incongruity in male-dominated contexts.

Gendered Leadership Signals in Response to #MeToo: A Regression Discontinuity in Time Approach

OB
Matthew Crayne (University at Albany, State University of New York); Jennifer Griffith (University of New Hampshire); Kelsey Medeiros (University of Nebraska at Omaha)
Organizations often rely on leadership appointments as visible signals of reform following legitimacy threats. Drawing on signaling theory and glass cliff research, this study examines whether the #MeToo movement prompted organizations to increase women’s representation in senior leadership as a response to a gender-coded reputational crisis. Using market data on S&P 1500 firms from 2009–2024 and a sharp regression discontinuity in time design, we analyze changes in the probability of appointing women to boards of directors and C-level positions. Contrary to expectations derived from performance-based crisis contexts, results reveal a short-term decline in the likelihood of female board appointments following the public onset of the #MeToo movement. These findings suggest that reputational, values-based crises change how organizations evaluate the signaling implications of gendered leadership appointments. Rather than appointments functioning as credible reform signals, appointing women in certain contexts or circumstances may have been perceived as politically risky or as signaling systemic failure. This study advances signaling theory by identifying boundary conditions for identity-based signaling and refines glass cliff theory by demonstrating that gendered appointments are not a universal response to crisis but depend on crisis type and audience interpretation.

Network Advantage and Informal Leadership: Gendered Pathways Across Organizational Contexts

OB
Francesca Pallotti (University of Exeter); Daniele Mascia (Luiss university); Alberto Monti (University of Genoa)
Leadership is increasingly understood as a relational attribution that emerges through everyday interactions rather than formal roles. Yet a central puzzle remains: why do similar network positions translate into informal leadership recognition for some employees but not for others—and why does this translation vary across organizational contexts? Integrating social network theory with tokenism and contextual leadership, we develop a signal– interpretation model in which advice networks generate leadership-relevant relational signals—reliance-based visibility, integrative boundary spanning, and coordination capacity—whose meaning depends on gendered expectations embedded in specific organizing contexts. We test this model using complete network data from two similarly sized organizations that differ sharply in gender composition and organizing logic: a male-dominated, hierarchical and specialized manufacturing firm (n = 281) and a female-dominated, flatter and multidisciplinary healthcare system (n = 231). Across both contexts, reliance-based visibility consistently predicts informal leadership recognition. By contrast, the leadership returns to boundary spanning and coordination-enabling positions vary systematically by gender and context, revealing distinct gendered pathways through which network advantage is converted into informal leadership recognition. Our findings show that network positions are not portable assets but context-dependent signals, highlighting how gender inequality can be reproduced upstream of formal advancement decisions through the contextual interpretation of relational structures.

Process of Junior Women’s Rejection of Senior Women as Role Models in Male-Dominated Contexts

OB
Chesta Ahuja (UCL School of Management); Clarissa Cortland (UCL School of Management); Raina A. Brands (University College London)
Female role models are critical for fostering women’s sense of belonging and career aspirations in male-dominated professions where women are underrepresented and negatively stereotyped. However, research shows that it is in these same settings that the nature and quality of professional relationships between women are negatively affected. How can junior women expect to identify with and be inspired by senior women with whom they share strained relationships? To answer this question, we conducted an inductive, qualitative study to examine the process through which junior women’s identification with female role models may be disrupted in contexts that simultaneously challenge these relationships. Using 52 semi-structured interviews with investment bankers (41 women and 11 men) supplemented with archival data from online communities, we found that junior women rejected senior women as their role models when they experienced a discrepancy between their idolized conceptualizations of senior women based on their cultural beliefs about women in male-dominated contexts and the reality of their interactions with senior women in male-dominated contexts. In these interactions, junior women interpreted senior women’s feedback to be hypercritical and gendered, and their interpersonal dynamics as more subordinating compared to their interactions with senior men. Contrary to their expectations of support and solidarity from senior women, junior women felt alienated by senior women and began to perceive their success as undesirable, dissolving the halo they had created around senior women’s success in the male-dominated context. The present study reveals how the inherent features of male-dominated contexts disrupt the process of junior women’s identification with female role models, which they seek to help them navigate the challenges to their career progression.

ESG-Linked Executive Compensation and the Moderating Role of CEO Masculinity in Corporate Carbon Disclosure

OB
LIU NA (Taylor University); wan nordin wan-Hussin; Chin Sok Fun
Corporate carbon disclosure has become a central component of climate governance, yet firms exhibit substantial variation in disclosure practices even under similar regulatory and market pressures. This study examines whether ESG-linked executive compensation promotes carbon disclosure and whether its effectiveness depends on CEO masculinity. The study argues that incentive-based mechanisms do not operate independently of CEO traits but are systematically conditioned by CEO masculinity. Using a panel of 1,014 firm-year observations from China’s A-share listed companies over the period 2021– 2023, CEO masculinity is measured using the fWHR. The results show that firms adopting ESG-linked executive compensation exhibit significantly higher levels of carbon disclosure. However, this positive association weakens at higher levels of CEO masculinity. Further analysis reveals a non-linear pattern, such that the negative moderating effect of CEO masculinity attenuates at extreme levels. The study contributes theoretically by integrating executive psychological traits into analyses of incentive-based climate governance and by documenting a non-linear conditioning role of CEO masculinity. Practically, the findings suggest that the effectiveness of ESG-linked executive compensation depends on executive characteristics, highlighting the need for complementary governance mechanisms when designing incentives to enhance disclosure credibility.
