Complicating and Strengthening the Behavioral Turn in Leadership: A Critical Realist Perspective

OB
Andrew George McBride (Santa Clara University); Roxanne Ross (James Madison University)
A new approach to leadership research is gaining momentum in the field. It is characterized by a focus on observable behaviors, causality through experimentation, and precise definition of constructs. This “new behavioral approach” to leadership has generated meaningful results and has pushed the field in valuable ways. Notably lacking from this movement, however, is a discussion of the ontological and epistemological stances implied in its work. This lack risks creating confusion, misunderstandings, and dismissal by critics. In this paper, we provide clarity on the ontological and epistemological assumptions implied in the new behavioral approach to leadership. We then use critical realism as a philosophy of science to complicate and add needed precision to those assumptions. We propose that critical realism would be a strong and coherent base from which the new behavioral approach could continue its work, and we highlight some key implications that a critical realist approach would have for the field.

What Makes Health Talk Safe at Work? Leader Awareness, Illness Disclosure, and Withdrawal in Policing

OB
Syed Musa Kazim (Macquarie University); Jun Gu (Macquarie University); Rebecca Mitchell (Macquarie University)
Mental health stigma remains a significant barrier to illness disclosure in high-risk occupations, limiting access to support and contributing to costly employee withdrawal outcomes. Drawing on social information processing theory, this study examines how distinct dimensions of health-oriented leadership shape employees’ illness disclosure and, in turn, absenteeism and turnover intentions in a policing context. Using survey data from police employees and estimating a cluster-robust structural equation model, we test the simultaneous effects of leader health awareness, leader health behavior, and leader health values on illness disclosure and downstream outcomes. The results show that leader health awareness is the only leadership dimension that uniquely predicts illness disclosure. Leader health behavior and values do not exert independent effects once awareness is considered. Illness disclosure, in turn, is strongly and negatively associated with both absenteeism and turnover intentions and fully mediates the effects of leader health awareness on these outcomes. By identifying illness disclosure as a central mechanism and leader awareness as the decisive leadership signal, this study refines health-oriented leadership theory and highlights the importance of attentional leadership practices for reducing employee withdrawal in high-stigma occupational settings. Keywords: HoL, Self-care, Stigma, Policing, Leadership.

How Paranoid CEOs Shape Their Corporate World: Implications for TMT and Board Composition

OB
Tobias Heinrich (RWTH Aachen University); Carolin Krieweth (RWTH Aachen University)
Chief executive officers (CEOs) play a central role in shaping firms’ strategic choices and organizational structures. Although strategy research increasingly recognizes the importance of CEO personality, we know little about how these traits shape internal strategic actors. This study examines how CEO paranoia – an underexplored yet salient trait characterized by heightened vigilance and threat sensitivity – affects the composition of two core strategic leadership bodies: the top management team (TMT) and the board of directors. Drawing on Upper Echelons and Agency Theory, we argue that paranoid CEOs shape these bodies to enhance environmental scanning and control, while boards also adapt to paranoia-related behaviors. Specifically, we focus on gender (surface-level) and education (task-related) as highly transparent TMT attributes that shape team cognitions and dynamics, while for the board, we examine its most prominent features: size and independence. We expect CEO paranoia to be associated with greater TMT gender diversity and educational heterogeneity, as well as with smaller, more independent boards. We test these arguments using a longitudinal sample of S&P 500 firms from 2005 to 2020 and find support for our hypotheses. This study contributes to strategic management research by identifying CEO paranoia as a strategic antecedent of leadership structure and by demonstrating how executive personality preserves managerial agency in shaping key strategic governance arrangements.

Charismatic Leadership in the Age of Generative AI: A Replication and Extension Study

OB
Sophie Kathrin Klüser (University of St. Gallen); Stefan Berger (University of Groningen); Janka Ireen Stoker (University of Groningen); Amanda Shantz (Not Associated); John Antonakis
Charismatic leadership tactics (CLTs) have been conceptualized as a set of observable behaviors through which charisma can be learned and trained. However, empirical evidence for their trainability rests largely on a single, small-sample study. We conduct a constructive replication of Antonakis, Fenley, and Liechti’s (2011) Study 2 using a substantially larger sample (N = 140), an upgraded measurement approach and a considerably greater number of 422 independent raters. Consistent with the original findings, leaders significantly increased their use of CLTs following training, and charismatic signaling generally improved follower evaluations. At the same time, linear effects were not uniformly supported across outcomes. Supplementary analyses revealed curvilinear relationships, specifically for verbal CLTs, suggesting diminishing returns – and even detrimental effects – for higher levels of use. Extending the original design, we further examine whether AI-assisted speech preparation influences the application or effectiveness of CLTs. We find no evidence that AI assistance enhances or attenuates charismatic signaling or its downstream effects. Overall, the results support the behavioral view of charisma as learnable, values-based leader signaling while contributing a novel perspective on the non-linearity of CLTs. The findings further suggest that AI assistance, in its current form, does not substitute for the human cognitive and emotional capacities required to effectively signal charisma.

Evolving Leadership: A Process Theory of Responsible Leadership Development in ICH Enterprises

OB
Wenjie Ye (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics Zhejiang College); Ping Ping Fu (Nottingham University Business School China); Jih-Yu Mao (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
The changes in the 21st-century global landscape demand leadership that can reconcile multi-stakeholder interests, making responsible leadership a critical imperative. However, a significant gap exists in the literature: while research on responsible leadership often lacks a process orientation, studies on leadership development studies frequently overlook the specific value dimensions required for it. To bridge this divide, this study constructs an integrated model of responsible leadership development. Based on qualitative analysis of 71 interviews with leaders in intangible cultural heritage (ICH) enterprises, we developed a three-stage process model: (1) Traditional Leadership, (2) Transitional Cognition, and (3) Responsible Leadership. We find that this evolution is driven by a sequential “trigger - cognition - capability” chain across multiple dimensions. Furthermore, we identify three synergistic mechanisms—namely, stakeholder connection, cultural connection, and responsibility sensemaking—that catalyze the transition between stages. This study offers a novel process theory that addresses critical gaps in the literature, provides an integrated framework for leadership development, and delivers practical guidance for cultivating responsible leaders.
