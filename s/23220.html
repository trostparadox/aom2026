Coding for Clout or Cash? Unraveling the Dual Paths to Knowledge Contributions in Open Source

ENT
Parisa Haim Faridian (San Diego State University); Martina Musteen (San Diego State University)
Open Source (OS) has become important context for entrepreneurial action, where independent contributors build reputational capital, pursue opportunities, and create value through their technical knowledge. Yet little is known about why some members in OS communities contribute more knowledge than others, or how their strategic goals and aspirations shape these behaviors. Drawing on literature in strategic entrepreneurship (SE) and social capital, this study examines how OS contributors leverage their social connections in OS communities to make knowledge contributions through both a direct pathway and an indirect pathway mediated by engagement across diverse knowledge domains. We further theorize two SE behaviors—social advantage-seeking and economic opportunity-seeking—as moderators that accentuate the efficacy of each pathway. drivers that condition how contributors convert social ties into productive knowledge outputs. Using longitudinal data from 619 contributors across six OS projects on GitHub, we find that social advantage-seeking strengthens the direct effect of embeddedness on contributions, while economic opportunity-seeking amplifies the mediated effect through domain diversity. By demonstrating how contributors strategically leverage social ties and domain engagement to advance personal and collective outcomes, this study extends entrepreneurial theory into digitally mediated, nontraditional innovation settings and offers new insight into how distributed communities sustain continuous knowledge production.

Contextual Selection: How Environment Shapes Founder Psychology and Startup Success

ENT
Brandon Freiberg (Columbia Business School)
Why do psychological traits so inconsistently predict entrepreneurial success? Although founder personality has long been linked to venture outcomes, empirical results remain mixed. Traits such as risk aversion, conscientiousness, and neuroticism often show positive associations in some contexts but negative or null effects in others. We argue that these inconsistencies arise in part from contextual selection: environmental conditions shape both who becomes an entrepreneur and what founder traits reveal about underlying idea quality. We develop a simple model in which individuals decide whether to found a startup based on their psychological disposition and their private assessment of idea quality. Entry requires overcoming a trait- and environment-specific threshold. In favorable conditions, even marginal ideas attract founders, weakening the signal value of traits. In adverse conditions, entry becomes more selective; only individuals with unusually strong evidence or conviction choose to found a startup, so traits that typically appear neutral or even detrimental become stronger predictors of success. Using large-scale data linking founder traits - derived from digital traces - to startup outcomes across market booms and busts, including the 2017-18 crypto crash, we find empirical patterns consistent with this selection mechanism. The results offer a behavioral theory of entrepreneurial entry that helps reconcile inconsistent evidence on founder psychology and startup performance.

Founder Networks and the Startup Wage Premium

ENT
Ajay Bhaskarabhatla; Emma Lappi (Aarhus University); António sérgio Ribeiro; Jolanda Hessels (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Rui Baptista (Universidade de Lisboa)
The compensation structure within startups remains a subject of considerable debate. This study shifts the focus from firm-level averages to the relational micro-foundations of wage setting, first investigating whether a shared prior employment history between a startup’s founder and an employee generates a discernible wage premium. We document evidence for such a premium and argue this premium arises from relational contracting, where trust and private information create economic value. To test the durability of this relational rent, we then exploit the pivotal event of founder-CEO succession. This transition, which marks a firm’s professionalization, provides a natural experiment to analyze how the wage premium changes after the founder’s direct influence and idiosyncratic knowledge is removed. This dual approach provides novel insights into how social capital is priced in wage contracts and how its value evolves as a new venture matures and formalizes.

Success That Misleads: How Early Marginal Success Leads Entrepreneurs to Persist Without Change

ENT
Hyunso Kim (Seoul National University); Saelon KANG (Seoul National University); Jaeyong Song (Seoul National University)
How do entrepreneurs respond when their first venture outcome is neither a failure nor a clear success, but instead a marginal success? Leveraging Kickstarter’s project success threshold, we construct balanced comparison groups of failed, marginally successful, and clearly successful entrepreneurs and examine how early marginal success shapes subsequent behavior. We find that entrepreneurs who are marginally successful in their first project make significantly fewer strategic changes in subsequent projects. Given their narrow success and limited slack resources, they engage in less exploratory search and rely more heavily on established routines. Consistent with this pattern, they are more likely to be marginally successful again. In addition, marginally successful entrepreneurs build an early base of backers whose support and expectations encourage persistence on the platform despite repeated marginal performance. Together, these dynamics generate a self-reinforcing, persistent cycle of limited learning, entrenched routine, and marginal performance. Our findings show that early marginal success anchors entrepreneurs into a distinctive behavioral pathway, highlighting the overlooked yet consequential role of intermediate outcomes in entrepreneurial learning and adaptation.

Unraveling People with Disabilities’ Networks to Entrepreneurial Success: A Dual Moderated Mediation

ENT
Talha Yousaf (National University of Sciences & Technology (NUST) Islamabad); Owais Anwar Golra (National University of Sciences & Technology (NUST) Islamabad); Fizza Khalid Chaudhry; Zeeshan Rafiq (Information Technology University of the Punjab)
Previous studies on disability entrepreneurship have overlooked and not modelled how people with disabilities use networks to achieve success, the critical effects of opportunity, risk and resources or how this process is strengthened or weakened. Drawing on Social Network Theory as a basis, we address this gap by examining the impact of networks on PwDs’ entrepreneurial success through a dual-stage moderated mediation framework. In particular, we propose opportunity recognition as the crucial underlying mechanism, and risk tolerance and access to resources as the boundary conditions shaping this process. Using survey interviews, data was collected over almost a year from 304 PwD entrepreneurs. The findings show that opportunity recognition mediates the relationship between networks and entrepreneurial success. Risk tolerance strengthens the effect of networks on opportunity recognition, whereas access to resources unexpectedly weakens the effect of opportunity recognition on success. This negative second-stage moderation qualifies assumptions within Social Network Theory that access to resources uniformly improves network outcomes. The study contributes to disability entrepreneurship research by identifying stage-specific boundary conditions through which networks enable or constrain PwD entrepreneurial success.
