Foreign Capital, Local Constraints: Assessing Gender and Embeddedness Penalties in Emerging Startup

ENT
GRACE NJOKI GITONGA (IESE Business School); Thomas M. Klueter (IESE Business School)
Access to venture capital is a critical determinant of entrepreneurial success. Yet funding decisions are often shaped by ascriptive founder characteristics that serve as heuristic signals in conditions of high uncertainty. While such disparities are well documented in developed economies, much less is known about how founder identity and geographic mobility shape funding outcomes in emerging markets. This study examines these dynamics within African entrepreneurial ecosystems. Drawing on a sample of 1,573 founders across Africa’s four largest startup ecosystems: Kenya, Nigeria, Egypt, and South Africa, we employ Ordinary Least Squares regressions and Cox Proportional Hazards models to analyze how gender and geographic mobility influence both the level and timing of venture capital acquisition across the funding lifecycle. We document significant penalties for both locally and regionally mobile founders who raise significantly less capital than their internationally mobile counterparts, indicating that, in emerging markets, venture capital disproportionately favors globally mobile trajectories. We also find a persistent gender gap, with female founders raising significantly less funding than male founders. Critically, these disadvantages are not static. Professional experience serves as a compensatory signal for female founders, mitigating funding penalties. At the same time, identity-based disadvantages are most pronounced in early funding rounds and attenuate as ventures progress and establish observable performance records. Together, these findings demonstrate how signal substitution unfolds over time and highlight how founders in nascent ecosystems can strategically deploy human capital to navigate structural inequalities in capital markets.

From Tweets to Term Sheets: Public Political Expression and Venture Funding

ENT
Andreas Bernhard Renner (Technical University Dortmund); Corinna Vera Hedwig Schmidt (TU Dortmund University)
Social media platforms like X have become an important arena for entrepreneurs to build visibility and publicly express values – including political positions. Yet, it remains unclear how entrepreneurs’ overall online activity and specifically their public political expressions relate to early-stage funding outcomes, when information asymmetries are strong and investors rely on as much information available as possible. In order to close this gap, we link Crunchbase venture-round data to entrepreneurs’ public X accounts and analyze more than 16 million English tweets from U.S.-based ventures (N=2,461 venture-round observations for next-round amounts; N=12,973 subjects and 505 events for time to next round). Using venture fixed effects models and Cox proportional hazard models, we find that higher tweet volume is associated with faster follow-up funding, but not larger rounds. Political intensity exhibits a robust inverted U-relationship with next-round amounts, while its association with funding speed is small and statistically not significant. Republican-leaning language is associated with slower and marginally smaller follow-up funding in the main specification – an effect that is not replicated in the robustness dictionary, indicating measurement sensitivity. Drawing on signaling and homophily perspectives, we interpret visibility as reducing investor search friction, thus affecting timing, whereas partisan-coded communication triggers screening for distraction, polarization, and ideological fit, therefore being associated with commitment and round size.

Process Velocity in New Ventures: Persistence Accelerates and Diversity Delays Milestone Achievement

ENT
Sophia Hess (University of Stuttgart); Bernd Wurth (University of Glasgow); Andreas Wahl (University of Stuttgart)
Process velocity, defined as the rate at which ventures achieve key developmental milestones, represents a critical but understudied dimension of new venture performance. While research has established that milestone achievement predicts venture success, we know far less about what determines how quickly ventures reach these milestones. Drawing on momentum theory, dynamic states, and resource orchestration perspectives, we develop a process velocity framework examining how entrepreneurial persistence, founding team heterogeneity, and ecosystem access shape the timing of first sales, first employee hires, and first funding. Using accelerated failure time survival models on 288 new ventures, we find that persistence accelerates milestone achievement for sales and funding, with the strongest effects on funding acquisition. Team heterogeneity delays milestone achievement, particularly for sales and hiring, revealing a ‘liability of diversity’ in nascent ventures: coordination costs outweigh informational benefits before teams develop shared mental models and integrative mechanisms. Ecosystem access significantly accelerates commercialization but shows no effect on hiring or funding. We make three contributions. First, we advance a process velocity perspective that shifts attention from whether ventures achieve milestones to how quickly they achieve them. Second, we reposition persistence as a temporal capability that sustains momentum through feedback-rich early development. And third, we identify temporal boundary conditions for diversity effects that challenge assumptions of uniform benefits. These findings carry implications for team composition decisions, investor evaluation criteria, and ecosystem program design.

Too Busy to Raise Funding? Start-Up CEOs’ Golfing and Board Memberships When Raising Venture Capital

ENT
Sebastian Fourne (University of North Florida); Hyungseok David Yoon (University of York); Abdul Mohamed (University of Leeds); Axel Buchner (ESCP-Europe Business School - Berlin)
This study examines how CEOs’ boundary-spanning activities beyond their focal ventures shape venture capital (VC) funding. Building on the attention-based view, we distinguish between leisure-based (e.g., golfing) and professional-duty-based (e.g., outside directorships) boundary-spanning activities that signal how CEOs allocate their attention. We argue that leisure-based boundary spanning sends negative signals about CEO dedication, while professional-duty-based engagement conveys credibility and competence, increasing the amount of VC investment. However, we argue that the interplay between these two forms of boundary spanning creates conflicting cues about CEOs’ attention and priorities, thereby decreasing the VC funding amount. Furthermore, we argue that start-up size attenuates the downsides and accentuates the benefits of CEOs’ leisure-based and professional boundary-spanning, respectively. Using longitudinal data on 1,318 U.S. start-ups seeking VC funding between 2010-2024 we find support for these predictions. We advance entrepreneurship research by showing how CEOs’ attention to extra-venture activities shape investor perceptions and venture resource acquisition.

When Does Acting Scientifically Accelerate Venture Funding? The Role of Venture Stage

ENT
Djordje Djokovic (University of St. Gallen); Thomas Markus Zellweger (University of St. Gallen)
When does communicating a scientific approach to entrepreneurship facilitate external financing? The entrepreneurs-as-scientists (EaS) perspective emphasizes that founders form beliefs, test them, and update them based on evidence. While these practices improve venture performance, their signaling value to investors is not uniform. Integrating the EaS perspective with signaling theory, we argue that scientific practices differ in their credibility as signals depending on observability, signal cost, and signal fit, and that signal interpretation varies with venture stage. Analyzing 27,836 observations from 821 UK ventures we find that belief updating consistently accelerates funding across all venture stages. Belief formation has no effect on funding as investors discount the ideas proposed by founders as unreliable signals. Belief testing produces an age-dependent pattern for funding: emphasizing experimentation delays funding for young ventures by approximately 15% but accelerates it for ventures beyond four years.
