Shining in Silence: How Covert Innovation Drives Radical Outcomes in Long-Term Oriented Cultures

OB
Jinzhao Qu (Hainan University); Svetlana Khapova (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Prior research suggests that employee innovation thrives in Anglo-Saxon contexts, where efficiency, measurable performance, and rapid decision-making are highly valued. Yet, countries shaped by Confucian traditions, which emphasize harmony, sustained effort, and delayed returns, also exhibit high innovation performance, revealing a cultural paradox. We address this paradox by proposing that employees engage in covert innovative behavior (i.e., a bottom-up, employee-driven innovative effort conducted without formal authorization), as a culturally nuanced behavior that fosters radical innovation in Confucian contexts. Specifically, we theorize that employees leverage covert innovative behavior to achieve radical innovation through innovative momentum (i.e., internal force that sustains directionality in organizational activities over time). Following rigorous scale development in Study 1, we test our conceptualization using both quantitative and qualitative methods and further validate the theoretical model in Study 2. Our results demonstrate that covert innovative behavior drives radical innovation via innovative momentum and that long-term orientation is a key cultural factor. Overall, our findings advance bottom-up innovation by highlighting how culturally shaped behaviors facilitate radical innovation in Confucian contexts.

Silent Language in Intercultural Conflict: Ambiguity, Attribution Accuracy, & Conflict Resolution

OB
Thomas Rockstuhl (Nanyang Technological University); Kok-Yee Ng (Nanyang Technological University); Soon Ang (Nanyang Technological University); Filip Lievens (Singapore Management University)
Intercultural research has emphasized spoken language, often overlooking silent language – the implicit, context-dependent cues through which meaning is conveyed. Integrating attribution theory with research on high- and low-context communication, we examine how ambiguity in silent language affects attribution accuracy and, in turn, intercultural conflict and performance. We argue that making isomorphic attributions – accurately inferring others’ intentions from culturally patterned cues – is both more difficult and more consequential in high-context (HC) communication, where meaning is embedded in indirect and relational signals. Across three studies using a multimedia intercultural situational judgment test (iSJT) with multinational employees (Study 1a: N = 63; Study 1b: N = 180) and undergraduates (Study 2: N = 147), observers make significantly fewer isomorphic attributions when interpreting HC versus low-context (LC) communicators. Qualitative analyses suggest that HC communication widens interpretive latitude and biases observers toward internal attributions. Further, we find that only attributions of HC communicators predict integrative conflict resolution and intercultural task performance. Study 2 further shows that individuals with high-context orientation tend to be more accurate in decoding HC communication, although challenges persist. Together, these findings identify isomorphic attribution as a central but under-examined mechanism in intercultural research.

Voice–Silence as Resource Regulation in Hierarchical Emerging Economies: The LMX Dependency Paradox

OB
Forrence HsinHung Chen (Yuan Ze University); Huu Tin Nguyen (Yuan Ze University); Chanidapha Sittikorn (Yuan Ze University)
While employee voice and silence are critical for organizational adaptability, existing research often assumes that supportive leadership uniformly promotes voice. However, in hierarchical emerging economies, speaking up may endanger valued relational resources, prompting employees to regulate disclosure and withholding as a resource portfolio. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, this study conceptualizes voice as resource investment and silence as resource protection, arguing that organizational and relational contexts shape employees’ loss–return calculus. Using a three-wave time-lagged survey of Thai employees (N = 185), we examine how perceived organizational support and competitive climate (organizational resources), servant leadership and leader–member exchange (relational resources), and psychological capital and organizational identification (personal resources) influence motive-based voice and silence. Results reveal that support reduces acquiescent silence, competitive climate simultaneously increases voice and silence, psychological capital is the strongest predictor of prosocial voice. Crucially, high-quality leader–member exchange—typically viewed as empowering—produces more prosocial silence and less prosocial voice, revealing a “dependency paradox”. Prosocial voice enhances innovation, while acquiescent (forced) voice reduces well-being. These findings reconceptualize voice–silence as dynamic resource regulation, identify relational dependency as a culturally contingent “golden cage,” and expose hidden well-being costs of compelled expression in hierarchical emerging economies.

Supervisors’ Downward Silence Toward Their Subordinates: Reasons, Antecedents, and Consequences

OB
Yoshinobu Nakanishi (Hosei University)
Communication is essential for organizational management. However, communication within an organization can be inhibited by members withholding their voice?known as silence. While employees’ upward silence toward their superiors has been extensively examined for over two decades, downward silence from supervisors toward their subordinates has remained unexamined, despite its severe impact. As a first step toward addressing this gap, this study explored supervisors’ downward silence through semi-structured interviews with Japanese corporate supervisors and a grounded theory approach. The key findings are as follows. Downward silence, similar to upward silence, comprises three types: acquiescent, defensive, and prosocial. We also identified the antecedents of supervisor silence. Typically, supervisors remain silent instead of speaking out to ensure smooth organizational management. On other occasions, they engage in defensive silence for fear of adverse consequences of speaking out to subordinates. In particular, they are afraid of facing allegations of abusive supervision and the resulting career disadvantages. Acquiescent silence derives from the resignation that subordinates will not change their behavior. Performance evaluation results are a difficult topic for supervisors to talk about with subordinates, as the characteristics of evaluation can lead to all three types of downward silence.

Breaking Information Security Silence: When Trust in Artificial Intelligence Meets Leadership Ethics

OB
jie deng (Tongji University); Yuheng Zhao (Tongji University); wenhao song; shence ren
Employees are often the first to detect information security risks in organizational, yet they frequently choose silence, creating serious ethical and governance failures. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, this study examines how and when employee trust in artificial intelligence (AI) can break information security silence. We theorize that trust in AI functions as a critical resource that fosters organizational security concern, thereby reducing silence. We further propose that this resource-gain process is contingent on leadership ethics. Specifically, leader immortality encouragement operates as an acute ethical threat, whereas perceived visionary leadership serves as a stable contextual resource that amplifies the silence-breaking effect of AI trust. We test our model across two studies. Study 1 uses a scenario-based experiment to establish mechanism under ethical threat, demonstrating that unethical leader encouragement weakens the indirect effect. Study 2 employs a three-wave time-lagged field design to examine the whole story. By shifting the focus from silence formation to silence disruption, this research advances ethical silence scholarship, extends COR theory into AI-enabled workplaces, and highlights leadership ethics as a critical boundary condition in information security silence contexts.
