The Division of Evaluative Labor: How Algorithmic Monitoring Reconfigures Performance Management

Monday, Aug 3 2026 · 8:30AM–9:30AM
Pennsylvania Convention Center — Poster Wall 4
HR

People

Author: Gabrielle Voiseux The University of British Columbia

Session overview

Performance management systems historically distinguished between outcome-based evaluation (measuring results) and behavior-based evaluation (assessing processes), treating these as competing alternatives that require trade-offs due to limited evaluation capacity. Algorithmic monitoring systems challenge this capacity assumption by analyzing all performance tasks, potentially eliminating measurement constraints. This raises an important question: what happens to performance evaluation when algorithmic systems remove outcome measurement constraints? Through an 18-month examination of algorithmic monitoring implementation in a Canadian government call center employing approximately 100 customer service agents, this study investigates how algorithmic monitoring redistributes rather than centralizes evaluative authority. The organization installed an AI platform analyzing every customer interaction and generating automated quality scores. Instead of algorithms becoming the primary evaluators, algorithmic monitoring created a "division of evaluative labor". Three actors now share performance evaluation: 1) algorithms assess routine procedural compliance automatically, 2) quality experts interpret algorithmic outputs, contextualizing scores and identifying when automated assessments miss performance nuances, and 3) managers focus on behavioral competencies that algorithms cannot measure. Each actor evaluates specific performance dimensions based on what they can reliably assess. This division solve the traditional tradeoff in performance management. When algorithms automate routine outcome measurement, human evaluators redirect their attention toward behavioral assessment. Organizations can emphasis both evaluation dimensions by distributing assessment across actors. This challenges both algorithmic management predictions of centralization and assumptions about persistent capacity constraints in performance evaluation systems.
