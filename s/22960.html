Getting My Foot in the Door: How Job Seekers Form and Activate New Ties for Referrals

OMT
Yingjian Liang (Johns Hopkins University)
How do new entrants into an industry or profession form and activate network ties to obtain key career resources? While some scholars have emphasized the importance to form direct ties with key resource holders, recent research has highlighted the returns from indirect ties through intermediaries. Prior research on indirect ties have highlighted how they facilitate trust with individuals with hard-to-reach resources, but existing research has not looked at the learning benefits from connecting with intermediary ties that could make indirect ties through intermediaries effective even when resource holders are widely accessible. Job seekers may gain capabilities to obtain help from new ties through intermediary ties, who 1) change the relational meanings of social ties, thus sustaining the motivation to seek help, and 2) coach styles of interaction, thus increasing the effectiveness of help-seeking. I developed this argument using a longitudinal qualitative study of 65 master’s students in computer science and engineering entering the IT industry in the US. Focusing on how respondents sought job referrals, a key career resource, I find that job seekers who connected with intermediary ties before connecting with ultimate referrers were more successful in gaining referrals than those who reached out directly to potential referrers. This paper advances our understanding of network processes that lead to varied successes in early-career job search.

Inversion in Cultural Markets: Launching Novice Careers Through Low-Status Projects

OMT
Ankur Chavda (HEC Paris); Minjae Kim (Yale University)
In cultural markets, prevailing theories suggest that novice workers advance their careers most reliably through high-status projects, i.e., those endorsed by industry insiders. Yet novices frequently launch lasting careers through low-status projects instead. We argue that in some settings such cases may be the rule rather than mere exceptions. To do so, we develop a formal model explaining when and why novices are more likely to advance their careers through low-status projects than through high-status ones—a pattern we term “inversion.” Our argument hinges on the idea that low-status projects tend to have a high dispersion of outcomes. When project and career advancement depends on surpassing demanding evaluation thresholds, this dispersion can give novice workers in low-status projects a greater chance of success than novices in high-status projects. We find support for our theory in the U.S. television industry, where, among pilots with novice lead actors, low-status pilots are more likely than high-status ones to be ordered for a full season. These findings uncover a novel boundary condition for theories of cumulative advantage, showing that novices can, under certain conditions, leverage low-status opportunities to better advance their careers in cultural markets.

Careers Building on Vague Categories? How Lenient Market Identity Affects Individuals’ Careers

OMT
Ankur Chavda (HEC Paris); Eugene Taeha Paik (University of Mississippi)
We examine how the ambiguity of an individual’s market identity shapes career outcomes in the labor market. Prior research suggests that market identity— formed through the job and product categories individuals have been associated with over their careers—influences labor market outcomes, as employers rely on identity to assess skills and fit. Turning attention from individuals’ career choices to the characteristics of the categories themselves, we investigate how market identities built on more lenient categories influence individuals’ likelihood of securing future jobs. Studying the feature film industry from 1950 to 2017, we find that market identities built on more lenient genres increase the likelihood of securing films in the near future. This positive effect of a lenient identity is accentuated when an actor has higher status, but attenuated when the actor’s has a more focused identity rooted in a narrower range of categories. This study contributes to the literatures on careers and labor markets by integrating insights from the category literature.

It’s Not Oversharing: When Job Candidates’ Entrepreneurial Aspiration Becomes a Signal of Commitment

OMT
Jaekyung Ha (emlyon business school); Kira Choi (emlyon business school)
Prior work in management and conventional hiring advice commonly posit that disclosing entrepreneurial aspiration can trigger a hiring penalty. We argue, however, that the meaning of such disclosure depends on the hiring firm’s organizational context. In established firms, entrepreneurial aspiration is likely to raise concerns about commitment, as employers may infer misaligned interests and limited willingness to invest effort in a long-term employment relationship. In startups, by contrast, the same aspiration can be interpreted as a signal of motivation and alignment with the demands of entrepreneurial work, thereby mitigating the penalty. We test these predictions in pre-registered survey experiments with respondents who have hiring experience. Our finding shows that entrepreneurial aspiration reduces interview and offer recommendations in established firms, whereas this penalty disappears in startups. Mediation analyses suggest that the mechanism differs by firm type: in established firms, entrepreneurial aspiration lowers perceived commitment, while in startups it does not undermine commitment perceptions and may instead enhance perceived capability for the role. Overall, our findings highlight that candidates’ forward-looking intentions can shape labor market evaluations, and that the inferences drawn from those intentions are contingent on the hiring firm’s needs and evaluative logic.

Learning to Discount Luck: How Experience Shapes Attribution of Near Misses

OMT
Brian Park (Georgia State University)
While research extensively examines luck in achieving positive outcomes, we know little about how professionals interpret luck when avoiding negative outcomes. Drawing on organizational learning theory, we theorize that career experience systematically reduces professionals' tendency to attribute near misses to luck, particularly under uncontrollable conditions. Using 125,292 incident reports from the Aviation Safety Reporting System (1993- 2024), we find experienced aviation professionals significantly less likely to use luck when explaining near misses. While inexperienced professionals significantly increase luck attributions under uncontrollable conditions (e.g., severe weather), experienced professionals maintain low luck attributions regardless of controllability. Our study illuminates how experiential learning shapes risk perception and attribution patterns, with implications for strategic decision-making under uncertainty.
