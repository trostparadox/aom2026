Holding a Moral Mirror: Newcomers’ Social Worth Expectations in Curbing Unethical Mentoring

OB
Zhaopeng Liu (Shandong University); Yamei Liu (University of Jinan); Danting Chang (Shandong University); Kan Ouyang (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Dongqian Yang (Shandong University); Yihan Shu (Shandong University)
Unethical mentoring—where senior employees transmit morally problematic methods to newcomers—threatens to organizational ethics. We propose a moral mirror model where newcomers shape mentor behavior by expressing social worth expectations—that mentors contribute positively and embody morally valuable roles. Drawing on role theory and cognitive appraisal theory, we argue that these expectations act as cues, leading mentors to internalize a moral role. We propose a dual-path model where social worth expectations reduce unethical mentoring when appraised as moral growth opportunities, but increase it when perceived as threats to moral identity, moderated by mentors’ incremental moral beliefs and newcomers’ communicative indirectness. Analyses based on 613 mentor–newcomer pairs from five subsidiaries of a large group Chinese company supported these hypotheses. This research highlights how newcomers shape ethical dynamics and clarifies how bottom-up moral influence can curb unethical mentoring during early organizational socialization.

A Meta-Analytic Review of Moral Character Traits and Their Relative Importance for Work Conduct

OB
Viet Quan Nguyen; Babatunde (Tunde) Ogunfowora (University of Calgary); Addison Maerz; Rosa Hendijani (University of Colorado); Piers Steel (University of Calgary)
For over half a century, the trait view of morality has been a dominant lens for understanding employee conduct. However, the proliferation of diverse moral traits has fragmented the literature, hampering cumulative knowledge. We address this by conducting a comprehensive meta-analytic review of 14 commonly studied moral traits (437 studies, N = 597,141) to estimate their relative validity for predicting counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs), unethical behaviors, and organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs). Results reveal moderate to strong intercorrelations among moral traits, suggesting significant conceptual overlap. Crucially, relative weight and dominance analyses identify a clear hierarchy: Honesty-Humility and empathic concern are dominant predictors of misconduct, whereas perspective taking and reflective moral attentiveness best predict OCBs. Furthermore, narrow traits consistently offer incremental validity over broad traits. We discuss the theoretical and empirical implications of these findings for advancing a more parsimonious science of moral character at work.

The Consequences of Leader Unethical Pro-Employee Behavior

OB
Jingni Dong (Renmin University of China); Xin Liu (Renmin University of China); Limei Chen (Chongqing University); Long Zhang (Hunan University)
Given its prevalence in organizations, unethical prosocial behavior (UPB) has received widespread attention in academic research over these years. Although existing literature has identified and distinguished various forms of UPB, a prominent and significant phenomenon, leader unethical pro-employee behavior (UPEB), has been overlooked. Our research expands the UPB literature by introducing a new construct, leader UPEB, and further elucidating when and how leader UPEB triggers approach- or avoidance-oriented reactions from employees as the primary recipients. Specifically, drawing on the approach-avoidance framework, we propose that when an employee’s moral identity is higher, leader UPEB is indirectly and positively related to the employee’s leader-directed OCB through increased loyalty toward the leader. By contrast, when an employee’s moral identity is lower, leader UPEB is indirectly and positively related to the employee’s avoiding contact with the leader via enhanced anxiety toward the leader. Using a pre-registered three-wave, multi-source survey of 258 leader-follower dyads in China, path analysis results supported our hypotheses. Theoretical and practical implications of these findings are discussed.

Moral Injury in Ordinary Work Contexts: Evidence from Mixed-Methods Research

OB
Matthew Crayne (University at Albany, State University of New York); Keaton Allen Fletcher (Colorado State University); Ho Kwan Cheung (University of Calgary)
Employees often expect their organizations to reflect or at least not contradict their core moral values. When organizations violate these values, the resulting harm may extend beyond dissatisfaction or disengagement to deeper forms of psychosocial strain. Drawing on the concept of moral injury, we examine whether firm-level moral transgressions function as harmful work experiences in non-extreme organizational contexts. Across three studies using qualitative, experimental, and longitudinal field methods, we show that employees commonly experience value violations at work that they appraise as potentially morally injurious events. Study 1 identifies recurring categories of organizational value transgressions in a diverse working sample, unified by perceived threats to dignity and humanity. Study 2 demonstrates that individuals distinguish morally transgressive organizational actions from non-moral negative events and associate them with heightened shame and guilt, as well as reduced organizational commitment. Study 3 follows public-sector employees during a period of institutional disruption and shows that appraisals of moral injury predict subsequent shame, which in turn mediates effects on fatigue and affective commitment, with stronger effects under greater psychological proximity. Together, these findings position moral injury as a meaningful and underrecognized occupational hazard in organizational life.

Seed or Soil? A Multilevel Model of Self-Value Actualization

OB
Mansura Nusrat (The University of Texas at El Paso); Prajya Rakshit Vidyarthi (The University of Texas at El Paso)
Drawing on social cognitive theory and the concept of emotional intelligence, this research introduces the self-value actualization (SVA) construct and its corresponding scale, a novel tool designed to measure the alignment of personal moral values with professional behavior by integrating ethical decision-making, social learning, and self-regulation. SVA is conceptualized as a dynamic, iterative process influenced by observational learning, self-efficacy, and continuous feedback from role models and the organizational environment. The scale's construct validity is supported by exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses across diverse professional samples to ensure robustness. At the same time, multilevel modeling reveals a negative relationship between self-value actualization and counterproductive workplace behavior. This validated and reliable scale provides valuable insights for both academic research and practical applications in the areas of ethical behavior. The study’s theoretical contribution lies in expanding social cognitive theory.
