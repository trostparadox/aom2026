Strategizing with Stakeholders: Building the Relational Foundations of Open Strategy (WITHDRAWN)

SIM
David Montens (IESEG School of Management); Susana Esper (IESEG School of Management)
Despite its growing popularity, the literature and practice of open strategy are charged with persistent dilemmas—particularly concerning process design, organizational commitment, information disclosure, stakeholder empowerment, and escalation dynamics. To address these challenges, this study integrates recent advancements in stakeholder engagement to develop a relational framework for understanding inclusion and transparency in strategy-making. For practitioners, this approach provides actionable insights for designing strategy processes that are both inclusive and sustainable. For academics, it anchors open strategy within the relational principles of stakeholder theory, reframing openness not merely as a procedural experiment but as a foundational capability for collaborative strategizing with stakeholders.

Who Counts and Who Decides? Firms, Problems, and Stakeholder Theory

SIM
Prem Sagar Menghwar (Darden Graduate School of Business)
Scholars and practitioners have a uniform view that stakeholder identification is crucial for the survival of organizations. This view predominantly assumes that the firm is the central organizing unit and that managers identify which stakeholders are important primarily based on power or salience. If this assumption holds, then stakeholders who possess less power or salience are likely to be deemed unimportant and consequently overlooked by organizations. In this study, we argue that the nature of the problem is a critical but missing element in existing explanations of stakeholder identification. We replace the firm as the unit of analysis and instead position the problem as the central organizing basis for stakeholder identification. We present a typology of mutuality problems: simple, complex, and wicked. By emphasizing the nature of the problem, our approach enables a more nuanced and dynamic understanding of stakeholder identification, moving beyond rigid categorizations such as powerful versus weak stakeholders. By developing theory at the problem level, we show how organizations can more accurately identify the stakeholders most essential to their long-term survival.

Flourishing and the Processes of Assembling a Nexus of Stakeholder Agreements

SIM
Amrit Panda (University of Arkansas); Ronald K. Mitchell (Texas Tech University); Rob Mitchell (Colorado State University)
To better understand the processes incident to the formation of organizations as nexuses of stakeholder agreements—we conduct an in-depth historical analysis of the evolving context leading up to the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer. We use a single-case design to enable detailed analysis in a well-documented setting, rather than dispersing attention across multiple, less-documented settings. Our core finding is that the processes of reaching an accord among stakeholders with both competitive and collaborative interests rest on how four dimensions of stakeholder relationships incident to human flourishing (collective clarity, partnership alignment, capability architecture and stakeholder trust and accountability) animate the processes of assembly of a nexus through a sensemaking mechanism that connects the temporal phases of Accord development over time. We offer four contributions that we believe will contribute to the social issues in management literature: (1) introducing the idea of stakeholder flourishing as one of the innovative mechanisms that explain the assembly of a nexus, (2) refining the stakeholder view through introducing the notion of stakeholder flourishing, (3) utilizing historical analysis to reveal underlying mechanisms of stakeholder management, and (4) addressing the challenges presented by deep stakeholder pluralism.

Is There a "Norm"ative Stakeholder Theory That “Works" for Purposive Organizing?

SIM
Eugene Yi Cheong TUNG (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Kevin Au (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Frank W. Ng (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
Stakeholders and communities can assume a more integral role in enacting their purposes in organizations through alternative organizing. However, the difficulty stems from the performativity of the market and the exclusion of purposes from the market paradigm on which most stakeholder theories are based. A rigid structure of transactions that reinforces the prevalent market norm prevents theorizing of critical enablers for purposive organizing with an alternative norm; yet, a normative stakeholder theory for purposive organizing may only be completed by incorporating “unanticipated consequences of purposive action”. In a thought experiment where transacting partners are not bound to exchange in the transactional way, we theorize and demonstrate that a just norm could emerge through optimization of collective values. We aspire to advance an alternative normative account for emancipating a “reimagined future” in the stakeholder approach.

Sustaining the Spark: The Role of Emotional Energy in Sustaining Stakeholder Relations

SIM
Anjali Chawla; Brent Alexander McKnight (McMaster University)
This study advances our understanding of how social ventures maintain enduring stakeholder relationships, particularly when sustaining such relations is challenging. Drawing on the qualitative case study of a social venture in North America, we show how these relations are sustained through ongoing, affective interactions between the social venture and its stakeholders within relational spaces and illuminate the role of emotional energy. Our analysis of 64 interviews and 54 hours of observational data reveals that stakeholders experience emotional energy through the dynamic interplay between the social venture’s practices and how they reinterpret those practices. This emotional energy encourages stakeholders to further engage with the social venture, thereby sustaining their relations. These findings shift our attention from entrepreneur-led strategies to the relational, affective processes that underpin long-term stakeholder relations in social entrepreneurship.
