Between Autonomy and Control: Relational Dynamics in Pivot Decisions

ENT
Janine Crivelli (University of St. Gallen); Carolin Kaufmann (University of St. Gallen); Joakim Wincent (University of St. Gallen)
Pivots are often portrayed as founder-driven responses to market uncertainty. This study challenges that view by theorizing pivoting as a relational process shaped by founder–investor dynamics. Drawing on an inductive multiple-case study of 40 ventures across diverse industries, we examine how investor engagement and funding timing influence the initiation, type, and execution of pivots. Our analysis reveals three relational configurations—autonomous, negotiated, and disengaged pivots—that differ in how investor involvement and relational trust shape founders’ pivot decisions. Founders without investors typically initiate product-focused pivots to attract external funding, while investor-backed ventures pursue parallel pivots aimed at scalability and market expansion. The quality of founder–investor relationships determines whether these changes foster learning or premature convergence. The study advances a relational theory of pivoting, re-centering investors as active participants in pivots and linking research on learning, resource dependence, and relational governance in venture development.

Drivers of Entrepreneurial Pivoting: Unpacking the Interplay Between Affect and Cognition

ENT
Sukanya K Roy (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore); Ludvig Levasseur (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore); Gideon D. Markman (Louisiana State University)
Pivoting is a critical decision for entrepreneurs and can significantly affect startup success. However, not all startups choose to pivot, even when market signals suggest change is needed. This paper investigates why some entrepreneurs pivot while others do not. We propose a Dual-Pathway Model that conceptualizes pivoting as a two-stage process: the psychological formation of intent to pivot and the behavioral commitment to an alternative opportunity. We examine how dispositional positive affect (DPA) and dispositional negative affect (DNA) influence pivoting decisions and how entrepreneurial alertness, and overconfidence create boundary conditions for these relationships. Using survey data from 272 US entrepreneurs collected via Prolific, we find that DPA is positively associated with pivoting, and DNA is negatively associated. Entrepreneurial alertness strengthens the positive effect of DPA but strengthens the negative effect of DNA (contrary to our expectations), while overconfidence buffers the negative effect of DNA. A replication study with UK entrepreneurs reveals cross-country differences, suggesting that institutional context shapes how affect translates into entrepreneurial action.

Entrepreneurial Experience and New Venture Performance: An Identity-Based Mechanism

ENT
Ying Cheng (Chongqing University); Jing He (Chongqing University); Andre Habisch
Prior studies on the relationship between founders’ entrepreneurial experience and new venture performance have yielded mixed findings. Most research treats experience as cumulative human capital or a cognitive resource, overlooking how it shapes entrepreneurial identity to influence behavioural and performance outcomes. Grounded in identity theory, this study proposes that entrepreneurial experience reinforces an entrepreneur’s expert identity, which reduces their feedback-seeking behaviour from new venture team members, thereby negatively affecting venture performance. We further differentiate between feedback-seeking sources (co-founder vs. employee) and examine the moderating role of extraversion. Using questionnaire data from 145 founders applying for the Bayer Foundation’s Women Empowerment Award, our analysis revealed that: (1) entrepreneurial experience significantly reduces feedback-seeking from both co-founders and employees; (2) feedback-seeking from co-founders significantly improves venture performance, while feedback-seeking from employees shows no significant effect; (3) feedback-seeking from co-founders partially mediates the relationship between entrepreneurial experience and venture performance, while the mediating effect of feedback-seeking from employees is not significant; (4) extraversion attenuates the negative effect of entrepreneurial experience on feedback-seeking from co-founders. This study advances an identity-motivation framework for understanding entrepreneurial experience and underscores the importance of differentiating feedback sources.

Pivoting in Business and Entrepreneurship: A Conceptual Review and Research Agenda

ENT
Anna Mader (University of St. Gallen)
Despite its ubiquity in management discourse, "pivoting" remains theoretically ambiguous and is frequently conflated with general strategic change. This conceptual fragmentation limits cumulative knowledge development and our ability to explain pivot antecedents and consequences. Through a systematic literature review of 66 peer-reviewed articles from 124 leading journals (ABS ranking 3+), we consolidate the fragmented landscape into five interrelated conceptualizations of pivoting: as a strategic decision, evolutionary landmark, unfolding process, narrative device, and experiment-based learning. We identify distinct pivot types—including opportunity versus survival pivots—and synthesize these perspectives into a unified definition: a fundamental reorientation of a venture's core strategy, value proposition, or organizational identity in response to belief disconfirmation, undertaken through iterative testing and learning while maintaining organizational identity continuity despite strategic discontinuity. This definition clearly distinguishes pivoting from general strategic change, resolving existing inconsistencies. We outline a research agenda prioritizing: (1) contextual generalizability beyond technology and startup domains, (2) theoretical mechanisms explaining when different pivot types succeed, (3) stakeholder and identity dynamics governing legitimacy and resource commitment, and (4) temporal and sequencing effects in multi-pivot trajectories and organizational transformation limits.

When Does Pivoting Pay? Learning, Strategic Adaptation, and Venture Performance

ENT
Gerardo Bastos Neto (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Jared Shaw Allen (Texas Tech University); J. Mauricio Galli Geleilate (University of Massachusetts Lowell)
Although pivoting is widely assumed to benefit new ventures, we know little about when and why it actually improves performance. Prior work has not fully explained why entrepreneurs pivot or when such adaptation pays off. We develop a dual-pathway framework in which pivoting arises from two distinct sources of learning: an external learning pathway shaped by venture novelty and an internal learning pathway grounded in entrepreneurial experience. Using a longitudinal dataset of new ventures, we find that novelty and experience both increase the extent of pivoting, but for different theoretical reasons. Novelty heightens assumption fragility and increases the need for iteration, whereas experience improves entrepreneurs’ ability to interpret early signals and recognize when strategic change is warranted. Pivoting subsequently improves performance, but this benefit is driven primarily by venture novelty rather than experience. These findings position pivoting as the process through which early learning becomes strategically enacted and reveal when strategic adaptation enhances venture performance.
