Innovative Organizational Identity and Problem-Solving Approach to Task Conflict

CM
Na Yoon Kim (Wilkes University)
A problem-solving approach to conflict is crucial for high-performing teams, particularly when addressing task-related disagreements that require integrative and creative solutions. Building on the idea that a problem-solving approach to conflict is inherently a creative act, this study examines how organizational identity influences individuals’ psychological readiness for problem-solving task conflict resolution. Study 1 tested whether a perceived innovative organizational identity enhances individuals’ creative self-efficacy. Study 2 assessed whether individuals with high creative self-efficacy are more inclined to adopt problem-solving approaches to task conflict. Lastly, Study 3 examined the mechanism by which creative self-efficacy mediates the relationship between perceptions of innovative organizational identity and a problem-solving approach to conflict. The findings demonstrate how identity-based signals foster adaptive conflict behaviors within teams.

Encouragement or Control? The Moderating Effect of Social Media Strategies on Omnichannel Conflict

CM
Ruoyi Wang (Northwestern Polytechnical University); Tingyu Lu (Northwestern Polytechnical University); Jun Zhang (Nanjing University of Science and Technology); Guijun Zhuang (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
With the rapid advancement of information and communication technology, manufacturers increasingly depend on social media to manage channels and enhance communication with partners in omnichannel operations. Drawing on media richness theory, this study explores how social media encouragement and control strategies influence the relationship between channel multiplicity, channel conflict (both horizontal and vertical), and firm performance. Based on survey data from 550 valid responses, most of the proposed hypotheses are supported. The results show that social media encouragement strategies mitigate the positive impact of channel multiplicity on horizontal conflict, while enhancing its positive effects on vertical conflict and firm performance. Conversely, social media control strategies weaken the positive effects of channel multiplicity on horizontal conflict and firm performance, with no significant influence on vertical conflict. Notably, the effect of channel multiplicity on firm performance varies depending on the type of social media strategy adopted, indicating a contingency-based relationship. These findings deepen the understanding of the role of social media strategies in channel conflict management and their impact on firm performance, providing important theoretical and practical insights for conflict management in an omnichannel environment. The article concludes with discussions on theoretical contributions, practical applications, and limitations of the study.

Finding the Groove: How Conflict Convergence Fuels Creativity in Global Virtual Teams

CM
Hwayoung Kim (University of St. Thomas)
Global virtual teams (GVTs), referring to temporary, culturally diverse, geographically dispersed and electronically communicating work teams, often struggle to achieve shared understanding, particularly in how they interpret conflict. This study reconceptualizes coordination in GVTs through the lens of Social Entrainment Theory, building on cognitive entrainment, as the dynamic convergence of team members’ interpretive frames over time. I theorize how asynchronous and synchronous communication jointly supports the emergence of conflict consensus, and examine how this process enhances team creativity. I also introduce individual team member’s anticipated cultural barrier, a forward-looking threat-based belief that cultural differences will hinder team collaboration, as a psychological disruptor of entrainment. Using multi-wave, multi-level data from 950 global virtual teams, I model perceptual convergence across task, relationship, and process conflict types. The findings reveal that consensus strengthens creativity but only when end-level conflict is low, and highlight the structural and psychological conditions under which shared meaning, not just shared behavior, enables creative synergy in distributed teams.

Fighting Smart: How Task and Relationship Conflicts Shape Innovative Job Performance

CM
Rahman Ullah
Purpose: Using the information exchange perspective, this study investigates the relationships between task conflict and various stages of innovative job performance: idea generation, promotion, and realization, and further explores how relationship conflict moderates these relationships in line with the contingency model of conflict. Design/methodology: The study’s hypotheses were tested using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (model-1), with data collected from 503 employees working in both manufacturing and service-sector organizations in Pakistan. Findings: Task conflict positively relates to the first two stages of innovative job performance —idea generation and idea promotion — while negatively relating to the final stage, idea realization. Moreover, relationship conflict moderates these connections such that higher relationship conflict weakens the positive association between task conflict and both idea generation and promotion, while strengthening the negative association with idea realization. Originality/value: The current study contributes to the existing research on conflict management and innovation by detailing how the interaction between task and relationship conflicts influences various stages of innovative job performance.

From Cooperation to Confrontation: How Commercial Disputes Shape the Longevity of Partnerships

CM
Yuezhen Han (Georgia State University); Mingyao Lyu (Renmin University of China)
This paper asks why similar commercial disputes lead some partnerships to end through breakdown, while others continue through planned exit or structural redesign. The study adopts a behavioral process perspective and builds on research in relational governance, conflict processes, and behavioral decision theory. It develops a process model that links dispute duration and dispute intensity to how partnerships evolve over time. The model explains how disputes influence interpretation, relational expectations, and governance responses during conflict. The key argument is that disputes with long duration and high intensity reduce cognitive and emotional capacity, weaken trust, and limit procedural control, which makes unintended termination more likely. Short and low intensity disputes instead preserve rational judgment and allow partners to negotiate an orderly exit. Mixed combinations, such as short but intense disputes or long but low intensity disputes, are more likely to produce governance redesign and structural transformation. The paper contributes by shifting the focus from static structural explanations and single conflict events to a dynamic behavioral process that unfolds across time. It also clarifies how different combinations of duration and intensity channel partnerships toward intended termination, unintended termination, or structural transformation, and shows how competitive overlap, institutional distance, and legal capability shape these outcomes.
