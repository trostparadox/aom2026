Substituting Talent with Transactions: Acquisitions as Responses to Immigration Restrictions

STR
Jens-Christian Friedmann (Rotterdam School of Management); Britta Glennon (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania); Exequiel Hernandez
We examine how firms respond to talent scarcity caused by restrictive immigration policies. We argue that when firms cannot build capabilities internally through hiring, they alter their boundaries by engaging in corporate acquisitions to make up for the foregone talent and capabilities. Using data on 3,861 U.S. firms and their use of the H-1B visa program (2001-2020), we leverage two exogenous shocks—the 2004 H-1B cap reduction and the 2007-2008 visa lottery— and find causal evidence that firms make more acquisitions as their exposure to immigration restrictions rises. This effect is stronger for deals with purposes related to the skills of the foregone talent, for small acquisitions, for domestic targets, and for targets in places with higher concentrations of skilled workers. Our findings contribute to research on corporate strategy, strategic human capital, and immigration policy.

Performance as a Random Variable

STR
Isaac Tucker (UCLA Anderson School of Management)
Individual performance is stochastic, but strategic human capital research evaluates workers and policies only in terms of mean or expected performance. This omission embeds strong but rarely stated assumptions about organizations’ ability to absorb bad draws and capitalize on good ones. Using a portfolio model in which hiring managers choose between reliable (lower-mean, low-variance) and volatile (higher-mean, high-variance) workers, I examine how organizational wealth, scale, team composition, and production interdependence shape exposure to performance risk. The analysis shows that fragile, resource-constrained, or tightly coupled organizations pay a volatility tax and should favor reliability, whereas large, well-capitalized, or modular organizations can take advantage of more volatile talent. I develop this insight as Human Capital Portfolio Management and discuss implications for research in strategic human capital, entrepreneurship, and organizational theory.

Firm Reputation and Human Capital Sourcing

STR
Ilhwan Na (Troy University); John Mawdsley (HEC Paris); Clint Chadwick (University of Kansas)
Information asymmetry in workers' evaluations of prospective employers poses a particular challenge for firms with lower industry reputation competing against higher-reputation rivals in the same labor market. We examine how lower-reputation professional service firms respond to this apparent labor market disadvantage. Using a longitudinal dataset of the largest U.S. corporate law firms from 2000 to 2020, we find that, relative to higher-reputation rivals, lower-reputation firms are more likely to: (i) communicate enhanced mentoring and career opportunity through organizational structure, (ii) access nascent workers at an earlier stage of their career, and (iii) offer immediate career advancement through promotion-through-hiring. Taken together, our findings illustrate how reputationally disadvantaged firms strategically adjust human capital sourcing practices to attract workers under conditions of information asymmetry.

Not All That Glitters is Gold: Firm Hiring in the Market for Knowledge Workers

STR
Justine Boudou (Harvard Business School)
Firms increasingly compete for scientific talent to drive innovation. While individuals’ productivity in an industrial setting is uncertain at the time of hire, firms can observe measures of academic productivity. However, because the institutional environments of academia and industry differ, the scientists most productive in academia may not be those most productive in industry. Using confidential administrative data on 40 cohorts of PhD graduates across all fields linked to wage and publication records, I estimate individuals’ productivity in both academia and industry, regardless of their actual sector of employment. I do so using a Marginal Treatment Effects framework that accounts for endogenous sorting into sectors and exploits variation in industry demand for PhDs at graduation. I find that individuals’ academic and industrial productivity are only weakly aligned, with an average correlation of 0.29. This alignment has declined over time and varies markedly across PhD majors. In majors with weaker alignment, firms’ hires are more likely to be laid off, consistent with greater difficulty identifying individuals whose skills translate effectively to industry. Together, the results suggest that relying on performance indicators generated in a different institutional context can increase organizations’ risk of employing individuals who subsequently underperform.

Hazardous Skies, Shifting Minds: Asymmetric Impact of Air Pollution on Talent Inflow and Outflow

STR
Xiaohan Li (Central University of Finance and Economics); Song Lin (Central University of Finance and Economics); Yong Li; Haifeng Wang (Hong Kong Baptist University)
This study investigates how regional air pollution influences the mobility of skilled inventors in China, focusing on the asymmetric effects on talent inflow and outflow across regions with different levels of economic development and talent density. Leveraging multiple data sources at the city-dyad level and employing both instrumental variable and difference-in-differences approaches, we find that increased PM2.5 concentrations significantly deter the inflow of skilled labor but do not uniformly spur outflow; instead, the impact on outflow is pronounced only in regions with poor economic performance and low talent density. These findings underscore the complex interplay between environmental quality, economic opportunity, and strategic human capital, revealing a nuanced health-wealth tradeoff in regional talent competition. Our study contributes to the literature on labor mobility and offers policy implications for fostering regional innovation and economic growth by improving environmental quality and addressing structural inequalities.
