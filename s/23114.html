How Unions and HR Support Shape the Health-Retirement Relationship: A Socioemotional Perspectives

HR
Ki-Jung Kim (Eastern Kentucky University); Yonjin Suh (Governors State University); Amit Kramer (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Population aging poses a grand societal challenge by intensifying labor shortages and threatening workforce sustainability. Retaining healthy older workers is therefore critical, yet existing research offers fragmented explanations of why many healthy older workers intend to retire early. Integrating socioemotional selectivity theory, conservation of resources theory, and job embeddedness theory, we develop a socioemotional resource framework to explain how older workers’ health interacts with unions and human resources (HR) practices to shape retirement intentions. Using nationally representative panel data from the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (2008-2022), we examine how unions and HR support moderate the relationship between health and intended retirement age. We find that healthier older workers generally intend to retire earlier. However, union presence reverses this pattern, leading healthier workers to delay retirement. HR support attenuates, but does not reverse the negative health-retirement relationship. We find no significant three-way interaction, suggesting that HR and industrial relations (IR) systems operate through distinct rather than synergistic pathways. Our findings challenge capacity-based assumptions about health and retirement, highlight unions’ underappreciated socioemotional role in aging workforces, and underscore the need to reintegrate HR and IR perspectives when theorizing and managing later-life employment.

Employee-Oriented HR Practices and Happiness: Mediating Role of Career Aspiration and Voice Behavior

HR
Shuaib Ahmed Soomro (KEDGE Business School - Marseille); Farooque Ahmed (Sukkur IBA University); Asif Nawaz
In today's dynamic work environments, fostering employee happiness and effective management presents persistent organizational challenges. Workplace happiness has received increasing scholarly and practitioner attention over the past decade, yet understanding of its antecedents remains limited. This study addresses this gap by investigating how employee-oriented HR practices enhance workplace happiness through the sequential mediation of career aspiration and voice behavior. The authors collected multilevel and multi-wave data from 230 French employees across multiple sectors. The hypotheses were analyzed using 2-1-1 framework using Jamovi 2.6. The results demonstrate that (a) career aspiration mediates the relationship between employee-oriented HR and workplace happiness, (b) voice behavior mediates the relationship between employee-oriented HR and workplace happiness, and (c) a significant serial mediation path exists wherein employee-oriented HRM influences workplace happiness through career aspiration and subsequently through voice behavior. These findings indicate that organizations can cultivate employee well-being by implementing HR practices that support career aspirations and promote voice behavior, thereby enhancing workplace happiness.

Beyond Automation: Union Voice and Workplace Injury Reduction

HR
Minjung Lee; Sunghoon Kim (The University of Sydney)
There is a belief that installing automation could reduce workplace injuries; thus, top management’s attention (top-down) is withdrawn. However, we argue that management’s continued attention to safety issues matters for reducing injury, as different types of workplace injuries arise post-automation. Drawing on the attention-based view (ABV), we introduce the interrelationship between top-down and bottom-up attentional engagement. In this process, we highlight the union's role as a bottom-up attention carrier. We argue that the positive effect of automation adoption on injury reduction is only possible when unions continue to capture management’s attention on safety issues, serving as an attention carrier from the bottom to top management. Using South Korea’s longitudinal panel data on workplace injuries from 2009 to 2021, we confirm that the higher the union ratio, the lower workplace injuries occur, consistent with the baseline finding that higher automation reduces workplace injuries. Managerial Versus Employee Perceptions of Wellbeing Practices and Obligations

HR
David Jonathan Watson (University of Essex); Roberta Fida (Aston Business School); Laura Byrne (Aston University); Claudio Barbaranelli
Organizations increasingly signal commitments to employee wellbeing, yet employees’ experiences depend on how organizational obligations for wellbeing are interpreted and enacted in practice. Drawing on pluralist employment relations theory and Fox’s frames of reference, this study conceptualizes perceived organizational wellbeing as a relational and multi-level phenomenon shaped by potentially divergent managerial and employee interpretations of organizational responsibility within shared organizational contexts. Using matched survey data from 3,050 employees nested within 113 medium and large private-sector organizations, alongside assessments from a senior management representative in each organization, we examine patterns of perceived wellbeing obligations and practices from both employees’ and managers’ perspectives. Multilevel analyses support treating perceived obligations and practices as organizational-level constructs, enabling an organization-centered perspective. We identify three distinct organizational profiles based on configurations of perceived obligations and practices as assessed by employees and senior managers: normatively aligned organizations; misaligned low-provision organizations characterized by low perceived wellbeing practices and managers and employees divergence; finally aspirational organizations in which unmet obligations are widely perceived despite relatively strong perceived practices and convergent views between managers and employees. Gaps between obligations and practices are not uniformly associated with poorer wellbeing: Employees in aspirational organizations report the highest wellbeing, whereas misaligned low-provision organizations show the poorest wellbeing. The study advances wellbeing and HRM research by demonstrating how organizational wellbeing is configured through the interaction of obligations, practices, and managerial–employee alignment, highlighting the value of organization-centered approach that understands wellbeing as shaped by unequal and plural interests. Catching the Uber Flu: How Gig Work Shapes Worker Behavior in Traditional Jobs

HR
Michael Maffie (Cornell University); Tashlin Lakhani (Cornell University); Ovul Sezer (Cornell University)
This paper examines how participation in the gig economy shapes workers’ behavior in their traditional jobs. Drawing on mixed-methods evidence from three studies, we show that the intersection of the open gig employment model and the closed traditional employment model gives rise to a novel behavioral response we term catching the Uber flu. In Study 1, qualitative interviews with 39 workers reveal that employees strategically call out sick from their traditional jobs to take advantage of temporarily higher paying gig opportunities. Studies 2 and 3 establish the boundary conditions of this phenomenon by demonstrating that high paying gig work increases the likelihood of calling out, while high commitment human resource practices in employees’ traditional jobs can mitigate this tendency. Together, these findings highlight how the coexistence of open and closed employment systems shapes employee behavior and offer insight into how organizations can respond to workforce challenges posed by the expanding gig economy.
