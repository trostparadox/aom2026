Workplace Fatalities in the United States: The Red State/Blue State Divide

SIM
Richard E. Wokutch; Michael G. Goldsby (Ball State University); Eric Olson (University of Tulsa)
In this paper we explore red-state-blue state disparities in workplace safety and health, focusing on work fatalities. We find significantly higher workplace fatality rates in red states relative to blue states while controlling for industry composition and macroeconomic conditions. We contend that these disparities reflect underlying differences in ideology (relatively greater emphasis on individualism in red states versus relatively greater emphasis on communitarianism in blue states) and differences in perspectives on a new social contract emerging among labor, management, and government. These differences will be increasingly important as many new and emerging technologies such as microchip manufacturing, solar panel fabrication and installation, wind turbine installation and maintenance, and the operation of AI data centers have substantial workplace safety and health risks associated with them. The paper concludes with ethical questions for government, business, and labor to ponder as workplace agreements evolve with rapid technological and economic restructuring.

Sorting Into Resistance: The Compositional Consequences of Moral Contestation

SIM
Tae-Ung Choi (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Hangjun Cho (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Hisan Yang (Louisiana State University)
Why do some organizations facing moral pressure entrench rather than accommodate? Existing accounts emphasize strategic response: targeted organizations manage impressions, cultivate allies, and craft counter-narratives. We propose an additional mechanism--workforce sorting--whereby moral contestation triggers a differential pattern of employee exits and entry that cumulatively reshapes who constitutes the organization. When external challenges intensify, employees for whom the challenge resonates--whether through values, identity, career concerns, or social pressure--tend to exit; those unmoved tend to stay. Because the employees who leave differ systematically from those who remain, this sorting may gradually reconstitute organizations around resistance rather than accommodation. Examining the U.S. fossil fuel industry following the 2012 divestment campaigns, we find that employee turnover increased by 14% above baseline, entry from divesting universities declined by 20%, and political donations shifted toward Republican candidates and conservative ideology. These findings point to a paradox: moral pressure, intended to force change, may inadvertently filter out internal allies who might have advocated for accommodation and leave organizations reconstituted around defense of the very practices activists sought to change.

Political Polarization and Organizational Life: A Cross-Disciplinary Review of the Evidence

SIM
Hannah Cohen (Claremont Graduate University)
Political polarization has emerged as a widespread characteristic of modern society, influencing how individuals perceive information, assess institutions, and interact with each other. These dynamics are becoming pervasive in organizational life, where political identities and value-based orientations affect psychological safety, trust, communication, conflict, and leader-follower relationships. This cross-disciplinary review combines evidence from political science, psychology, communication, and organizational research to clarify the mechanisms by which polarization develops and operates. Using bibliometric coupling as an evidence-identification strategy, the review examines over 9,000 publications to identify the core empirical domains of polarization, which include identity-based affect, motivated cognition, digital information structures, institutional trust, ideological value systems, and epistemic beliefs. Emerging research highlights additional mechanisms such as depolarization strategies, trust repair, and algorithmic amplification. The review concludes by discussing the implications for organizational behavior and identifying future research directions on how to navigate work environments during times of increased political polarization.

Diversity Matters? Societal Conflicts and FDI Choices: Evidence from Indian Firms

SIM
Suyash Garg
Prior research on occupational minority CEOs (female CEOs and CEOs from non-dominant social groups) finds convergence in their strategic decisions, in part due to their shared experiences. Here, we study how early-life experiences may introduce divergence in the international business decisions of occupational minorities. Drawing upon imprinting and categorical social cognition perspectives, we theorize how CEOs’ early-life exposure to intergroup religious conflict weakens their firms’ likelihood of foreign direct investment (FDI) in outgroup-majority countries, wherein outgroup members form the preponderant demographic. Our analyses of Indian firms reveal that Hindu CEOs’ early-life exposure to Hindu-Muslim religious conflict reduces their firms’ likelihood of FDI in Muslim-majority countries. More importantly, we find that this effect is strengthened for women CEOs (v. male CEOs) (amplified threat perception from the outgroup) but weakened for lower-caste CEOs (v. higher caste CEOs) (reduced negative attribution for the outgroup). We contribute to a better understanding of the divergence among strategic decisions of occupational minority CEOs, heterogeneity in imprinting effects, and both sources and consequences of executives’ biases regarding demographic groups.

Socio-Political Disruption and the Dynamics of Organizational Sentiment

SIM
Ipsu Khadka (University of South Carolina)
Socio-political disruptions can shape how organizations are perceived and experienced, even when firms themselves do little or nothing in response. The 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson U.S. Supreme Court ruling, which overturned federal protections for abortion access, provides a salient context for examining how external political change spills over into organizational sentiment. While prior research on CEO and corporate activism emphasizes public signaling to external audiences, this paper shifts attention to the internal organizational consequences of socio-political events and the role of leadership communication during periods of uncertainty. Using survey data from 137 large U.S. firms, we document whether organizations responded to the DVJ decision, the timing of those responses, and the nature of their communication, noting that many firms communicated internally rather than publicly. We then track firm-level sentiment trajectories over 30 days using social media data and model how sentiment evolved before and after the ruling. Results show that the DVJ decision was associated with a gradual weakening of sentiment trajectories rather than an immediate shock, indicating spillover effects of socio-political change on organizational sentiment. In contrast, whether firms communicated, how quickly they responded, and how substantively they communicated did not systematically moderate post-event sentiment dynamics. These findings highlight the limits of communication as a stabilizing mechanism in politicized contexts and extend the CEO activism and sensegiving literatures by showing how socio-political events can reshape organizational sentiment independently of firm action.
