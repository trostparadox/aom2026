Between Passion and Paychecks: Liminality and Identity Work for Lifestyle Entrepreneurs

OB
Abhipsa Dash Bhatt (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Pavni Kaushiva (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow)
This study examines voluntary, non-institutionalised career transitions from salaried professional work to expression-driven lifestyle entrepreneur, a growing phenomenon enabled by digital marketplaces, pop-ups and changing consumer preferences. Drawing on 27 qualitative interviews with individuals operating craft ventures either alongside or instead of full-time employment, we investigate why such transitions occur and how individuals sustain the liminal phase that accompanies them. Using a liminality and narrative identity work lens, we show that ELEs occupy a prolonged “betwixt-and-between” state in which neither artisanal nor entrepreneurial identities become dominant. Rather than forming a stable hybrid identity, individuals continuously switch between artisanal and entrepreneurial roles in response to situational demands. This ongoing identity work both enables venture survival and leads to perpetual liminality. The findings extend theories of entrepreneurial identity and voluntary career transitions by showing that, in lifestyle entrepreneurship, artisanal identity remains primary while entrepreneurial identity operates as a performative and contingent role.

Beyond the Referral Tie: Social Exchange Norm, POS, and Post-Hire Outcomes

OB
Zonghui Li (Missouri State University); Marie Augusta Boyo (Jacksonville University); John C. Shaw (Jacksonville University); Zachary Davis (Jacksonville University)
Referral-based hiring is widely used due to its cost efficiency and positive post-hire outcomes. Prior research explains these advantages primarily through realistic job information and social enrichment processes within the referrer-referral dyad. Drawing on social exchange theory, this study advances the literature by moving beyond this dyadic focus to examine broader post-hire social exchanges between new employees and their organizations. Adopting a restricted versus generalized social exchange perspectives, we theorize that referral-based employees are more likely to develop generalized reciprocity norms, which enhance their perceptions of organizational support. Using survey data, we find that perceived organizational support (POS) mediates the relationship between referral hiring and both affective organizational commitment and job satisfaction. Moreover, organizational rewards as a contextual factor weaken this relationship by compensating for the absence of referral ties among non-referral hires. The findings highlight the importance of social exchange norms and organizational context in shaping equitable post-hire adjustment experiences.

How Recruiter Behavior Shapes Job Seekers' Pre-Entry Organizational Identification and Reactions

OB
Kuoching Yen (National Defense University); Tingko Lee (National Defense University); Tsung hsien Yu; Yi-Lin Chen
Drawing on social identity theory, this study examines how recruiter service behavior shapes job seekers’ organizational identification and subsequent reactions during the recruitment process. We conceptualize recruitment interactions as early identity-relevant encounters and argue that recruiters function as organizational representatives who influence the formation of pre-entry organizational identification before formal membership. Using a two-stage, time-lagged field design, we collected survey data from 312 young adults applying to voluntary military programs in Taiwan. Structural equation modeling and mediation analyses were employed to test the proposed relationships. Results indicate that recruiter service behavior is positively associated with job seekers’ organizational identification before entry. Pre-entry organizational identification, in turn, mediates the relationship between recruiter service behavior and identity-consistent reactions, including retention intention and positive word-of-mouth. These effects remain robust after controlling for organizational attractiveness and demographic factors. Supplementary analyses further show that pre-entry organizational identification positively predicts post-entry organizational identification, and that the hypothesized mediation effects remain significant even when post-entry organizational identification is included as an additional control variable. By demonstrating that organizational identification begins to take shape during recruitment rather than only after organizational entry, this study challenges prevailing assumptions about the timing of identification processes and extends social identity theory to early recruitment contexts. The findings highlight recruitment as a critical site of organizational identity construction with enduring implications for understanding how organizational attachments emerge.

Reputations for Exceptional Loyalty and Racialized Inferences About Promotion Prospects

OB
Sun Young Lee (UCL - University of London)
Employee loyalty is widely regarded as a virtue that benefits both organizations and individuals. However, in this research, challenging the implicit assumption that loyalty is ubiquitously beneficial to employees, I explore whether reputations for exceptional loyalty can undermine employees’ promotion prospects. Furthermore, drawing on role congruity theory, I propose that this reputational penalty is racialized, such that it is more pronounced for White employees than for East Asian employees as such reputations contradict White stereotypes to be agentic. Across a workplace survey and two experiments, I find evidence that exceptional loyalty can hinder promotion prospects, particularly for White employees. This effect is mediated by the perception that White employees known for exceptional loyalty are less dominant and self-assured than their counterparts without such a reputation. However, reputations for exceptional loyalty did not affect either perceived agency or promotion prospects in East Asian employees. These findings contribute to research on employee loyalty, ethnic stereotypes, and personnel decisions by revealing how a behavior traditionally celebrated in organizations may backfire under certain reputational and racialized conditions.
