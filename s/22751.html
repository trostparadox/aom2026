Unpacking the Curvilinear Relationship Between Telework Extent and Creativity

OB
Yixin Wang (Nanjing University); Weiwei Huo; Bingqian Liang (Shanghai University); Yi Shi (Shanghai University)
Telework has become an increasingly common practice in the digital age, spurred by technological advancements. This shift has sparked a renewed interest in exploring the effects of telework on employees, and understanding its impact on creativity is crucial for fostering organizational innovation and competitiveness. Grounded in the conservation of resources theory, we developed and tested a model considering a potential curvilinear relationship between telework extent and creativity. We further examined the mediating role of workplace anxiety (employees’ feelings of nervousness, uneasiness, and tension regarding work) and the moderating role of temporal leadership (leaders’ behaviors that help subordinates improve their time management efficiency). Using time-lagged data from 521 employees, our analyses revealed a U-shaped relationship between telework extent and workplace anxiety, which became nonsignificant under low temporal leadership. Moreover, workplace anxiety mediated the inverted U-shaped relationship between telework extent and creativity. Overall, our findings provide significant theoretical and practical implications for understanding the relationship between telework and creativity and underscore the important role of workplace anxiety.

A Meta-Analysis of Perceived Overqualification: Resource-Based Mechanisms with Cultural Moderators

OB
Peiyu Ge (Kyoto University); Tomoki Sekiguchi (Kyoto University)
Perceived overqualification (POQ), the belief that one’s qualifications exceed job requirements, is an increasingly prevalent phenomenon, yet empirical findings regarding its effects remain mixed. Based on Conservation of Resources theory, this meta-analysis of 233 studies (257 samples, N = 89,617) examines how POQ influences work behavior through two competing mechanisms: personal-resource sufficiency and workplace-resource depletion. Results indicate that POQ simultaneously builds personal resources and depletes workplace resources, generating opposing effects on behavior. Personal-resource sufficiency promotes proactive and prosocial behavior and reduces counterproductive work behavior, whereas workplace-resource depletion shows a reversed pattern. Overall, POQ exhibits a positive total effect on proactive and counterproductive work behavior, and a negative total effect on prosocial behavior. In terms of cultural moderation, the positive link between POQ and personal-resource sufficiency is weaker in high-power-distance and high-individualism contexts, whereas the depletion pathway remains invariant. These findings integrate the dual pathways of POQ and clarify the cultural boundary conditions of its effects. The theoretical and practical implications of these findings are discussed.

Different Sizes of Fish in Small Ponds: Individual Perceived Overqualification Diversity in the Team

OB
Qijie Ma (Zhejiang Gongshang University); Lynda Jiwen Song (University of Leeds); Ningyu Tang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
The existing literature on perceived overqualification has examined employees’ reactions to differences between their perceptions of their own overqualification and that of their coworkers using a single, generalized reference point, yet has overlooked how individual perceived overqualification diversity in the team may have more complex implications at the team level. Based on social identity theory, this study suggests a theoretical model for investigating when, why, and how individual perceived overqualification diversity in the team is linked to team identification and team creativity. Using 74 teams from 18 companies in China, our results show that individual perceived overqualification diversity in the team is linked to lower team creativity through its negative association with team identification. These relationships are more negative when individual perceived overqualification mean in the team is higher or when task interdependence is higher. Our findings provide insights into how organizations and leaders can develop and manage teams with different diversity and mean levels of member perceived overqualification.

Constructive Feedback and Performance: The Roles of Feedback-Taking Behaviors and Perceived Control

OB
Zhuofan Kan (Nanjing University); Sun Young Sung (Nanjing University)
While constructive supervisor feedback is widely regarded as a vital tool for improving employee performance, empirical evidence regarding its effectiveness remains inconsistent. Drawing on cognitive appraisal theory and the affect-based circumplex model of responses, this study deconstructs constructive feedback into two distinct forms (i.e., supportive and challenging) and examines how they shape job performance through feedback-taking behaviors, including active acceptance, passive acceptance, and resistance. Using matched supervisor–follower dyadic data, the findings reveal that different forms of constructive feedback are associated with distinct patterns of recipient responses, which subsequently have important implications for job performance. The results further suggest that perceived control serves as a meaningful boundary condition that shapes how followers appraise and respond to constructive feedback. These findings contribute to the feedback literature by shifting the focus from simple valence to a bidimensional framework that considers both valence and activation in recipient responses. The study also advances understanding of social appraisal processes in feedback contexts and offers practical insights for managers on how to balance supportive and challenging communication to promote more effective feedback outcomes.
