Bias In, Symbolic Compliance Out? GPT’s Reliance on Gender and Race in Strategic Evaluations

OMT
Tristan L. Botelho (Yale University); Qingyang (Iris) Wang (Yale University)
Strategic decision-making often requires evaluating more candidates than can be thoroughly assessed, leading evaluators to rely on proxies like gender and race and disadvantaging underrepresented minorities (URMs). As organizations increasingly adopt large language models (LLMs) such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT to support high-stakes evaluations, it is critical to understand whether these tools reinforce or mitigate inequality in evaluation processes. We examine whether and how LLMs use gender and race in strategic evaluations using computational experiments involving 26,000 assessments of innovative offerings (e.g., startup pitches). We find that GPT evaluators do not disadvantage—and even modestly support—URMs, primarily by avoiding negative outcomes. We theorize that this reflects symbolic compliance: A superficial response to avoid overt discrimination rather than a genuine commitment to fairness. To test this mechanism, we conduct “Second Opinion” experiments in which GPT evaluates pitches alongside simulated human inputs. Across 18,000 evaluations, GPT is more likely to correct overt bias (e.g., identity-based stereotypes) than justified bias (e.g., framed as business critiques), and these corrections are limited in size. Together, the findings suggest that while may not reproduce historical and societal bias in overt form, they remain sensitive to gender and racial cues and exhibit shallow bias-correction capacity. This study highlights the implications of LLM adoption in strategic evaluations.

Praising Without Validating: Gender Disparities in the Qualitative Content of Referrals in STEM

OMT
Marco Dongil Jang (Nanyang Technological University, Singapore)
Despite women earning half of STEM bachelor’s degrees, they remain underrepresented in the workforce due to gendered professional reputations. This study analyzes 3 million LinkedIn recommendations to determine whether social endorsements counteract or reproduce these stereotypes. I utilize a novel “occupational domain signaling” framework, measured through a domain-contingent specificity dictionary derived from O*NET competency statements. This approach moves beyond general warmth-competence binaries to capture the technical validation necessary for professional legitimacy. The results reveal a significant paradox: referrals for women in STEM are longer and more emotionally positive, yet contain substantially less task-oriented content than those for men. While women receive significantly higher sentiment scores, they suffer a substantial penalty in task specificity. These findings suggest that current sponsorship efforts inadvertently reproduce a legitimacy gap by prioritizing social cohesion over technical expertise. Ultimately, this linguistic pattern maintains the cultural image of STEM as a masculine domain by withholding the specific technical signaling afforded to men, despite the meritocratic veneer of the evaluation process.

Gaming the Lottery: A Study of Institutional Toying and Gendered Migration (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Reed Keller Eaglesham (Cornell University)
Organizations may face regulatory barriers to accessing top talent. In response, firms can shape or maneuver around these legal obstacles; this is referred to as institutional toying or legal avoidance. Common barriers to accessing workers include regulatory mobility frictions, which are legal limitations on the geographic movement of workers within and between countries. Successfully reducing these frictions can have profound benefits for workers, but who do firms prioritize for institutional toying? Given documented gendered hiring practices by firms, I argue that firms will prefer male applicants. Drawing on theories of gender inequality and human capital signaling, I propose several potential explanations for why firms prioritize certain individuals, including gendered organizations, occupational stereotypes, and tokenism. Moreover, I examine whether human capital signals (e.g., higher education, domestic credentials) reduce gender differences. I test these explanations using a case of toying that took place between 2021 and 2024 in the American high-skilled temporary visa program (H-1B visa), which allows skilled immigrants to work in the U.S. for a fixed duration. To avoid a lottery imposed by the U.S. government, firms realized they could submit the same candidate multiple times under different subsidiaries or business entities. Using data on the population of H-1B visa lottery winners from the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), I find a gender preference for male candidates. This preference is maintained even within stereotypically female occupations and female-dominated job queues. However, gender differences in toying behavior are muted under foreign credentials and at higher levels of education. This work contributes to our understanding of how structural advantages are formed and highlights the role firms play in shaping immigration systems.

How Oral Communication Shapes Gender Differences in Idea Evaluation: Evidence from TED Talks

OMT
Raquel Pruna (Carlos III University of Madrid)
Women’s ideas are often evaluated less favorably than men’s, reflecting a bias that extends beyond individuals to their contributions. This disadvantage is partly rooted in gendered perceptions of authority. However, efforts to project such authority—via assertive speech or dominant body language—can backfire for violating gender norms. This paper explores whether and how oral communication can help women navigate this double bind. I argue that when women express their ideas using language that signals cognitive complexity, it helps shift audience focus away from stereotypes and toward the content of their ideas. Moreover, when cognitive complexity is combined with another signal of authority—a low voice pitch—evaluative bias may be further mitigated. To test these ideas, I examine audience reactions to TED Talks. Results show that while audiences generally respond more favorably to male speakers when language reflects low cognitive complexity, this gap narrows when language reflects high cognitive complexity—and even reverses when paired with a lower voice pitch. The findings help elucidate boundary conditions to the evaluative double bind, and highlight language and voice as mechanisms through which ideas are evaluated, offering insight into communicative strategies that can help reduce gender disparities in knowledge markets and leadership.
