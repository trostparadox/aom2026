Bricolage, Ambidexterity, and Leadership in SMEs: A Moderated Mediation Model

ENT
Arsenii Bystrov (Higher School of Economics); Galina Shirokova (HSE University); Anna Tyutneva
This study investigates the impact of entrepreneurial bricolage on the performance of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), with particular attention to the mediating role of organizational ambidexterity and the moderating influence of entrepreneurial leadership. Drawing on data from 338 SMEs and employing a moderated mediation analytical framework, the findings reveal that entrepreneurial bricolage exerts a positive effect on firm performance and facilitates the development of ambidexterity. Notably, however, organizational ambidexterity is found to have a negative direct effect on performance. Entrepreneurial leadership attenuates this adverse impact, thereby alleviating the performance-related disruptions of ambidexterity. These results indicate that while bricolage serves as a valuable strategic approach for resource-constrained firms, its effectiveness is contingent upon the firm’s ability to balance exploratory and exploitative activities, a balance shaped significantly by the leadership of the CEO. The study advances current entrepreneurial discourse and offers practical implications for SMEs striving to innovate within constrained environments.

Disruptive Innovation Pathways: A Configurational Approach in Diverse Economic Contexts

ENT
Isabelle Krupp (HHL Leipzig Graduate School of Management); Justin Siljeur (Stellenbosch University); Jerome L. Antonio (HHL Leipzig Graduate School of Management); Dominik K. Kanbach (HHL Leipzig Graduate School of Management)
Today’s developing economies are becoming critical engines of innovation with growing relevance for democratizing access to products and services. In this context, disruptive innovations are a widely discussed phenomenon, referring to the emergence of simpler, more affordable solutions that initially appeal to underserved customer segments, eventually reshaping mainstream markets, offering a potentially promising approach to further democratize access to innovations. Despite being well-established, the theory of disruptive innovation often fails to deliver on its promise, especially in resource-constrained contexts. This raises the question of how and under what conditions disruptive innovation manifests in developing economies. To address this gap, this study employs a mixed-methods design combining inductive qualitative theory building with fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis to identify configurations that enable or hinder disruptive innovation. The study draws on 52 semi-structured interviews with startup founders and leaders of incumbent spinoffs in developing economies (South Africa) and developed economies (Switzerland and Germany) in the finance and insurance technology sectors. The findings reveal that disruptive innovation can follow multiple context-specific configurations, shaped by the interaction with institutional, technological, leadership, market and resource conditions. Digital technologies, particularly artificial intelligence, play a critical role by enabling affordability, accessibility, and scale, specifically by reshaping underlying processes of disruptive innovation. This study also contributes to extending the theory of disruptive innovation by mapping context-specific pathways of disruption effectiveness and offering actionable insights that guide entrepreneurs, incumbent organizations, scholars, and policymakers in fostering inclusive innovation ecosystems.

Pathways to Formalization: Business Registration in Sub-Saharan Africa

ENT
Chad David Coffman (Boise State University); Susana C. Santos (Florida State University); Jeffrey S. Hornsby (University of Missouri-Kansas City); Brian S. Anderson (University of Kansas)
This paper examines the factors that influence the decision to formally register a new venture by nascent entrepreneurs in Sub-Saharan Africa. Understanding these factors is important because registration is the inflection point where entrepreneurs move from the informal to the formal economy. Registered businesses are more likely to pay taxes, hire employees, access capital for growth, and participate in other activities that contribute to economic development. In Study 1, we interviewed entrepreneurs and institutional actors in Ghana and Ivory Coast to identify factors that influence the registration decision, and data was analyzed using the grounded theory development approach. In Study 2, we develop a model from Study 1 and use a survey design to explore how these influence the registration decision. In Study 3, we create a decision tree based on our findings from Study 2 and use a scenario design to test the external validity of our findings.

The Illusion of Support: How ESOs Shape Entrepreneurial Realities in Sub-Saharan Africa

ENT
Krista Charlier Sarlje (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Holger Ernst; Franz Kellermanns (University of North Carolina at Charlotte); Justin Wolfgang Webb
Young entrepreneurs face low survival rates; therefore, it is assumed that any support to them is beneficial but value of Enterprise Support Organizations (ESO) in non-WEIRD contexts is not well understood. We conducted 76 interviews and 23 field visits in Sub-Saharan Africa and found that instead of focusing on entrepreneurs, ESOs rather focus on social issues and self-preservation activities which are highly opportunistic. Moreover, current setup incentivizes entrepreneurs to engage in ESO hopping – joining multiple support programs simultaneously. This creates never-ending demand for ESOs in Sub-Saharan Africa and stimulates continuous need for donor funding which largely comes from Global North.
