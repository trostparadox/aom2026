When Alignment Leads to Risk or Restraint: CEO-Board Ideological Alignment and Strategic Risk-Taking

STR
Md Kamrul Hasan (Louisiana State University); Guclu Atinc (Valdosta State University)
Why do firms with similarly ideologically aligned CEOs and boards pursue systematically different levels of strategic risk-taking, with some embracing bold strategic risks while others consistently avoid them, despite facing comparable conditions and governance structures? Prior research on strategic risk-taking has mainly focused on incentives, resources, and formal governance arrangements, offering limited insight into how shared beliefs and values within leadership teams shape firms’ risk-taking behavior. Drawing on regulatory focus theory, political psychology, and value congruence theory, this study examines how the beliefs and values shared between CEOs and boards influence strategic risk-taking decisions. We argue that differences in ideological content help explain variation in strategic risk-taking, with liberal alignment encouraging risk-taking and conservative alignment favoring more cautious strategic behavior. We also argue that CEO duality strengthens these relationships by concentrating decision-making authority and limiting internal challenge, thereby allowing shared beliefs to have a stronger influence on firm behavior. Analyzing panel data from S&P 500 firms over the period 2013-2022, comprising 4,717 firm-year observations, we find support for these arguments. Overall, the findings show that when decision power is concentrated and leaders share the same ideological views, strategic risk decisions are shaped in ways that have important ethical implications for oversight and responsibility in corporate governance.

Ideological Disruption Without Decline: Owner Shifts and Firm Growth

STR
Henrique Minetto Brabo; Paulo Arvate (FGV EAESP)
How do organizations perform when internal ideological alignment changes? While prior management research shows that political ideology shapes governance practices and employee sorting across firms, little is known about whether ideological (mis)alignment between owners and employees affects organizational performance - particularly when owners’ ideological orientations change over time. We address this question by examining whether changes in owner ideology reconfigure internal ideological alignment and whether such reconfiguration constrains firm growth. Using linked administrative data from Brazil that combine corporate ownership records, voter registration data, and the universe of formal employment relationships from 2003 to 2017, we track owner ideology, workforce composition, and firm-level employment. Our empirical strategy exploits within-owner changes in partisan affiliation as quasi-exogenous variation in the firm’s ideological environment and implements a staggered difference-in-differences design estimated with doubly robust inverse probability weighting. We find that ideological shifts by owners lead to persistent reductions in internal ideological alignment, indicating meaningful workforce reconfiguration. However, these disruptions are not associated with declines in firm performance as measured by employment growth. These patterns are robust across firm sizes, owner gender, political environments, and alternative ideological classifications. Together, our findings show that political ideology structures organizational sorting while firms adapt to ideological change without systematically constraining growth

‘True Colors’ in Private Equity: Political Ideology and Purposeful Growth

STR
Deirdre Coveney (University of Leeds); Koen Heimeriks; Huimin Liang (Warwick Business Schoool); Irina Minodora Surdu
Environmental, social, and governance considerations have become an increasingly prominent yet contested element of investment strategy, creating variation in how private equity firms incorporate purposeful objectives into their funds. This study examines the extent to which political ideology shapes purposeful growth in private equity by analysing how the political characteristics of a firm’s headquarters environment and the political behaviour of its decision-makers influence the allocation of capital to ESG-positive investments. Drawing on a dataset of 2,281 fund-level observations that links portfolio composition, political contributions, and headquarters location, we find that republican political environments are associated with systematically lower levels of purposeful growth, while political engagement among executives and democratic non-executive actors corresponds to greater purposeful investment. These effects operate in distinct and asymmetric ways, with republican non-executives reinforcing the influence of republican contexts. By identifying political ideology as a source of strategic heterogeneity in purposeful investment, the study contributes to research on corporate governance, institutional embeddedness, and the political foundations of firm strategy.

Political Polarization in Strategic Leadership Systems

STR
Maximilian Weis (ESCP Business School); Michelle Weck; Max Braun (LMU Munich School of Management); Fabian K. Ahrens (Universität der Bundeswehr München)
Increasing polarization among strategic leaders at the apex of corporations poses unique challenges for strategy. Such polarization leads to greater divergence in strategic priorities, heightening the threat of a breakdown in the coordination routine within the strategic leadership system (SLS) and potentially leading firms into strategic paralysis. In this study, we examine the link between polarization in the SLS and strategic paralysis, and whether independent and interdependent coordination routines in the SLS mitigate or exacerbate it. Using S&P 500 firms from 2011 to 2023, we find that increasing ideological divergence of the actors in an SLS weakens ESG, a heavily polarized strategic issue. Our findings further suggest that coordination routines may overcome the SLS’s inability to make strategic ESG decisions. Independence routines safeguard effective monitoring, and interdependence routines ensure collective strategic visioning. However, when either is missing, strategic paralysis is aggravated. Our study contributes to the SLS literature by positioning polarization within SLS as an antecedent of strategic paralysis, which is an alternative outcome to bold strategies emphasized in prior work in response to polarization. We highlight that increasing polarization challenges strategy not only by increasing the risk of misalignment with stakeholder expectations but also by increasing the risk of inertia. We also show that independent and interdependent coordination routines act as complements rather than substitutes in preventing SLS from sliding into strategic paralysis.

How Investors’ Political Positions Shape CEO-Employee Pay Disparity

STR
Fabian Leeners (RWTH Aachen University)
Prior research has established that political ideology shapes compensation decisions among executives and boards and that institutional investors exert substantial influence over executive pay. Yet these insights have largely evolved in parallel, leaving unexamined how investors’ political positions interact with boards’ ideological orientations in shaping internal pay structures. Adopting a governance-system perspective, we argue that boards should not be viewed in ideological isolation. Instead, distributive outcomes emerge from the ideological interplay between investors and directors. Grounded in agency theory, we hypothesize that firms with more liberal investor bases exhibit lower CEO-employee pay disparity, reflecting stronger pressure to constrain excessive internal inequality. Drawing on social identity theory, we further propose that this relationship is contingent on ideological alignment between investors and boards, such that investor influence is amplified when boards are also politically liberal. We test these arguments using panel data from S&P 500 firms from 2008 to 2020, combining data on executive compensation, institutional ownership, firm financials, and political donation records of investors and directors. Results show a negative association between investor liberalism and CEO-employee pay disparity, which is stronger when boards are more liberal. These findings extend corporate governance research by shifting attention from individual governance actors to ideological interactions across governance layers.
