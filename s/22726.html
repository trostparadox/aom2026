Reexamining Operational Slack and Scope in Dynamic Environments:A Replication of Kovach et al.(2015)

OSCM
Oumayma Elbouchtaoui (Rabat Business School, International University of Rabat); Ahmed Hamdi (Rabat Business School, International University of Rabat); Arash Azadegan (Rutgers University-New Brunswick); Tarik Saikouk (Excelia Business School)
Responding to calls for replication research in the operations management literature, this paper revisits Kovach’s (2015) study, Firm Performance in Dynamic Environments: The Role of Operational Slack and Operational Scope. Using a more recent and broader panel of the U.S. manufacturing firms from Compustat, we reexamine how operational slack and operational scope influence firm performance and assess whether the original findings hold under current environmental conditions. We replicate the core model specifications and extend the analysis with broader firm and time coverage, providing a stronger test of performance implications in dynamic environments. Results indicate that the effects of slack and scope on performance are not uniform and vary with environmental conditions and sample composition, highlighting both similarities and departures from Kovach’s original conclusions. Overall, this research updates and reinforces evidence on the performance value of operational slack and scope, clarifies their boundary conditions, and offers practical insights for managers on when to prioritize buffering capacity versus broadening operational scope in response to environmental dynamism.

Understanding Industrial Symbiosis Readiness: A TOES-Based Configurational Analysis

OSCM
Maria Di Gregorio (University of Naples Federico II); Stefano Abbate (Pegaso Telematic University); Piera Centobelli (University of Naples Federico II)
Within the transition toward circular economic models, Industrial Symbiosis (IS) represents a strategic approach to optime resource flows by transforming waste and by-products into valuable inputs for new production processes. Despite its significant potential, the adoption of symbiotic practices faces various technological, organisational, and institutional barriers. This research explores the driving factors and obstacles influencing the adoption of IS in the agri-food sector. A systematic literature review was carried out to identify critical success factors, which were then organised within the Technology– Organisation–External–Sustainability (TOES) framework. These factors were subsequently operationalised through a structured questionnaire distributed to a sample of agri-food firms. The collected data were analysed using Fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA). The findings show that readiness for IS stems from various causal configurations, not just one. Three sufficient configurations were identified, all characterised by a clear recognition of circular economy advantages and the efficient valorisation of residues. By adopting a configurational approach, this study deepens the understanding of the complex dynamics underlying IS and offers both theoretical and practical insights to promote the adoption of circular practices in the agri-food sector.

Passing It Along: Behavioral Drivers of Information Diffusion in Supply Chains

OSCM
Derek Dubois (University of Rhode Island); Anis Triki (University of Rhode Island); Muhammad Hasan Ashraf (California State University-Long Beach); Mehmet G. Yalcin (University of Rhode Island)
This study examines how digitalization impacts the news media service supply chain (NMSSC) by positioning supply chain (SC) managers as consumers who act as super-distributors of industry information. It explores how source professionalism, perceived credibility, and outrage rhetoric interact to shape these professionals’ willingness to extend supply chain flows through news sharing. Anchored in legitimacy theory and social identity theory, the research highlights how these dynamics enable or constrain operant resources of knowledge and collaborative communication in broader supply chains. A 2x2 between-subjects behavioral experiment was conducted with 269 U.S.-based participants. Respondents viewed manipulated news vignettes that varied in source professionalism (professional vs. amateur) and in rhetorical tone (outrage present vs. absent). Measures captured perceived credibility, intention to share, and issue alignment. Professional sources are generally considered more credible, but this advantage diminishes when outrage rhetoric is present. While credibility significantly predicts news-sharing intention, outrage rhetoric moderates the mediating effect of credibility, dampening the influence of professionalism on sharing. The effect is most pronounced when source professionalism is high, and outrage is low. The study frames news media as a supply chain that enables operant resources relevant to SCM decision-making and coordination mechanisms. It extends legitimacy and social identity theories into digital supply chain contexts, showing how SC managers’ perceptions and identity alignment shape the diffusion of knowledge. For practitioners, the findings guide how media narratives influence decision-making, coordination, and vulnerability to misinformation across supply chains.

Exploring DEI from Employment to Supply Chains: A Dynamic Capability Perspective

OSCM
Asad Ali Qazi (Sukkur IBA University); Abdul Rehman Shaikh (Sukkur IBA University)
Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) are widely discussed in organizational research, particularly in relation to internal policies, employment practices, and supporting functions. At the same time, a growing body of supply chain research examines supplier diversity and inclusive sourcing. However, these two streams remain largely disconnected, offering limited insight into how DEI moves from internal organizational practices into core operations, supply chain decision making, and the inclusion of suppliers from marginalized communities. This gap is especially pronounced in developing economy contexts, where operational priorities and institutional conditions differ markedly from those assumed in much of the existing literature. This study examines how firms in Pakistan navigate the transition of DEI from internal employment practices to operational and supply chain contexts. Using a qualitative research design, the study draws on 23 semi-structured interviews with practitioners primarily from operations and supply chain functions, complemented by human resource representatives. Data were analysed using the Gioia approach to develop a process-oriented understanding of how DEI evolves across organizational domains. The findings show that DEI is well recognized within internal organizational practices but remains weakly understood and unevenly applied in supply chain contexts. Firms typically extend DEI into operations only after performance validation, while supplier inclusion remains rare and often driven by historical, necessity-based, or symbolic considerations. Applying a dynamic capability perspective, the study shows that firms engage in sensing and seizing activities, yet struggle to fully reconfigure supply chain practices. The study provides empirically grounded insights into why DEI transitions stall before reaching meaningful supplier inclusion in developing economies.
