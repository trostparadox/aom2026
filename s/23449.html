AI Chatbots in Education: Ethical Challenges, Student Performance Orientation, and Trust in AI

TIM
Nagina Gul (Balochistan University of Information Technology, Engineering and Management Sciences); Qaiser Mohi Ud Din (Harbin Engineering University); Tiansen Liu (Harbin Engineering University); Fanshuai Hu (Harbin Engineering University); Qiaowei Zhang (Harbin Engineering University)
Background: Integrating AI into education presents both transformative opportunities and ethical challenges. In Pakistan’s higher education sector, the adoption of AI-powered chatbots, such as ChatGPT, has accelerated, prompting concerns about their influence on academic integrity and traditional educational values. This paper explores how these technologies can enhance student learning while safeguarding ethical standards. Methods: Guided by the Social Information Processing Theory, this quantitative study surveyed 875 students across public universities in Pakistan. A structured questionnaire was used to assess students’ usage patterns, perceptions of AI chatbots in academic settings and ethical concerns. Mediation and moderation analyses were conducted using structural equation modeling to examine the roles of performance orientation and trust in AI in shaping ethical perceptions. Results: Our findings reveal that AI chatbots significantly improve students’ academic experience by offering real-time feedback, personalized learning, and enhanced engagement. However, their usage further intensifies ethical concerns, including risks to academic honesty, potential content bias, and diminished transparency in decision-making. The performance orientation was seen to mediate the relationship between chatbot adoption and ethical concerns, while trust in AI moderated how students interpreted and managed these concerns. Conclusions: AI chatbots hold considerable promises for enriching educational qualities, yet their integration should be carefully managed to uphold the academic integrity and ethical standards. Our findings recommend developing clear institutional policies, ethical guidelines, and transparency measures to govern the responsible use of AI in educational contexts. Educators, policymakers, and technology developers are suggested to build a close collaboration to foster fairness, accountability, and trust in AI systems. These findings create actionable and important insights for highlighting that technological innovation complements, rather than compromises, the foundational values of higher education.

Differential Effects of AI Capabilities on Latent Customer Need Identification

TIM
Ziqing Feng (Jilin University); Runping Guo (Jilin University); Peng Lu (Jilin University); XIANTAO WANG (Southeast University)
Nowadays, firms increasingly rely on AI not only to enhance operational efficiency but also to uncover new sources of customer value. Yet, how different AI capabilities shape firms’ ability to identify latent customer needs remains insufficiently understood. Drawing on the multiple-AIs perspective, this study develops and empirically tests an integrated framework that examines the differential effects of AI-enabled automation, analytics, and relational capabilities on the identification of latent customer needs, while also considering the moderating role of AI readiness. Using survey data from Chinese AI firms, our findings reveal a nuanced pattern of effects. Specifically, AI-enabled automation capability exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with the identification of latent customer needs, whereas AI-enabled analytics capability and AI-enabled relational capability exert positive effects. Moreover, AI readiness functions as a capability-specific boundary condition. While it does not significantly moderate the nonlinear effect of AI-enabled automation capability, AI readiness strengthens the positive effects of AI-enabled analytics capability and AI-enabled relational capability. Overall, this study challenges the prevailing assumption of uniformly positive returns to AI adoption and advances a more differentiated understanding of how AI reshapes the identification of latent customer needs.

Disrupting the Human Benchmark: Rethinking Perceived Service Quality in the Age of AI

TIM
Maike Netscher (Neu-Ulm University of Applied Sciences); Sven M. Laudien (University of Bayreuth); Alexander H. Kracklauer
AI service agents are reshaping customer experiences, yet little is known about how their use affects perceived service quality in high-involvement purchase settings. This study addresses this gap by examining biases in customer evaluations of AI-based versus ostensibly human services in fashion retail. Drawing on research on AI and service quality, we argue that cognitive biases systematically shape how customers evaluate service encounters with both human and AI agents. We analyze this relevant aspect using a self-programmed AI service agent in a two-group scenario experiment. One group is explicitly informed that the service is delivered by an AI service agent, while the other is not. Data from Gen Z customers - a critical segment for both fashion and digital service adoption - enables the identification of core drivers of perceived service quality and the detection of cognitive biases. Our findings advance service research by integrating an up to now widely new, non-rational behavioral perspective into the evaluation of AI-enabled interactions, challenging rational-choice assumptions that dominate extant literature. For business practice, the results offer guidance on how retailers can design and communicate AI services to mitigate biases, enhance quality perceptions, and foster the development of customer trust and loyalty.

Effective Personalized AI Tutors via LLM-Guided Reinforcement Learning

TIM
Angel Tsai-Hsuan Chung (University of Pennsylvania); Ling-Chieh Kung; Osbert Bastani (University of Pennsylvania)
Generative AI is reshaping work faster than education and training systems can adapt, creating an urgent need for efficient skill acquisition. Existing adaptive learning algorithms have limited visibility into learners’ progress, constraining their ability to personalize training effectively. We address this limitation by designing an AI-powered learning platform that integrates large language models (LLMs) with reinforcement learning (RL): the LLM generates exercises, provides tutoring support, and extracts rich behavioral signals from learner interactions, while the RL agent uses these signals to adaptively sequence practice question difficulty to maintain productive struggle matched to each learner’s evolving skill level. In partnership with the Taipei City Government and the American Institute in Taiwan, we launched a five-month “AI for Python Learning” program to evaluate this platform by conducting a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with 1,047 high school students. Despite a strong control condition that all students had access to an LLM tutor, the treatment group receiving additional RL-driven adaptive sequencing achieved a 0.15 standard deviation increase in in-person, paper-based exam performance—equivalent to 6–8 months of additional schooling. Mediation analysis suggests that these gains were driven by increased learner engagement. These results provide large-scale field evidence that combining rich interaction signals via LLM with adaptive learning algorithm can significantly accelerate skill acquisition beyond access to generative AI alone.

Examining the Evolution of Agent Communication and Sentiment Through Adaptive Structuration Theory

TIM
Triparna De Vreede (University of South Florida); Nathaniel Hartman (University of South Florida); Gerardo Wibmer (University of South Florida); Ankur Mali (University of South Florida)
How do service workers appropriate AI tools, and what determines whether appropriation enhances or undermines organizational sustainability? This study examines technology appropriation through Adaptive Structuration Theory, analyzing 19,900 insurance call center interactions before and after implementing an AI note-taking feature. Using interrupted time series analysis with agent fixed effects, we find that agents increased communication richness and call duration immediately post-implementation, but experienced declining emotional engagement over time. This paradox—where freed cognitive resources flow toward measured efficiency dimensions while unmeasured emotional authenticity deteriorates—reveals that technology appropriation is not determined by technology affordances alone but by organizational measurement and incentive systems. Agents rationally appropriate freed capacity toward what is measured and rewarded. We develop the "appropriation paradox" as a theoretical construct and offer concrete guidance for organizations seeking balanced outcomes across efficiency, customer relationships, and employee wellbeing. Our findings suggest that measurement system redesign must precede technology implementation to enable sustainable appropriation patterns.
