Awareness by Association: Technology for Crowd Coordination in Interdependent Physical Tasks

CTO
Ehsan Nouri (University of Virginia); Terri L Griffith (Simon Fraser University); Nilesh Saraf (Simon Fraser University)
Crowds in critical scenarios such as disaster relief and protest mobilization increasingly leverage digital technologies to coordinate participants’ engagement in physical actions. Physical constraints, such as co-location and the scheduling of shared material resources, however, limit the ability of collaboration technologies to support coordination. Prior research explains how crowds organize knowledge collaboration in online environments through affordances and collective knowledge exchange mechanisms; however, the physicality of action extends this understanding. Advancing research on emergent collaboration in physical work, we propose that association mapping of physical elements, defined as the provision of real-time cues linking actors, tasks, and resources, improves coordination performance through the mediating process of developing shared situational awareness. To test this theoretical model, we developed Civic Grid, an online multiplayer experimental environment simulating disaster relief, in which groups of four participants coordinate to repair structures distributed across a grid-based map. Using this platform, we conducted an online experiment with 512 participants (128 groups) assigned to four conditions varying in association mapping and physical task interdependence. We measured groups’ shared situational awareness and coordination performance across conditions. The results verify the mediating role of shared cognition in translating association mapping into coordination performance and show that this effect is moderated by the complexity of physical interdependencies. These findings extend our understanding of technology support for emergent collaboration in physically grounded tasks and clarify its boundary conditions.

Resource-Stratified Meritocracy: Why Fair Evaluation Produces Unequal Advancement in OSS Communities

CTO
Helene Nicole Hellwig (LMU Munich); Luca Haaks (LMU Munich School of Management)
Open-source software communities espouse meritocracy, yet most contributors never gain authority. We investigate why, by using eleven years of data from Kubernetes, one of the world's largest open-source software communities. At first glance, corporate contributors advance at 1.5× the rate of individuals, suggesting organizational favoritism. However, when we controlled for contribution volume, this advantage reversed: corporate affiliation was associated with lower odds of advancement. We found that reaching authority requires approximately 150 contributions over three years, equivalent to 450- 600 hours of work. Most contributors cannot sustain this without paid time and organizational support. Thus, evaluation within the community is largely fair, but access to the conditions needed to demonstrate merit is unequal. We introduce the concept of resource-stratified meritocracy to describe systems where merit governs within resource levels, but capacity constraints determine who can participate at advancement-eligible levels. As a result, fair evaluation can still generate unequal outcomes when the capacity to demonstrate merit is unequally distributed. This pattern can be extended beyond open-source software to academic careers, political organizing, volunteer leadership, and any domain requiring sustained, intensive engagement.

Perceived Intergenerational Workplace Digital Divides and the Erosion of Gen X Employee Contribution

CTO
Rui Ma (Xiamen University); Jin Cheng (Xiamen University); Yafeng Song (Xiamen University); congjing Zheng (Xiamen University)
The aging workforce increasingly encounters digital transformation, however, the psychological mechanisms through which senior employees experience intergenerational digital differences remain underexplored (Rudolph & Zacher, 2022). This research examines how perceived workplace intergenerational digital divide (PWIDD) affects Generation X employees’ generativity at work. It investigates the serial mediating roles of perception of learning futility and qualitative job insecurity, and the moderating effects of fixed mindset and organizational tenure. Based on conservation of resources (COR) theory, this research proposes that PWIDD causes a resource loss spiral wherein perceived digital disadvantage generates learning futility, which produces job insecurity about professional standing, ultimately weakening knowledge transfer to younger colleagues. Data from 285 Chinese Gen X employees (aged 45+) were collected through a three-wave time-lagged survey and analyzed using moderated serial mediation analysis. Results show that PWIDD negatively relates to generativity at work through the serial mediation of learning futility and qualitative job insecurity. Fixed mindset and organizational tenure strengthen this indirect effect. The negative impact of PWIDD on learning futility intensifies when employees hold fixed beliefs about ability or possess longer tenure, consequently reducing generativity. These findings reveal a paradoxical effect of organizational tenure, challenging assumptions about experience as a universal buffer, while fixed mindset operates as a personal vulnerability factor intensifying the loss spiral. This research contributes by demonstrating that perceived digital gaps operate as socio-comparative threats rather than technical deficits. The findings provide insights into how senior employees manage digital uncertainty and highlight the importance of interpretive interventions beyond skills training.

From Dispersed to Engaged: Multilevel Dynamics of Employee Engagement in Global Virtual Teams

CTO
Farheen Fathima Shaik B; Upam Pushpak Makhecha (Indian Institute of Management Tiruchirappalli); Biju Varkkey; Sirish Gouda
Global virtual teams(GVTs), critical to multinational organizations, comprise dispersed members who rely on technology to cooperate across cultural, national, and temporal boundaries. Though GVT research has examined various organizational issues, the dynamic, multi-level nature of engagement of GVTs has been less embedded in research. This paper gives insights into the contextual, emergent conditions that enable engagement in GVTs, aided by a 19-month-long ethnographic study. Our findings suggest that team engagement in GVTs begins gradually with the interaction of individual, team-related contextual, and organizational aspects. Some emergent conditions like trust, psychological safety, and cultural sensitivity arise because of team interactions, while contextual factors like organizational culture and technology infrastructure provide the stage upon which engagement occurs. Collectively, such conditions give rise to cognitive, emotional, and physical investments of GVT members while positing the claim for engaging multi-layered and elaborate grounds for understanding engagement. Setting up such conditions and their interlinks gives building blocks for fostering engagement in GVTs. Our study has practical implications for HR practitioners and team leaders and provides insight into how one may improve communication, build trust, and better harness cultural diversity to improve overall team engagement in GVTs.

The Role of Digital Affordance on the Hospitality Employees’ Well-Being

CTO
Xiya Zhang (Beijing Normal University, Hong Kong Baptist University, United International College); M. S. Balaji (RMIT University); Yangyang Jiang (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
This study investigates the influence of digital affordances on employees’ work experiences within the hospitality sector. It specifically examines how employees perceive the affordances of digital technologies and how such perceptions shape their engagement in their work context, interactions with customers, and overall wellbeing. Furthermore, it explores the moderating roles of organizational support and employee learning orientation in the relationship between digital affordance and employee engagement. By using a mixed-methods design, the study first conducted in-depth interviews with eight hotel employees in China who actively utilized digital technologies in their daily operations. These qualitative insights informed the development of a subsequent quantitative survey administered to 291 hotel employees, enabling empirical examination of the relationships among digital affordance, employee engagement, customer participation, organizational support, employee learning orientation, and employee wellbeing. The findings identify five key attributes of digital affordance—service synchronization, workflow coordination, task autonomy, service adaptive and data-enabled empowerment—that shape employees’ perceptions affordance of digital technology. Additionally, organizational support significantly amplifies the positive relationship between digital affordance and employee engagement in service delivery, whereas employee learning orientation moderates this effect by attenuating it. Moreover, both employee engagement and customer participation serve as mediating mechanisms linking digital affordance to employee wellbeing. While the study advances the conceptual understanding of digital affordances in hospitality workplaces, its empirical findings are based on data collected from Chinese hotel employees. Future research is encouraged to replicate the study across diverse cultural contexts to enhance generalizability.
