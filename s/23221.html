Artificial Intelligence, Knowledge Problems, and Business Venturing Lifecycle

ENT
Parul Manocha (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Rick Hunt (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Entrepreneurial venturing unfolds as a nonlinear journey in which entrepreneurs confront heterogeneous knowledge problems across the business venturing life cycle. AI now enters this journey as an additional interpretive layer, shaping how entrepreneurs perceive possibilities, form judgments, and act. Amid bold claims that AI will either supplant entrepreneurs or simply boost productivity, what is missing is a theory explaining how AI actually intervenes in this trajectory. We argue that AI generates solution sets—bundles of interpretations and action paths—that interact with the five core knowledge problems of uncertainty, ambiguity, equivocality, risk, and complexity. These solution sets, to varying degrees, mitigate and confound judgment. We develop a process theory of venturing with AI that positions knowledge problems as the connective tissue of venture development.

From Prank to Purpose: A Process Model of Playfulness-Based Venturing

ENT
Simone Ferriani; Eleonora Grassi (University of Bologna)
Entrepreneurship is commonly understood as a purposeful endeavor, driven by opportunity recognition, intentional planning, and calculated risk-taking. Yet a growing number of ventures challenge this foundational narrative. This paper examines the origins and evolution of Rockin’1000, a globally successful music project that began as a playful prank to attract the Foo Fighters to a small Italian town. Through an in-depth case study, we examine how playful behavior influences the processes of pursuing radical venturing opportunities. We develop a process model that shows how playfulness, as a behavioral orientation, can catalyze entrepreneurial action by enabling individuals to suspend normative expectations, engage creatively with uncertainty, and mobilize communities and resources around seemingly frivolous acts. In doing so, we bridge social psychology and entrepreneurship theory to reveal how playfulness contributes to the micro-foundations of entrepreneurial action, particularly in the pre-intentional stages of venture emergence. Our findings extend research on motivation, emotion, and the early development of economic activity, challenging the dominant assumption that entrepreneurship is the product of reasoned intent.

Navigating Ambiguity: The Role of Playfulness in New Venture Creation

ENT
Katelyn Reed Sharratt (Technical University of Denmark)
Entrepreneurs face ambiguity in the early stages of venture creation, where goals, problems, and solutions are poorly defined or unknowable. These conditions present challenges, such as knowledge problems, biases and difficult emotions which can inhibit entrepreneurial decision making and action. While prior research has highlighted the role of cognitive constraints and emotional strain under uncertainty, less is known about the enabling mechanisms that allow entrepreneurs to remain engaged in highly ambiguous contexts. This study addresses this gap by introducing playfulness as a behavioral orientation through which entrepreneurs reframe ambiguity, take exploratory action, and generate creative possibilities. Drawing on an inductive qualitative study of 11 early-stage startups in a founder-first venture accelerator, I develop a process model that captures how playfulness unfolds through cognitive reframing, creative experimentation, and affective absorption in discovery. Based on participant observation and interviews, I show how playfulness enables founders to construct meaning, maintain momentum, and remain emotionally engaged in the face of ambiguity. These findings contribute to entrepreneurship theory by expanding understandings of action under ambiguity and by theorizing playfulness as a patterned mode of cognitive-affective engagement with ambiguity in early-stage new venture creation.

Strategic Ambiguity in Turbulent Environments: Leader-Member Communication in Early-Stage Startups

ENT
Jihyun Kang (Ball State University)
The study explores how strategic ambiguity is enacted and interpreted by leaders and organizational members in early-stage startups operating in uncertain and dynamic environments. Prior research suggests that ambiguous communication can be beneficial for established organizations facing uncertainty by opening up opportunities to interpret it from multiple angles and ease the tension. However, little is known about whether and how strategic ambiguity functions in the context of early-stage startups. Drawing on multiple qualitative data from observations, in-depth interviews with entrepreneur-CEOs and employees, and case studies, this study examines communicative practices surrounding uncertainty in small, emerging ventures. Using constant comparative analysis, the findings reveal that strategic ambiguity is relatively rare in early-stage startups. Frequent interactions, close working relationships, and task-oriented communication patterns constrain leaders’ ability to sustain ambiguity, while employees often seek immediate clarification to coordinate action. These findings challenge assumptions about the universal applicability of strategic ambiguity and highlight how organizational size and interactional intensity shape communication practices under uncertainty. The study contributes to research on strategic ambiguity and entrepreneurship by specifying boundary conditions under which ambiguity is enacted in emerging organizations.

Too Late To Be Good: Entrepreneurs’ Ambiguity Tolerance and Firms’ Growth in Turbulent Markets (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Giovanni Orlando (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Joeri Van Hugten (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Wouter Stam (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Arjen Van Witteloostuijn (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Market turbulence constitutes a context where consumers’ preferences change more rapidly than usual. In a turbulent market, ambiguity increases, and as a reflection of this, the entrepreneurial reality becomes more fragmented. This is a double-edged sword for entrepreneurs and their ventures, as a turbulent market generates more growth opportunities, but also more precariousness. In a cross-sectional study conducted on 248 entrepreneurs and their SMEs, we studied the relationship between market turbulence and growth. We do so in two steps. First, we observe the direct relationship between our two variables, then we investigate the role played by the ability of the firm to adaptively respond to the changes by being ambidextrous, and the role played by the entrepreneur to be resistant to the ambiguity brought by the changes by being ambiguity-tolerant. Our results reveal that there is no direct relationship between market turbulence and firms’ growth, but that ambiguity tolerance and ambidexterity together negatively affect the relationship between the two variables. At the same time, when market turbulence is left aside in the interaction, meaning that there is no or average turbulence in the market, our findings reveal that ambiguity tolerance and ambidexterity lead to positive growth. We interpret our findings by suggesting that, contrarily to traditional entrepreneurship literature, ambiguity tolerance is not an only positive entrepreneurial characteristic, but can lead to negative consequences for the firm. Firms’ growth is not a matter of organizational behavior or entrepreneurial behavior distinctively, but it is a consequence of the collaboration of the two. This is reflected both when the consequences are negative (i.e., when under market turbulence, growth is negative) and positive (i.e., when at average levels of market turbulence, growth is positive).
