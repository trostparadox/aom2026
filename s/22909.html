Learning the Wrong Lessons: Changes in Physician Practice After Allegations of Malpractice

OMT
Victoria (Shu) Zhang (Northeastern University); Patrick Bergemann (University of California Irvine)
When something goes wrong in organizations, someone is often blamed. Blame can have ambiguous effects as it may or may not be accurately placed. Yet despite the prevalence of blame, we know surprisingly little about how it shapes individual workplace behavior. Emerging research suggests that blame can prompt individuals to become more cautious or withdraw responsibility, but we know little about when blame produces behavioral change and who is most responsive to it. In this paper, we theorize that blame shifts practices by triggering reputational threats, such that individuals who faced greater threats are most likely to change their practices. This can happen even when the blame is without merit. We test our theory using medical malpractice claims against 1,809 physicians and a matched sample of physicians overseeing 3.5 million hospital inpatient discharges in Florida from 2010 to 2021. Leveraging quasi-random variations in the timing of malpractice claims and a difference-in-difference design, we find that neither the content nor the accuracy of the malpractice allegation meaningfully predicted whether physicians change their practices after being blamed. Instead, physicians who face stronger reputational threats—those newer to the profession, trained at less prestigious institutions, or working in the least-litigated specialties—were the ones most likely to change their practices in response to blame. Our work offers new insights to the literature on blame and learning.

Actor-Centred Unlearning: Active Actors, Memory Agents, and an Unlearning Process in Post-

OMT
Hitoshi Iwashita (Cardiff University)
An unlearning process, although tacitly recognized at the conceptual level, has not yet been empirically traced and modelled. The current literature often treats unlearning as a static attribute or one-off antecedent of learning and therefore does not explain how unlearning takes place in an organization; as a process filled with actors and agency. This paper, therefore, explores the process of how active actors continue to unlearn practices in reference to former negative experiences framed as failure. Using an exploratory longitudinal case study, examining multiple cross-border post-merger integrations (PMIs), we identify a group of active actors who create, utilize, and solidify their former negative experiences while facilitating organizational unlearning. We characterize those actors as collective memory agents, who gradually make unlearning organizational. In so doing, we provide the first empirically grounded process model of organizational unlearning. This actor-centred model reveals how collective memory agents repeatedly disregard prior negative experiences and transform themselves from emergent and empowered agents to authorized ones through three distinctive mechanisms: 1) Disrupting, 2) Decoupling, and 3) Insulating formerly institutionalized practices.

When Helping is Not Helpful: The Negative Impact of Coworkers’ Help on One’s Experiential Learning

OMT
Sunkee Lee (Carnegie Mellon University); Christopher Dishop
This study examines how the process through which individuals acquire experience shapes their learning trajectories. Building on the experiential learning literature, we argue that learning depends not only on the amount of experience accumulated, but also on how that experience is acquired. Specifically, we theorize that receiving solution-oriented help from peers—help that directly resolves major task challenges or provides ready-made solutions—can diminish the developmental value of experience by limiting independent problem solving, constraining tacit knowledge formation, and fostering mislearning. Using daily-level panel data on 86 sales employees at a flash-deal company over 2.5 years, we test this theory. As a baseline, we replicate the positive relationship between accumulated task experience and subsequent performance documented in prior experiential learning work. We then show that receiving greater amounts of solution-oriented help from peers undermines this relationship. Finally, we find that this negative moderating effect is amplified when individuals outperform their peers as a result of receiving such help. Overall, our findings demonstrate that the acquisition process of experience fundamentally conditions how experience translates into capability, offering new insights for research on experiential learning, the microfoundations of organizational capability and performance, and the consequences of helping behaviors within organizations.

Designed to Fail: Toward an Evolutionary Theory of Zemblanity in Organizational Routines

OMT
Marco Balzano (University of Trieste); Christian Busch (University of Southern California)
Despite extensive research on organizational failure, dominant explanations emphasize exogenous shocks, strategic missteps, or cultural drift, offering limited insight into how internally coherent routines evolve into liabilities. We ask: How do persistently functional organizational routines evolve into shadow liabilities? Using a longitudinal case of Boeing, we develop silent zemblanity, a process in which persistent routines maintain internal functionality while drifting out of alignment with their environment. Drawing on the theory of the artifact, the notion of functionality, and the normalization of deviance, we identify two paths: design-locked reinforcement of legacy assumptions and drift-induced persistence amid change. Both conceal growing misfit. We introduce shadow liabilities, hidden exposures emerging when silent zemblanity suppresses early warning signals, showing how purposeful routine persistence produces endogenous failure in stable systems.

The Strategy of “Near Losses”: Organizational Learning from Near Failures

OMT
Peter Micah Madsen (Brigham Young University)
Organizational learning theory suggests that failures can be extremely valuable learning experiences, particularly small failures. Indeed, organizational learning from small failures (dubbed the “strategy of small losses”) has been heralded as a uniquely valuable form of learning that provides both larger samples from which to learn and less resistance and blame-shifting than attempts to learn from large organizational failures. However, organizational learning theory has not yet considered whether the advantages of learning from small failures extend to a class of failures so small that they result in no meaningful loss—so-called near failures (also commonly referred to as near misses)—in which conditions that could have produced a failure are present, but no meaningful failure materializes due to random good luck. We theorize that the inherent ambiguity of near failures makes the identification and reporting of near failures a significant challenge that leads to a unique process of organizational learning from near failures in which near failure reporting quality is much more significant than near failure reporting quantity. We test our theory in the context of 230 hospital trusts within England’s National Health Service between 2011 and 2021, finding broad support for our theory and hypotheses.
