From DAGs to Epistemic Maps: Mapping Causal Assumptions in Empirical Strategy Research

RM
Kyeongki Park (University of Maryland College Park); Brent Goldfarb (University of Maryland College Park)
Strategy research relies heavily on observational data and flexible modeling choices to support causal claims. While such flexibility enables the study of complex strategic phenomena, it also creates epistemic opacity: readers rarely observe the causal assumptions and specification choices that underlie reported results. As a consequence, empirical findings that appear robust may support multiple, incompatible causal interpretations. This paper proposes an integrated causal-epistemic framework that combines directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and epistemic maps to address this challenge. DAGs are used to formalize causal assumptions and discipline identification by clarifying estimands and distinguishing among confounders, mediators, and colliders. Epistemic maps visualize how empirical estimates vary across model specifications that are consistent with a given causal structure. Together, these tools reframe robustness and replication as exercises in assumption mapping rather than binary tests of whether results replicate. The framework is illustrated through a structured replication of Hegde and Tumlinson (2021). By comparing alternative, theory-consistent causal foundations and overlaying their admissible estimates onto an unconstrained epistemic map, the analysis shows how causal conclusions depend critically on identifying assumptions and measurement choices. The paper contributes a generalizable workflow that enhances transparency and supports cumulative knowledge without overturning prior findings.

Measuring Path Dependence in Strategic Decisions: An Attention-Based Transformer Approach

RM
Ming Lyu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Feifei Jiang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Tingting Song (Antai College of Economics and Management, Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Path dependence—the extent to which historical choices shape current strategic decisions—remains conceptually central yet empirically difficult to measure. Existing proxies conflate history with genuine dependence and provide limited insight into which past periods matter. We train an attention-based Transformer model on longitudinal data of Chinese listed firms to predict strategic decisions and recover attention weights as a direct measure of path dependence. The measure exhibits strong predictive validity, reveals systematic cross-industry variation, and anticipates organizational distress. Our approach offers a novel empirical lens to quantify how organizational history shapes strategy, bridging an important gap between theory and measurement.

Why You Should Not Use the ITCV With Robust Standard Errors (And What to Do Instead)

RM
Sirio Lonati (NEOMA Business School); Jesper N. Wulff (Aarhus University)
The Impact Threshold of a Confounding Variable (ITCV) is typically interpreted as the minimum product of correlations—if exhibited by an omitted variable with both the independent and dependent variables—that would overturn a study’s conclusions. In this paper, we show that this interpretation breaks down in regression models with heteroskedasticity- or cluster-robust standard errors. We illustrate the problem both formally and through simulated examples, and document how it remains largely unrecognized in the field, leading to frequent misinterpretations in top journals. To address this issue, we recommend moving beyond the ITCV and adopting a bootstrap-based solution derived from Cinelli and Hazlett’s sensemakr framework, which we demonstrate by reproducing a published study. We provide Stata and R implementations through our bootmakr package to facilitate adoption of this improved approach.

Better LATE Than Never: A Guide to Instrumental Variables and the Local Average Treatment Effect

RM
Sirio Lonati (NEOMA Business School); Mikko Rönkkö (University of Jyväskylä)
Instrumental variable estimation is widely used in management research to address endogeneity. When the effect of an endogenous predictor varies across firms or individuals, however, instrumental variables generally do not recover the average effect in the population. Instead, they can identify the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE): the effect of the predictor for units whose behavior is shifted by the instrument. Although LATE interpretations are growingly common in organizational journals, the additional assumptions required to justify them are often left implicit, and the broader consequences of treatment effect heterogeneity are generally overlooked. In this paper, we review how effect heterogeneity affects the interpretation of instrumental variable estimates, clarify the conditions under which they identify LATE, and discuss the wider implications for management theory, empirics, and practice. We conclude with recommendations for interpreting and reporting instrumental variables analyses under effect heterogeneity, which we demonstrate by reproducing three published studies.

Anti-Takeover Statutes and Advisor Capabilities:Industry Expertise and Network Centrality in Target

RM
Gwangyeong Lim
How do institutional shocks reshape the value of capabilities in professional service markets? We examine this question in the context of target-side M&A financial advisory services following the staggered, state-level implementation of U.S. anti-takeover statutes (ATS). Conceptualizing advisor selection as a capability-matching problem embedded in institutional environments, we focus on two theoretically central capabilities: industry expertise, reflecting domain-specific human capital accumulated through repeated sector transactions, and network centrality, capturing coordination capacity through positions in advisor collaboration networks. Using an integrated SDC Platinum dataset of 7,726 deals announced between 1985 and 2010, we operationalize these capabilities from advisors' rolling five-year deal histories and estimate difference-in-differences models with target-incorporation-state and year fixed effects. Our results indicate that ATS adoption does not systematically change the probability of hiring a high-expertise advisor, but it is associated with a 6.7 percentage point higher share of high-centrality advisors within multi-advisor teams (ß = 0.067, SE = 0.036, p < 0.10). Complementary analyses reveal that co-hiring is strongly associated with high-centrality advisors (ß = 0.11, p < 0.01), consistent with a coordination mechanism whereby central network positions facilitate the assembly and management of advisory coalitions. We contribute to strategy research on dynamic capability valuation under institutional change by demonstrating how governance shocks selectively reweight demand toward network-based coordination capabilities rather than specialized expertise in transaction advisory teams.
