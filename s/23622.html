Session overview

This session brings together papers that investigate how race, bias, and collective identity shape organizational outcomes under contested and polarized conditions. The papers examine discriminatory cues in labor markets, racial backlash in public controversy, indigenous community resilience, and digital inclusion for migrant platform workers. Together, they highlight how inequality is reproduced and challenged across institutional, cultural, and technological settings.

Digital Inclusion Under Algorithmic Management: A Multi-Criteria Study of Migrant Platform Workers

DEI
Damla Köroglu Ünver (Istanbul University); Emrah Önder (Istanbul University)
This study conceptualizes and measures inclusion in algorithmically managed platform work from the perspective of migrant workers. Responding to growing concerns about algorithmic control, precarity, and fairness in the gig economy, it develops an empirically grounded framework that captures how migrants prioritize different dimensions of inclusion. Using a mixed-methods design, a focus group discussion with four migrant platform workers was conducted to identify core inclusion dimensions, which were then systematically evaluated by nineteen migrant workers through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). Findings reveal a clear hierarchical structure of inclusion, where Income and Job Security and Occupational Health & Safety constitute foundational needs, followed by Algorithmic Fairness, Communication and Voice, and Digital Accessibility. This hierarchy indicates that for migrant workers, procedural fairness and participation only gain significance once basic material and safety conditions are secured. The results introduce a “Maslowian logic” of digital labor inclusion, empirically linking distributive, protective, and procedural justice within platform-mediated work. The study advances theory by integrating insights from digital HRM, algorithmic management, and migration studies, demonstrating how socio- technical systems reproduce or mitigate precarity. Practically, it offers a prioritized roadmap for policymakers and platform designers to promote inclusive governance— grounded in economic stability, occupational safety, and transparent algorithmic procedures—as the ethical foundations of equitable digital labor.

When You Discriminate, I See Opportunity: Appearance of Bias as Corroboration for Racial Cues (WITHDRAWN)

DEI
Zhefei Li (Fudan University)
This paper explores how racial stereotypes, alongside the perceived presence or absence of prior discrimination in a candidate’s previous role, influence subsequent rehiring prospects. We argue that while immutable characteristics like race can unfairly shape perceptions of social status and worth, these negative racial biases can turn into positive cues when relevant information is taken into account. We examine this proposition in the head coach labor markets in the National Basketball Association (NBA). We discover that organizations reinterpret dismissal contexts through a racialized circumstantial lens: when a candidate's prior termination creates an appearance of possible racial biases based on statistical or taste-based discrimination, it may signal a labor market exploitation opportunity. Conversely, the absence of such biases in a candidate’s prior position exacerbates racial discrimination through stereotype confirmation. This study reframes racial discrimination as a strategic cue in human capital acquisition, revealing how employers may take advantage of labor market imperfections by exploiting the appearance of prior bias while penalizing Black candidates when such cues are absent. These findings explain the persistence of racial inequality by demonstrating how discrimination is selectively engaged as a calculated—rather than purely animus-driven— organizational practice.

Role of Collaborative Community Dynamics in Community Resilience: A Case of Indigenous Community

DEI
Monika Gupta (S.P. Jain Institute of Management and Research); Preeta George (S.P. Jain Institute of Management and Research); Ravi Roshan (S.P. Jain Institute of Management and Research)
The objective of this study is to understand the characteristics and dynamics of a collaborative community model in relation to community resilience, using the case of an indigenous community in India. The study is based on the principles of Relational Leadership Theory and uses primary research of the Koli fishing community residing in the coastal region of the Western Ghats, particularly in the Maharashtra region of India, employing a mixed-methods approach. Qualitative analysis was conducted using Gioia’s method, and partial least squares-structural equation modelling was used for quantitative analysis. Our initial analysis reveals that the characteristics of the collaborative community, such as shared purpose, an ethic of contribution, the process of coordination, and the infrastructure of collaboration, are helpful in driving community resilience, especially in the case of Indigenous communities. For this analysis, we have explored the role of the collaborative community framework in enhancing resiliency and recovery during the COVID-19 pandemic among an indigenous fishing community in India. We investigate whether community resilience is facilitated by self-governance and catalysed by the principles of relational dynamics expounded in the Relational Leadership Theory. Our results contribute to the collaborative community literature by shedding light on its characteristics and their implications for enhancing the community's resilience. The results will have broader implications for similar communities in the Global South and are expected to contribute to policy suggestions for ensuring survival and sustainability in terms of the socio-economic development of these communities, in a globally competitive environment.

The Architecture of Controversy: Response to Racial Backlash in Polarized Contexts

DEI
Humberto Reis dos Santos-Souza (Federal Institute of Education, Science and Technology of Rio de Janeiro); Pedro Jaime
This study challenges the prevailing view in the Woke Capitalism literature by investigating how Global South organizations legitimize racial diversity strategies amid extreme inequality and polarization. We analyze the public controversy surrounding Magazine Luiza’s Black-only trainee program in Brazil. Adopting the Pragmatic Sociology of Critique and the French School of Data Analysis, we map the moral topography of the dispute through a geometric analysis of 85 news reports. The data reveal that polarization is not structured by economic rationality. Instead, we identify an intra-civic rift: a hermeneutic battle between the formal equality of legal universalism (critics) and the substantive equity of historical reparation (defenders). We show that structural racism acts as a resistance metacontext, compelling the organization to construct a civic-industrial compromise. The study expands Economies of Worth theory by demonstrating that in post-colonial societies, legitimizing diversity requires subordinating market logic to political competence, where social justice must be translated as an efficiency imperative to survive public scrutiny.
