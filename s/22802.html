From Closing Gaps to Changing Norms: Career Narratives of Racially Diverse Women Executives

CAR
Poonam Zantye (Boston College); Chi Lan Nguyen (Oklahoma State University); Lex Nicole Smith Washington (Oklahoma State University); Jamie Jocelyn Ladge (Boston College)
With significant progress towards gender equality over the last few decades in the domain of work and leadership, women leaders have had the opportunity to reshape the evolving yet predominantly White male leader prototype. Although extant research has utilized multiple theoretical lenses to study women’s leadership journeys, it largely adopts static views of these women’s identities and tends to emphasize constraints rather than agentic processes. Drawing on the career narratives of 106 racially diverse women in senior leadership positions in the United States, we develop a theory of dynamic intersectionality that explains how women construct leadership identities over their careers while navigating their varying distances from the prevalent dominant White, masculine leadership prototype. We show that women’s leadership journeys unfold through three stages: an identity calibration stage during early to mid-career focused on reducing distance from the dominant leadership prototype, a transitional legitimacy-enabled identity integration stage, and a prototype disruption stage as they gain seniority within organizations. These stages are shaped by both individual career progression and a broader institutional context of optimism or ‘hope culture’. By theorizing intersectionality as dynamic, temporal, and contextually embedded, this study extends intersectionality theory, and research on leadership identity and hope cultures.

The FLAN Framework: Navigating Leadership Barriers for Ethnically Diverse Women in Australia

CAR
Huong Nguyen (La Trobe University); Suzanne Young (La Trobe University)
Ethnically Diverse Women in Leadership (EDWIL) remain under-represented in Anglo-Saxon-dominated corporate cultures and face entrenched cultural barriers. Despite this, little research from an intersectional perspective examines the strategies enabling their leadership navigation. This study analyses 21 semi-structured interviews with EDWIL to uncover the personal strategies EDWIL employ to navigate leadership barriers in Australia. Using intersectionality, agency-structure, and ecological career system theories, this paper draws on the data to develop a theoretical framework encompassing four leadership navigation strategies that are interlocking across individual, interpersonal, organisational and societal levels. These are – F: Forging one’s career and leveraging intersectionality; L: Leveraging networks, mentorship, and sponsorship; A: Adapting to Anglo-male corporate culture; and N: Navigating leadership stereotypes and biases. The FLAN framework extends existing theories and frameworks in the domains of intersectionality, ecological career systems, and institutional logics. The study also provides empirical insights for EDWIL, along with their mentors, managers, sponsors, and organizational career decision-makers, with potential to improve opportunities and outcomes in leadership progression for EDWIL participants.

Organizational Coaching Needs of Asian American Leaders:A Socio-Cultural and Multi-Level Perspective

CAR
Hongguo Wei (Robert Morris University); Pooja Khatija (Case Western Reserve University); Lili Bao (Eastern Washington University)
Asian American leaders remain underrepresented in top leadership positions despite their competence at work and professional achievement. Previous research has suggested that cultural values and associated stereotypes result in the bamboo ceiling for them and organizational coaching that is culturally informed is likely to support Asian American leaders conquer the challenges. However, our understanding of their cultural-related stereotypes is still fragmented, and we do not have an integrative framework to address their coaching needs. Drawing on a socio-cultural and multi-level perspective, we conducted a theory-driven thematic analysis to address this gap. With a systematic analysis and theory-guided coding of 64 studies about Asian American leadership, we developed a multi-level framework about how culture values (i.e., collectivism, respect for authority, emotional moderation, and work ethics and excellence) inform organizational-level behaviors and individual-level behaviors, which implicate paradoxical tensions that could both benefit and harm the career advancement of Asian American leaders. We then integrate these findings to fathom the coaching needs of Asian American leaders, thus providing culturally informed coaching practices that support Asian American leaders to break the barriers for better career advancement and leadership development.

Shaping a Career in STEM: Exploring the Career Work of Women Professionals in STEM With Mental Health

CAR
Dulini Fernando (Aston University); Elina Meliou (University of Southampton); Krystal Wilkinson (Manchester Metropolitan University); Gustavo Sanchez Munoz
How do highly skilled women working in STEM (science, technology, engineering and mathematics) research and innovation, with experience of mental health issues (MHIs) shape their careers? We draw on the social-symbolic theory framework’s construct of career-work to examine the accounts of thirty women working in STEM research and innovation in the UK who identify with experiencing MHIs. Our findings provide insights into how women reflect on the incompatibility between disembodied ideal worker expectations in STEM and their experience of MHIs as embodied subjects. We theorize how three strategies undertaken by women with the experience of MHIs work together to facilitate shaping a career in STEM, contributing to both maintaining and modifying disembodied ideal worker expectations.

Safety Without Status: The Threshold Effect of Identity Disclosure on Career Capital

CAR
Shovna C Tripathy (University of Houston-Downtown); Chris Zhang (University of Houston-Downtown); Xinyao Zhou (Ontario Tech University)
While identity management is often conceptualized as a spectrum from secrecy to transparency, the career implications of the “middle ground”, selective disclosure, remain under-theorized, particularly in non-Western contexts. Drawing on semi-structured interviews with 65 lesbian and gay (LG) professionals in China and guided by the Disclosure Process Model as a sensitizing framework, we examine whether intermediate disclosure generates career capital or primarily mitigates risk. Our analysis reveals a nonlinear threshold pattern: partial disclosure can provide safety but often carries a high cognitive tax that limits sponsorship, high-visibility assignments, and advancement; career gains are most evident when individuals cross a critical threshold of fuller disclosure. We identify four mechanisms that facilitate this shift from safety to status: Resource Liberation, Agency Activation, Attribute Signaling, and Relational Filtering within guanxi networks. Institutional logic further conditions these processes: the mechanisms operate most strongly in meritocratic foreign and private firms but are constrained in State-Owned Enterprises (SOE). Overall, the findings challenge a dominant risk-minimization narrative by advancing an asset-maximization perspective in which disclosure can function as a strategic signal of leadership potential under supportive institutional conditions. Keywords: Identity; disclosure; career
