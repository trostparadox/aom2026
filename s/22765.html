Crisis Event Characteristics as Boundary Conditions of Death Awareness and Social Entrepreneurship

SIM
Nan Guo (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Riki Takeuchi (The University of Texas at Dallas); Ryan Scott Teschner (Baruch College, CUNY); Haemin Dennis Park (The University of Texas at Dallas)
Crises have profound effects on societies, leading to high mortality rates that can motivate individuals to act in ways that contribute to crisis management and resilience. For instance, such as they could thinking about developing new products or services that benefit society or the environment, and contribute to crisis management and resilience. The role that death awareness, due to increased mortality rates, plays in establishing a social entrepreneurial venture during crisis, however, is unclear. The existing literature on death awareness implies a diverging impact of death awareness on social entrepreneurship intentions via death anxiety (negative effect) and death reflection (positive effect). In the current study, we employ an overarching theoretical framework— transactional theory of stress—to elucidate the relationship between death awareness and social entrepreneurial intention. We hypothesize that this relationship is mediated by death anxiety and death reflection, and these mediation effects vary based on the criticality and novelty of the crisis, respectively. We conducted two experiments and one time-lagged field study. Across three studies, we consistently find that the relationship between death awareness and social entrepreneurial intention is mediated by death reflection, and such mediation effect is moderated by novelty of crisis. We also find the a negative mediation effect of death anxiety for the relationship between death awareness and social entrepreneurial intention in Study 3 (the field study). This research contributes to the understanding of social entrepreneurship amid crises, as well as challenges existing literature of death awareness and provides new insights for future research.

Winning the ‘Right’ Way? Normative Perspectives on the Business-Government Interface

SIM
Catherine Erica Alexina McDonald (University of St. Gallen); Florian Wettstein (University of St. Gallen)
In light of recent mandatory human rights due diligence (mHRDD) legislation, many emerging economies face both an opportunity - for a competitive advantage should they meet the required human rights and environmental criteria - as well as a challenge - if they are unable to meet the requirements and are excluded from increasingly compliant global supply chains. This paper, therefore, makes three contributions to scholarship at the intersection of business and human rights (BHR) and international business (IB). First, we advance the literature on the governance of the global factory, by investigating the implications of mHRDD for emerging markets. Second, drawing from the institutional systems literature, we develop a conceptual framework to shed light on three additional factors pertaining to mHRDD implementation that have been largely overlooked, namely, 1) a minimum living wage, 2) a National Action Plan on BHR, and 3) a ‘smart-mix’ of adequate labour protections. Finally, drawing from the VIS framework proposed by Fainshmidt and colleagues (2018), we develop illustrative examples and propositions, before suggesting future research directions. Building upon extant IB and BHR literature, we argue for more normative scholarship on regulatory isomorphic pressures and propose that mHRDD may provide an opportunity to bridge the ‘great divide’ between corporate social responsibility (CSR) and BHR.

A Tale of Two Warnings: Decoding Misconduct Risk from Accruals and Audit Opinions

SIM
Xin Jin (FGV EBAPE); Ricardo Lopes Cardoso (Fundação Getulio Vargas)
This study develops a dynamic temporal framework integrating internal reporting practices (Discretionary Accruals, DA) and external monitoring (Modified Audit Opinions, MAO) to predict corporate misconduct from t+1 to t+4 using Chinese A-share listed firms (2003–2024). We find that both signals predict misconduct in the short term, while DA’s effect fades quickly and MAO’s effect weakens and reverses over longer horizons. Mechanism tests show DA influences outcomes by improving analyst forecast accuracy, while MAO reflects external monitoring and financial constraints. Importantly, we find a substitution effect, whereby MAO reduces the incremental predictive power of DA, suggesting overlapping information content.
