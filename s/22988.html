The Geopolitics of Code: Decoupling and Substitution in the Digital Ecosystem

IM
Jiachen Liu (Tsinghua University); qinglingzi li (Tsinghua University); Zhengyao Kang (University of Bristol)
Does geopolitical friction fragment the “borderless” digital economy? We exploit the 2019 U.S. sanctions against Huawei as an exogenous shock to identify the causal impact of geopolitical tension on global open-source collaboration. Using granular data from GitHub, we document a structurally profound “digital decoupling” phenomenon. We find that the shock triggered a sharp retraction of contributions from BRICS developers (the chilling effect), which was largely offset by a compensatory influx of G7 developers (the substitution effect). Consequently, while the aggregate volume of innovation remained stable, the collaborative network became significantly polarized. Heterogeneity analyses reveal that this resilience is highly stratified: the substitution effect happens in high-visibility projects, whereas peripheral projects suffered a net decline in output. Furthermore, organizations demonstrated superior institutional capacity to manage this transition compared to individual developers. Our findings suggest that geopolitical shocks do not simply destroy digital cooperation but reconfigure it, exacerbating inequality and driving the decoupling of the global collaboration in the digital ecosystems.

Casting Long Shadows: The Cross-Domain Organizational Impact of State Intervention

IM
Lewei Huang (The University of Hong Kong); Zijie Song (South China Normal University); Qianhui Huan (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
This paper reveals an unintended organizational consequence of state intervention. We propose that the impact of state intervention is not merely contained within its designated policy domain; rather, it can be interpreted by external observers as credible signals of the state’s willingness to subordinate market-based principles to political objectives, which threatens organizations predicated on market integrity, such as foreign independent venture capital (IVC) firms. Testing this perspective across 85 countries using foreign exchange intervention (FXI) as a pervasive form of state intervention, we find strong FXI signals deter foreign IVC entry. The signaling perspective is further supported by the deterrent effect’s high contingency: it is attenuated when high central bank independence provides a technocratic buffer, and when investors exhibit higher tolerance of state intervention. Our study is among the first to theorize and find the hidden, cross-domain costs of state intervention for organizations, showing how organizations’ interpretation of these subtle signals reshapes their global market entry decisions.

The Limits to Institutional Arbitrage: Resource Fungibility, Agglomeration and Regional Collocation

IM
Heather Berry (Georgetown University); Aseem Kaul (University of Minnesota)
Scholars of global strategy have long emphasized the potential for multinational corporations (MNCs) to engage in institutional arbitrage, relocating their activities to take advantage of changing institutional conditions. Yet multinational investments are subject to path dependence and favorable institutions may create agglomeration benefits that are difficult to replicate elsewhere, which may make it difficult for MNCs to relocate their foreign activities, even after policy changes reduce arbitrage benefits. We test and find support for this argument using a difference in differences analysis of a policy change (the US Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017) that reduced the tax benefits from operating in foreign countries. We find that although US MNCs did respond and reverse some of their foreign investment for their more fungible assets in response to the policy change, this reversal was far less than anticipated. Consistent with our theory, we find that US MNCs did not relocate their less fungible assets, especially in countries with other benefits beyond the favorable regulation and where the MNC had other investments collocated in the nearby region. Our study thus highlights the limits to institutional arbitrage strategies while also emphasizing the policy implications of persistence in institutional arbitrage investments by MNCs.
