How Algorithmic Reductionism Triggers Resistance: Role of Anger and Belief in Free Will

OB
Tian Cao (Renmin University of China); Xin Wei (Renmin University of China)
With the rapid advancement of technology, algorithmic management has become a dominant mode for managing workers in the gig economy. However, algorithmic reductionism—the tendency of algorithms to rely on limited descriptions and rigid, oversimplified concepts—may provoke resistance among workers. Drawing on cognitive–motivational–relational theory, this study investigates the antecedents, mechanisms, and boundary conditions of workers’ resistance to algorithmic management. Using a scenario experiment (N = 201) and a three-wave field survey of food delivery riders (N = 360), we find that algorithmic reductionism is positively associated with worker anger, which in turn triggers resistance behaviors. Furthermore, the relationship between algorithmic reductionism and anger, as well as the indirect effect on resistance, is moderated by workers’ belief in free will. Specifically, these effects are attenuated when individuals hold a higher belief in free will. This research illuminates how algorithmic reductionism shapes workers’ emotional and behavioral responses and provides practical insights for designing more human-centric algorithmic management systems.

Self-Promotion On Professional Online Social Networks: Norms And Expectancy Violations.

OB
Isabel Villamor (IESE Business School); Margaret Ormiston (The George Washington University)
Self-promotion can enhance perceptions of competence yet also invite reputational costs if seen as arrogant. We examine how professional online social networks (OSNs), such as LinkedIn, reshape norms governing self-promotion and, in turn, evaluations of those who engage in it. Drawing on expectancy violation theory (EVT), we argue that OSNs alter what counts as appropriate self-promotion by making peer behavior highly visible and algorithmically reinforced. We introduce the construct of norm calibration, defined as the strategic adjustment of self-promotion to align with collectively inferred appropriateness norms, to explain how individuals anticipate and adapt to digital standards of professionalism. Across three preregistered experiments and one archival study of 685 MBA students’ LinkedIn use, we show that OSNs foster more permissive self-promotion norms than face-to-face settings, that platform affordances explain this difference, and that exceeding these norms produces reputational penalties. Theoretically, we extend impression-management research by specifying norm calibration as a distinct process through which visible peer practices and algorithmic feedback create convergence and sanction deviation. We advance research on digital work by showing how affordances organize, not merely enable, professional self-presentation. Finally, we broaden EVT beyond reactive judgments of norm violations to encompass anticipatory calibration in algorithmically mediated contexts. Together, these insights explain how digital infrastructures institutionalize self-promotion, delineating both its opportunities and its reputational limits as careers increasingly unfold online.

Algorithmic Monitoring and Digital Delivery Workers’ Resistance to Algorithmic Control

OB
Qi Xu (Dalian University of Technology); Ting Liu (Kyoto University); Zhenduo Zhang (Dalian University of Technology); Gang Qu (Dalian University of Technology)
Algorithmic monitoring has become a central feature of gig platforms, yet its effects on worker autonomy and resistance remain contested. Drawing on the avoidance–approach framework, this study examines how two forms of algorithmic monitoring, observational and interactional, shape delivery workers’ resistance to algorithmic control through situational prevention and promotion regulatory focus. Based on a three-wave lagged questionnaire survey from 280 digital delivery workers and a laboratory experiment (N = 483), we find that observational monitoring activates situational prevention focus and increases resistance, whereas interactional monitoring activates situational promotion focus and reduces resistance. Algorithmic fairness weakens the association between observational monitoring and situational prevention focus and strengthens the association between interactional monitoring and situational promotion focus. By demonstrating that algorithmic monitoring comprises two structurally and psychologically distinct forms rather than a single, uniformly coercive practice, this study refines the construct of algorithmic monitoring and underscores the importance of motivational mechanisms and fairness perceptions. The findings contribute to ongoing debates on ethical algorithm design and offer practical guidance for developing gig platforms that support both operational efficiency and worker dignity.
