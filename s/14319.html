The Asymmetric Lobbying of Incumbent and Entrant MNEs in the Era of Geopolitical Rivalry

Authors: Caroline Tianyuan Sun Ivey Business School; Guy Holburn University of Western Ontario

Session overview

Industrial transition has become an inexorable trend in many sectors. To increase competitiveness in emerging sectors, many governments are deploying extensive fiscal and policy support, which makes Corporate Political Activity (CPA) a more strategic imperative for Multinational Enterprises (MNEs) to influence policy and subsidy allocations. Prior literature has studied extensively the lobbying strategies in general, yet it lacks a theoretical understanding of how firms devise CPA strategies during a paradigm shift. Traditional views suggest that competing firms "fight" over the same policies or legislators. However, this study aims to reveal a more sophisticated reality for competing groups during industrial transition, asymmetric lobbying: competing groups adopt divergent strategies targeting different people during industrial transitions. This study utilizes the Canadian automotive sector as a context to develop a generalizable framework for lobbying strategies of both incumbent and entrant firms in transitioning sectors. Specifically, firms with higher legacy dependence (incumbents), constrained by legitimacy concerns and immobile assets, tend to lobby to delay or soften regulations while prioritizing elected officials to leverage their local employment weight. In contrast, new entrants with low legacy dependence focus on technical engagement with bureaucrats to secure compliance standards open to innovation. This study will also uncover two moderators amplifying the incumbents’ strategic intensity: geopolitical rivalry spillover and politician rivalry. Empirically, I will employ a two-way fixed-effects (firm and month-year FE) panel regression design using the data over a 16-year period from a unique analytics platform of the Canadian federal registry of lobbyists, LobbyIQ.
