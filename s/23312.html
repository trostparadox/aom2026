Janus-Faced AI Disclosure: How Conference Call Enthusiasm Masks Workforce Setbacks (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Joao Cotter Salvado (Católica Lisbon School of Business & Economics); Diego Ravenda (TBS Education)
Using 71,433 quarterly earnings call transcripts from 4,327 U.S.-listed firms (2019–2024), we quantify the intensity and tone of AI-related and employee-centred disclosures with BERTopic and FinBERT, and link them to Refinitiv Social-pillar ESG scores. Panel regressions with firm and industry-year fixed effects, an instrumental variables strategy based on leave-one-out peer disclosure, and staggered difference-in-differences around AI-event calls show that greater intensity and positivity of AI-related disclosure predict lower subsequent workforce and overall social ESG performance. At the same time, firms that trumpet AI most loudly inject a surge of pro-employee rhetoric into those calls, consistent with symbolic “AI-washing.” The findings reconcile legitimacy and stakeholder theories by showing how managers balance innovation signalling with reputational cushioning, and they refine the text-as-data paradigm by demonstrating that sentiment-weighted topics from live earnings calls are leading, yet Janus-faced, indicators of social outcomes. Regulators, investors, and rating agencies should therefore triangulate narrative AI enthusiasm with hard labour metrics before inferring ethical AI commitment.

Multiple Stakeholders, One Purpose: Value Creation by Protecting Biodiversity in the Amazon Forest

STR
Elis Regina Monte Feitosa (University of São Paulo); M Sylvia Macchione Saes; Leandro S. Pongeluppe (University of Pennsylvania)
How can collaboration among diverse stakeholders transform sustainability challenges into value-creation opportunities? The Amazon, the world's largest tropical forest, plays a crucial role in regulating the climate, preserving biodiversity, and supporting local livelihoods. Yet deforestation and inequality persist, partly because conventional economic logics treat sustainability and value creation as trade-offs. This study examines how polycentric governance systems, particularly those that foster deep collaboration among local communities, firms, and public institutions, can align ecological protection with economic development. Drawing on the management of pirarucu (Arapaima gigas), a high-value Amazonian fish at risk of extinction, we compare two grassroots governance systems in the Médio Juruá and Mamirauá regions. The results show that biodiversity preservation and value creation can be mutually reinforcing when coordination mechanisms distribute benefits fairly and leverage local ecological knowledge. For managers, these findings illustrate how engaging in Amazon-based bioeconomy initiatives can generate differentiated, high-value products. For policymakers, the results highlight how supporting such collaborative governance can create new territorial industries rooted in the Amazon's unique locational asset specificity, offering a sustainable alternative to low-differentiation industrial subsidies currently adopted.

Learning from the Margins: How Innovation for Marginal Stakeholders Benefits Firms’ Future R&D

STR
Xiangting Wu (University of Minnesota)
Marginal stakeholders typically represent limited returns for organizations. Private firms often lack incentives to serve marginal stakeholders, especially when doing so entails substantial fixed costs. Prior research has explored how regulatory interventions change the cost-return structure of serving marginal stakeholders and how main stakeholders perceive such activities positively, generating additional returns. This study highlights the knowledge transfer as an underexplored incentive. I argue that when marginal stakeholders remain understudied by public and private communities, innovating for marginal stakeholders can yield greater learning benefits for future innovation targeting main stakeholders than innovating for main stakeholders directly. Focusing on the development of 3,332 drugs by 246 publicly traded U.S. biopharmaceutical firms, I found that a firm’s experience in rare-disease drug development has a greater positive association with its future mainstream R&D success rates than experience in mainstream drug development in the same indication area. To strengthen causal identification, I exploit the European Orphan Regulation as an exogenous policy shock and construct an instrumental variable. I propose two mechanisms underlying this differential learning effect. First, serving marginal stakeholders compels firms to search in technological domains where cross-domain relatedness remains underexplored, leading to further mainstream discovery. Second, it can deepen their understanding of problem-context characteristics of mainstream innovation, enabling them to recognize overlooked pathways to better outcomes. Using a well-categorized keyword system and publications to track the flow of these distinct types of knowledge, I found statistical patterns that closely align with both theoretical mechanisms.

Framing the Hustle: Experimental Evidence from Motivating Recycling in Lagos, Nigeria

STR
Wesley Wu-Yi Koo (Johns Hopkins University); Diana Jue-Rajasingh (Rice University); Oluwagbemileke Jegede (Harvard Business School)
Social ventures in the Global South often rely on voluntary participation to address societal and environmental challenges, yet participation decisions are interdependent, costly, and uncertain. When individuals doubt whether collective benefits will materialize or perceive that personal costs outweigh collective benefits, rational nonparticipation can prevail. We examine how participation frames shape participation by influencing beliefs about the viability of joint action and the costs of contributing. We conduct a 2x2 field experiment with a recycling social venture in Lagos, Nigeria, that varies participation frames along two dimensions: organizing logic (community versus platform) and role articulation (supplemental earner versus entrepreneur). We find that community frames increase enrollment and deposits on average, but the effectiveness of role-based frames depends on the perceived credibility of the organizing logic. When collective action appears viable (community frame), the low-risk supplemental earner frame outperforms the entrepreneur frame. When viability is uncertain (platform frame), the entrepreneur frame is more effective. Our findings contribute to research on social ventures and joint value creation by showing that participation frames, as a complement to financial incentives, can mobilize participation under interdependence.
