From Strategies to Organizing Principles: A Culturally Integrative Review of Upward Influence

OB
Ching-Fang Wei (Yuan Ze University); Huang Min-Ping (Yuan Ze University)
Upward influence has long been examined as a set of tactics through which subordinates attempt to shape supervisors’ decisions. Although prior research has generated rich insights by adopting Western indigenous, East Asian indigenous, and cross-cultural approaches, these streams have largely developed in parallel. As a result, existing findings remain fragmented, and meaningful theoretical dialogue across cultural perspectives has been limited. More importantly, conceptualizing upward influence primarily as a collection of discrete strategies risks obscuring the deeper cultural–psychological logic that organizes such behaviors. In this review, we adopt a culturally integrative perspective and reconceptualize upward influence from a repertoire of strategies to a set of organizing principles grounded in cultural meaning systems. Through a systematic synthesis of prior research across different cultural traditions, we identify 28 representative upward influence behaviors and propose a two-dimensional classification framework defined by the integration–autonomy orientation and the particularism–universalism orientation. This framework is intended to capture how cultural assumptions structure the ways in which upward influence behaviors are enacted and interpreted. Using a Chinese sample as a theoretically generative starting point, we employ multidimensional scaling (MDS) to examine the plausibility of the proposed organizing framework. Rather than positioning the Chinese context as a cultural boundary condition, we illustrate how this classification logic facilitates the integration of diverse research traditions. We conclude by outlining directions for future cross-cultural exploratory research, arguing that the proposed framework offers a culturally portable analytical lens for advancing theory on upward influence beyond isolated cultural comparisons.

The Dark Side of Leader Power Distance: A Multilevel Examination of LMX and CWB

OB
Farid Jahantab (Sam Houston State University)
In this study, I integrate social identity theory with relative deprivation theory to suggest that employee perception of leader power distance has implications for the quality of the leader-follower relationship (i.e., LMX), which in turn affects employee counterproductive work behavior (CWB). I further theorize that workgroup collectivism moderates the relationship between employee perception of leader power distance, LMX, and CWB. Multi-level modeling using time-lagged multi-source data from 554 employees and 114 supervisors nested in 114 workgroups demonstrated a negative relationship between employee perception of leader power distance and LMX, with LMX transmitting the indirect effect of employee perception of leader power distance on CWB. Moderation analyses further revealed that workgroup collectivism strengthens the relationship between employee perception of leader power distance and LMX. Finally, results of Monte Carlo simulations indicated that the indirect effect of employee perception of leader power distance on CWB via LMX was stronger under the condition of lower workgroup collectivism. Supported by the study results, I assert that power and LMX dynamics should be examined within the broader context of employee workgroup membership.

Multicultural Experience as a Collective Resource for Learning in Culturally Diverse Teams

OB
Longjiao Li (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Julija Mell (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University); Sujin Jang (INSEAD); Andre Calmon (Georgia Institute of Technology)
Multicultural experience—exposure to or interaction with cultures different from one's own—has been widely recognized in extant research as a valuable resource for individual outcomes. We extend this view by examining whether and when multicultural experience might benefit others, specifically in the context of individual learning in culturally diverse teams. Drawing on Experiential Learning Theory, we propose that team members with prior multicultural experience enhance their peers' learning in culturally diverse teams by scaffolding the learning environment. We further posit that these peer effects are strongest for individuals who lack multicultural experience themselves. Across two field studies of individuals working in culturally diverse teams, including an innovation hackathon (Study 1, N = 687) and a business school course project (Study 2, N = 318), we find support for the peer effect of teammates' multicultural experience on individual learning, although the pattern varied across outcome measures. We also find evidence that this effect is particularly pronounced for individuals without prior multicultural experience, consistent with a scaffolding mechanism. These findings shift the focus from multicultural experience as an individual advantage to its role as a collective team resource that structures the learning environment for all team members.
