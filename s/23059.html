Do Supranational Enforcement Institutions Produce Asymmetric Outcomes?

IM
Yamlaksira Getachew (Babson College); Yongsun Paik (Loyola Marymount University); Roger Fon
Despite their considerable influence on international business strategy and outcomes, supranational enforcement institutions (such as the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID)) have received limited attention in international business research. This study seeks to address this gap by examining whether and how MNEs’ origin and destination contexts influence the probability of prevailing in investment disputes and the magnitude of monetary awards. Using a sample of 546 ICSID cases for the period 1972-2021, we find that MNEs from developing economies are less likely to prevail and, when they do, receive smaller awards than firms from developed economies. By contrast, operating in a developing host does not increase the likelihood of prevailing; however, firms that do prevail in such hosts receive larger compensation. We also find that cross-border interactions exacerbate these disparities: the compensation disadvantage for MNEs from developing countries is most pronounced when they file claims against developed-country hosts. These results demonstrate that arbitration outcomes are shaped by differences in MNEs’ origin and destination contexts, rather than legal merits alone, offering novel theoretical insight and practical guidance for policymakers and firms seeking to address structural inequities in international dispute resolution.

A Magna Carta for Space

IM
Arpit Raswant (The University of Newcastle); Sarianna Lundan (Aalto University); Bo Bernhard Nielsen (The University of Sydney); Peter J. Buckley (The University of Manchester)
The commercialization of outer Space has created a business environment in which multinational enterprises deploy, operate, insure, and finance assets under fragmented and evolving regulatory regimes. Governments face the challenge of encouraging private investment while safeguarding competition, safety, sustainability, and equitable access to shared resources. An effective international business policy for Space should prioritize procedural constraints on regulatory discretion over fixed allocations of rights. A prospective Magna Carta for Space would establish a treaty-based, non-territorial framework emphasizing transparency, due process, proportional sanctions, and limits on enclosure, enabling investment amid persistent regulatory fragmentation.

Diplomacy-Enabled Market Entry into Security-Sensitive Sectors

IM
Jessi Collier (University of Jyväskylä); Saara Matala; Christian Stutz (University of Jyväskylä)
Our study examines how multinational enterprises (MNEs) from small states mobilize diplomatic networks to enter security-sensitive sectors in powerful host countries. Building on the network theory of diplomacy-supported foreign market entry, we argue that small-state MNEs face distinct opportunities: while their home-country governments have limited diplomatic leverage, these MNEs may benefit from being perceived as less threatening partners in security-sensitive sectors. We investigate this argument through a historical case study of Wärtsilä, which attempted to enter the U.S. icebreaker market between the 1960s and 1980s. Drawing on extensive archival records from Finland and the United States, our analysis identifies three phases of diplomatic interaction: (1) the use of home-country diplomatic connections with limited host-country influence, (2) a geopolitical opening that temporarily elevated the small-state’s diplomatic importance, and (3) eventual host-country opposition that blocked entry. Our findings extend IB theory by highlighting the role of asymmetric power relations, security-sensitive market sectors, and host-country opposition in shaping diplomacy-supported foreign market entry.

When Guns Fire, Markets Cry - International Businesses’ Responses to War: A Temporal Lit. Review

IM
Konrad Albert Graf (Vienna University of Economics and Business); Evelyn Reithofer
Amid a rise in cross-national armed conflicts around the world, from Russia’s invasion of Ukraine to the Israel-Gaza war, this article synthesises what is currently known about their implications for international businesses. We conducted a structured literature review, screening 1,699 articles from 75 top-tier business journals, and ultimately analysing 78 peer-reviewed articles published between 1975 and summer 2025. Thematic coding and temporal mapping revealed six converging research streams: (1) international finance and investment; (2) global supply chains and trade disruptions; (3) corporate strategy, risk and governance; (4) CSR, legitimacy and activism; (5) SMEs and entrepreneurship in conflict; (6) policy and historical perspectives. Across these streams, business scholarship has progressively moved from state-centric, macro-institutional explanations towards fine-grained, firm-level and behavioural accounts that frame war as both a destabilising shock and a catalyst for strategic renewal. By integrating these fragmented insights, this review offers a comprehensive agenda linking geopolitical volatility with international business, finance, operations and social responsibility, and identifies paths for real-time, cross-level research on business resilience within persistent cross-national armed conflicts.
