Imprints of Leadership Incubation Phase: First-Time CEOs and Firms’ Strategic Conformity

STR
Krati Jain (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Manish Popli (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Anish Purkayastha (The University of Sydney)
This study extends imprinting theory beyond its traditional emphasis on CEOs’ early career experiences by examining how formative events in the leadership incubation phase shape first-time CEOs’ strategic actions. We theorize that cognitive imprints formed during prior top management team roles influence CEOs’ behaviours in their initial tenure. We find that first-time CEOs exposed to performance-related supervisor dismissals events during their formative years prioritize job security and adopt strategically conforming behaviours early in their tenure. However, this effect attenuates as the gap from the imprinting event and CEO appointment increases. Moreover, we show that these imprinting effects interact with the firm’s task environment, leading first-time CEOs to recalibrate actions under conditions of high task-environmental uncertainty. These findings advance imprinting research by shifting attention from early career imprints to those stamped during leadership incubation phase and demonstrating how such imprints evolve and shape firm-level outcomes.

When a Rebel Breaks the Mold: How CEO Nonconformity and TMT Regulatory Focus Affect CVC Investments

STR
Tahmineh Borhani (University of Idaho); Oleksandr (Alex) Tsaruk (University of Idaho); Abhishek Srivastava (Ball State University)
This study examines how CEO nonconformity drives corporate venture capital (CVC) investment by integrating cognitive frames, social identity, and regulatory focus theories. CVC investments are recognized for their capacity to expand organizational boundaries through knowledge absorption and market exploration. Yet, research has largely overlooked executive-level antecedents. We theorize that CEOs who deviate from conventional norms possess a nonconforming cognitive frame composed of distinctiveness and rebelliousness lenses. This cognitive frame enables them to identify distant but valuable opportunities while motivating innovative actions to affirm their unique social identity among corporate leaders. However, while recognizing novel opportunities is essential, the motivation to act upon these insights depends on regulatory focus. A top management team (TMT) with a high promotion focus is oriented toward gain-seeking behaviors and views uncertainty as strategic opportunity. In such contexts, the relationship between CEO nonconformity and CVC investments is strengthened. Conversely, when the TMT is prevention-focused—prioritizing security over gains—the same uncertainty may be perceived as risk, thereby attenuating the expression of nonconforming behavior in venture decisions. This study contributes to the upper echelon and corporate venturing literatures by highlighting how executive cognitive frames and identity motivations influence strategic decisions. Keywords: CEO nonconformity; cognitive frame; social identity; regulatory focus; corporate venture capital

Strategic Leader Attributes and Firm Performance: A Multilevel Framework

STR
Yang Liu (The University of Texas at San Antonio)
Research on how strategic leader attributes influence firm performance through their strategic choices has been fruitful. However, the successful implementation of a strategy encounters organizational inertia and requires strategically aligned human assets and organizational practices. The leadership roles that strategic leaders play, as well as the organizational practices on which they make decisions, are largely neglected. Our collective understanding of how strategic leaders influence their followers at the micro level and other organizational practices, beyond strategy, at the macro level to achieve organizational goals, is limited. Yet, this is an important area to study because it reveals the underlying cross-level mechanisms that explain the effects of strategic leaders on firm performance. As scholars have suggested, a multilevel theory is required to understand the effects of strategic leaders. Emerging studies indicate that strategic leader attributes influence firm employee job outcomes and other key organizational practices; however, these studies remain highly fragmented, hindering a deeper understanding of the effects of strategic leaders on firm outcomes. Therefore, we review and integrate the disparate streams of literature on the multilevel effects of strategic leaders. By doing so, we advance a multilevel framework that explains the underlying mechanisms linking strategic leader attributes to firm performance and charts future research directions.

Dancing with CEO Personality in Resource-Constrained Contexts: The Role of TMT Potency

STR
Asghar Afshar Jahanshahi (IPADE Business School); Ozan Kalaycioglu (Karadeniz Technical University); Shasanka Chalise
When organizations operate under severe resource constraints, does it matter whether CEOs are primarily self-oriented and instrumentally focused on their engagement with others, or whether they are guided by broader moral commitments that orient leadership toward collective rather than purely individual interests and foster a shared belief in the team’s ability to act effectively? Building on Upper Echelons Theory, this paper examines how CEOs with “dark side” traits-Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and narcissism-and “bright side” traits-faith in humanity, humanism, and Kantianism-affect entrepreneurial bricolage, the ability to creatively “make do” with limited resources, through the mediating role of top management team (TMT) potency. This question is especially salient in unstable contexts such as Iran and Turkey, where international sanctions, extreme inflation, and resource scarcity make adaptive responses critical. In Study 1, using a multi-wave survey data from 112 CEOs and 422 TMT members in Iranian firms, we found that psychopathic CEOs undermine bricolage both directly and indirectly by weakening TMT potency, whereas Machiavellian and narcissistic CEOs impair bricolage entirely through reduced potency. In Study 2, drawing on data from 130 CEOs and 390 TMT members in Turkish firms, we observe the opposite pattern. Firms led by CEOs who exhibit stronger moral orientations such as faith in humanity, humanism, and Kantianism, are better able to adapt creatively to resource constraints. These leaders foster stronger collective confidence and cooperation within their top teams, which, in turn, enables more effective use of limited resources. Together, these findings show that CEO personality is not merely an individual attribute but an ethically consequential force that shapes how top management teams’ function and how organizations respond to scarcity. By highlighting the role of collective confidence at the top of the firm, this study extends Upper Echelons Theory and underscores the ethical importance of executive dispositions in shaping organizational adaptability under constraint.

Reconceptualizing Strategic Leadership Beyond Top Management: A Cross-Level Capability Perspective

STR
Immanuel Lutzeyer (Ruhr University Bochum)
Strategic leadership research remains anchored in upper echelons as the primary locus of strategic leadership, despite a growing body of adjacent research suggesting that adaptive strategic responses increasingly emerge through bottom-up processes extending well beyond top management. This conceptual article addresses this tension by advancing a cross-level perspective and conceptualizing strategic leadership as an adaptation-oriented capability cluster comprising four interrelated leadership capabilities (i.e., visioning, evaluating, initiating, aligning) that can be enacted by strategic leaders across hierarchical levels. Drawing on the dynamic capability view, cross-level strategic leadership is positioned as a microfoundation that enables the development of higher-order organizational capabilities (i.e., sensing, seizing, transforming) thereby supporting sustained organizational adaptability in dynamic environments. The proposed framework specifies three testable propositions and provides illustrative practice-based examples. By reconceptualizing strategic leadership as a cross-level, capability-based phenomenon, this study challenges the dominant top-centric assumption in strategic leadership research and contributes to strategy scholarship by providing a behaviorally grounded account of how distributed leadership capabilities underpin dynamic capabilities and organizational adaptability.
