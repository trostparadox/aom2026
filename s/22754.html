Innovation Under Exclusion: How Passion for Work and Job Embeddedness Buffer the Effects of Workplace

OB
Muhammad Rafiq (UCSI University); Duan Xiuqing (UCSI University)
Workplace exclusion poses a growing challenge to organizational innovation and employee well-being. This study investigates how workplace ostracism (WOS) affects employee innovative behaviour (EIB), while highlighting the protective roles of passion for work (PFW) and job embeddedness (JEM). Drawing on the conservation of resources (COR) theory, we conceptualize PFW as a critical emotional resource that mediates the negative effects of ostracism, and JEM as a structural buffer that moderates these dynamics. Using data from 377 employees in Malaysia’s manufacturing sector and employing SPSS PROCESS models 4 and 7, we find that WOS significantly reduces EIB. However, this effect is attenuated when employees possess high PFW, and further moderated by JEM, which helps preserve PFW even under exclusionary conditions. Our findings offer theoretical insights into the psychosocial mechanisms underpinning innovation and extend the COR framework by integrating both emotional and structural job resources. From a practical standpoint, this study underscores the need for inclusive organizational cultures that foster emotional engagement and strengthen employee ties to their roles. By cultivating passion and embeddedness, organizations can counteract the detrimental effects of social exclusion and sustain innovation even in challenging work environments. This research contributes to broader discussions on social inclusion, resilience, and creativity in contemporary organizational life.

A Study of the Impact of Workplace Relational Exclusion on Employees Radical Innovation Behavior

OB
Chien-Quinn Franklin WANG (Nanjing University); Shuming ZHAO (Nanjing University)
Radical innovation is a highly effective way for companies to achieve technological excellence and gain a competitive edge. However, existing studies have not paid much attention to employee behavior in radical innovation, especially the mechanism of workplace exclusion on employees' radical innovation behavior has not been properly explored. Therefore, this paper constructs a moderated mediation model based on social contagion theory and then makes theoretical assumptions about the transmission mechanism. Then, the study made an in-depth test of its transmission mechanism through three research methods. The results of the empirical tests show that workplace exclusion has a negative impact on radical innovation behavior, and workplace deviance partially mediates the relationship between workplace exclusion and radical innovation behavior. Team interdependence not only moderates the relationship between workplace deviance and radical innovation behavior but also further moderates the mediating effect of workplace deviance in the relationship between workplace exclusion and radical innovation behavior. This study provides insights into radical innovation behavior from a new perspective of innovation capability and effectively guides radical innovation management practices.

How Colleagues’ Task Types Using AI Agents Affect Employee Resistance to AI Agents and Ostracism

OB
Shuhui Wu (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Mi Zhou (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
Research on the use of AI agents has primarily focused on their positive or negative impacts on users. However, discussion on when and how the use of AI agents leads to bystander behavioral reactions remains limited. Based on the cognitive appraisal theory of stress, we explore employees’ differential exclusionary reactions to colleagues’ use of AI agents for completing routine tasks (information search tasks) and nonroutine tasks (innovation exploration tasks), as well as the mediating role of cognitive appraisals and the boundary role of competitive climate. Our findings show that colleagues using AI agents to complete information search or innovation exploration tasks trigger employees’ challenge appraisals, thereby reducing workplace ostracism. Conversely, colleagues’ use of AI agents to complete the two tasks trigger employees’ threat appraisals, intensifying employees’ resistance to AI agents and workplace ostracism. Additionally, competitive climate strengthens the positive relationship between colleagues’ use of AI agents to complete the two tasks and employees’ threat appraisals. This study expands the types of tasks that AI agents assist individuals with. It also deepens understanding of how AI agents use alleviates and exacerbates workplace “cold violence”, providing managerial implications for organizations to prevent employee resistance to AI agents and workplace ostracism.

Exploring the Difficult Terrain of Ambient Social Sexual Behavior at the Workplace

OB
Sabine Hommelhoff (LMU Munich School of Management); Julia Luise Seitz (Friedrich-Alexander University of Erlangen-Nuremberg); Susanne Scheibe (University of Groningen)
Ambient social sexual behavior (SSB) at work involves non-work-related sexual jokes and conversations that sexualize the work atmosphere, typically below the harassment threshold. Research suggests that such behavior is fairly common and usually affects employee well-being moderately negatively. Despite the prevalence and often negative outcomes of ambient workplace SSB, it is not well understood how exactly it is perceived and how it unfolds its effects. We explored these questions in 28 in-depth interviews, where employees described in detail what they thought and felt when encountering ambient workplace SSB. An abductive thematic analysis revealed that initial perceptions ranged from amusement or feeling flattered over mixed feelings to outrage and complete bewilderment. In some cases, initial perceptions changed over time due to reappraisals. It became evident that ambient workplace SSB had the potential to strengthen and built but also to deteriorate and destroy psychosocial resources at work (e.g., feelings of belongingness, appreciation, and ultimately self-esteem), with corresponding consequences for well-being, relationships, and turnover. Initial perceptions of ambivalence often turned out negative, as pondering over ambient SSB was unpleasant and resource-intensive. We discuss implications and propose future studies building on this psychosocial resource theory perspective and, relatedly, the sociometer model of self-esteem.

The Dark Side Of Mindfulness: How Mindfulness Training Enlivens Machiavellian Incivility

OB
Adam Austen Kay (University of South Florida)
Organizational mindfulness interventions are widely promoted as tools for enhancing ethical awareness, self-regulation, and prosocial work behavior. Yet emerging research suggests that their moral consequences may be more contingent than commonly assumed. Drawing on the self-awareness, self-regulation, and self-transcendence (S-ART) framework, this paper theorizes that mindfulness training can yield morally inverted outcomes for employees high in dispositional Machiavellianism. Results from a multi-wave field experiment (n = 473) with both active and passive control conditions support this account. Mindfulness training was shown to enliven affective condemnation by Machiavellians of their coworkers, which in turn translated into greater workplace incivility – a subtle, deniable form of interpersonal mistreatment – while not increasing more overt forms of interpersonal deviance. These effects were robust to alternative explanatory mechanisms and relevant dispositional controls. By identifying Machiavellianism as a critical boundary condition to the S-ART framework, this research challenges the assumption that mindfulness interventions are ethically neutral and underscores the responsibility of organizations to consider how such practices may exert foreseeable, disposition-contingent influences on moral affect and conduct at work.
