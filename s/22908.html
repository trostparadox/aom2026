Implicated Management: How Managers Respond to Frontline Employee-Escalated Paradoxes

OMT
Safoora Wajahat (Macquarie University); Eric Knight (Macquarie University); Jaco Lok (Macquarie University)
This study explores paradoxical tensions in frontline healthcare work arising from the simultaneous demands of relational care, organizational targets, and regulatory compliance, and their managerial implications. Using ethnographic research, we examine how these paradoxes are reconfigured as they escalate from frontline staff to managerial levels. We show that when managers are less implicated in the source of the tension, they tend to mediate paradoxes without assuming ownership, thereby maintaining affective alignment. Conversely, when managers are highly implicated, escalation transforms frontline paradoxes into managerial ones, leading to affective distancing and defensive responses. We contribute to paradox theory by providing a multilevel understanding of how responses to organisational paradoxes are structurally and affectively conditioned.

Paradoxical Intensity: Why Not All Paradoxes Are Created Equal

OMT
Medhanie Gaim (OsloMet – Oslo Metropolitan University); Marco Berti (Nova School of Business and Economics); Miguel Pina e Cunha (New University of Lisbon); Stewart R. Clegg (The University of Sydney)
Not all paradoxes are created equal. Some remain background tensions while others erupt as urgent and overwhelming. To explain this distinction, we introduce the concept of paradoxical intensity, highlighting how paradoxes differ in the strength with which they are experienced and acted upon. We show how organizational members experience paradox through three dimensions: the relative importance of contradictory demands, the felt urgency and consequence, and the perceived capacity to respond. Building on this, we develop a model of zones of paradox and identify four dynamic pathways of amplification, attenuation, displacement, and fluctuation that capture how paradoxes evolve over time. This perspective extends paradox theory beyond salience as a binary condition and offers a nuanced, processual, and politically attuned account of paradox management. In doing so, we suggest that paradoxes differ not only in kind but also in the intensity with which they are lived and managed.

Persistence as a Temporal Engine of Paradox: A Recursive Account of Organizing Through Contradiction

OMT
Roger van den Heuvel (ESCP Business School); Gorgi Krlev (ESCP Business School)
Paradox theory has become an important lens for understanding organizational tensions, typically defining paradoxes as contradictory yet interrelated elements that persist over time. Yet persistence itself is often treated as a definitional attribute rather than as a phenomenon requiring explanation. We still know relatively little about how organizational contradictions become durational, persisting as paradoxes rather than fading, remaining dormant, transforming into other forms of tension, or resolving altogether. This article develops a conceptual process model of paradox persistence. We theorize persistence as an emergent temporal mechanism generated through the interaction of latency and salience. Latency is contradiction structurally and temporally embedded in routines, architectures, metrics and institutional logics. Salience is the episodic activation of contradiction through attention, sensemaking and practice. We conceptualize persistence as the joint product of latency and salience and we articulate two coupled recursive loops. A constitutive loop describes how enactment reproduces and reshapes salience across episodes. An inherent loop describes how structural and temporal responses regenerate latency. Together these loops help explain recurrent temporal patterns observed in paradoxical organizing. The model sharpens paradox theory’s treatment of persistence and highlights contradiction as a generator of temporal structuring. It also enriches process perspectives on how organizations are continuously reproduced through enduring tensions.

A Fair Face May Hide a Foul Heart: The Hidden Struggles of Employees Beneath Optimal Distinctiveness

OMT
Yoonkyung "Yoon" Jin (Indiana University Bloomington)
While existing optimal distinctiveness literature highlights the strategic advantages of pursuing moderately distinctive positions, the impact on the employees executing this strategy remains underexplored. We propose that firms pursuing moderate distinctiveness experience lower employee satisfaction due to increased role ambiguity. We further argue that this ambiguity is mitigated in relatively stable contexts, which helps employees better navigate their roles. Employing Glassdoor data from 2012 to 2024 across a sample of S&P 1500 firms, our analysis reveals a U-shaped relationship between strategic distinctiveness and employee satisfaction: employees in firms pursuing moderately distinctive strategies report lower satisfaction compared to those in firms pursuing either conforming or highly distinctive strategies. The negative effect is alleviated when firms conduct persistent strategies. Our findings highlight the internal challenges of strategic choices and contribute to the research on optimal distinctiveness.
