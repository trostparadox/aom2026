A Rotten Orange? Towards ‘Grounding’ Entrepreneurial Ecosystems and Not Taking Place for Granted

ENT
George Redhead (University of East Anglia); Zografia Bika (University of East Anglia)
Much entrepreneurial ecosystem (EE) research has predominantly focused on macro-level structures, strategic outputs, and high-growth firms, often abstracting EEs from the everyday entrepreneurial activity that surrounds and sustains them. This neglects to ‘ground’ EEs in regional economies and risks eulogising ‘free-floating’, static, and spatially-blind ‘recipes for success’, obscuring the micro-level dynamics through which inclusion and exclusion are produced over time. Addressing this limitation, our longitudinal qualitative case study investigates how everyday ancillary entrepreneurs engage with, adapt to, and withdraw from a globally celebrated regional EE characterised by both innovation and inequality. We offer an inside-out perspective of EEs as a ‘rotten orange’, theorising how strong external performance can coexist with internal fragilities, complementing dominant outside-in EE orchestration viewpoints. By showing how micro-level entrepreneurial practices aggregate into macro-level consequences, including reduced diversity, heightened inequalities, and diminished EE resilience, we argue that excluded everyday ancillary entrepreneurs constitute a vital yet largely invisible infrastructure ‘rooting’ EE functioning, thus advancing a more inclusive and grounded policy lens for addressing rising inequalities.

Dynamics of Inclusion and Exclusion in Entrepreneurial Ecosystems

ENT
Arian Ahlers (University of Münster); Eduard Esau (Eindhoven University of Technology); Frederik Maibaum (University of Münster); India Jana Kandel (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz University of Hanover); Johann Nils Foege
Entrepreneurial ecosystems (EEs) are often comprised of conventional entrepreneurs, i.e., entrepreneurs with traits that are aligned with (local) customs and beliefs. This composition limits the inclusion of individuals with different traits, constraining diversity, knowledge exchange, and innovation. While prior research has examined barriers for unconventional entrepreneurs, it has largely overlooked the systemic dynamics through which exclusion and inclusion emerge. Drawing on the concept of EEs as complex adaptive systems, we employ a longitudinal case study to develop a process model that explains how homogeneity and structural fuzziness generate perceived barriers to inclusion and how targeted inclusion initiatives can activate micro- and meso-level mechanisms that create feedback loops promoting inclusion. Our study thus shifts the focus from identifying excluded actors to explaining how processes of exclusion and inclusion unfold over time within EEs, thereby providing a dynamic foundation for the design of adaptive inclusion initiatives.

Micro and Macro-Level Configurations in Entrepreneurial Ecosystems: Evidence from a QCA Study

ENT
Tanurima Dutta (Baruch College, CUNY); Scott L. Newbert (Baruch College, CUNY); Romi Kher (Baruch College, CUNY)
Although entrepreneurial ecosystems are characterized as complex interdependent systems, existing research frequently investigates the linear and additive effects of isolated ecosystem elements on regional entrepreneurship. This study addresses this gap by adopting a configurational perspective to examine how micro-level ecosystem elements jointly interact with macro-level actors and factors to drive productive entrepreneurship. Utilizing fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) on a dataset of 216 U.S. Metropolitan Statistical Areas, we identify multiple equifinal pathways to high startup density. The results demonstrate that financial capital is not a universal prerequisite for regional entrepreneurship but functions effectively only within specific configurations. Furthermore, the analysis establishes political ideology as a critical ecosystem attribute, revealing that liberal sociocultural environments can promote entrepreneurial activities even in the presence of adverse economic conditions such as high unemployment. This study contributes to the systems perspective by empirically validating the complex interdependence between a region’s tangible and intangible resource configurations.

Micro-Level Strategic Responses and the Evolution of Entrepreneurial Ecosystems

ENT
Siyu Fei (Concordia University); Alex B. Bitektine (JMSB – Concordia University)
Entrepreneurial ecosystem (EE) research has largely emphasized deliberate design and orchestration, often portraying EE evolution as the intended outcome of coordinated intervention of macro-level actors. At the same time, the microfoundations of EE evolution remain underexplored. We explore how decentralized actions of interdependent organizations shape ecosystem-level outcomes following macro-level policy interventions. We address this question through a qualitative case study of InnoWay, a dense EE composed of diverse entrepreneurial support organizations (ESOs) in China. We develop a process model showing how a policy initiative triggers heterogeneous categorization strategies among ESOs. These strategies activate evaluative mechanisms—legitimacy evaluation, comparative evaluation, and status attribution—through which interdependent actors interpret micro-level actions, compare and rank each other. Through the recursive interaction between categorization and evaluation processes micro-level actors’ actions aggregate into an enduring ecosystem structure: a status hierarchy among ESO categories that shapes future resource flows and reinforces the hierarchical structure within the ecosystem. By theorizing evaluation as a core mechanism linking micro-level action to macro-level EE evolution, this study advances the understanding of microfoundations in EE and highlights the unintended consequences of policy-oriented EE development for policymakers and practitioners.
