Government Environmental Attention, Resource Dependence, and Corporate Environmental Decoupling

ONE
Yi Tang (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Firms increasingly face heightened governmental attention to environmental issues, yet substantial gaps persist between their stated environmental commitments and actual environmental practices. Drawing on a resource dependence perspective, this study examines how local government environmental attention—an agenda-setting and non-coercive form of state influence—shapes firms’ environmental decoupling. Using a panel of Chinese listed firms from 2013 to 2020 and text-based measures of government environmental attention derived from city government work reports, we find that greater government environmental attention is associated with higher levels of corporate environmental decoupling. This relationship, however, is systematically conditioned by firms’ resource dependence relationships with local governments. Specifically, the positive association is weaker for firms subject to heightened regulatory dependence, such as key pollution monitored enterprises, but stronger for firms operating in government-supported industries. Moreover, financial interdependence between firms and governments further moderates how firms interpret and respond to governmental environmental signals. By shifting attention from formal regulation and enforcement to attention-based institutional signals, this study advances research on environmental decoupling and firm–government relations by showing how firms interpret government priorities through configurations of reciprocal resource dependence that structure firm–state power relations.

When Environmental Regulations Ripple: Institutional Spillovers and Corporate Asymmetric Responses

ONE
Lin Fan (Tianjin University); HUIBIN DU; Helen Feng Liang (Western Kentucky University); Zhongguo Lin (Tianjin University)
How institutional pressure influences firms’ strategic responses is a critical topic. While existing studies have primarily focused on how focal firms comply with environmental policy goals, they often overlook the broader transmission mechanisms of explicit and mandatory environmental policy pressures directed at individual firms. From the perspective of institutional logics, our study uses the quasi-experiment of the Top 1,000 Energy Conservation Program which helps identify focal firms and their stakeholder firms to examine the strategic how these firms respond to mandatory environmental regulations. The results show that regulated focal firms reduce pollution emissions. However, this is done by cutting production rather than improving technology or efficiency. Our research also reveals how institutional pressure directed at specific firms is transmitted asymmetrically among stakeholder firms based on cooperative and competitive relationships. Suppliers in the supply chain significantly reduce emissions, particularly in industries with high environmental pressure. On the other hand, customer firms show no significant changes in pollution emissions. Competitor firms, in contrast, increase pollution emissions as they absorb the reduced output from focal firms, highlighting a compensatory mechanism. Our study highlights how mandatory environmental policies targeting individual firms can trigger complex spillover effects across organizational networks, shaped by interorganizational resource dependence and institutional complexity.

Organising Phase-Out for Just Industry Transitions: A Review and Research Agenda (WITHDRAWN)

ONE
Anna Stepanyuk (Utrecht University); Christina Bidmon (Utrecht University); Sanne Akerboom (Utrecht University); Flor Avelino (Utrecht University)
The transition to a net-zero society by 2050 raises questions of fairness in how costs and benefits are distributed when phasing out unsustainable practices. Yet, the concept of a ‘just phase-out’ remains underexplored, particularly concerning how firms and industries phase out unsustainable activities. This review therefore examines how academic literature conceptualizes the just organization of phase-out in an industrial context, and what empirical settings have been studied. Based on a scoping review of peer-reviewed articles since 2015, we identify focus themes in current literature, most prominent frameworks and disciplines, and the key justice concerns that scholars have mentioned in relation to organizing phase-out. Based on these insights, we propose a research agenda to advance understanding of the just organizing of phase-out in an industrial context.

Keeping the Lights On: How Brown Firms Invest Under Climate Policy Uncertainty

ONE
Shikha Khurana (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Anish Sugathan (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
How do carbon-intensive firms keep growth engines running while climate policy remains uncertain? Drawing on real options theory, we develop a formal framework that highlights the option value of delaying irreversible investments while preserving operational and financial flexibility. We test the predictions using a difference-in-differences design around the Paris Agreement with a cross-country panel of listed manufacturing firms. Brown firms reduce capital expenditure more than other firms, shift toward more flexible production inputs, and adjust debt maturity toward shorter horizons. At the same time, they intensify operations to sustain sales and profitability and show only limited reductions in emissions intensity. These results reconcile the apparent paradox of continued growth alongside tentative decarbonization and show how policy uncertainty reshapes capability investments through a commitment– flexibility trade-off.
