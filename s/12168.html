Session overview

Newcomer socialization is often viewed as a straightforward process of reducing uncertainty through onboarding, role clarification, and the establishment of supportive relationships. Yet organizational entry is rarely so tidy. Early entry is a short, consequential window in which newcomers must rapidly interpret what is expected of them, whether they belong, and how their contributions are evaluated, often with limited direct feedback and uneven access to guidance. In contemporary workplaces, these processes are shaped not only by formal programs and intentional support, but also by subtle interpersonal signals, emotional experiences, and organization-wide systems that operate simultaneously and sometimes with considerable tension. This symposium advances a more complete view of socialization as a multi-level process of sensemaking, where newcomers adapt based on the cues embedded in everyday interactions and the broader HR architecture that structures the entry environment. A central theme across the symposium is that socialization is shaped by informational and relational signals that are often informal, indirect, and easily overlooked. Newcomers learn norms, performance standards, and relational standing through micro-events, small gestures, emotional expressions, and third-party exchanges that convey inclusion, value, and expectations. Although such cues can be supportive, they may also generate mixed messages and unintended consequences: signals that boost early confidence or effort may, over time, undermine sustained learning, well-being, or retention. By highlighting the dynamic and sometimes paradoxical effects of social cues, the symposium moves beyond the notion that “more support is better” to clarify when and why particular signals help, backfire, or require buffering conditions. A second theme is that newcomer entry is not only stressful, but also emotionally rich and developmentally meaningful. Rather than focusing exclusively on felt anxiety and threat, the symposium brings positive and ambivalent emotional experiences to the center of socialization theory, demonstrating how emotions can expand connections and newcomer motivation while simultaneously shaping self-views in ways that affect adjustment. This emphasis broadens the field’s affective lens and underscores that emotions during entry are not static reactions, they evolve quickly and influence how newcomers engage with learning and relationships. Finally, the symposium elevates the role of the organization as a system. Socialization does not occur only within dyads or teams; it unfolds within firm-level HR architectures that differ markedly across organizations and send powerful, enterprise-wide signals. When these systems are distinctive, consistent, and shared, they can reduce ambiguity and scale effective adjustment; when they are weak or inconsistent, they can amplify confusion and variability in newcomer experiences. This perspective shifts the explanatory focus from isolated antecedents to coherent signaling environments that shape socialization at scale. Overall, this symposium is important because it integrates micro-level exchanges and emotional dynamics with macro-level HR systems into a unified agenda. It offers scholars unique mechanisms and boundary conditions for theorizing socialization as a trajectory, and it provides practitioners with actionable insights: improving newcomer adjustment is not only about adding resources, but also about designing and sustaining clear, coherent, and humane signals that foster learning, belonging, and long-term retention.
