Do Employees Have Good Sleep After a Creative Workday? Workaholism as a Moderator

OB
Sunghyun Chang (Arizona State University)
This study examined how employees’ daily creative experiences at work shape their after-work recovery process. We proposed that daily creativity positively predicts psychological detachment from work and sleep quality, and that these relationships is moderated by workaholism of employees. Using 436 day-level observations from 109 knowledge workers across five workdays, we tested a moderated mediation model linking daily creative events to sleep quality through psychological detachment, contingent on employees’ levels of workaholism. The results demonstrated that daily creativity was positively associated with psychological detachment after work, which in turn predicted higher sleep quality. Supplementary analyses further revealed that the indirect effect of daily creativity was moderated by compulsive workaholic motivation, but not by excessive workaholic behavior. Overall, the findings highlight the beneficial role of daily workplace creativity in enhancing employee recovery, particularly for individuals with strong compulsive workaholism.

Going to Flavortown? Effects of Palatable Food Consumption on Employee Well-Being

OB
Henry R. Young (National University of Singapore); Seonghee Cho (Sungkyunkwan University); Sungwon Kim (Seoul National University); Haebeen Yi (Seoul National University); Sooyeol Kim (Seoul National University); Lance Ferris (University of Ottawa)
As evidenced by the “foodie revolution”, there has been growing societal recognition that the palatability of one’s food matters for well-being. Indeed, popular press often reports that eating palatable food can have restorative effects––particularly after a demanding day at work. These benefits, however, have yet to be acknowledged by organizational researchers. To address this omission in the literature, we conducted a 10-day experience sampling study (Level 2 N = 83, Level 1 N = 553) to determine whether eating palatable food after work facilitates a state of recovery in the evening. We also theorized that these benefits of evening recovery would extend to higher levels of proactive behavior the next day at work. Finally, because palatable eating is often treated as a “guilty pleasure” (e.g., palatable eating tends to be viewed as unhealthy), we investigated whether the positive effects of palatable food consumption would diminish when employees felt higher (vs. lower) levels of guilt. As expected, we found support for our hypothesized effects: those who benefited the most in their state recovery and next-day proactive behavior felt the lowest levels of guilt when consuming palatable food. We discuss how our findings contribute practically and theoretically to the literature on food consumption at work.

Mindful Measurement: Theoretical Implications of the Workplace Mindfulness Scale

OB
Arran Caza (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Tyler Sullivan (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
Workplace mindfulness is as a key construct in organizational scholarship, with growing evidence of its benefits for employee well-being, performance, and relationships. Yet fundamental questions remain about its structure. Specifically, is mindfulness a unitary phenomenon, or does it comprise distinct skills with different functions? Zheng et al.’s (2023) Workplace Mindfulness Scale (WMS) measures three skills: awareness, attention, and acceptance. However, the scale has predominantly been modeled as a single composite score, obscuring any distinctions among its components. We reanalyze the WMS validation data (N = 609) to examine the three skills’ distinct effects on workplace outcomes. After reproducing the original composite-score results, we use dominance analysis to compare the predictive power of awareness, attention, and acceptance across nine outcomes. Results reveal a striking pattern: each outcome is best predicted by a single skill, with complete dominance in eight of nine cases. Awareness dominates prediction of positive states and prosocial behaviors (work engagement, well-being, job performance, self-reported OCB), attention best predicts both mitigation of negative experiences (emotional exhaustion, negative emotions) and job satisfaction, while acceptance shows general dominance for supervisor-rated interpersonal behavior. These patterns imply distinct psychological mechanisms for each skill: awareness as metacognitive monitoring that enables opportunity detection and savoring of positive experiences, attention as executive control that disrupts rumination, and acceptance as nonjudgmental orientation that reduces interpersonal reactivity. Our findings challenge the conceptualization of workplace mindfulness as a unitary construct, demonstrating that awareness, attention, and acceptance operate as distinct skills with different functional roles. We discuss implications for mindfulness theory, targeted workplace interventions, and the broader question of studying multidimensional positive constructs.

From Flexibility to Fulfillment: How Coordination Flexibility Boosts Psychological Well-Being

OB
Bibi Zhang (Swansea University); Jianing Song (Swansea University); Mariya Mathai (University of Birmingham); Jia Li (Swansea University)
Coordination flexibility enables organizations to be adaptive and resilient in dynamic environments by deploying, adjusting, and reassigning employee roles and responsibilities. Yet little is known about how coordination flexibility affects employee psychological well-being. Drawing on self-determination theory, we propose that coordination flexibility enhances psychological well-being because it fulfills employees’ need for autonomy. Furthermore, leader vision can strengthen the positive effect of coordination flexibility, such that when leader vision is stronger, coordination flexibility can have a more positive impact on psychological well-being through supporting employees’ need for autonomy. The results of an experimental study and two three-wave surveys have provided support for these hypotheses. This research adds to our understanding of the boundary conditions and mechanisms through which coordination flexibility shapes psychological well-being.

AI Assistants at Work: Unpacking Their Dual Impacts on Employee Well-Being and Work Outcomes

OB
Minya Xu (Peking University); Shengming Liu (Fudan University)
As AI assistants increasingly take on coworker-like roles in workplaces, their impact on human resource management and employee well-being warrants careful examination. Grounded in Social Information Processing (SIP) Theory, this paper develops a theoretical model and tests it using a multi-wave, multi-source field study, supplemented by one experiment. The findings reveal that the frequency of interaction with AI assistants has a bidirectional effect on employees’ work well-being and performance. On one hand, increased interaction frequency with AI assistants indirectly negatively affects employees’ work well-being by reducing feelings of team belonging, which in turn diminishes job performance and heightens turnover intention. On the other hand, increased interaction frequency indirectly positively influences employees’ work well-being by reducing image threat, thereby improving job performance and lowering turnover intention. Additionally, the study finds that coworker support mitigates the negative impact of frequent interaction with AI assistants on team belonging, as well as the negative effects on image threat. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the dual effects of workplace AI assistant adoption and highlight the interplay between interpersonal interactions and human-machine interactions.
