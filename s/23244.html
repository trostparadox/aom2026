Artificial Intelligence in Venture Capital Investment: A Systematic Literature Review

ENT
Mehrdad Alikhani (Old Dominion University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming the venture capital (VC) industry by reshaping how investors source deals, evaluate startups, structure investments, and support portfolio firms. Despite growing scholarly interest, related research is still in its infant stage, remaining fragmented across disciplines. This study conducts a systematic literature review (SLR) in multiple disciplines, including entrepreneurship, finance, management, and computer science, to synthesize and structure the emerging body of knowledge on AI in VC investment and to propose a future research agenda. Following PRISMA guidelines, we analyzed 24, Q1-ranked journal articles published between 2010 and 2025, identified through the Web of Science database. Using thematic synthesis, we classify the studies according to AI users (VCs, startups/entrepreneurs, authors), VC process stages (deal sourcing, investment selection, valuation, deal structure, value-added, and exit), AI technologies (e.g., machine learning, natural language processing, network analytics), and methodological approaches. We found that existing AI–VC research is heavily concentrated in pre-investment stages, especially in deal sourcing and investment selection, where AI is primarily framed as a screening and prediction technology. In contrast, valuation, deal structuring, post-investment governance, and exit remain underexplored. Although quantitative and machine-learning-based studies dominate, theory-driven and process-oriented research is limited. Overall, the literature has advanced methodologically but remains conceptually narrow, motivating the integrative framework and future research agenda proposed in this review.

Bad Luck and the Pessimism Bias: Terrorist-Stricken VCs and the Likelihood of Investment Termination

ENT
Jun Xu (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Peggy M. Lee (Arizona State University); Qi Zhu (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
This study explores how unexpected, negative events (bad luck) can induce a pessimism bias that alters investment behavior. Using the context of terrorist attacks and mass shootings that occur near venture capital (VC) firms, we examine whether the investment decisions of terrorist-stricken VCs reflect a desire to minimize risk (terminating less risky investments) or to minimize spillover effects (terminating investments that are contained within the terrorist-stricken area). Drawing on a dataset of U.S.-based VC investments from 1997 to 2020, we find that, relative to non-affected VC firms, terrorist-stricken VC firms are significantly more likely to terminate investments in their portfolio ventures. Moreover, this relationship is weaker if the ventures are co-located with the VC firms and is stronger if the ventures are early stage. In other words, termination patterns reflect a risk aversion consistent with a pessimism bias (defined as a tendency to overestimate negative outcomes); this lends support for the importance of managing risks rather than minimizing spillover effects.

Dark Side of Embeddedness: Embeddedness in VC Syndicates and VC Performance Manipulation

ENT
Shrijata Chattopadhyay (State University of New York at Binghamton); Umit Ozmel (Purdue University)
This paper emphasizes the drawbacks of relational embeddedness in multiparty partnerships as embeddedness can facilitate collusion among the members. We show that as relational embeddedness among VC investors in a VC syndicate increase, it facilitates collusion among the syndicate members and results in higher performance manipulation by the VC funds. This effect holds even after controlling for the endogeneity in the extend of the relational embeddedness in the VC syndicate. Reputational concerns and better economic conditions can moderate some of the effect of embeddedness on performance manipulation. On the other hand, embedded VC firms with higher stakes, or more to lose, manipulate more.

Deal-Maker or Deal-Breaker? How Violent Conflicts Influence Investments in African Startups

ENT
David Antons (University of Bonn); Christina Dienhart; Erk Peter Piening (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz University of Hanover); Torsten Oliver Salge (RWTH Aachen University)
This study aims to shed new light on the previously inconclusive relationship between violent conflict and investment, focusing on venture capital (VC) funding in Africa. By giving rise to uncertainty, violent conflicts (e.g., wars, terrorism, riots) are generally assumed to constrain startups’ access to VC funding, which is essential for their growth and survival as well as economic development in Africa more broadly. Using a unique dataset covering 3,845 investor decisions nested in 1,043 venture rounds in all African countries between 2019 and 2024, however, our analysis reveals that the number of violent conflicts in a country has no negative effect on VC investments. Instead, we find that under certain conditions (e.g., for later-stage startups) this relationship is positive and that violent conflict, a venture’s stage of development, and investor origin (domestic vs. foreign) interact in explaining VC investments. These findings have important implications for research on entrepreneurship and beyond.

Politics of the Deal: How Political Homophily Affects VCs’ Investment in Ventures

ENT
David Weng (California State University-Fullerton); Sheng-Bin Wang (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University)
Venture capitalists’ (VC) investment is crucial for stimulating entrepreneurship, but little is known whether VC managers’ personal values matter. In this study, we postulate that political homophily, or the extent that a VC and a venture have similar political ideologies, will affect a VC’s likelihood of investment. We furthermore contend that this proposed effect will be moderated by the geographic distance and industry relatedness between the VC and venture. Results derived from a sample of VCs based in the United States support our arguments, suggesting that VC-venture relationships are politically divided.
