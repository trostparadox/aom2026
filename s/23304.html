Exit Before Explosion: The Reputational Effect of Investors’ Exits from Fraudulent Portfolio Firms

ENT
Willow Wu (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Meitong Li; Qianhui Huan (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Xufei Ma (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
Firm fraud is an escalating global concern and is particularly difficult to detect in newly public firms. Although venture capital (VC) investors are often viewed as key monitors capable of deterring misconduct, their involvement does not necessarily prevent fraud. This raises an important question: Do markets discipline investors for governance failures, or can investors avoid penalties through strategic exit? Drawing on research on organizational stigmatization and reputation, this study examines how an investor’s early exit affects the investor’s reputation after misconduct is revealed. We find that investors who exit prior to negative exposure tend to enjoy reputational benefits relative to those who remain affiliated with the fraudulent firm. We further test boundary conditions and find that the reputational benefits of early disengagement are stronger when misconduct is more severe and when investors serve on the board of the fraudulent firm. Additional analyses show that, even compared with VCs whose portfolio firms are not involved in fraud investigations, VCs that successfully exit before a portfolio firm’s fraud investigation becomes public experience a reputational boost. This boost appears to stem from larger subsequent investment amounts in startups, rather than from increased capital from limited partners. Overall, these findings shift attention from ex post remediation to ex ante stigma avoidance, offering a dynamic view of reputation management and a more nuanced assessment of investors’ gatekeeper role in financial markets.

How Commitment Shapes the Development and Enactment of Entrepreneurial Identities

ENT
Laëtitia Gabay-Mariani; Saulo Barbosa (emlyon business school)
How do nascent entrepreneurs develop and enact an entrepreneurial identity over time? While prior research emphasizes narratives and self-definitions, we know little about how identity emerges through sustained entrepreneurial action. Drawing on symbolic interactionism and commitment theory, this study examines how early entrepreneurial commitment shapes action trajectories and, in turn, long-term identity outcomes. We follow a cohort of nascent entrepreneurs over six years using a three-wave longitudinal design combining survey data and LinkedIn profiles. We distinguish affective and instrumental commitment to both the venture and the entrepreneurial profession, and examine their effects on two key trajectories: persistence in the initial venture and habitual entrepreneurship. Our findings show that commitment does not directly predict long-term identity; rather, it channels entrepreneurs into distinct action pathways that crystallize into different identity forms. Persistence fosters a venture-based identity (“I am my business”), whereas habitual entrepreneurship fosters a profession-based identity (“I am an entrepreneur”). By integrating commitment, action, and time, this study reconceptualizes entrepreneurial identity as an outcome of lived entrepreneurial trajectories rather than a stable antecedent of action.

Serial Entrepreneurs’ Failure as Fuel or Foil? Analyzing the Role of External Changes

ENT
Carrington Motley (Carnegie Mellon University); Madeleine Gates (Carnegie Mellon University)
This study integrates research on serendipity, organizational learning, and entrepreneurial strategy to examine how prior venture failure influences the actions and subsequent venture outcomes of serial entrepreneurs. We develop a comprehensive framework explaining the heterogeneity in external changes serial entrepreneurs make and analyze the relationship between these changes and subsequent venture survival, incorporating the role of negative serendipity in this process. Our analysis of 893 serial entrepreneurs indicates that failure increases the likelihood of external changes, particularly in industry and location, and that these changes are associated with worse subsequent venture survival outcomes and negative serendipity. Additionally, these negative effects intensify with each additional change. We discuss how these findings contribute to research on serendipity, serial entrepreneurship, and learning from failure.

Serial Versus Portfolio Entrepreneurs: The Critical Role of Identity

ENT
Elham Asgari (Michigan Technological University); Gavin Joseph Williamson (Rowan University)
Remarkably little is known about successful entrepreneurs. Stereotypes abound – fueled in large measure by popularized misconceptions of celebrity entrepreneurs and their unicorns – yet the reality is vastly more complex, varied, and human than popular media, and much research, conveys. Existing scholarship has aptly demonstrated that the utility functions of those engaging in entrepreneurship are highly heterogenous and, quite often, intensely personal. Motivations range from short-term emphases on wealth generation, exit strategies, and swift transition to new opportunities, to longer-term, more panoramic aspirations of starting, building, and sustaining multiple vibrant businesses in pan-generational fashion. This is particularly evident in a detailed comparison of portfolio versus serial entrepreneurs. Both forms of successful entrepreneurship leave a legacy, but the nature of these legacies – and the motives impelling them – could not be more dissimilar. Social identity theory (SIT) offers a useful perspective for investigating the motivational drivers underlying entrepreneurial journeys, especially when examining successful entrepreneurs and the extent to which their respective legacies are pursued in serial or portfolio fashion. Drawing upon SIT, we extend and enhance existing work on communitarianism by developing and testing a framework that links communitarian-minded motives to the pursuit of portfolio entrepreneurship. Importantly, our findings reveal that the communitarian-portfolio link is stronger among racial and ethnic minorities, women, and elderly entrepreneurs. Our findings debunk widely held beliefs about successful entrepreneurs, thereby inviting a constructive reappraisal of the financial and non-financial drivers of entrepreneurial action.

The Pursuit of Entrepreneurial Identity in an (In)Equitable Ecosystem

ENT
Charlie Wall-Andrews (Toronto Metropolitan University)
This paper examines artists in the Canadian music industry through an entrepreneurial identity lens. It begins by explaining the focus on the intersection between artistic and entrepreneurial identities. While the development of the hyphenated artist-entrepreneur identity is of interest, many artists initially lack an entrepreneurial identity. Instead, they engage in various activities that, over time, lead them to identify as both artists and entrepreneurs. The paper reviews the literature on artist-entrepreneurs to identify key concepts that influence how musicians advance in their entrepreneurial careers. These concepts are organized into two arguments concerning positionality and precarity traps , highlighting the challenges of becoming an entrepreneur in an inequitable ecosystem. Using a large-N repeated cohort qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), this study is one of the first to explore the development of hyphenated identities as artist-entrepreneurs and explain how intersectionalities influence the progression pathways from artist to artist-entrepreneur.
