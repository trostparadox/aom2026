Beyond the Black Box: Perceived Sophistication and Trust in Algorithmic Recommendations

CTO
Sofia Calderon (University of Notre Dame); Nicholas Berente (University of Notre Dame)
Despite extensive research on algorithm aversion, existing frameworks treat algorithms as homogeneous entities, overlooking how perceptions of technological sophistication shape acceptance. Building on Castelo et al. (2019), we examine whether perceived algorithmic sophistication moderates the well-established finding that individuals trust algorithms more for objective versus subjective tasks. We conducted a 2×3 factorial experiment (N=384) on Prolific, manipulating task framing (objective vs. subjective) and sophistication labels (generic algorithm, high-sophistication AI, low-sophistication app) across movie and dating recommendation tasks. Results strongly replicate the baseline task framing effect, confirming that subjective framing substantially reduces algorithmic trust. However, contrary to predictions, sophistication operates as a universal competence signal with parallel effects across both task types rather than as a targeted moderator that closes the trust gap in subjective domains. Mediation analysis reveals that sophistication increases trust primarily through cognitive pathways while paradoxically decreasing affective trust, yet still driving adoption because competence perceptions dominate the decision calculus. Binary choice analysis demonstrates that sophistication functions as a "threshold-crosser" rather than "gap-closer," generating sufficient absolute utility gains to shift adoption behavior in subjective tasks despite persistent relative trust differentials. These findings advance understanding of how technological attributes shape algorithm acceptance and offer strategic insights for organizations deploying decision-support systems across diverse task domains.

Fair Pricing for All: A Generative Adversarial Pricing Framework for Risk-Commensurate Interest Rate

CTO
Ram Gopal (University of Warwick); Xiao Qiao (City University of Hong Kong); Moris Strub (University of Warwick); Zonghao Yang (Stevens Institute of Technology)
We design Generative Adversarial Pricing (GAP), an algorithmic pricing framework for personal loans based on Generative Adversarial Networks, which embeds the loan-level fairness condition of equalized risk-return trade-off across loans. This condition is preserved under aggregation and prevents any group of borrowers from becoming systematically disadvantaged. We analyze nearly two million loans on Prosper and LendingClub from 2013 to 2025 and find that GAP alleviates potential biases against African-American borrowers and borrowers who receive public assistance, while maintaining equity across male and female borrowers. GAP also rectifies interest rate distortions by reducing differences in the cost of credit for borrowers of different creditworthiness. Consistent findings across two platforms suggest a common challenge in the current underwriting system design that our framework helps overcome.

Efficiency vs. Warmth? The Double-Edged Sword Effect of AI Monitoring on Employees' Behaviors

CTO
Junjiang Li (Northwestern Polytechnical University); Wang Juan (Northwestern Polytechnical University)
Although a growing number of companies have widely applied artificial intelligence (AI) monitoring to improve organizational performance, existing research on its impacts remains scarce. Drawing on goal shielding theory and feedback intervention theory, this study explores how AI monitoring influences employees’ work outcomes (work procrastination and workplace loneliness), and further explores the moderating role of negative feedback agents (AI vs. human leaders). We conduct an eye-tracking experiment (N = 68) and a field study using three-wave data from 248 employees. The results show that AI monitoring is negatively related to work procrastination but positively related to workplace loneliness. Employees’ bottom-line mentality mediates the relationship between AI monitoring and work procrastination, while social avoidance mediates the relationship between AI monitoring and workplace loneliness. Negative feedback from the AI strengthens the positive effect of AI monitoring on bottom-line mentality, which in turn amplifies the indirect effect of AI monitoring on work procrastination via bottom-line mentality. In contrast, negative feedback from human leaders strengthens the positive effect of AI monitoring on social avoidance, which in turn amplifies the indirect effect of AI monitoring on workplace loneliness via social avoidance. This study enriches the understanding of AI monitoring and provides important implications for scholars and managers.

The Logic of Algorithms: AI’s Role in Homogenizing Organizational Structures

CTO
Shivani Patel (University of North Carolina at Charlotte); Camille Grace Endacott (University of North Carolina at Charlotte)
Organizations are rapidly adopting artificial intelligence (AI) systems to optimize decision-making and enhance efficiency. Although research has examined how institutional isomorphism drives the adoption of AI technologies, less attention has been paid to how AI technologies can drive the homogenization or organizational practices from within. This theoretical paper argues that AI represents a novel mechanism of endogenous isomorphism that operates through recursive embedding in everyday work. Drawing on institutional theory and structuration theory, we explore how AI systems homogenize organizational practices across traditional field boundaries. First, we describe how AI differs from previous technologies by continuously learning from diverse users and propagating those learned patterns back into organizational contexts. Second, we explain how these AI-selected practices could become recursively embedded and naturalized through repeated use. Third, we use professional identity as an illustrative case, demonstrating how AI homogenizes the enacted performance of professional roles by optimizing towards aggregate patterns rather than field-specific norms. This analysis presents AI as both a product and producer of institutional logics, simultaneously subject to traditional isomorphic adoption pressures while generating novel forms of cross-field convergence though use. We discuss implications for organizational diversity, professional autonomy, and adaptive capacity, arguing that AI-driven homogenization may reduce requisite variety and increase systematic vulnerabilities.
