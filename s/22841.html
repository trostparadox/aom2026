Managing Risk and Legitimacy: A Study of Corporate Indigenous Policy and Adoption Drivers

SIM
Poornima Mishra (EGADE Business School sede Monterrey); Bryan W. Husted (Monterrey Institute of Technology)
As global norms like the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) gain prominence, and as corporate activity increasingly intersects with Indigenous territories, firms face mounting pressure to formalize their commitments. In response, a growing number of firms are adopting formal Indigenous policies. This study investigates how firms translate these broad norms into specific organizational practice by analysing the content and adoption of corporate Indigenous-specific policies. Through a two-phase analysis of 471 firms in Australia, Canada, and the U.S., we first map the substantive landscape using structural topic modelling. We identify four thematic clusters that coalesce into two distinct corporate postures: a compliance-oriented posture (emphasizing procedural governance and risk mitigation) and a relationship- oriented posture (prioritizing reconciliation and partnership).These postures are not uniformly distributed; they are shaped by national institutional environments. In the second phase, event-history analysis reveals that policy adoption is driven both by ESG controversies, which act as legitimacy shocks, and by mimetic pressure from industry peers, though this peer influence diminishes after an initial diffusion wave. This study offers the first typology of Indigenous policy content and clarifies the institutional mechanisms driving their adoption.

Does Anyone See Us? Press Freedom and Sovereign Wealth Fund Disinvestment after ESG Controversies

SIM
Maria Roszkowska-Menkes (Université catholique de Louvain); Maria Aluchna (Warsaw School of Economics); James Thewissen (Université catholique de Louvain); Shuo Yan (Southern University of Science and Technology)
Sovereign wealth funds (SWFs) are increasingly expected to integrate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) considerations into their investment decisions, yet little is known about how these typically passive, long-horizon state investors respond when portfolio firms become involved in ESG controversies. Drawing on legitimacy theory, we argue that ESG controversies constitute negative shocks that can trigger SWF disinvestment by heightening both firm-level risk and associative legitimacy risks for the investor, and that these effects depend on institutional information environments. Using a panel of 27,969 firm-year observations across 56 countries from 2012–2023, we examine changes in SWF ownership following the first recorded ESG controversy at a firm, employing staggered difference-in-differences and event-study designs. We find that SWFs reduce ownership following an ESG controversy, but only when portfolio firms are headquartered in countries with high press freedom. In low-press-freedom environments, we observe no systematic disinvestment and, in some cases, increased ownership. These findings shift attention from ex ante ESG screening to ex post investor responses to misconduct, extend legitimacy theory to state-owned investors, and show that media scrutiny conditions whether ESG controversies translate into observable disinvestment by SWFs.

When Hostility Fuels Cooperation: National Animosity and Multinational Firms’ Use of CSR Alliances

SIM
Jiawen MA (Hong Kong Metropolitan University); Jian Hu (Singapore Management University)
Cross-border alliances are typically understood as fragile under conditions of political hostility. Challenging this view, we develop a signaling-based theory of how multinational corporations (MNCs) strategically adapt alliance portfolios in response to national animosity. We argue that national animosity heightens MNCs’ reputational vulnerability and impression-management pressures, motivating firms to form CSR strategic alliances as observable, partner-endorsed signals of social responsibility in host countries. We further theorize that this tendency is amplified when host-country geopolitical risk politicizes stakeholder evaluations and when firm visibility magnifies reputational spillovers. Analyses of U.S. MNCs operating across host countries support these arguments. Our study reconceptualizes hostility not only as a constraint on collaboration but also as a condition that reshapes firms’ alliance choices toward credibility-enhancing governance forms.

CSR and Macro-Institutional Variables: The Moderating Effects of Institutional Context

SIM
Ana Rita Oliveira (ISEG); Pedro Rino Vieira (ISEG Lisbon School of Economics and Management, University of Lisbon)
Several studies investigate differences in Corporate Social Responsibility policies across firms, often relating them to institutional differences between countries. Institutional factors such as cultural values and legal frameworks significantly influence CSR practices. However, some macro-institutional variables, such as perceptions of corruption, the promotion of human rights, and gender equality, have been little examined. Moreover, in civil law countries, CSR disclosure is mostly mandatory, resulting in higher ESG ratings. Conversely, in common law countries, where CSR investment is lower and reporting is voluntary, CSR primarily serves as a tool to build legitimacy with stakeholders. This study aims to explore the relationship between CSR, corruption perception, and countries’ tendencies towards human rights and gender equality. For this purpose, we utilise a sample of 8,125 firm-year observations, covering 1,179 listed companies across 31 countries classified as either emerging or developed, over the period from 2018 to 2023. The results indicate that firms operating in countries with higher corruption perceptions and lower attitudes towards human rights and gender equality tend to have lower Environmental, Social, and Governance ratings. Furthermore, our findings suggest that both the legal system and cultural values influence this relationship. This research enhances both academic literature and practical understanding by emphasising cross-country variations in CSR. It also assists investors in recognising issues related to corruption perception, human rights, and gender equality, thereby supporting a more informed evaluation of investment risk based on macro-institutional factors.

Opting Out to Do Right: How Small Incentives Harness Self-Interest to Reduce Deception

SIM
Long Wang (City University of Hong Kong); Fei Song (Toronto Metropolitan University)
Financial incentives, while intended to motivate performance, can inadvertently encourage fraud and deception in organizations. This research investigates a novel approach whereby organizations can harness self-interest to reduce dishonesty by incentivizing individuals to opt out of situations in which they might otherwise cheat or lie for greater financial gain. Across four complementary experimental studies, we examined how providing an opt-out option—with and without a small monetary incentive—affected people’s propensity to lie. Study 1 demonstrated that offering a $2 incentive to opt out significantly reduced lying even when participants could have earned up to $5 by cheating, with the effect mediated by participants’ simultaneous desire to earn money and maintain honesty. Study 2 replicated these findings in a context involving private, non-verifiable information, where the small incentive encouraged individuals to forego doubling their payoff through dishonesty. Study 3 extended this effect to high-stakes settings, showing that a $2 opt-out option reduced lying even when potential gains reached $20 outside the lab. Finally, Study 4 revealed that the opt-out option similarly reduced lying in risky decision-making contexts, particularly when the probability of winning was low. Collectively, these findings illuminated a subtle yet powerful mechanism through which organizations may align financial incentives with ethical behavior, offering new insights into the interplay between self-interest, moral motivation, and decision-making in organizational settings.
