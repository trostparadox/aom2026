How Digital Technology Reshapes Markets: Expansion, Reallocation, and Selection

STR
Rowan Clarke (Harvard Business School)
Does digital technology primarily expand markets or reallocate demand from competitors, and does diffusion translate into longer-run selection and performance heterogeneity? I study a platform-upgrade of payment terminals in South Africa covering 158,344 firms and 25 million consumer cards. Exploiting variation from a natural experiment, I show upgrading increases weekly sales by 18.5% and reduces payment failures by 2–4 percentage points, with larger gains for initially weaker firms. This comes partly at competitors’ expense: weaker non-adopters within 500 meters lose 7–11% of sales when nearby rivals upgrade. Linked card–firm data reveal the mechanism: holding consumer spending fixed within local markets, shoppers reallocate toward upgraded firms, increasing their spending share by 43%. Aggregating, roughly 90 percent of adopters’ gains reflect platform expansion while 10 percent reflects business stealing—yet this reallocation drives meaningful selection because displacement concentrates among weaker, proximate rivals. These dynamics cumulate into differential survival, concentrating market structure. The findings show that capability-building technologies simultaneously raise performance and intensify competition: even market-expanding technologies amplify heterogeneity through localized reallocation. As digital tools from payments to AI diffuse unevenly, adoption timing—not merely adoption—shapes which firms survive.

Dancing with the Platforms: Strategic Responses of Small Businesses to Platform Dependence (WITHDRAWN)

STR
HYEMIN KWAK (Yonsei University); Kyung Min Park (Yonsei University)
Delivery platforms serve as key intermediaries, connecting restaurants to distant markets, thereby expanding customer reach and enabling market expansion. Yet similar levels of platform dependence can yield divergent outcomes across performance dimensions. Drawing on resource dependence theory, we define platform dependence as reliance on platform-mediated demand and examine its implications for profitability and sales growth under four boundary conditions: (1) competitive intensity, (2) environmental munificence, (3) platform concentration (transaction allocation across platforms), and (4) franchise affiliation. Using restaurant-level panel data from Korean restaurants, we estimate fixed-effects models with Wooldridge’s two-step selection correction to address non-random platform participation and observed platform sales. Results reveal a profitability–sales growth trade-off under platform governance: greater platform dependence is associated with lower profitability but higher sales growth. Moreover, the boundary conditions differ across the two outcomes. Overall, platform dependence can compress profitability by weakening restaurants’ bargaining power while also increasing sales growth through expanded platform-mediated demand. These opposing effects are contingent on market environments and organizational characteristics.

Reverse Imitation, Product Performance, and Leading Firms on Digital Platforms

STR
Jian Guan (Central South University); Diya Hu (Central South University); Xiongbin li (Central South University)
Studies have focused on how lagging firms imitate leaders, but pay less attention to imitative actions of leading firms. Based on reverse imitation of leading firms on digital platforms, our study differentiates within-category and cross-category contexts and suggests that reverse imitation may lead to conflicts between competitive effects and legitimacy, with their relative influence varying across categories. From dynamic competition and institutional perspectives, this study investigates how the extent of reverse imitation influences product performance and reveals the moderating effects of the speed of imitation and status. Using game data from the iOS platform of China between 2019 and 2024, this study employs machine learning techniques to construct text-based semantic similarity measures to capture the degree of reverse imitation, ultimately identifying 306 within-category and 1,090 cross-category reverse imitation applications. The results show that within-category reverse imitation negatively affects product performance, whereas cross-category reverse imitation has a positive effect, with the speed of imitation and status exerting significant moderating effects. This study deepens the understanding of competitive mechanisms and institutional implications of reverse imitation by leading firms on digital platform, offering boundary-conditioned theoretical and practical implications for choosing imitation targets and recognizing imitation timing in complex competitive environments.

How Does Concurrent Institutional Pressure Drive Digital Capability Development?

STR
liu yunli
Under the backdrop of digital transformation, enterprises face concurrent institutional pressures that compel them to enhance their digital capabilities. Although prior studies have examined the effects of individual institutional pressures on corporate digital capabilities, the findings remain inconclusive. Moreover, existing research provides limited insight into the underlying mechanisms and boundary conditions of this relationship. Drawing on the knowledge base perspective and network embeddedness theory, this study investigates Chinese manufacturing firms listed on the Shanghai A-share market and Shenzhen A-share market from 2010 to 2023. We develop a research framework in which the integration of digital technology knowledge and business knowledge within TMT serves as a mediating mechanism. Network betweenness centrality and network intensity function as moderating variables. This framework examines how concurrent institutional pressures jointly influence corporate digital capabilities. The findings indicate that the integration of digital technology and business knowledge within TMT mediates the relationship between concurrent institutional pressures and corporate digital capabilities. Network betweenness centrality positively moderates the relationship between concurrent institutional pressures and TMT knowledge integration. Network intensity, in turn, strengthens the relationship between TMT knowledge integration and corporate digital capabilities. This study elucidates the mechanism through which concurrent institutional pressures affect corporate digital capabilities from a knowledge integration perspective. It also clarifies the boundary conditions of this process by incorporating network embeddedness theory, thereby enriching the literature on institutional pressures and digital capabilities.

Dropping the F-Bomb: Vocabulary Control and Novelty in User Community Innovations

STR
Jay Park (The University of Texas at Austin)
With the help of AI and other advanced technologies, organizations increasingly regulate how members speak and write. We argue that such vocabulary control disrupts existing vocabulary structures and can unintentionally harm the novelty of ideas generated in organizations by constraining the feedback process/dynamics. We theorize three cognitive mechanisms: (1) vocabulary control reduces associative recombination that constrains the richness of feedback; (2) vocabulary control narrows affective expression that diminishes the salience of feedback; and (3) vocabulary control heightens self-monitoring that slows the exchange of feedback. We test these predictions using the staggered rollout of an automated profanity filter on Steam Workshop—an online community where users create and refine new game content—analyzing 7,145 idea updates and 61,175 feedback comments across 1,045 communities. Difference-in-differences estimates show that idea novelty declines after the filter is introduced, and mediation analyses align with the theory. We contribute to research on organizational design and control, vocabularies, and user innovation by showing that interventions in organizational vocabularies can inadvertently curb creative outcomes.
