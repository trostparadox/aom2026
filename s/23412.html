Measuring What Matters: Assessment Integrity, Accountability, and Student Success

Session overview

If you cannot measure it, can you manage it? This session applies rigorous empirical scrutiny to what business schools measure, reward, and incentivize: examining the effectiveness of online proctoring frameworks, the dynamics of grade inflation across faculty employment types, the return on investment of corequisite academic support programs, the institutional climate conditions that predict GPA, and the multidimensionality of MBA student performance beyond grades. An essential session for accreditation-focused educators, institutional research leaders, and anyone who believes evidence should drive institutional decision-making.

Papers (5)

Testing a Framework for Proctoring Effectiveness in Online Assessment

MED
Gerard Beenen (California State University-Fullerton); Sinjini Mitra (California State University-Fullerton)
The increase in online instruction makes business schools vulnerable to a surge in cheating behavior and in need of a framework to assess the potential effectiveness of exam proctoring conditions. We propose a social facilitation theory framework to assess how four prototypical proctoring conditions (1. web recording, 2. video summarization, 3. live online, 4. in-person) may be associated with decreasing levels of cheating. The framework was tested with three studies. Study 1 assessed students’ (n=395) perceptions of being watched and monitored and instructors’ (n=113) perceptions of controlling and monitoring the testing environment. Study 2 showed students (n=397) had weaker intentions to cheat for proctoring conditions posited as more actively monitored. Consistent with social facilitation theory, evaluation apprehension and self-awareness were motivational deterrents to cheating. Study 3 replicated and extended prior findings of higher student scores for unproctored versus proctored exams with a field study (n=224) of actual exam results for the same four proctoring conditions. Results mostly supported the framework, with interesting asymmetries in how students and instructors perceived live online versus in-person proctoring conditions. This framework can help researchers and instructors classify proctoring effectiveness and align proctoring approaches with assessment goals.

When Student Evaluations of Teaching Rise: The Interplay of Faculty Status and Grade Inflation

MED
Sana Tauseef (Institute of Business Administration); Madeeha Omer Lakhani (Not Associated)
The increasing reliance on part-time faculty and growing evidence of grade inflation make it essential to investigate how these dynamics shape student evaluation of teaching (SET). This research explores whether faculty employment status impacts students’ grades and, in turn, influences the SET. Using a class-level dataset of 1,267 management and related courses at a public sector university in Pakistan, we assessed patterns in SET scores using a mediated-effects framework. We document that part-time faculty assign better grades to students. Furthermore, students give better evaluations to part-time faculty due to their higher course grades. The findings raise significant concerns for institutions that use SET scores as a primary criterion in contract renewals, promotions, and performance appraisals. They also emphasize the importance of standardizing teaching and assessment rigor, as well as adopting grading rubrics, to ensure greater consistency, promote fairness, and support high-quality education.

What is the Return on Investment for Implementation of Corequisite Support in West Virginia?

MED
Cory A Campbell (Indiana State University); Philip A. Cola (Case Western Reserve University); Corley Dennison
The higher education sector faces mounting fiscal pressures, as public institutions contend with declining state appropriations, rising operational costs, and sustained enrollment declines. These challenges heighten the need for cost effective strategies that improve student success while maintaining institutional financial stability. The post-implementation effects from corequisite support have shown that such support improves student success while providing a positive return on investment from the institutional perspective. Our research focuses on the fiscal impact of corequisite education. The results of this study show that the corequisite support is cost neutral for public institutions in West Virginia, which is a powerful confirmatory finding. In the aggregate, the marginal gain from increased retention (18.68% for four-year institutions and 12.26% for two-year institutions) more than offset the cost of implementation and the change of instruction associated with corequisite support. In addition, the corequisite support model is proven to be effective across demographics including minority, age, low income, and gender. Taken together, these results reinforce the value of corequisite education as a financially and academically sound policy option for public higher education systems.

Institutional Support, Relational Climate, and Academic Performance in Management Education

MED
Gareth Craze (University of Ecomomics Ho Chi Minh City); William Luse (University of La Verne); Loren R. Dyck (University of La Verne)
This study examines whether the perceived organizational support (POS) provided by their academic institutions is associated with academic performance among undergraduate management students, and additionally whether the influence of relational climate (RC) moderates such a relationship. We tested a model linking POS to academic performance as measured by grade point average (GPA), with RC as a moderating factor. The results demonstrate that POS is positively associated with GPA, and that relational climate significantly moderates this relationship, such that the positive association between the support of the academic institution and the student’s academic performance is stronger when students experience the institution as a high-quality relational environment. These findings suggest that institutional support is a quantifiably positive contributor to students’ academic outcomes, while being most effective when also embedded within a positive interpersonal climate. By integrating institutional and relational perspectives, we build upon prior theoretical and empirical work that has explored the contextually multifaceted antecedents of student performance, as well as the relationships between institutional support and performance more broadly. The results also highlight the importance of business schools and management educators not only investing in formal support structures, but additionally in taking steps to foster supportive academic environments which better enable students to fully leverage the institutional support available to them.

Performance and Preference Heterogeneity in MBA Students: A Two-Dimensional Perspective

MED
Aaron S. Wallen (Columbia University in the City of New York); Zachariah Brown; Michael W. Morris (Columbia Business School)
Research on MBA student performance traditionally relies on GPA as the indicator of success. However, business schools aim to develop future leaders for diverse career paths and, as such, value different forms of performance. Admissions decisions are made with the hope that students will excel not only in the classroom, but also through leadership roles and extracurricular engagement. In this research, we challenge the unidimensional conception of MBA success. Across two large-N studies using archival and survey data from an elite U.S. business school, we demonstrate that MBA academic performance falls along two distinct dimensions: systematizing and social. These dimensions align with the “poet vs. quant” distinction and are only weakly correlated, indicating that students often specialize in one domain. Moreover, we show that the social dimension of performance is uniquely associated with leadership success. Finally, we investigate how different admissions variables can predict success along each dimension. Our findings suggest that different predictors are more or less useful depending on the form of success being forecasted. This research contributes to scholarship on talent assessment, educational outcomes, and leadership development, while offering practical guidance for MBA admissions officers seeking to select high-potential candidates across multiple domains of performance.
