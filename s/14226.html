Knowing Who Feels What: Building Emotion-Related Transactive Memory in Teams

Authors: Jigyasu Shukla North Carolina Agricultural and Technical State University; Dana Joseph University of Central Florida; Nikhil Awasty University of New Hampshire; Brent Scott Michigan State University

Session overview

Teams rely on transactive memory systems (TMS) to coordinate “who knows what,” yet existing work has primarily treated that knowledge as task-focused. This leaves a core feature of team functioning under-theorized: how teams build shared knowledge about members’ emotional tendencies and how that knowledge shapes whether teams get along as well as get ahead. We address this gap by developing and testing emotion-related transactive memory systems (ETMS), a shared system through which teams encode, store, and retrieve emotion-relevant knowledge about members’ emotional strengths, vulnerabilities, triggers, and regulation tendencies. ETMS reflects both consistent knowledge of “who responds how” and an emotion-focused division of labor that allocates emotion work to the right members. Drawing on the emotions-as-social-information perspective, we propose that ETMS develops through team relational information exchange that includes explicit discussion of feelings and tacit emotional cues in everyday interaction. We further theorize that this developmental process depends on the team’s emotional context: emotional display rules shape the social costs of emotional expression, whereas team emotion perception ability shapes how accurately members detect and interpret emotional cues. We test these predictions in a two-wave field study of 74 student project teams working together over a semester. Results support the proposed link between relational information exchange and ETMS and show that this link is stronger in teams higher in emotion perception ability. Emotional display rules do not reliably attenuate the development of ETMS. ETMS, in turn, predicts higher team satisfaction and stronger task performance, above and beyond task-related TMS. These findings extend TMS theory by introducing an affective form of “who knows what” and by identifying relational interaction and emotion perception capability as key ingredients for building emotion-relevant team knowledge that matters for both affective and instrumental outcomes.
