GAI and Professional Investors’ Information Processing: Evidence from GAI Platform’s Upgrade

TIM
Zhixiang Rong (Tsinghua University); JIAN XUE (Tsinghua University)
Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) is transforming information processing in capital markets, yet how it affects institutional investors’ traditional information activities remains unclear. Unlike physical cost-reducing or new data supply technologies, GAI represents a distinct technological shock, which is an information processing capability-enhancing technology. This study exploits a major GAI platform upgrade in mid-2024 as a quasi-natural experiment to examine its impact on institutional investors’ site visits. Leveraging one GAI platform query data, we find that GAI upgrade significantly promotes site visits, supporting complementarity rather than substitution. Mechanism tests reveal “functional differentiation”: GAI queries increased most for finance and stock topics while site visits increased most for business, technology, and industry topics, which reflects GAI’s capability boundaries between processing public information and acquiring private information. The effect is stronger for companies with higher disclosure quality and greater organizational complexity. GAI application also increases institutional ownership and reduces post-earnings-announcement drift. This study reveals a “resource release to resource reallocation” mechanism through which cognitive-enhancing technology stimulates private information demand, providing new insights for understanding AI’s impact on investor behavior.

Profiting from AI Innovation

TIM
Senem Aydin Ozden (Bayes Business School); Raffaele Conti (ESSEC Business School); Pooyan Khashabi (ESSEC Business School)
Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a distinct form of general-purpose technology (GPT), introducing novel challenges for value capture by innovators. Building on the profiting from innovation (PFI) framework, this study examines how the unique characteristics of AI—its data dependency and its generative capabilities—reshape traditional value appropriation mechanisms. Unlike prior GPTs, AI’s performance is intricately tied to access to public or proprietary training data and often improves through user interactions, complicating the vertical boundaries of AI firms. We theorize how these traits alter the strategic positioning of AI innovators along the value chain, particularly in relation to upstream data source owners and downstream users. We develop a two-dimensional typology based on data dependency (public vs. private) and generativity (predictive AI vs. generative AI), yielding four idealized business models that delineate the horizontal scope of AI innovations and the vertical scope of AI innovators. Our framework reveals how value capture is driven by the interplay between data access, the nature of the AI model, and the AI innovators’ vertical and horizontal scope decisions. We contribute to the innovation literature by adapting the PFI framework to the AI context and clarifying the strategic trade-offs in capturing value from AI-based GPTs. The study offers actionable insights for AI innovators on managing data assets and integration strategies to enhance appropriability in rapidly evolving AI markets.

Seeing the Possibilities: How Firm Knowledge Shapes Perceived Affordances

TIM
Siyi Shen (Zhejiang University); Linan Lei (Zhejiang University)
A persistent puzzle in digital innovation is why firms, facing the same powerful technologies like Artificial Intelligence (AI), pursue radically different innovation paths and achieve heterogeneous outcomes. Existing literature rooted in resource-based or dynamic capability perspectives falls short by usually treating technology’s potential as objectively given. This study advances a cognitive, knowledge-based view, arguing that the divergence in innovation begins at the microfoundation: the subjective perception of affordances - the action “possibilities” that firms believe a technology offers. We argue that what a firm “sees” in a technology is systematically shaped by its unique knowledge accumulated from prior innovation. Analyzing a panel of 961 Chinese high-technology firms from 2017 to 2022, we find that the diversity of a firm’s domain knowledge has an inverted U-shaped relationship with perceived affordances, while architectural knowledge has a consistently positive effect. These perceived affordances, in turn, fully mediate the link between firm knowledge and innovation effort. Futhermore, we find that the value of domain knowledge is reshaped by the context: market turbulence steepens the inverted U-curve, whereas technological turbulence flattens it. This study contributes to understanding innovation heterogeneity by integrating a cognitive lens with the knowledge-based view. We demonstrate that a firm’s perception of technology affordance is shaped by its unique knowledge, a process that is also contingent on environmental turbulence.

Too Much of a Good Thing? AI Engagement, High-Growth Firms, and Innovation Capability

TIM
Chao Zhang (Northumbria University); Yuqing Xiao (University of Barcelona); Abdullah Al Mamun (Birmingham Business School); Meng Xing (Northumbria University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) has become a critical driver of firm growth, yet its effects remain theoretically ambiguous. Drawing on a dynamic capability perspective, this study examines how AI engagement shapes the likelihood of becoming a high-growth firm (HGF) using panel data from Chinese publicly listed firms. Specifically, the results support our arguments that AI engagement exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with high-growth outcomes. At low to moderate levels, AI engagement enhances productivity, improves managerial decision-making, and generates positive signaling effects, thereby increasing firms’ growth potential. However, excessive AI engagement yields diminishing returns as over-automation undermines human collaboration, suppresses creativity, and weakens customer relationships, ultimately constraining sustained growth. We further identify firm innovation capability as a key boundary condition. Less innovative firms benefit more from early AI engagement but suffer greater performance declines at high levels of AI use, whereas innovative firms are less reliant on AI initially and better able to mitigate the negative effects of excessive AI engagement.
