Political Control in the Workplace: How Autocrats Use Firms to Discipline Citizens

OMT
Ye Zhang (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
How do authoritarian regimes maintain control as their private sectors grow? While existing research emphasizes control mechanisms outside the private sector such as security forces and surveillance, this paper examines how control extends from within firms. Focusing on China, I study the expansion of Communist Party cells inside private enterprises and their effects on firms and employees. An original panel of 1,208 publicly listed private enterprises (2012- 2022) shows that while party cells have expanded rapidly, they exert little influence on firm behavior. Yet employee-level evidence tells a different story. Using original surveys, survey experiments, and fieldwork, I find that even weak and ritualized party cells heighten the visibility of the regime inside the workplace, increase perceived political risks, and induce self-censorship and conformity. Survey experiments show that merely priming the presence of a party cell suppresses dissent in workplace discussions. These findings highlight a market-compatible form of authoritarian control: targeting individuals, not business, and ruling through presence, not punishment.

Not All Political Ties Are Equal: Guanxi, Political Potential, Local Embeddedness, and Investment

OMT
Haoning Wang (IESE Business School)
Are all political ties equally influential in shaping corporate behavior? Drawing on a unique dataset of 3,342 face-to-face meetings between municipal party secretaries and Chinese listed firms, we examine two dimensions of politicians’ political capital: political “stock” (political potential) and local “conversion” capacity (local embeddedness). We find that meetings are generally followed by higher firm capital expenditures, but the magnitude of this response depends strongly on the combination of star status and local embeddedness. Locally embedded secretaries are associated with the most robust increases in investment, whereas star officials without local roots exert weaker and less consistent influence. Comparing ownership types, state-owned enterprises respond most strongly to locally embedded officials, while private firms are especially sensitive to marginal advantages in politically disadvantaged situations, reacting more sharply when meetings involve relatively more favorable political partners. To summarize, these findings show that political ties differ systematically in how much they can be converted into real investment, highlighting the importance of who firms meet and where those officials are embedded for understanding corporate political behavior in emerging markets.

Relational Strategies of Resilience:Underground Journals in the Soviet Union (1956-1990)

OMT
Jose Luis Estevez (University of Helsinki); Stoyan Vassilev Sgourev (New Bulgarian University)
To reinforce the concept of resilience, we develop a processual-relational approach with three key dimensions: time, absorption-adaptation and (relational) strategies. We explore the use of relational strategies by actors to pursue simultaneously imperatives of stability and adaptation, while managing tensions between them. Our research context is underground (“samizdat”) journals in the Soviet Union, where a community of practice emerged in the 1960’s to organize self-publication. Resilience is the dynamic capability to withstand repressive pressure by the state through enduring operations. Our dataset catalogs 304 journals founded between 1956 and 1986. The results of Cox and ERGM regressions enable the identification of three relational strategies. Reliability, manifested as a strong tendency for thematic homophily and scarcity in ties between politically oriented and non-political journals. Redundancy, displayed in the positive effect of publishing in more than one location on the probability of endurance. Reactivity transpires in a large network of editors, shifting responsibilities across it in response to security threats. By analyzing the relational duality of resilience and providing a methodological template, we promote the broader use of the concept in organization studies.

Building Media in Authoritarian Country. Organizational Identity Formation Under Pressure

OMT
Denis Borodin (emlyon Business School); Saulo Barbosa (emlyon business school)
This study investigates how independent media organizations (IMOs) form and adapt their organizational identities under the escalating pressures of authoritarian repression. Drawing on a grounded, longitudinal multiple-case study of six Russian IMOs operating between 2012 and 2025, we develop a process model that illustrates how targeted and dynamic state actions ranging from legal constraints and market manipulation to stigmatization and outright repression systematically constrained identity enactment opportunities. Our findings show that IMOs initially attempted to navigate repression through niche strategies and partial alignment with state expectations, balancing commercial, social, and political identity features. However, as repression intensified and identity enactment became increasingly untenable, organizations were forced to converge around an oppositional identity defined more by resistance to state-imposed boundaries than by proactive strategic differentiation. We contribute to the literature on authoritarian institutional contexts, organizational identity, and resilience by theorizing identity formation as a dynamic, coercion-driven process shaped by a powerful external actor. In doing so, we challenge existing models that assume open or competitive environments and highlight the adaptive cognitive processes organizations employ to preserve identity amid unpredictable and adversarial conditions.

Legitimation Through Identity Brokerage: State Influencers Navigate Competing Institutional Logics

OMT
Elton Li-Yi (University of Wisconsin-Madison); Huiyang Dai (Zhejiang University of Technology)
In questioning the dominant wisdom that compromising and transforming organizational identity amid conflicts of multiple institutional logics, this study introduces identity brokerage as an alternative mechanism that enables such organizations (i.e., state-run media) to preserve their stable and unalterable identity and gain legitimacy from diverse audiences while confronting the competing institutional logics between government and market. Drawing on videos between July 2024 and June 2025 from 33 state-run media influencers operating on Bilibili, a leading Chinese digital platform, we quantitatively found that brokering through independent influencers enhances audience attention (i.e., views), while brokering through official-affiliated influencers expands both audience attention and audience base (i.e., followers). Furthermore, topic similarity strengthens the effect of identity brokerage on audience growth but not on audience attention. By offering new insights into how state-owned organizations under responsive authoritarianism can navigate market-oriented platforms while upholding government-mandated identities, this paper contributes to institutional logics, organizational identity, platform-based propaganda, and category spanning.
