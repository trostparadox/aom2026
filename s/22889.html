Unpacking the Unethical Consequences of Leader Voice Endorsement

OB
Wenjia Zhang (Beijing Jiaotong University); Meihan Liu (Dalian University of Technology); Mengting Zhu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Although leader voice endorsement is generally regarded as a desirable leadership behavior, it remains unclear when and whether such endorsement may entail ethical risks. Drawing on social exchange theory, we examine both the positive and negative effects of leader voice endorsement on employee unethical behavior, contingent on employees’ levels of narcissism. We collected matched supervisor–employee data from 290 dyads across three companies in Shanghai, China. The empirical results reveal a dual-path pattern: when employee narcissism is low (vs. high), leader voice endorsement is negatively related to unethical behavior via increased gratitude; conversely, when employee narcissism is high (vs. low), voice endorsement is positively related to unethical behavior through heightened psychological entitlement. This study offers a more balanced and nuanced framework for understanding when and how leader voice endorsement can lead to unethical outcomes.

A Multi-Study Investigation of Prohibitive Voice and Leader Interpersonal Behaviors

OB
Jingxian Yao (Singapore University of Social Sciences); Noriko Tan (National University of Singapore)
Research on how leaders respond to employee voice has primarily focused on performance appraisal and voice endorsement as outcomes, overlooking leader interpersonal behaviors toward voicing employees. Moreover, how prohibitive (vs. promotive) voice may trigger unique leader responses is underexplored. In this research, we integrate self-enhancement, social identity, and implicit followership theories and propose that employee prohibitive voice, more so than promotive voice, triggers leader perceived threat. Perceived threat, in turn, is positively related to leader incivility and negatively related to leader citizenship behavior. Further, we propose that employee emotional intelligence, which helps regulate one’s own emotions and appraise others’ emotions, mitigates the relationship between employee prohibitive voice and leader perceived threat. We present three methodologically complementary studies (Study 1, a multi-wave, multi-source field study; Study 2, a vignette study; Study 3, a recall-based critical incident study) that test our theoretical model. The studies provide convergent support for the hypotheses, substantiating the threatening nature of prohibitive voice and drawing attention to the concerning phenomenon that voicing employees may be mistreated by leaders. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the findings.

Resolving the Paradox of Employee Voice Through the Quality of Interpersonal Relationships

OB
Emmanuel Kwasi Mawuena
The quality of relationships varies significantly in collectivist cultures, where affection, trust, and cooperation differ sharply depending on who is involved. Yet, employee voice research has largely overlooked how such relational differences shape voice behaviour. This study examines how the quality of interpersonal Relationships (IPRs) (High Quality versus Poor Quality) influence voice behaviour, including enacting quality of voice towards endorsement or rejection within surgical hierarchies. Drawing on thematic analysis of 57 in-depth interviews with multidisciplinary surgical professionals in a West African country, the study found that the quality of IPRs fundamentally shapes mutual care, respect, and goodwill, creating contrasting conditions for self-worth, psychological safety, and cooperation that determine whether individuals speak up or remain silent, the quality of voice and whether voice is endorsed or rejected. It further demonstrates that high-quality IPRs through intermediaries are strategically used in navigating voice barriers, enabling voice in otherwise restrictive contexts. By illuminating a deeper the relational dynamics underlying the paradox of employee voice in collectivist cultures, this study highlights the critical role of fostering respectful and trusted relationships in enabling effective voice.

Voice in Employee-Customer Settings: A Process Model of Self-Presentation, Framing and Resonance

OB
Audrey Bélanger (HEC Montréal)
Despite the prominence of the service sector and the critical role of voice behavior in organizations, our understanding of employee voice within boundary-spanning interactions remains limited. Drawing on Goffman’s impression management and frame analysis, we examine how employee-customer voicing unfolds in service-oriented contexts, where frontline service employees (FSEs) must proactively align customers’ needs with organizational expectations. To this end, we first highlight key self-presentation goals (i.e., warmth and competence) that shape voice behavior. We then propose a theory of voice resonance, which explores whether the ideas or concerns expressed by FSEs are perceived to meet the audience’s own interpretations. Additionally, we consider how the framing of ideas or concerns is critical to the FSE’s sense of resonance with the customer. Altogether, we expand the conceptualization of voice, highlighting the role of duality of interactional strategies and social judgment when speaking up, while pointing out avenues for future research.

Voice as a Developmental Experience: An Episodic-Relational Framework

OB
Jinhwan Jo (Wright State University); Rachel E. Sturm (Wright State University); Matthew Deeg (Abilene Christian University)
Employee voice has been widely studied for its organizational benefits and for instrumental individual outcomes. Yet we know far less about voice as a developmental experience for employees themselves. We reconceptualize voice as a developmental experience by treating each speaking up instance as a temporally unfolding episode comprising preparation, expression, observation of others’ reactions, and reflection. Integrating this episodic view with self-determination theory, we propose that each voice episode constitutes a micro-context in which employees’ basic needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness may be satisfied or thwarted, yielding personal growth or developmental stagnation. We further argue that relational resources—informational, emotional, instrumental, and appraisal support—operate as cross-cutting moderating conditions that shape how employees prepare for voice, enact agency under risk, interpret feedback, and make meaning from their experiences. Over time, repeated cycles of need-satisfying voice episodes promote re-engagement and consolidate into a voice identity, while repeated need-thwarting experiences can crystallize a silence identity. By shifting attention from instrumental outcomes to employees’ developmental trajectories through voice, this framework extends voice research, applies self-determination theory to episodic discretionary behavior, and clarifies how personal growth through speaking up is socially co-constructed in relationships.
