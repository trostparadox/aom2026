The Creativity Conundrum In Health Care And The Bridges And Beams For Overcoming It

OB
Yuna Lee (Columbia University in the City of New York); Ingrid Nembhard (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania)
Despite concerted efforts, health care providers persistently struggle to provide good patient care experiences (PX). A now-prominent suggestion to improve PX is to harness the workforce’s creative (i.e., novel and useful) ideas. Our 18-month study of 12 health centers’ experiences with 220 staff-improvement ideas suggests that this strategy faces a creativity conundrum, a term that we introduce to capture the observation that ideas with greater creativity are associated with better PX outcomes but less likely to be implemented. Creative ideas that are implemented have two features. First, they are “bridge” ideas that bring standardization of care and customization to patient needs, a notorious tension and goal in health care. Second, their implementation is enhanced by certain structures for both personnel and process management that serve as support “beams.” These discoveries demonstrate the merit of creative ideas for health care improvement, the conundrum these ideas face, and conditions under which they overcome their conundrum. Health care, a context where tensions like standardization-customization exist, thus offers a revealing lens into the value of creativity in complex service settings and the value of structure for variance-inducing organizational activities. We discuss implications of our discoveries for practice and research on creativity and idea implementation.

Can Innovative Behavior Enrich Employees’ Regulatory Resource Capacity?

OB
Thomas Ng (The University of Hong Kong); Lawrance Yuejun CAI (The University of Hong Kong)
Our scholarly understanding of the outcomes of innovative behavior is rather limited, as only a small number of studies have examined these consequences thus far. In these studies, a resource depletion view has typically been adopted, with researchers proposing that innovative behavior depletes employee resources to the point of creating stress. In this research, we offer an opposing perspective based on self-determination theory. We suggest that innovative behavior can in fact enrich rather than deplete employees’ regulatory resource capacity, and that this enrichment strengthens employees’ work vigor, improves their job performance, and promotes their organizational citizenship behavior. Data collected from (a) 265 employee-supervisor dyads in China at four time points (separated by 2-week intervals) and (b) a scenario experiment converged to provide preliminary support for this proposed resource enrichment view of innovative behavior. Crucially, the personality traits of agreeableness and openness to experience jointly moderated the proposed mechanism: the positive effects of innovative behavior on regulatory resource capacity were stronger for low-agreeableness and high-openness individuals. This result suggests that some people are more likely than others to experience innovative behavior as resource-enriching, a distinction that is crucial in determining whether the long-term outcomes for an organization will be positive or negative.

Searching Problem Formulations Under Member Uncertainty in Creative Problem Solving

OB
Ryo Taniguchi (Meiji University); Naoki Takada (Hitotsubashi University); Takehiko Murai (Waseda University); Toshio Murase
As organizations increasingly rely on ad hoc and self-managing teams to solve ill-defined and novel problems, understanding how teams search for workable problem formulations is critical. Prior research emphasizes objective constraints but offers limited insight into how perceptions of teammates shape collective search and creative outcomes. We examine how perceived ability and perceived effort function as subjective constraints that structure how teams explore the problem space, coordinate their work, and generate creative solutions. We conducted a laboratory experiment in which expertise-differentiated teams solved a creative task, combining experimental manipulations with behavioral trace to track early exploration, interdependence, and division-of-labor. Results show that perceived effort governs the breadth of search by influencing early exploration and the acceptance of interdependence, whereas perceived ability governs the stability of problem formulation by shaping how persistently teams refine a chosen approach. Early exploration predicts success when effort is doubted, whereas formulation stability predicts success when ability is doubted. Among successful teams, higher perceived effort is associated with rarer discoveries, whereas lower perceived effort with unusual combinations. These findings reconceptualize creative problem solving as search across problem formulations under subjective constraints and demonstrate how perceptions become microfoundations of team dynamics.

Seeking Creative Explorers: How Dispelling Genius Myths Enhances Organizational Recruiting

OB
Grusha Agarwal (University of Toronto)
Organizations increasingly seek creative talent, yet little is known about how creativity is defined and communicated at the point of recruitment. We develop a theory of recruitment language as a cultural signal that conveys lay beliefs about the nature of creativity, which, in turn shapes who sees themselves as a fit for creative roles. Drawing on research on lay theories, we distinguish between two conceptions of creativity embedded in organizational discourse: a dominant creative genius view that portrays creativity as rare, innate, and internal to the self, and an alternative creative explorer view that frames creativity as iterative, effortful, and external to the self. Across a multi-method set of studies, we show that, though genius-oriented language is more prevalent than is explorer-oriented language, genius (vs. explorer language) language can deter applicants by signaling narrow and exclusionary expectations about who is creative. Together, these findings suggest that framing creativity as a process of exploration, rather than innate brilliance, has broad appeal and reshapes who is drawn to creative roles. By theorizing creativity-specific recruitment language as a mechanism through which beliefs about human potential are conveyed, our research advances understanding of creativity, recruitment, and organizational signaling.
