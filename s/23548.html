Session overview

This session centers the lived experiences of Black professionals and entrepreneurs as they navigate authenticity, tokenism, code-switching, and collective coping. The papers show how racial inequality is experienced not only through formal exclusion, but also through pressures to self-manage, perform, and protect oneself in environments shaped by bias. Collectively, they foreground the emotional and identity-related costs of visibility in organizational life.

Obligatory Inauthenticity: The Impact of Authenticity on Black Technology Workers

DEI
Brian Weathersby
Obligatory Inauthenticity: The Impact of Authenticity on Black Technology Workers Abstract Evidence presents a connection between inclusion, belonging and authenticity. This study explored authenticity, cognitive dissonance, and double consciousness with the experiences of African American technology workers to introduce the concept of Obligatory Inauthenticity (OI). OI, or the feeling of being obliged to be inauthentic, moves the conversation regarding how authenticity impacts workplaces to a focus on how to leverage authenticity to improve performance. Although this study focused on Black technology workers, the concept of OI potentially applies to other populations. The general problem was that research has shown employee inauthenticity leads to lower motivation and engagement and higher burnout. The specific problem was that Black technology workers potentially feel inhibited from being their authentic selves in their workplaces, reducing employee retention. Examining how authenticity, cognitive dissonance, and double consciousness impede Black technology workers from advancing or staying in the technology field contributes to leveling Black technology worker representation in technology jobs especially at the underrepresented executive levels. Grounded theory served as the methodology for this study. Triangulation, memoing, and a focus group, maintained quality and credibility. Findings included OI had a connection to authenticity, cognitive dissonance, and double consciousness. Emergent theories included cultural stigmas, preincident viewpoints, postincident viewpoints, organizational culture impact, Black tax, and Black trauma. Keywords: Obligatory Inauthenticity, authenticity, cognitive dissonance, double consciousness, cultural stigmas, preincident viewpoint, postincident viewpoint, organizational culture impact, Black tax, and Black trauma

Racialized Tokenism: A Qualitative Interpretive Meta-synthesis of Black Tokens’ Experiences

DEI
Marla L. White (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Ifeyimika O. Ajaiyeoba (University of Wisconsin-Parkside); Joy Obioma Ibedionu (The University of Texas at Arlington); Myrtle P. Bell (The University of Texas at Arlington)
Black Americans are often the only ones or among the few in their organizations, relegating them to a token status (or demographically atypical compared to their peers), which may be consequential for individual and organizational outcomes. While management scholars have taken an interest in studying tokens’ experiences, research centered on Black people’s token (or other) experiences is scarce. Using a qualitative interpretive meta-synthesis (QIMS) approach and Kanter’s (1977) tokenism framework, we examine the lived experiences of Black tokens. QIMS enables the synthesis of data collected across previously published articles on Black tokens’ lived experiences, fostering the integration of siloed knowledge into a broader, deeper perspective. Our analysis of fifteen articles involving Black professional women and men (N = 431) across industries (e.g., Corporate Sector, Healthcare, Higher Education) and occupations (e.g., C-suite executives, Physicians, Deans) provides a synergistic perspective not apparent in any individual article. Drawing on Black professionals’ own narratives across the synthesized studies, our findings reveal five main themes—tokenism experiences (per Kanter, 1977), constrained authority and emotional labor, symbolic representation and discrimination, agency and active resistance strategies, and bittersweet experiences. Through these themes, we uncover the persistent negative experiences of Black men and women in the workplace. We conclude with a discussion of research implications.

Black Entrepreneurs’ Stereotype Threat, Racial Code-Switching, and Psychological Outcomes

DEI
Tanja R. Darden (Towson University); Ashley N. Robinson (Towson University)
Entrepreneurship is an avenue for employment creation, innovation, and economic growth. However, Black entrepreneurs have long faced unique challenges in the pursuit of successful financial performance. Some entrepreneurs may engage in racial code-switching to deal with the challenges of being a Black entrepreneur in a predominantly White economy. This study focuses on how threats to identity, i.e., stereotype threat have relationships with authenticity and well-being via racial code-switching. Additionally, we examine whether the entrepreneur’s racial ideology (i.e., nationalist or assimilation) affects the relationships between code-switching and authenticity and wellbeing. We conducted a time-lagged survey to collect data. Our analyses show evidence that the entrepreneur’s ideology influences predicted relationships. Contributions, limitations, and future research opportunities are discussed.

Crafting Relational Identity: How Black Lives Matter Affected Craft Entrepreneurs

DEI
Ace Beorchia (Kennesaw State University); David Gras (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville)
How do social movements reshape the day-to-day decisions of craft entrepreneurs who are not direct movement targets, but still affected by the movement’s grievances? In this study, we examine how the Black Lives Matter (BLM) movement spilled into the U.S. fiber craft industry (e.g, knitting, crochet, yarn) and how craft entrepreneurs responded. Using an abductive grounded constructivist approach, we conducted 36 semi-structured interviews with 21 entrepreneurs in two waves—supplemented with social media data and participant-observation. We develop a process model showing how movement spillovers trigger personal and relational identity work, which shape strategic responses. While white entrepreneurs typically experienced modest changes, Black entrepreneurs faced more volatile consequences—including rapid growth, harassment, intensified emotional labor, and performative support. Our study contributes to research on social movement spillovers by theorizing their microfoundations in entrepreneurship, highlighting identity work as a strategic response and deepens understanding of underrepresented entrepreneurship by documenting the unequal burdens placed marginalized entrepreneurs during a social movement.
