Technological Empowerment, Vulnerability, and Intimacy in the Use of Smart Assistive Technologies

CTO
Eleunthia Wong Ellinger (Stockholm School of Economics); Elma Thorarinsson (Rotterdam School of Management); Magnus Mahring (Stockholm School of Economics); Rebecka Cedering Ångström (Stockholm School of Economics)
A new class of embodied technologies, defined as software or devices that aid individuals to increase, maintain, or improve their functional capabilities, is starting to blend or even fuse human cognition with technological affordances, promising to assist and enhance the lives of individuals. These devices have started to blend or even fuse human cognition with technological affordances, creating new levels of both empowerment and dependency as their use becomes increasingly interwoven into the fabric of the user's social life. We argue that existing conceptualizations of IS use need to be extended to account for the intricate ways in which both individual identity and social interaction of users are affected through the use of these devices. Theoretical development in this context could be foundational for advancing knowledge in the future use of brain-computer interfaces and AI integration in assistive and enhancing cognitive devices. Our qualitative study focuses on users and developers of smart assistive technology and devices for communication, which provide those who cannot physically speak with the means to communicate. We present a process model of technological intimacy, contending that this novel IS use pattern is habitually in a state of adaptation with continuous coping processes in ongoing cycles of human-technology engagement.

Beyond Trustless Systems: An Integrative Review and Framework of Trust in Blockchain Ecosystems

CTO
Tariku Ayana Abdi; Hannah Bensussan (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Carlotta Cochis (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Paula Ungureanu (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Elisa Villani (University of Bologna)
Blockchain has been described both as a trustless technology and as a trust machine, yet research still lacks a systematic understanding of how trust is built and sustained in blockchain ecosystems. Drawing on a systematic review of 111 peer-reviewed studies, this paper maps the antecedents, processes, and outcomes of trust in blockchain systems. We show that trust is simultaneously directed at technological, human, and institutional targets and fueled by economic, psychological, and social motives leading to outcomes such as adoption, performance, participation, and satisfaction. However, current research often treats these dimensions in isolation, overlooking how trust unfolds across layers and over time. Through an analytical synthesis of the literature, we identify four mechanisms, reification, distribution, disintermediation, and inscription, that describe how trust circulates and transforms within blockchains. We conceptualize their interplay as multiplex blockchain trust, a recursive process through which technological, interpersonal, and institutional assurances reinforce and reshape one another. This framework advances a dynamic and integrative understanding of trust as a sociotechnical process, offering a foundation for future research on the governance and evolution of blockchains.

Social Media and Governance Hegemony in Reducing DAO Volatility

CTO
Mamadou Dosso (University of Southampton); PK Senyo (University of Southampton Business School); Ishmael Tingbani (University of Southampton)
The decentralised autonomous organisation (DAO) phenomenon has emerged as a combination of social media, decentralised governance, and automation, empowering token-holders to monetise their business ventures. However, the lack of a risk assessment framework gives a limited understanding of what measurements can constitute a reliable benchmark, putting token-holder investments at risk due to the high token volatility. Furthermore, little research has investigated the concurrent role of social media and token wealth inequality on DAO volatility. Drawing on herding behaviour and equity theories, this research empirically analyses the impact of social media dominance and the Gini index, two non-traditional performance indicators, on DAO volatility reduction. We combine a unique dataset using Santiment and Sentora, two crypto analytics platforms powered by AI-based machine learning, totalling 76 DAOs, and spanning from 2015 to 2024, for the GMM estimation. We find that a strong social media dominance in the crypto media sphere and some degree of wealth inequality significantly reduce DAO volatility. Additionally, we find evidence of a strong normative social media influence pattern that token-holders follow to make informed investment decisions. Our paper presents new empirical evidence and associated theoretical explanations for selected non-traditional indicators as essential benchmarks for developing a DAO risk assessment framework.

Speculative Futures and Stagnant Realities: Prospective Theorizing in Blockchain Adoption

CTO
Nicole Mäkineste (Durham University); Spyros Angelopoulos (Durham University)
Blockchain applications continue to be portrayed by the academic literature as transformative technological innovations capable of reshaping industries and governance models. Evidence from practice, however, reveals persistent challenges in achieving real-world adoption of such applications. We investigate the gap between the imaginative promise of blockchain applications as portrayed by the academic literature as opposed to their materialization in practice. Drawing upon the framework of prospective theorizing, we assess the speculative rigor of the academic discourse on blockchain applications by examining 138 review articles published between 2009 and 2025. Our methodological approach combines sentiment analysis, automated classification of adoption-maturity descriptions, and systematic verification of the operational status of cited real-world blockchain applications. We find that academic sentiment remains persistently neutral-to-optimistic, while descriptions of maturity overwhelmingly situate blockchain applications in early conceptual or design stages, with no temporal progression toward operational deployment. Moreover, we identify that many frequently cited exemplars of successful adoption have since ceased their operations. These patterns reveal a structural misalignment between scholarly narratives and empirical evidence, resulting in homogeneous, weakly grounded future imaginaries that offer limited guidance for practice. We argue that advancing speculative rigour in blockchain applications research requires stronger contextual embeddedness, empirical verification, and imaginative plurality to ensure that academic futuring remains credible, responsible, and socially relevant.
