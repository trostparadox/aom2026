The Impact of Daily Stock Gains and Losses on Employees’ Risk-Related Behaviors at Work

OB
Joseph Kim (University of Illinois at Chicago); Kyoung Yong Kim (Villanova University); Ran Ahn (University of Illinois at Chicago); Youngduk Lee (The University of Hong Kong)
Despite the widespread accessibility of stock trading, research has rarely examined how fluctuations in employees’ personal stock holdings shape their daily experiences at work. Integrating cross-domain spillover and regulatory focus theories, we propose that daily gains and losses in personal stock holdings spill over into the workplace by influencing employees’ risk propensity—their willingness to take or avoid risks—and, in turn, their risk-related behaviors. In a preliminary experiment, we show that stock gains (vs. losses) elicit promotion (vs. prevention) focus, which subsequently increases (vs. decreases) risk propensity. Building on this mechanism, a 10-day experience sampling study of full-time employees who actively invest in the South Korean stock market revealed that daily increases in stock value predicted higher state risk propensity, which led to greater risk-taking and voice behavior but less expectancy-driven behavior. These indirect effects were moderated by long-term orientation: employees lower (vs. higher) in long-term orientation showed greater day-to-day sensitivity to stock fluctuations. Together, these findings reveal that even short-term daily market movements can shape how employees think and act at work, highlighting personal financial experiences as an overlooked source of daily behavioral variability.

Promotion Focus, Growth I-deals, and Service Creativity: A Regulatory Focus Perspective

OB
Lingyi Wang (Macau University of Science and Technology); Li-Yun Sun (Macau University of Science and Technology); Wen Pan
In view of mixed effects of idiosyncratic deals (i-deals) across studies in i-deals literature, this study aims to address the issue by assessing the motivation of the i-deals to accurately delineate the effects of i-deals. Drawing on regulatory focus theory and ability-motivation-opportunity (AMO) framework, the study examined antecedents and outcomes of growth i-deals and their mechanisms. We collected multi-wave, multi-source and multi-level data from 600 employees in 100 teams in China and tested a cross-level moderated mediation model, yielding four major findings. First, promotion focus positively relates to growth i-deals. Second, growth i-deals have positive impact on three AMO variables (creative self-efficacy, intrinsic motivation and perceived participation opportunity) that further lead to employee service creativity. Third, servant leadership moderates the main effect of promotion focus on growth i-deals. Finally, servant leadership moderates the indirect effect of promotion focus on service creativity through the chain mediating effects of growth i-deals and three AMO variables. The main and indirect effects are stronger for high level of servant leadership. The study contributes to i-deals and service creativity literature.

Customers’ (Un-)Just Social Comparisons, Employees’ Stress, (Co)-Rumination & Job Satisfaction

OB
Philipp Schreder (Seeburg Castle University); Clemens Hutzinger (Seeburg Castle University); Zofia Laub; Melanie Bowen
Although employees regularly compare themselves to each other, there is little literature on customer-induced social comparisons of employees. Results from a qualitative pre-study (n1 = 8), two online experiments (n2 = 394; n3 = 357) and an online survey (n4 = 1.531) demonstrate that such comparisons have a significant impact on frontline employees’ job satisfaction. Specifically, downward comparisons (i.e., being compared with a worse-performing colleague) lead to greater job satisfaction than upward comparisons (i.e., being compared with a better-performing colleague). The underlying mechanism is as follows: Upward – as compared to downward – social comparisons lead to higher hindrance stress. The effect of social comparisons is stronger, when customers’ assessment of frontline employees’ competence is just, as compared to unjust. Furthermore, hindrance stress has a positive effect on frontline employees’ rumination and co-rumination. Most importantly, while rumination has a negative effect on job satisfaction, co-rumination has a positive effect on job satisfaction. Implications for theory and practical recommendations for companies high in customer contact are provided.

Share or Hide? Dual Pathways Between Leadership Succession and Employee Knowledge Hiding

OB
Bao Wu; Wenxin Huang (Zhejiang University of Technology); Linyun Chai (Zhejiang University of Technology); Jiaqi Huang; Luke Zhu (York University)
Leadership succession is a consequential organizational event that prompts employees to reassess their work environment and adjust their psychological orientations and behavioral strategies. Drawing on the perceived uncertainty framework and regulatory focus theory, we develop and test a dual-path model explaining how leadership succession both suppresses and amplifies employee knowledge hiding directed toward the leader. Across two scenario-based experiments (Studies 1 and 2) and a two-wave field study (Study 3), we find that leadership succession directly reduces employees’ knowledge hiding toward the leader. At the same time, leadership succession increases employees’ perceived uncertainty, which in turn weakens promotion focus and strengthens prevention focus, ultimately heightening knowledge hiding toward the leader. Moreover, digital performance appraisal moderates the effect of leadership succession on perceived uncertainty and, in turn, the indirect pathway linking leadership succession to knowledge hiding via perceived uncertainty and regulatory focus. Specifically, when digital performance appraisal is high, the positive indirect effect of leadership succession on knowledge hiding through perceived uncertainty and regulatory focus is attenuated. By showing that leadership succession simultaneously activates countervailing psychological processes, this research advances understanding of employee knowledge hiding, extends the perceived uncertainty framework and regulatory focus theory, and offers insights into leadership management under digital performance regimes.
