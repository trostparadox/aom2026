A High-Scale Assessment of Social Media and Mainstream Media in Scientific Communication

TIM
Yang Yang; Yuan (Tanya) Tian (New York University); Brian Uzzi (Northwestern University); Benjamin Jones (Kellogg School of Management, Northwestern University)
The rapid growth of social media and contraction of mainstream media raises fundamental questions about the nature of science that is reported to the public. Examining 20.9 million scientific publications, we compare research coverage in social media and mainstream media in a broad corpus of scientific work. We find substantial shifts in the scale, impact, and heterogeneity of scientific coverage. First, social media significantly alters what science is, and is not, covered. Mainstream media accentuates eminence in the coverage of science and focuses on a limited set of fields. By contrast, social media reports research more evenly across fields, institutions of all prestige, journal rankings, and the demography of scholars while increasing the scale of scientific ideas covered more than eightfold. Second, despite concerns about the misrepresentation of science in social media, we find that social media typically covers works that are impactful and novel within science. Third, scientists on social media, as domain experts, tend to surface high-impact research in their own fields while sampling widely across research institutions. These findings reveal that social media expands and diversifies science reporting by highlighting high-impact research and bringing a broader array of scholars, institutions and scientific concepts into public view.

Citations in 280 characters: Is Social Media Presence Worth it for Top Journals?

TIM
Philipp Lucas Wähler (Warwick Business Schoool); Christos Kolympiris (Warwick Business School, U. of Warwick); Nicos Nicolaou (University of Warwick)
Is it worth it for leading journals to maintain an active social media presence? We address this question by leveraging the staggered adoption of Twitter by three top economics journals: Journal of Political Economy; Quarterly Journal of Economics; and Review of Economics and Statistics, to compare citation counts for promoted versus non-promoted articles. Our findings show that Twitter exposure increases citations by exposing research to audiences beyond the field of economics, thereby fostering interdisciplinarity. High-status authors benefit disproportionately, with tweets featuring visual aids further amplifying impact. These results highlight social media’s potential to enhance research dissemination, even for journals that already command the lion’s share of academic attention. The findings further indicate that social media promotion can advance interdisciplinarity while raising concerns about inequities in scholarly recognition.

Designing Research Funding in High-Pressure Systems: Result-Oriented Versus Process-Oriented Grant

TIM
BOYAN LIU (Tsinghua University); Yuchen Gao (Tsinghua University); Jing Zhao (Tsinghua University)
This study investigates how the micro-level design of public R&D funding influences scientists’ policy support within high-competition research environments. We distinguish between result-oriented designs (e.g., funding intensity, success rates) that define material opportunities, and process-oriented designs (e.g., application modes, evaluation rules) that define the administrative interface. Utilizing a conjoint experiment with 1,775 researchers under the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC), we find that scientists’ support is a strategic trade-off between expected utility and administrative burden. While increasing funding intensity and success rates significantly boosts support, streamlining processes—such as implementing pre-applications or limiting proposal length—also yields substantial positive effects. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that researchers facing career development pressure are more sensitive to process-oriented design, while researchers responsible for PhD students or more experienced with the application system are sensitive to both process-oriented and result-oriented design. These findings provide a strategic roadmap for funding agencies to optimize grant schemes and enhance the sustainability of scientific careers by balancing material incentives with administrative efficiency.

The Professional Costs of Speech Sanctioning Events: Evidence from Academia

TIM
Kris Gulati (University of California Berkeley); Lorenzo Palladini (Stockholm School of Economics)
Public controversies over employee speech where individuals face backlash for expressing views deemed controversial, unpopular, or misaligned with prevailing norms - sometimes colloquially referred to as “cancel culture” - have become increasingly prevalent. Yet, systematic evidence of their professional consequences remains limited. Leveraging a novel dataset of U.S.-based academics involved in such events, combined with bibliometric data, we employ a series of difference-in-differences designs to measure the effects on individuals’ productivity and recognition. We find that, following these events, affected scholars experience a decline in the recognition of their prior body of work, with citations declining by 4.8%, reflecting a form of peer-to-peer distancing. Furthermore, affected scholars experience a decline in their productivity, publishing 20% fewer papers compared to a comparable set of scholars. These citation declines are disproportionately driven by scholars who are closely connected to the affected individual, consistent with a mechanism of professional distancing. Our findings highlight the professional and career costs associated with speech sanctioning events.

Tracking Scientific Decoupling: Shifts in International Collaborations on Semiconductor Research

TIM
Lorenzo Palladini (Stockholm School of Economics); Valentina Tartari (Stockholm School of Economics)
International scientific collaboration is central to technological progress, yet it is increasingly shaped by geopolitical tensions and rising techno-nationalist policies. Despite growing attention, systematic evidence on how such policies affect international research collaborations and scientific outcomes remains limited. This paper examines how rising U.S.–China tensions have reshaped international research collaboration in semiconductors, a strategically critical and highly globalized technology. Using bibliometric data linked to patent citations, we track collaboration patterns, publication output, research quality, and applied relevance of semiconductor science before and after the escalation of U.S. techno-nationalist policies in 2019. We compare U.S.–China collaborations with other international partnerships and exploit heterogeneity in firms’ pre-existing reliance on Chinese collaborators. We find that U.S.– China research collaborations decline sharply after 2019 and that U.S. firms more exposed to the loss of Chinese partners experience persistent reductions in high-impact and application-relevant research, despite stable publication volumes. At the same time, Chinese institutions expand both the quantity and quality of semiconductor research. These findings suggest that techno-nationalist policies may have unintended consequences for domestic innovation capacity.
