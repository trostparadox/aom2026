Knowledge Protection and Modularity of Innovation Activities in Firms

STR
Eunkwang Seo (Oklahoma State University); Christopher P. Dinkel (Oklahoma State University)
Scholars have theoretically explored the relationship between knowledge protection and modularity of innovation activities in firms, but this relationship has yet to be empirically tested in the literature. Leveraging state-level staggered changes in U.S. trade secrecy law and using a network modularity measure, this paper examines how trade secret protection affects the modularity of inventor collaborations in firms. Analyzing intrafirm collaboration patterns in U.S. patent data from 1,022 high-tech firms between 1976 and 2017, we find that increased trade secret protection is negatively associated with the modularity of inventor collaborations within firms. This effect is more pronounced for firms with superior technologies and those geographically collocated with competitors—factors that enhance firms’ knowledge-spillover concerns––and less pronounced for firms in industries where alternative appropriability mechanisms are highly effective. Moreover, our findings suggest that the increased betweenness centrality of new hires (skilled and junior) serves as an important underlying mechanism for the treatment effect. These results contribute to the literature by illuminating how firms strategize their internal innovation activities for knowledge protection and by underscoring its underlying mechanism.

Anchors and Architects: The Microfoundations of Portfolio Structure in Knowledge Redeployment

STR
Alessio Delpero (Glion Institute of Higher Education)
Does the structural configuration of firm innovation portfolio reside in organizational routines or individual capabilities? We investigate this micro-foundational question by examining how the loss of strategic human capital disrupts knowledge redeployment. Using a stacked difference-in-differences design and natural language processing of patent text, we analyze the semantic topology of firm innovation following the exogenous shock of inventor deaths. We find that, while the firm's strategic orientation exhibits high structural inertia, its portfolio structure is fragile. Our results reveal a divergent mechanism driven by the type of human capital lost. The loss of a specialist leads to strategic de-anchoring, characterized by an expansion of semantic breadth as the firm loses its technical anchoring. Conversely, the loss of a generalist triggers an architectural collapse, characterized by a significant contraction in semantic breadth. Furthermore, we document that firms losing generalists attempt to mask this collapse with a paradoxical spike in administrative patent filings. These findings challenge the view of generalists as mere bridges for distant redeployment, demonstrating instead that generalists provide the architectural capability to sustain portfolio complexity, while specialists provide the technical anchoring of strategic focus.

Organizational Learning with a Knowledge Aggregator: Impact of Organizational Knowledge Diversity

STR
Natarajan Balasubramanian (Syracuse University); Samir Biswas (Indian Institute of Management Udaipur); Palash Deb (North Carolina Agricultural and Technical State University); Preetam Basu (University of Kent)
Knowledge aggregators (KAs), which access knowledge across organizations, are becoming increasingly important sources of knowledge for organizations. Yet, KAs can bring both accurate and inaccurate knowledge, potentially reshaping organizational learning (OL) that critically hinges on absorbing correct information while avoiding errors. Using a novel extension of March’s simulation-based model of probabilistic imitative learning, we analyze the impact of two KA attributes—aggregation capability and the ability to generate new knowledge—and their interplay with two forms of organizational knowledge diversity— knowledge convergence rate (a longitudinal measure) and diversity of the superior or reference group that the organization learns from (a cross-sectional measure)—on OL. We find that while KAs are beneficial, counterintuitively, the benefits are not the highest for the most powerful aggregators. Both slower convergence and optimal superior group diversity enhance OL, but in different ways, as they balance absorption and avoidance differently. Importantly, there appears to be no single level or form of organizational knowledge diversity that is optimal across all KA attributes, resulting in potential organizational tradeoffs when dealing with KAs. Together, by studying the role of KAs as intermediaries that actively aggregate knowledge to reshape OL, we contribute to the literature on knowledge aggregation and transfer.

Technological Complementarity Versus Related Constructs: Evidence from the USPTO Patent Data

STR
Catherine HK Park (The Ohio State University)
Although researchers frequently examine the influence of firms’ technology portfolios on competitive and cooperative outcomes, inconsistent definitions and measures often conflate distinct constructs such as similarity, relatedness, and complementarity. To resolve these conceptual ambiguities, this study develops a unified framework that distinguishes five inter-firm technological relationships – similarity, consanguinity, co-productiveness, relatedness, and complementarity – each defined by a specific underlying mechanism. I operationalize these constructs using fine-grained patent data and follow best practices in construct development to evaluate their reliability and discriminant validity. The results show that these five constructs are distinct, and that treating similarity or relatedness as proxies for complementarity may produce misleading inferences about strategic value creation. By clarifying the distinction between application overlap, developmental lineage, and functional interdependence, this study offers a validated measurement methodology to advance research on the relationship between innovation strategy and firm performance.

Attention Lost, Attention Regained: Revitalizing Unutilized Knowledge as a Response to Spillovers

STR
Sara Ryoo (Baruch College, CUNY); Seungho Choi (Ewha Womans University); Wen Zheng (ShanghaiTech University)
This study explores how firms revitalize unutilized knowledge as a response to knowledge recipient-firm-generated knowledge that has been created from the focal firm’s own unutilized-knowledge spillovers. Drawing on the Attention-Based View, this study maintains that when knowledge recipient firms leverage a focal firm’s unutilized knowledge to create new knowledge that closely aligns with the focal firm’s core technological expertise, the likelihood that the focal firm revitalizes that unutilized knowledge increases. In addition, we argue that revitalization is likely to occur when the unutilized knowledge is proximal to the focal firm’s core technological domain. Furthermore, we investigate the moderating effects of 1) the novelty of complementary knowledge and 2) technological salience of the knowledge recipient firm on the relationship between the proximity of the recipient firm’s new knowledge to the focal firm’s core technological domain and the unutilized knowledge revitalization. We examine 341 U.S. pharmaceutical firms from 1986 to 2018 and find support for our hypotheses, highlighting the importance of the knowledge domain within which newly created knowledge is situated in shaping firm attention during the process of revitalizing unutilized knowledge. This study shows that knowledge spillovers can offer opportunities for the knowledge-originating firm to generate new innovations with the use of other firm’s experience to leverage unutilized knowledge.
