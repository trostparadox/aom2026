Sensemaking in Strategic Openness: Reconfiguring a Precarious Profession in a Brazilian Municipality

SAP
Daniela Soares Santos; Maria-Elisa Brandão-Bernardes (Dom Cabral Foundation); Rosalia Aldraci Barbosa Lavarda (Federal University of Santa Catarina)
This study engages with sensemaking and open strategy as interrelated processes, examining the meanings created and negotiated when strategizing becomes more open, participatory, and socially distributed. The goal was to understand how the creation and attribution of meaning are effectively articulated in a public management context, considering the interaction of its multiple dimensions. Presenting the case study of the Vila Velha City in Brazil, the methodology combined non-participant observation of strategy workshops, semi-structured interviews with key actors and documentary analysis. The findings evidenced that inclusion and transparency coexisted with deliberate restrictions on final decision-making. Final strategic alignment was ensured through a practitioners' strategic triad composed of the political sensegiver the mayor, responsible for political legitimation; methodological sensegivers, the professors- consultants, who mediated interactions and ensured technical coherence; and the alternating leadership in the sensemaking process by the technical team, who contributed with specialized and local knowledge, across different phases of the process. This research contributes to the open strategy literature by indicating that openness does not diminish the role of the strategists, but rather reconfigures them, as agents of intermediation.

Utopifying: Organizational Identity Formation in Real Utopias

SAP
Verena Meyer; Sarah Stanske (University of Bremen)
Real utopias are organizations whose members work toward desirable futures based on potentials afforded by the present. For real utopias, the forming of an organizational identity is crucial be-cause a shared sense of “who we are as an organization’ provides members with the guidance needed to navigate through the imperfect present toward desirable futures. Based on a two-year ethnographic study at “Utopia”, an emerging German entrepreneurship center, we develop a model of organizational identity formation in real utopias. Through our analysis, we identify three practic-es of “utopifying”—demarcating, prioritizing, and reifying—through which members of real utopi-as form a shared sense of the distinctive, central, and enduring features of their organization’s iden-tity, whilst providing orientation toward desirable futures. Furthermore, we show how this process is shaped by the context of a real utopia. Our findings contribute to the literature on real utopias by providing an in-depth account of “utopifying” as a specific way through which members of real utopias engage with desirable futures. Our study also extends understandings of organizational identity formation by shedding light on the role of desirable futures in this process.

The Last Mile of Transparency: How Female Disclosure Gatekeepers Improve Market Understanding

SAP
Wokun Zhou (East China Normal University); Bo Wang (Kean University); Jianrong Wang (East China Normal University)
Previous studies typically assume a seamless flow of information from firms to investors, yet this perspective often neglects the significant frictions introduced by human intermediaries. This study investigates the firm’s internal disclosure framework to examine whether the gender of the primary gatekeeper, the board secretary, influences the precision of stock price formation. Drawing on Upper Echelons Theory, we posit that the distinct cognitive and behavioral traits of these officers leave a tangible imprint on the information environment. Exploiting the unique institutional setting of China, where board secretaries hold legally mandated executive power, we find that firms with female board secretaries exhibit significantly higher stock price informativeness. This finding suggests that female gatekeepers facilitate a more accurate translation of firm-specific fundamentals into market prices. Robustness tests that include a difference-in-differences design based on board secretary turnover support a causal interpretation. We document two distinct channels for this effect. First, a communication channel exists where female board secretaries cultivate richer dialogue with analysts. Second, a governance channel operates in which female risk aversion strengthens the credibility of internal controls. Cross-sectional analyses reveal that this interpreter advantage scales with task complexity. The effect is most pronounced when the board secretary possesses financial expertise and in high-stakes environments characterized by opacity, intense investor attention, or weak institutional infrastructure. By shifting focus from strategic decision-makers to the agents responsible for information dissemination, this study demonstrates that gender diversity is critical in the final stage of capital market efficiency.

Negotiating Status: How Non-Engineers Navigate Status Hierarchies in Tech

SAP
Paul Olorunnisola (Drexel University); Rajiv Nag (Drexel University)
We present a study that explores how individuals occupying perceived low-status roles within organizations actively navigate and challenge the essentialized identities ascribed to them due to their lower status. Specifically, the study focuses on the technology industry, a sector often characterized by a hierarchical valuation of functions, with engineering functions occupying a privileged position. Drawing upon in-depth interviews with 26 professionals employed in non-engineering roles across a range of tech firms, we propose novel theoretical pathways that illuminate the multifaceted ways in which individuals negotiate and contest their prescribed identities within the organizational context. We discovered three distinct pathways individuals utilize to cope with and transcend identity essentialization: Feigning Ascendancy, Dissenting Reclamation, and Subversive Submitting.
