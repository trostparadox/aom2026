Session overview

When labor How and when can managers expand their span of control while sustaining performance? Hierarchic organizations have an inherent drawback: as they expand, managers become increasingly burdened with coordinating a growing number of subordinates (Urwick 1956, Puranam et al. 2012). One way organizations can cope with this is by buffering themselves from complexity through additional hierarchical layers (extending vertically), enabling managers to process information in a more compressed form—yet at the cost of information distortion and potential incentive misalignments (Simon 1962, Thompson 1967, p. 76–81, Tirole 1986). Alternatively, organizations can flatten their structure (extend horizontally), whereby managers extend their span of control but, given cognitive constraints, risk becoming overburdened with overseeing an excessive number of operations (Simon 1962, Williamson 1967, Levinthal and Workiewicz 2018). Thus, within a given hierarchical level, managers with narrower spans of control tend to achieve better performance, while reliance on wider spans of control imposes additional coordination costs (Gittell 2001). But must this trade-off always hold? This study’s answer to the opening research question suggests that it need not.
