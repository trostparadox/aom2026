The Proximity of Creative Expertise: Designers, Generative AI and Betrayal in Daily Work

CTO
Mark Dowsett (Bayes Business School)
Technological advances are challenging established conventions for establishing, managing and disseminating expertise. Central to many occupations, technology helps expert communities generate, communicate, and protect knowledge. The creative industries are full of such communities experiencing transformation in their work because of the increasing accessibility and cognitive computing power of artificial intelligence (AI). Nonetheless, the specificity of traditional AI technology has not hitherto significantly affected creative workers. That was until the latest development, generative AI (GenAI), which, unlike traditional AI, has general intelligence and broad capabilities that can imitate human knowledge and expertise. Drawing on analysis of 88 in-depth, first-hand interviews with the same 44 design experts at 12-month intervals, I use an affordance lens to explore creative workers’ dynamic relationship with GenAI, introducing the construct of proximity of creative expertise as a practical framework for theorising when, where, and how designers use generative AI in their daily work. The closer the expertise of GenAI is perceived to be to the designers’ ‘signature skill’, the less usefulness it affords them. Most intriguing was designers’ exploitative use of GenAI in other domains, a phenomenon described as creative trespassing, as they betray well-established relationships and knowledge boundaries.

Human–AI Complementarity in Knowledge Transformation: Evidence from Chinese Medicine Manufacturing

CTO
Yi Kuang (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Martin J Liu (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Dandan (Wendy) YE (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Boyi Jin (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
In knowledge-intensive manufacturing, the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) makes human–AI collaboration in knowledge transformation (KT) increasingly unavoidable. Prior research on knowledge transformation has focused primarily on human–human interaction, while studies of human–AI collaboration largely examine data-rich and highly structured settings. Consequently, how knowledge is transformed through human–AI collaboration in tacit-knowledge-intensive and regulated contexts remains underexplored. Drawing on a qualitative multiple-case study of Chinese patent medicine manufacturing firms, we examine how humans and AI collaboratively transform knowledge under conditions of high variability, regulatory constraint, and limited data formalization. We find that knowledge transformation does not proceed through a fixed division of labor between human and algorithmic actors, but through an evolving process of coordination in practices. We conceptualize a process model of human–AI knowledge transformation and identify three interrelated mechanisms—knowledge concealment, knowledge co-construction, and knowledge complementarity—that operate across structural, interactional, and processual levels. This framework represents our primary contribution, explaining how organizations progressively coordinate human judgment and AI-generated outputs, giving rise to emergent complementarity over time. We offer practical guidance for organizations on how to manage human–AI collaboration as an ongoing process of coordination in practice.

Algorithmic Attention Scaffolding: AI Copilots and the Search–Adaptation Tradeoff

CTO
Hantong Zhao
Generative AI copilots are spreading rapidly across knowledge work and early field experiments report sizable individual-level productivity gains. Yet copilots do not merely automate tasks—they algorithmically structure what users notice, consider, and select. We develop a theory of algorithmic attention scaffolding: the degree to which a copilot curates, sequences, and fluently completes candidate actions, thereby reallocating human attention away from open-ended search toward machine-suggested options. Integrating the attention-based view with choice architecture and organizational learning, we theorize a search–adaptation tradeoff: scaffolding increases short-term throughput but produces attentional homogenization (within-unit convergence in problem framing and solution pathways), which narrows exploratory search and can undermine innovation and adaptation over time. Using the HC3 Human– ChatGPT Comparison Corpus, we provide calibration evidence that repeated model responses to the same prompt are highly convergent (mean within-prompt TF–IDF cosine similarity = 0.63; 0.65 for opening frames), and that stylistic similarity across prompts is higher for ChatGPT than for human experts. We then report results from a field panel study of a staggered copilot rollout and a randomized experiment that manipulates design levers (suggestion diversity and autonomy support, including dominance/friction cues). Framework advances CTO research on AI-enabled organizing and offers actionable guidance for designing copilots that preserve exploratory capacity.

When Knowing Is Unknowable: Trust and Distributed Cognition in Human–AI Systems

CTO
Akhil S G (Case Western Reserve University)
The study examines how distributed cognition (DCog) is reorganized in AI-augmented radiological practice, where diagnostic reasoning increasingly relies on stochastic, opaque computational systems. While classical accounts of DCog conceptualize knowing as a socially distributed and coordinated activity across people, tools, and environments, they assume epistemic stability, deterministic artifacts, and implicit closure of knowledge claims. Drawing on cognitive ethnography and longitudinal activity mapping in radiology, this paper problematizes these assumptions by analyzing how epistemic trust, redundancy, and monitoring function under conditions of unknowability. The findings show that epistemic trust operates as a routing condition that governs how uncertainty moves through the socio-technical system, shaping transitions between provisional acceptance, validation, escalation, and deferral. Layered redundancies enable AI-generated knowledge claims to become actionable despite epistemic opacity, while simultaneously redistributing the burden of monitoring system performance onto practitioners. By representing distributed cognition as a flow-based system of epistemic state transitions rather than a sequence of stable stages, this study reframes distributed cognition as a form of epistemic governance under stochastic delegation. The paper contributes to debates on human-AI collaboration by identifying trust not as a background condition of coordination, but as an organizing logic that sustains agency and accountability in high-stakes knowledge work.

Who Contributes More to Innovation: AI or Colleagues?

CTO
Peng Lu (Jilin University); Runping Guo (Jilin University); Ziqing Feng (Jilin University)
When artificial intelligence (AI) is deeply embedded in organizational work, a critical question has emerged: which contributes more to innovation—AI or human collaboration? This paper argues that the answer does not lie in an “either-or” substitution logic, but rather in how employees jointly leverage AI and interpersonal knowledge sources. Drawing on the Knowledge-Based View (KBV) and Social Exchange Theory, we propose that the congruence between employees’ AI usage and knowledge-sharing behaviors is a key mechanism driving ambidextrous innovation. Using response surface analysis on paired survey data from 263 employee–supervisor dyads in digital new ventures, we find that: (1) The congruence between AI usage and knowledge sharing promotes ambidextrous innovation; (2) under incongruent conditions, the incongruence between the two exerts differential effects on exploitative and exploratory innovation. This study is the first to apply response surface analysis to the domain of human–AI collaborative innovation, revealing that the “matching logic” of knowledge acquisition behaviors is more decisive for innovation outcomes than the intensity of any single behavior alone.
