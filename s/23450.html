An Exploration on the Importance of Edison and Pasteur Stars in Advancing Innovation (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Anubha Shokhand (Masters' Union School of Business); Nilam Kaushik
A small number of star inventors account for a disproportionate share of technological progress, yet prior research often treats stars as a homogeneous group. Building on Pasteur's Quadrant framework, this work distinguishes between two types of star inventors: Pasteur stars, who are prolific both in scientific publishing and patenting, and Edison stars, who are prolific inventors but not prolific publishers. Using data on over three million U.S. patents granted between 1995 and 2014, we examine how these two forms of star expertise differentially shape invention performance and how their effects vary across technological, scientific, and institutional contexts. We show that both Pasteur and Edison stars are associated with higher patent performance, measured by forward citations, but that Edison stars exhibit a larger performance premium. The two star types act as substitutes rather than complements, reflecting distinct modes of problem solving: scientific translation versus system-level integration. We further demonstrate that technological, scientific and institutional context conditions these effects. The findings highlight heterogeneity among star inventors and show that their contributions depend critically on the nature of knowledge, technology, and organizational setting.

Beyond Dyads: The Role of Network Level Collaborative Stability on Breakthrough Innovation

TIM
Yanjiao Liu (South China University of Technology); Tianyu HOU (University of Science and Technology of China); si yi zhou (Zhengzhou University)
While prior research has examined how collaborative stability influences breakthrough innovations, most studies predominantly emphasize dyadic inventor relationships, overlooking dynamics at the collaborative network level. Moving beyond this dyadic focus, we examine how collaborative stability affects breakthrough innovations and propose two moderating contingencies: structural holes and degree assortativity. Based on social resource theory, we posit that collaborative stability fosters cognitive rigidity, resource ossification, and social ossification, thereby hindering breakthrough innovation. This negative effect weakens when focal inventors span structural holes but intensifies under conditions of high degree assortativity. An analysis of a large longitudinal dataset of U.S. pharmaceutical patents provides robust support for our hypotheses. We conclude by discussing the theoretical contributions and practical implications.

Fueling or Fettering? The Network Effects of Institutional Separation on Breakthrough Innovation

TIM
Yuan Zhou (Tsinghua University); Ning Kang (Tsinghua University); Guannan Xu (Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications)
Breakthrough innovation increasingly arises from cross-boundary networks spanning diverse institutional contexts. While prior research has emphasized network structures (e.g., structural separation) as sources of heterogeneous knowledge, it has largely overlooked how institutional separation within networks shapes breakthrough innovation. We conceptualize institutional separation as a multidimensional construct comprising formal separation, rooted in rule- and nation-based boundaries, and informal separation, embedded in relational and community-based divisions within collaboration networks. Drawing on network theory, we further distinguish three network roles—gatekeepers, liaisons, and itinerants—through which institutional separation exerts heterogeneous effects on breakthrough innovation. We test this framework using longitudinal data from the global 3D printing industry (1998–2025), covering 29,642 organizations and 109,135 patents. Formal institutional boundaries are identified by national affiliation, while informal boundaries are detected through clustering algorithms applied to collaboration networks. Breakthrough innovation is measured using the KI index derived from natural language processing of patent texts. Results from negative binomial regressions reveal a clear fueling–fettering duality: formal institutional separation hinders breakthrough innovation, whereas informal separation facilitates it. Role-based analyses show that gatekeeper and itinerant positions under formal separation impede breakthrough innovation, while gatekeeper and liaison positions under informal separation promote it. This study advances research on networks and institutions by demonstrating how distinct forms of institutional separation generate divergent network effects on breakthrough innovation, with implications for innovation policy and network strategy.

How Inventions Become Breakthroughs (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Peter Inho Nahm (American University of Sharjah)
Prior literature on breakthrough inventions has elucidated the various ways in which these inventions are created. However, prior research has not yet examined the social and temporal processes through which these inventions, after their initial creation, actually achieve breakthrough status. With patent and inventor data from the United States Patent and Trademark Office spanning nearly four decades, we reveal distinct patterns in the process through which inventions become breakthroughs. More specifically, we show that star status or greater network centrality of an inventor who first cites an invention increases the likelihood of the invention becoming a breakthrough. We additionally find that this positive effect may diminish for star inventors with high centrality (i.e., the interaction of star status and network centrality) who first cite an invention, but our interaction plot further reveals that star inventors are always more likely than non-stars to turn an invention into a breakthrough regardless of the level of centrality. Our study contributes to organization and innovation studies.

The Star Illusion: Why Non-Stars Matter More Than We Think in Scientific Discovery (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Omid Aliasghar (The University of Auckland); Arash Sadeghi (University of Leicester); Rajat Khanna (Tulane University)
Star scientists drive innovation within firms by shaping team search direction and accessing critical resources. However, the advancement of science occurs via collaborative process, and the role of non-star team members in this process is also important. This study examines how two key attributes of non-stars, creative role identity and autonomous motivation, moderate the effects of star presence on team-level search behavior. Using a unique dataset comprising 108 scientific teams funded by New Zealand’s national research programs, we find that the presence of a star scientist enhances local search but reduces distant search. In addition, we find that a strong creative role identity among non-star team members intensifies the positive effect of a star on local search and the negative effect of a star on distant search. Finally, autonomous motivation of non-star team members also increases local search by stars but does not impact stars’ distant search. By integrating microfoundations of innovation with research on star scientists, we theorize and demonstrate how the presence of a star scientist can shape team search trajectories in asymmetric ways, and how these effects are contingent on the characteristics of non-star team members.
