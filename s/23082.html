Generative AI-Based Workplace Learning: A Systematic Review Grounded in Self-Determination Theory

HR
Jisung K. Lee (Université de Montréal); Mario Passalacqua (Université du Québec à Montréal (UQAM)); Marc-Antoine Gradito Dubord (University of Montreal)
The rapid diffusion of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) systems such as ChatGPT has transformed workplace learning by enabling continuous, self-directed, and interactive skill development. Yet, existing reviews on artificial intelligence in Human Resource Development (HRD) remain largely technology-centered and provide limited insight into how employees psychologically experience and internalize GenAI-supported learning. Grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT), this systematic review addresses this gap by examining how GenAI may shape employees’ basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness in workplace learning contexts. Focusing on 13 empirical studies published since the public release of large language model–based systems such as ChatGPT in 2022, we synthesize emerging evidence on the uses, outcomes, and design characteristics of GenAI in personnel training and development. Interpreted through an SDT lens, we propose an integrative conceptual model explaining how specific GenAI features are theorized to relate to employees’ psychological need satisfaction, while highlighting the moderating role of individual, task, and organizational conditions. We further argue that the workplace learning outcomes reported in the reviewed literature (i.e., skill development, work efficiency, and reflective learning) can be understood as being supported by basic psychological need satisfaction. This review advances HRD research by shifting the focus from adoption and efficiency toward employee psychological needs and offers theory-driven directions for future research and practice.

When Expertise Is No Longer Read off the Page: Legitimacy in AI-Enabled Learning

HR
Vishal Rana (Griffith University); Rola Al-Massalkhi (University of Doha for Science & Technology)
Generative artificial intelligence disrupts the inferential relationship between professional outputs and expertise claims. When algorithmic systems produce communicative artefacts that resemble professional work yet remain epistemically unreliable, the basis on which knowledge-intensive professionals sustain legitimacy becomes uncertain. This study examines how learning and development professionals reconstitute legitimacy and maintain epistemic control as they integrate generative AI into practice. Drawing on interviews with twenty-four learning and development professionals across the Middle East and North Africa region, analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, we identify four interconnected processes. These include strategic transparency oriented toward external audiences, intra-professional boundary reconstruction that differentiates legitimate from illegitimate uses of AI, verification labour through which epistemic control is maintained, and organisational deferral that shifts accountability onto individual practitioners. The findings show that generative AI integration involves professional reconfiguration rather than straightforward adoption. Professionals engage in sustained legitimacy work as the material basis of expertise becomes technologically ambiguous. The study contributes to human resource management scholarship by theorising legitimacy as an active accomplishment under conditions of technological uncertainty, by conceptualising verification labour as a central yet largely invisible mechanism of professional control, and by demonstrating how organisational temporality and governance absence intensify legitimacy burdens placed on individuals.

Human vs. AI coach: Longitudinal Effects Contingent Upon Employees’ Competence and Firm-Specific HC

HR
Yue HE (Renmin University of China); Xin Wei (Renmin University of China); Huaikang Zhou (Tsinghua University)
In rapidly evolving business environments, AI coaches are reshaping corporate training by providing personalized learning paths and continuous guidance through high-frequency, timely, and interactive feedback, yet prior research largely adopts a static perspective, overlooking dynamic changes in individual potential development. This study examines the nonlinear trajectories of potential development and their mechanisms using two longitudinal field studies and advanced modeling approaches. Study 1 analyzes training data from 3,574 sales personnel at a domestic jewelry brand across human and AI coach phases, while Study 2 tracks training and sales performance of 221 employees at a domestic medical aesthetics company to validate trajectory robustness and explore the roles of baseline competence and firm-specific human capital. Using latent growth models and three-period short-panel individual fixed-effects analyses, results reveal inverted U-shaped trajectories for both coach modes, with AI coaches eliciting higher and more sustained potential development. Baseline task competence negatively predicts initial potential, and firm-specific human capital constrains the growth slope. Importantly, the slope of potential development significantly predicts subsequent sales performance, demonstrating a lagged effect.

Making GenAI Work: Recursive Learning Processes in Self-Directed Workplace Development

HR
Vishal Rana (Griffith University); Juliet Gladies Jayasuria (University of Doha for Science & Technology)
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is rapidly diffusing into knowledge work, yet human resource development research has paid limited attention to how workers learn to use these systems effectively in practice. While prior studies document productivity and quality gains from GenAI, the learning processes through which such gains are realized remain underexplored. Drawing on self-directed learning (SDL) theory, this qualitative study examines how knowledge workers develop capability with GenAI in organizational contexts where formal training and governance lag adoption. Based on semi-structured interviews with 24 expatriate knowledge workers in Qatar and analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis, the study identifies recursive learning as the central learning pattern. Recursive learning describes a self-reinforcing process in which GenAI tool proficiency and domain expertise co-develop through mutually constitutive feedback loops rather than sequential stages. Three interrelated processes constitute this dynamic: iterative experimentation, comparative tool orchestration, and calibrated trust development. These processes are amplified by policy vacuums and shape how workers negotiate productivity gains, professional identity, and expertise in AI-saturated workplaces. The findings extend SDL theory by reconceptualizing learning resources as responsive and interactive, and they offer actionable insights for HRD practitioners seeking to support capability development under conditions of rapid technological change.

AI at Work: Training, Organizational Adaptation, and Career Resilience in the Canadian Context

HR
Andrey Kasimov (Diversity Institute, Toronto Metropolitan University); Wendy Cukier; Mary Han (Toronto Metropolitan University); Guang Ying Mo; Matthew McDonald (Toronto Metropolitan University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in everyday work, reshaping how employees learn, perform tasks, and imagine their future careers. Organizations have responded with training, guidance, and upskilling as central HR strategies for supporting adaptation to AI-enabled work. Yet these interventions may generate an employability paradox: supports intended to build resilience can also heighten employees’ awareness of automation risk and perceived replaceability. Drawing on sociotechnical theory, complementarity theory, and the Social Construction of Technology (SCOT), we examine how AI adoption in Canada shapes workers’ employability and how these relationships vary across perceived organizational responsiveness and worker groups. Using Wave 8 of the Environics Institute’s Survey on Employment and Skills (n = 5,603; March–April 2025), we operationalize employability through a resilience index combining AI familiarity, AI training, and perceived training sufficiency, alongside concerns capturing automation worry and employees’ perceptions of organizational adaptation. Results show that training and organizational guidance are strongly associated with higher automation worry, suggesting that HR interventions can amplify anxiety rather than reduce it. Resilience is more common among AI users, but it operates differently by exposure: among AI users, higher resilience is associated with greater automation worry, whereas among non-users resilience appears more protective. Perceived organizational adaptation further conditions these dynamics, with slow-to-adapt workplaces amplifying worry among AI users. Notably, the training–worry association is particularly pronounced among Black and Indigenous workers. Overall, the findings refine theories of technological change by showing that complements such as training can simultaneously stabilize and destabilize careers, and that employability outcomes depend on the interaction between individual preparedness and perceived organizational responsiveness.
