Alliance Formation Under Pressure: The Role of R&D Pipeline and Partner’s Knowledge Combination

STR
Changhong Yi (National University of Singapore); Amit Jain (National University of Singapore); Annapoornima Manathattai Subramanian
When firms face pipeline pressure, they are often expected to “look outside” for solutions. We revisit this intuition in the context of biopharma–AI partnerships and find that it does not always translate into R&D alliancing. We theorize that internal R&D pipeline pressure discourages R&D alliancing because alliances are coordination-intensive, slow to yield returns, and highly uncertain, which is misaligned with the urgency and resource constraints of pressured firms. We further argue that technologically appealing AI partners can become strategically costly under pressure: although partners with broad and novel AI knowledge combinations expand the focal firm’s solution space, they heighten screening, integration, and learning demands. We test these arguments using 279 biopharma–AI R&D alliances formed between 2013 and 2024 and leverage natural language processing to construct realistic choice sets for focal biopharmaceutical firms. Results show that pipeline pressure is associated with a lower likelihood of alliance formation, and that this negative association strengthens when potential AI partners offer broader and more technologically distant AI knowledge combinations. These findings position alliance formation as a state-contingent external search decision and clarify why, under heightened internal pressure, firms may avoid partners that appear most innovative.

Microfoundations of Alliance Formation from a Dyadic Perspective

STR
Sarosh Asad (University of Groningen); Carolin Dohle (University of Cologne); Matthias Schulz (University of Göttingen); Philip J. Steinberg (University of Groningen); Christian Schwens (University of Cologne)
While prior research has largely explained alliance formation through interfirm characteristics, emerging work highlights the role of chief executive officers as key decision-makers. However, because alliance formation is inherently dyadic and requires mutual agreement between two firms and their chief executive officers, examining this process calls for a dyadic perspective that captures the personal characteristics of both decision-makers. We develop a theory on how similarity in chief executive officers’ overconfidence shapes the likelihood that two firms form an alliance. Drawing on interpersonal similarity theory, we argue that similarity in chief executive officers’ personal characteristics fosters cognitive alignment in how potential partners interpret alliance opportunities and risks, reducing negotiation frictions. We further theorize that this relationship is contingent on product market overlap. We test our hypotheses using a sample of 264 alliances among publicly listed U.S. firms. Based on a set of realized and non-realized dyads, our results show that CEO-to-CEO similarity significantly increases the likelihood of alliance formation.

Buying Growth, Sacrificing Collaboration? Acquisition Premiums and Alliance Formation

STR
Jiawen MA (Hong Kong Metropolitan University)
This study explores how acquisition premiums influence the acquirers' subsequent alliance formation. We argue that the pressures to achieve economic returns due to high acquisition premium make acquirers tend to engage in fewer new alliances afterward which require resource investment. Also, acquisition premiums become signals to potential partners that the acquires face challenges in achieving performance targets and make the acquirers less attractive to potential partners. The findings also suggest that this tendency diminishes when the relational embeddedness between the acquirer and the target increases, or when the acquirer holds a higher centrality or brokerage position. Our analyses of 4,519 acquisition deals support our theoretical framework. This research expands the existing literature on acquisition premiums by shedding light on their influence on acquirers' external interorganizational relationships.

When Status Travels but Experience Doesn’t: Partner Selection in Unfamiliar Domains

STR
Demetrius Lewis (University of California Riverside); Wei Xia (Rutgers Business School); Xiaoou Bai (Georgia State University)
This paper investigates how high-status organizations use strategic partnerships to address experience gaps that arise when they enter unfamiliar domains. Status and experience are typically assumed to be coupled, as status hierarchies emerge from firms’ observable histories of participation and performance within a focal domain. However, when high-status firms undertake strategies in unfamiliar domains, the absence of domain-specific experience may shape their preferences to choose high-status versus lower-status prospective partners. Using a sample of more than 34,000 investment syndicates assembled by venture capital firms between 1996 and 2017 as they diversified into industries in which they had not previously invested, we find that higher-status lead investors generally favor status-homophilous partnerships. However, higher-status lead investors are more likely to partner with lower-status firms when those prospective partners possess relevant experience in the target industry. We argue that the decoupling of status and experience reduces high-status firms’ credibility to add value outside their established domains. When prospective partners bring industry-specific experience that offsets this experience gap, high-status organizations become more willing to form status-mismatched partnerships, leveraging status asymmetry to mobilize partner expertise and facilitate effective value creation. This pattern highlights how high-status firms may trade endorsement-based legitimacy for access to experiential knowledge, incorporating lower-status but experienced partners to support the growth of the ventures they fund.

When ESG Takes a Back Seat: Network Brokerage and Alliance Partner Selection

STR
Margarita Ortiz (University of Groningen); Paula M Infantes (University of Groningen); Florian Noseleit (University of Groningen); Isabel Estrada Vaquero (University of Groningen)
This study examines how firms evaluate prospective alliance partners when legitimacy-related considerations and network-based strategic value point in different directions. We argue that partners’ ESG performance increases alliance attractiveness by enhancing perceptions of legitimacy, but that this effect depends on the strategic value provided by partners’ positions in the interorganizational network. In particular, brokerage positions offer access to information, control benefits, and network reach that can reduce firms’ reliance on ESG performance in partner selection. Using dyadic alliance data on S&P 500 firms from 2002 to 2023, we find that higher partner ESG performance is positively associated with the likelihood of alliance formation. However, this association weakens significantly as prospective partners’ brokerage positions become more advantageous, while brokerage itself strongly increases alliance likelihood. These findings qualify prior work that treats ESG as uniformly beneficial in alliance formation and show that ESG considerations are contingent on network structure. More broadly, the study contributes to research on alliances and interorganizational networks by highlighting how firms trade off legitimacy and network-based strategic value when selecting alliance partners.
