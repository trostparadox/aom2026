Session overview

This session investigates how organizational climates and equity signals form, operate, and influence employees' interpretations of inclusion. The papers address diversity climate dimensions, women's leadership representation, gender equity culture, HRM strategies for D&I, and the role of workforce heterogeneity in innovation. Together, they offer important evidence on how organizations communicate commitment to diversity and how those signals shape meaning, attitudes, and outcomes.

AI Competencies, Workforce Heterogeneity, and Innovation Impact in OEMs and SaaS Firms

DEI
Tia Crawford (University of Maryland Global Campus)
Original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and software-as-a-service (SaaS) firms rely on human capital to sustain innovation and remain competitive. As AI reshapes the technology landscape, these firms face a dual challenge: developing AI-related skills while cultivating workforce heterogeneity that enhances dynamic capabilities. Guided by Dynamic Capabilities Theory (DCT), this study employs a rapid evidence assessment (REA) to examine how AI-related competencies and heterogeneous workforce factors jointly contribute to innovation and competitive advantage in OEM and SaaS environments. Using CEBMa-aligned REA procedures, Gough’s Weight of Evidence appraisal, and ATLAS.ti-based coding, seventeen peer-reviewed studies published between 2021 and 2025 were critically analyzed. The synthesis identifies gender diversity as the most influential heterogeneity factor associated with innovation, and soft skills, such as communication, creativity, and adaptive leadership, as the most critical AI-related competencies driving competitive advantage. The findings extend DCT by demonstrating that AI-related skills and workforce heterogeneity function as intertwined micro-foundations of dynamic capabilities in OEM and SaaS firms. In practice, the study suggests that firms seeking AI-enabled innovation must pair technical skills with intentional strategies to build gender-diverse, communicative, and creatively engaged teams.

Diversity Climate: An Investigation of Its Dimensions and Their Distinct Nomological Networks

DEI
Y. Lillian Kim (New York University); Lisa Michelle Leslie (New York University)
Diversity climate, defined as beliefs about how people in an organization approach demographic differences, has received increasing attention from scholars. However, the dimensionality of diversity climate remains unclear, which has likely contributed to mixed findings regarding how diversity climate relates to other constructs. In the current study, we provide new insight into the dimensionality of diversity climate and demonstrate that different diversity climate dimensions have distinct nomological networks. We propose and test whether diversity climate includes three distinct dimensions: the value of diversity, fairness, and employee inclusion (Study 1a-b). We also theorize and find that different diversity climate dimensions have distinct relationships with other constructs, including the presence of diversity initiative practices (Study 2a), as well as employee job attitudes and diversity support (Study 2b). Our work contributes to the literature by further clarifying the dimensionality of diversity climate and by substantiating that diversity climate dimensions have divergent nomological networks.

Communicating Organization Culture as Supportive of Gender Equity Protects Against Sexual Harassment

DEI
nikta khalilkhani (Cornell University); Emily Zitek (Cornell University)
Despite the general hope that equity initiatives will produce positive change in organizations, reports and literature suggest that sexual harassment training and similar initiatives may be ineffective at reducing workplace sexual harassment. However, our research demonstrates that a communicating organizational culture as supportive of gender equity reduce the sexual harassment rate. In two studies, we tested the effect of communicated company culture as supportive or neutral to gender equity initiatives. Using EEOC sexual harassment and workforce gender composition data from 2010 to 2022, and the #Metoo movement as a natural experiment, we found that post-#MeToo sexual harassment rate has declined significantly. Moreover, the harassment rate has decreased in post-#MeToo years at faster rate than pre-#Metoo years. To further confirm these findings experimentally, we had 636 cis-gender heterosexual male participants complete an onboarding scenario about a fictional company, then rate their intention to sexually harass a female coworker. When company culture was communicated as supportive of gender equity, participants reported a significantly lower intention to sexually harass. These findings highlight the importance of promoting anti-harassment policies and inclusive norms as structural conditions that not only protect against misconduct but also foster greater representation of women in leadership and advance broader gender equity goals across organizations.

Female Representation, Work-Life Balance, and Perceptions of Diversity

DEI
Kristen Fultz (The University of Texas at El Paso); Aaron Nelson (The University of Texas at El Paso)
Despite extensive evidence linking diversity and inclusion climates to favorable employee and organizational outcomes, relatively little is known about how such climates emerge. Using social role theory and signaling theory, this study examines women’s representation in company leadership and flexible work arrangements as potential antecedents of diversity and inclusion climates. We hypothesize that greater women’s representation in leadership increases the likelihood of flexible work policies in organizations, which in turn signal an authentic organizational commitment to diversity and inclusion. As a result, firms with greater women’s representation in company leadership should exhibit stronger diversity and inclusion climates, with employee tenure moderating this relationship. Using longitudinal data from S&P 500 firms between 2020 and 2025, and in combination with large-scale employee sentiment data, we tested a moderated mediation model with both industry and year fixed effects. Results suggest that women’s leadership representation is positively associated with diversity and inclusion climates, and that this relationship is partially mediated by flexible work arrangements. Supplemental analyses using a substantially larger sample replicate these findings, while revealing differences between perceived and policy-based measures of work–life balance. Together, our paper seeks to advance theory on diversity and inclusion climate emergence. Keywords: diversity and inclusion climates, flexible working arrangements, women’s representation
