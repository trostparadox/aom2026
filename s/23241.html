High-Status Venture Capital and Foreign Market Entry: Evidence from European New Ventures

ENT
Francesca Tenca (University of Eastern Piedmont "Amedeo Avogadro"); Massimiliano Guerini (Politecnico di Milano)
The internationalization of new ventures has become a central challenge for entrepreneurial firms seeking to grow and compete globally. Yet, many new ventures face resource constraints and lack legitimacy in foreign markets, often relying on external affiliates to support their international expansion. This study examines the role of Venture Capitalists (VCs) in determining new ventures foreign market entry decisions and long-term performance. We theorize that high-status VCs provide access to more and higher-quality complementary resources, as well as offer certification in foreign markets, enhancing the likelihood that new ventures decide to enter these markets. Using a dataset on 5,471 private European-based new ventures that received VC from 2005 to 2018, we find that high-status VCs significantly increase new ventures foreign market entry decision, and the effect is stronger for new ventures with lower pre-entry technological capabilities. Contrary to expectations, we also find that high-status VCs alone do not improve exit outcomes following new ventures foreign market entry. Our results remain robust across a series of analyses that address endogeneity concerns related to high-status VC selection.

Is Innovation Indigenous? Social Foundations of Innovative Entrepreneurship

ENT
Saul Estrin; Maribel Guerrero (Arizona State University); Tomasz Marek Mickiewicz (Aston University)
We extend the Schumpeterian perspective by examining the role of social context—specifically, the presence of an Indigenous population—in shaping innovative entrepreneurship, distinguishing further between informal and formal entry modes. We argue that a higher proportion of Indigenous people increases local diversity, thereby enhancing entrepreneurship. Using individual-level data from the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) for Chile (2022– 2024), combined with municipality-level data on ethnic composition, we analyse how the share of the Indigenous population affects the entry of innovative entrepreneurs. The key finding is that a higher share of the indigenous population has a significant positive effect on innovative entry, but not on non-innovative entry. Furthermore, innovative entry tends to be concentrated in the informal sector. The robustness of these results is supported by instrumental-variable estimations, in which the municipal-level share of the Indigenous population is instrumented with geographic variables. Finally, the findings suggest that the effects of the indigenous population operate through contextual rather than compositional channels, as the individual indigenous status of respondents is not significant once contextual effects are taken into account.

Strategic Orientation, Sequential Signaling, and Interplay of Public and Private Venture Capital

ENT
Jiuqi Meng (Nanjing University); Daxin Sun (Nanjing University)
Research on entrepreneurial finance has long recognized the importance of venture capital (VC) for high-tech startups but has paid remarkably little attention to how the intrinsic strategic orientation of a venture interacts with the distinct institutional logics of different investor types. We address this gap by integrating two theoretical perspectives: institutional logics and signaling theory. Our framework posits that a startup’s orientation—pursuing frontier scientific research (science-oriented) versus mature technology applications (application-oriented)—acts as a primary filter for investor selection. Specifically, science-oriented ventures align with the public-good logic of Government VC (GVC), while application-oriented ventures resonate with the efficiency logic of Private VC (PVC). Crucially, we argue that this initial matching creates a dynamic signal: the identity of the early-round investor shapes subsequent cross-type financing. We test these propositions using a comprehensive dataset of 6,875 AI startups in China. Empirical results support our theoretical predictions: science-oriented startups are significantly more likely to attract GVC initially, whereas application-oriented startups attract PVC. Furthermore, early investor identity acts as a critical signal that mitigates specific risks: for science-oriented startups, initial PVC backing serves as a market validation that facilitates subsequent GVC, while initial GVC backing de-risks the technology for subsequent PVC. These findings demonstrate that strategic orientation and investor identity co-evolve to shape financing trajectories in emerging technologies.

Venture Capital and Startup Organizations: New Evidence from Individual-Level Data

ENT
Chenan Zhong (Politecnico di Milano); Benedetta Montanaro (Politecnico di Milano - School of Management); Massimo Gaetano Colombo
Startups face significant organizational challenges as they grow, requiring early structures to adapt to increasing complexity. Venture capital (VC) represents a key external actor that can accelerate organizational professionalization, yet evidence on how VC reshapes leadership structures over time remains fragmented. Using a novel longitudinal dataset linking VC investment data, firm-level information, and individual-level employment histories, we examine how independent venture capital (IVC) involvement alters CEO succession and top management team (TMT) dynamics. Drawing on a matched sample of IVC-backed and non-VC-backed startups founded between 2007 and 2021 across 29 European countries, we implement a difference-in-differences design to track organizational changes before and after IVC entry. We find that IVC investment is associated with higher CEO replacement, driven by both external appointments and internal promotions. Beyond the CEO level, IVC-backed firms expand their TMTs more rapidly, with growth concentrated in commercial, strategic, and financial functions, while expansion in R&D and operations is more limited.

Venture Debt and the Reallocation of Strategic Priorities

ENT
Mina Lee (KAIST); Yongwook Paik (KAIST)
Prior research in strategy and entrepreneurship implicitly equates venture capital with equity financing—overlooking venture debt (VD), a fast-growing yet understudied financing mechanism. A common concern is that layering risky debt onto already high-risk startups may increase distress and failure. The study instead examines that VD functions as a stakeholder governance mechanism that adds contractual discipline (e.g., covenants and repayment-linked milestones) and reshapes entrepreneurial resource allocation and timing. Drawing on agency theory and real options theory, we theorize that VD extends runway without immediate ownership dilution while increasing the salience of near-term, externally verifiable milestones that support refinancing and exit readiness. Using a matched sample of U.S. VC-backed startups, we show that VD backing is not associated with greater failure-related exits, but predicts higher likelihood of successful liquidity events (M&A and IPO) and larger subsequent equity financing after an initial lag. Patent outcomes suggest a complementary mechanism: post-VD patenting declines—particularly among technologically capable and later-stage firms—consistent with more selective long-horizon R&D and greater emphasis on commercialization-oriented investments. Together, our findings reposition VD as growth capital that governs the timing and composition of strategic investment, shaping how VC-backed startups convert technological potential into market outcomes.
