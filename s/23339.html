Changes in Attention Focus and Capacity: How Human Resource Slack drives MLB Team Performance

STR
Sebastian Fourne (University of North Florida); Jason Sigler (University of North Florida); Tiberiu Sergiu Ungureanu (Appalachian State University)
Human resource (HR) slack research makes mixed predictions about whether excess personnel improve or hinder organizational performance. Building on the attention-based view, we explore whether and how HR slack levels and HR slack trajectories facilitate performance by enabling high-stakes decision-making. Using data from Major League Baseball franchises (1980–2018), we find no support for the impact of front-line HR slack levels on team performance but note the benefits of increases in attention focus through territory reorganization in high-slack organizations and of increases in attention capacity through adding front-line HR slack (rather than adding managerial or administrative HR slack). Mechanism tests provide evidence for how attention is reallocated as front-line HR slack increases. We contribute with a nuanced perspective on HR slack that explains when and how organizations may convert additional personnel into better performance and extend the attention-based view to the deployment of human capital in knowledge-intensive organizations.

Celebrities and Fundraising by Early-Stage Firms

STR
Pranav Garg (Stevens Institute of Technology); James Chukwubudom Chikelu (Stevens Institute of Technology); Anand Goel (Stevens Institute of Technology)
Management research on celebrities has focused on CEOs. However, the primary metric underlying CEO celebrity is prior firm performance, which is problematic when examining how celebrities impact a firm’s subsequent performance. We address this conflation by leveraging the context of Special Purpose Acquisition Companies (SPACs). SPACs are early-stage firms with no history or performance record when they raise funds. This unique feature implies that individuals’ celebrity is not conflated with prior firm performance. How do celebrities impact fundraising by early-stage firms? Using a novel dataset that matches celebrities with SPACs, we find that each additional pre-IPO affiliated celebrity is associated with a 7.1% increase in funds raised by a SPAC. Explaining this result are the industry-specific expertise, social capital, and visibility of business celebrities, but only the social capital of non-business celebrities. When analyzing the roles that celebrities assume in SPACs, we find that business celebrities in both governance and arms-length roles matter for fundraising. However, non-business celebrities matter only in governance roles such as CEO-Chair and top management executive. These results highlight that the nuanced value of celebrity signaling—while business celebrities signal expertise, non-business celebrities compensate for the lack of business expertise with personal commitment to the firm.

Bucks for Bumps: How Paid Parental Leave Pays Off for Firms

STR
Hillary Cookler (UCLA Anderson School of Management)
Firms use benefits such as paid parental leave (PPL) to attract and retain employees. In the U.S., which lacks broad public PPL benefits, firms have steadily increased private, employer-based versions of this benefit over the last several decades. Despite its growing prevalence, systematic evidence on the effects of private firm PPL is limited. This is due in part to the absence of comprehensive data on firm policies and employee outcomes. Utilizing a novel, hand-collected database of U.S. firm PPL policy changes and mapping them to individual work histories, this paper estimates the causal effect of private firm PPL expansion on employee retention at the focal firm. A differences-in-differences approach is used to estimate within-firm retention effects. Results show that PPL expansions by U.S. firms lead to a statistically significant increase in the relative retention of female workers in the period following the policy change. Analysis of subgroup heterogeneity reveals that retention effects are strongest among the youngest, least paid, least senior, and least educated female workers. Among firms, effects are strongest at the largest firms, those with the lowest measured internal labor market flexibility, those with moderate baseline churn, and those with low to moderate revenue growth. These effects are concentrated in states without public paid parental leave programs and exhibit diminishing or non-monotonic returns with respect to leave duration. Finally, combining individual-level fertility estimates from the American Community Survey (ACS) with firm policy data, the paper estimates the expected costs of PPL provision and compares them to retention gains, providing novel evidence on the net benefits of PPL programs to firms.

Managerial Experience as a Double-Edged Sword: Ethical Capability and Bribery Across Institutional E

STR
Juan Carlos Morales (The University of Akron)
This study examines how managerial experience shapes bribery behavior in small and medium enterprises (SMEs) across varying institutional environments. Drawing on the resource-based and institutional-based views, we argue that SMEs with greater managerial experience—specifically, industry-experienced managers—develop stronger ethical capability through accumulated industry knowledge and embedded ties with suppliers, creditors, regulators, and other resource providers, enabling them to resist bribery pressures. However, we propose that this experience-based capability is contingent on institutional conditions. As SMEs face greater informal pressures (e.g., competition from unregistered firms) and formal pressures (e.g., tax burden), managerial experience may increasingly facilitate adaptive responses that accommodate, rather than constrain, bribery practices. We test our hypotheses using firm-level data from SMEs operating in 133 countries over the period 2009–2022. Our findings show that managerial experience is associated with lower bribery incidence on average, but this relationship weakens under adverse institutional conditions.
