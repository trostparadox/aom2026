Sustainability Pursuit to Place-Based Outcomes: Urgency and Infrastructure in Data Center Expansion

STR
Angela Soomin Ryu (University of Southern California)
This paper explores the understudied role of place-based infrastructure in shaping how corporate sustainability efforts translate into environmental outcomes under conditions of urgency. Research on sustainability typically assumes that sustained effort leads to improved outcomes, while research on organizations and place emphasizes how localized infrastructure influences economic activity. We bring these perspectives together to theorize how urgency can make place-based infrastructure a binding constraint on environmental outcomes, even when sustainability pursuits persist. We adopt an abductive, pattern-then-probe approach and develop a mechanism where environmental outcomes arise from operational execution tightly coupled to local electricity systems. In contrast, many sustainability initiatives operate through less place-bound, longer-term pathways. When urgency accelerates execution timelines beyond the pace of infrastructure adjustment, capacity expansion must proceed through existing systems, whose characteristics vary across places, thereby influencing realized environmental exposure. Empirically, we leverage the public release of ChatGPT in 2022 as a plausibly exogenous increase in urgency for firms developing and deploying proprietary AI models. We analyze U.S. data center expansion around the release and exploit heterogeneity in exposure to urgency across firm types and facility configurations. The findings suggest that the capacity expansion most exposed to urgency (i.e., large-scale facilities operated by firms that develop and deploy proprietary models) is disproportionately executed in electricity grid regions with greater power availability. These regions also exhibit higher fossil fuel dependence, even as firms continue large-scale renewable energy procurement. These findings advance a conditional account of sustainability under urgency and extend place-based research by identifying a previously overlooked consequence of leveraging inherited infrastructure for innovation.

How Do Firm Sustainability Dynamic Capabilities Elevate Regional Corporate Sustainability Norms? (WITHDRAWN)

STR
xin huang (Beijing Normal University); Chenghua Guan (Beijing Normal University); Honglan Yu (The University of Sheffield)
While prior research has extensively examined the adaptive function of dynamic capabilities in helping firms respond to exogenous environmental change and secure evolutionary fitness, their environment-shaping function remains underdeveloped. We address this gap by examining how firms’ sustainability dynamic capabilities (SDCs) scale beyond organizational boundaries to elevate regional corporate sustainability norms. We theorize that frequent sustainability-oriented resource renewals enabled by superior SDCs diffuse sustainability requirements, best practices, and values within local regions. This diffusion unfolds through three core mechanisms: (1) accelerating the circulation of sustainability-oriented human capital among local firms, (2) transmitting coercive governance requirements through supply-chain exchanges, and (3) prompting local financial intermediaries to routinize sustainability-based screening criteria. We further posit that these diffusion processes are structurally contingent on regional industry agglomeration, the focal firm’s supply-chain bargaining power, and local credit-market tightness. Analyses of a longitudinal panel of 4,182 Chinese listed firms from 2013 to 2023 support our theoretical predictions.

How Institutional Complexity Shapes Supplier Environmental Sustainability

STR
Valentina Marano (Northeastern University); Yejee Lee (Auburn University); Stephanie Wang (Kelley School of Business, Indiana University); Stefano Franco (Mercatorum University)
Suppliers’ progress in reducing emissions remains uneven. Adopting a supplier-centric lens, we study how suppliers respond to buyers’ sustainability pressures amid institutional complexity. We argue that buyers play a pivotal role in translating sustainability norms into actionable expectations, but their influence depends on the convergence of environmental priorities from suppliers’ transnational organizational fields, i.e., other buyers, competitors, and the cross-country institutional context. We test these ideas using a dyadic panel dataset of S&P 500 buyers and their suppliers. We found that buyers with a greater depth of environmental supply chain management are associated with significantly better suppliers’ emissions reduction performance. The effect diminishes when the supplier’s buyer portfolio is highly diverse or when the supplier faces divergent cross-border institutional pressures, but remains unchanged when competitor heterogeneity is high.

Building Long-Term Strategic Human Capabilities to Drive Sustainability-Focused Innovation

STR
Suzan Dsouza (American University of the Middle East); Prof (Dr) Mujtaba M. Momin (American University of the Middle East); Ariz Naqvi (American University of the Middle East)
This study examines how environmental management training as a sustainability business practice, and an investment in employees’ general human capital, shapes long-term sustainability-focused innovation. Prior work has largely emphasized short-term performance, often testing mediation processes inappropriately and under-investigating organizational and national-level contingencies. Addressing these gaps, the paper theorizes that environmental management training predicts long-term sustainability-focused innovation through the mediating role of strategic human capabilities, drawing on human capital resources theory, upper echelons theory, and the attention-based view. The hypotheses are tested using an unbalanced 14-year panel comprising 5,119 firm-year observations from 952 publicly listed companies across 16 European Union member states (2010–2023), with national-level human capital derived from UNESCO. Using a two-step System Generalized Method of Moments (System-GMM) estimator, the results show that year-on-year investments in environmental management training enhance long-term sustainability-focused innovation and strategic human capabilities, and that strategic human capabilities partially mediate this relationship. Contrary to the hypotheses, national human capital attenuates the link between environmental management training and strategic human capabilities, and both top management team gender diversity and corporate social responsibility organizational embeddedness attenuate the effects of strategic human capabilities on long-term sustainability-focused innovation. Overall, the findings support human capital resources theory while demonstrating that firm- and country-level contingencies can operate in ways that differ from the theoretical expectations advanced.

Who Cares for the Planet? A Cross-Sector, Cross-Country Exam of Nonmarket Sustainability Strategies

STR
Fernanda Lemos (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute); Sergio Giovanetti Lazzarini (Insper); Burkard Eberlein (Schulich School of Business, York University); Dirk Matten (Schulich School of Business, York University); Esteban Sabbatasso (Schulich School of Business, York University); Federico De Cristo; José-Carlos Marques (Telfer School of Management, University of Ottawa); Roberto Vassolo (IAE BUSINESS SCHOOL - AUSTRAL UNIVERSITY)
Research has unveiled myriad strategies that firms, individually or collectively, can pursue to respond to calls for improved sustainable practices. These strategies include nonmarket efforts to adapt to and influence changes in the institutional environment and can lead to diverging societal outcomes. We examine an array of nonmarket strategies affecting sustainable production in natural resource industries, either promoting or derailing the adoption of sustainable practices. We conduct a multiple-case study involving two natural resource-intensive sectors (mining and soybeans) and three countries (Argentina, Brazil, and Canada), leveraging data from documentary analysis and 132 interviews. We propose a set of nonmarket strategies likely leading to positive sustainability outcomes (e.g., globally integrated, multi-stakeholder action to promote and enforce improved standards), as well as nonmarket strategies more conducive to negative outcomes (e.g., counteracting private collaboration to keep legal requirements at a minimum). We also suggest antecedent conditions that lead to those strategies. We contribute to the literature by demonstrating the importance of examining asymmetric societal outcomes in nonmarket strategies, and, in the context of sustainability, we explain types of nonmarket strategies that can either promote or derail efforts to promote sustainable practices.
