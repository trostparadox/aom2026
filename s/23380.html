Not Another You: How Boards Drive Change Through CEO Personality Contrast

STR
Pascal Flurin Meier (University of Zurich); Marc Kowalzick (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Jerayr M. Haleblian (University of California Riverside)
The contrast effect describes a psychological phenomenon in which a present target is compared to a preceding referent, leading decision makers to perceive the target as more different from that referent than it would appear in isolation. We draw on research on the contrast effect to better understand the comparative nature behind CEO succession. We posit that in response to performance shortfalls, boards will select an incoming CEO that differs on key personality traits from the outgoing CEO. In addition, we examine CEO, firm, and environmental moderating conditions under which CEO leadership is more tightly linked to firm outcomes, such that boards interpret poor performance as a clear and credible signal attributable to the CEO, thereby amplifying the contrast effect. Finally, we examine the downstream consequences of CEO personality contrast, showing it leads to genuine strategic change. We find results consistent with our hypotheses as we contribute to the development of a contrast effect approach to CEO succession.

Beyond Advocate or Avoid: Racial Minority CEOs and Racial Strategy Decoupling

STR
Yishu CAI (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Yuan SHUI (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Haibin Yang (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
Are racial minority elites more or less likely to engage in racial equity efforts? Both scholarly literature and empirical observations offer conflicting conclusions. We argue that prior work treats racial engagement as unitary and overlooks the double bind that holds minority leaders to neutrality while expecting identity-linked representation. Drawing on Goffman’s frontstage-backstage framework, we theorize that minority CEOs strategically decouple public-facing and internal arenas. They suppress frontstage voice to preserve legitimacy while institutionalizing backstage equity. We further theorize that conditional recoupling occurs when backstage engagement builds a legitimacy buffer and generates pressure for alignment, making later public advocacy more feasible, especially when strong financial performance provides discretion to align internal action with external positioning. We test these hypotheses in S&P 1500 firms between 2012 and 2020, using news and social media data to capture frontstage responses and internal workforce records to assess backstage equity. The study reconceptualizes minority leaders’ racial engagement as a dynamic frontstage-backstage process and advances theory on how initial decoupling can evolve into recoupling.

From Anticipated Bad Luck to Good Deeds: How Zodiac Year Motivates Employee-Related CSR

STR
ZHU MENG (City University of Hong Kong); Jane Lu (City University of Hong Kong)
This paper examines how anticipated luck influences managerial decisions on employee-related Corporate Social Responsibility (E-CSR). Prior research has predominantly viewed and treated luck as random and evaluated its effects ex post. In contrast, we examine contexts where luck is socially constructed and can be anticipated, allowing managers to make ex ante strategic responses. We propose that firms led by board chairs experiencing their zodiac year (a period culturally associated with temporary bad luck) engage more actively in E-CSR. We further find that this effect is stronger for firms located in regions with a strong Daoist culture and for those led by female chairs. Using a sample of 3,253 Chinese listed firms from 2010 to 2019, we find robust support for our predictions.

The CEO’s “Final Act”: Position Mortality and the Quest for Existential Continuity

STR
Yasser Basstawy (Free University of Bozen-Bolzano); Paola Rovelli (Free University of Bozen-Bolzano); Fabio Zona (University of Trento)
This study examines how CEOs’ career horizons shape engagement in prosocial behavior, focusing on ESG initiatives, and introduces the concept of position mortality salience, intended as the awareness of symbolic finitude associated with organizational roles. Drawing on Terror Management Theory, we argue that CEOs’ motivations evolve across the tenure cycle: in early tenure, executives engage in visible prosocial actions to establish legitimacy and mitigate career concerns, while in late tenure, the impending end of their position triggers existential concerns, prompting renewed engagement to preserve self-worth, reputation, and symbolic legacy. Using a sample of 979 European Union firms, we find a U-shaped relationship between CEO tenure and ESG performance, which is amplified in family firms, a context of heightened symbolic value. Our findings illuminate the psychological foundations of strategic behavior across the CEO career cycle, showing that executives’ decisions are also shaped by deeper symbolic and existential concerns. Consistently, the study offers a novel perspective on the drivers of prosocial engagement in organizations, suggesting that such actions may reflect personally meaningful efforts to preserve self-worth, reputation, and symbolic significance as leadership roles approach their natural endpoints, rather than being driven solely by firm-centered or purely strategic objectives.

An Ideal Fit? Gamma CEOs: A Holistic Personality Profile for the CEO Role

STR
Silja Schwanekamp (Universität Duisburg-Essen); Ben David Dalka; Sebastian Firk (University of Duisburg-Essen); Yannik Gehrke (University of Hamburg)
This study builds on a person-centered personality approach and introduces Gamma CEOs—a holistic personality profile that combines the positive poles of all Big Five personality traits—as an important subgroup within the CEO population. Executive job demands theory suggests that firm success relates to how well CEOs can manage the challenging demands of the CEO role. We argue that Gamma CEOs’ inherent abilities enable them to outperform their peers, because they strongly match these job demands. Based on 14,157 firm-year observations, we find that Gamma CEOs are associated with substantially higher firm performance and that this associa-tion is particularly pronounced in complex industries. We further show that Gamma CEOs pur-sue more complex strategies, simultaneously drive exploration and exploitation, and manage their firms more efficiently.
