Session overview

Talent acquisition has long relied on the judgment and experience of human resource professionals, with recruitment decisions primarily made by humans. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has been widely introduced across multiple stages of talent acquisition, from résumé screening to interview evaluation, gradually transforming traditional recruitment processes. This symposium explores how the involvement of AI in talent acquisition influences stakeholders’ perceptions, judgments, and behaviors. Drawing on research in human resource management and organizational behavior, we examine judgment mechanisms and recruitment processes in AI-enabled hiring contexts. We further explore how these mechanisms relate to key issues such as fairness and bias, human judgment and responsibility attribution, and the perceived legitimacy of recruitment decisions, and how these effects vary across different recruitment stages and organizational contexts. These themes are explored through five presentations and integrated by a discussant. The first paper by Ashneet Kaur and Sudhanshu Maheshwari examines how AI-led screening influences candidates’ trust, anxiety, and intentions to pursue job offers. Drawing on fairness heuristics and organizational legitimacy theory, the authors show that these reactions hinge on perceived judgment quality, or whether candidates feel meaningfully understood rather than mechanically processed. The paper underscores the importance of hybrid human-AI hiring systems for sustaining trust in AI-mediated recruitment. The second paper focuses on bias and fairness in AI recruitment. Deepa Sethi and Ashish Malik explore gender bias in AI-based recruiting, showing how AI tools can inherit and automate gendered patterns from past hiring routines and seemingly “neutral” proxies. They identify where bias can enter through data design, measurement, labeling, representation gaps, and AI assessments such as résumé screening and asynchronous video interviews. Building on this theme, Mohid Sayed, Ashish Malik, and Charles Dhanraj investigate how new firms can implement “fairness-by-design” in AI recruitment. Drawing on organisational justice theory and a longitudinal qualitative study of an AI recruitment platform, they propose a capability framework across data, model, interface, and governance layers, mapped onto key recruitment stages. The third paper focuses on organizational decision-makers. Connie Zheng examines HR and line managers’ perceptions of AI tools in supporting hiring decisions for workforce diversity. Based on in-depth interviews with HR and line managers in Australia, the study reveals that AI is primarily adopted for operational efficiency rather than for advancing diversity and inclusion goals, highlighting tensions between efficiency-driven adoption and ethical aspirations in recruitment. The fourth paper turns to the applicant perspective. Guofan Li, Patrick Dunlop, and Sharon Parker investigate how different purpose-oriented AI–human augmented decision agents shape job applicants’ reactions in recruitment. Distinguishing between efficiency-oriented, effectiveness-oriented, and fairness-oriented augmented decisions, the study uses a résumé-screening experiment to examine their effects on applicants’ perceived fairness and dehumanization. The fifth paper by Immanuel Lutzeyer and Uta Wilkens explores heterogeneity in human reliance on AI-supported hiring recommendations. Adopting a person-centred approach informed by research on algorithmic trust, technology acceptance, and AI literacy, the study shows that deviations from AI recommendations systematically differ across decision-maker profiles characterized by varying levels of trust, AI understanding, and HR experience. Professor Sharon Parker is a senior scholar in the fields of work design, leadership, and organizational change. Her research has made influential contributions to organizational design and leadership in support of effective and sustainable work. She will provide a synthesis of the symposium themes and findings across the presentations, and facilitate discussion, questions, and comments among the audience and participants.
