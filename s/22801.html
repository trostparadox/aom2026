Leader Feedback-Seeking and Individual Performance: The Roles of Team Information Exchange and LMXD

OB
Zhenmin Lai (Shandong University); Shiwen Fu (University of Technology Sydney); Song Wu; Yue Zhang (Shandong University)
Prior research has primarily focused on employee-initiated feedback-seeking, overlooking the potential influence of leader feedback-seeking behavior on employee performance. Drawing on social information processing (SIP) theory, this study examines the joint effects of leader feedback-seeking and leader-member exchange differentiation (LMXD) on employee performance, with team information exchange as a mediating variable. Study 1 (N = 383) found that higher LMXD weakens the positive relationship between leader feedback-seeking and team information exchange, which in turn, influences self-rated job performance. Study 2 (N = 172 employees across 32 teams) employed a multilevel model and revealed that higher LMXD weakens the indirect effect of leader feedback-seeking on supervisor-rated performance via team information exchange. Theoretical and practical implications of these findings are discussed.

Will Subordinates Tell the Truth? How Motive Attributions of Leader Downward Feedback-Seeking Shape

OB
Meng Zhong (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Wenjing Cai (University of Science and Technology of China)
Leaders’ behaviors of seeking feedback from subordinates are often seen as humble leadership, improving leaders’ self-view accuracy and effectiveness. However, our research challenges this assumption by showing that subordinates may not always offer candid feedback, which is key to leaders’ self-view accuracy, because subordinates may not perceive leaders’ feedback-seeking as genuinely humble. Drawing on the model of intentionality and behavior labeling, we identify two target-focused motives that subordinates attribute to leader downward feedback-seeking: a supporting motive (i.e., genuinely caring for subordinates) and a controlling motive (i.e., manipulating and controlling subordinates). These attributions shape how candidly subordinates provide feedback. Study 1, with 251 subordinate-leader dyads in China, found that leaders received more flattering, less candid feedback when subordinates attributed leaders’ feedback-seeking to a controlling motive, controlling for perceived leader effectiveness. Study 2, with 301 working adults in the UK, found that supporting motive attributions increased subordinate feedback candidness by enhancing perceptions of leader humility and reducing perceptions of leader hypocrisy, while controlling motive attributions had the opposite effect. This research contributes to the feedback-seeking literature by advancing understanding of how leader downward feedback-seeking behaviors are perceived and how subordinates respond.

Ask About The Journey, Not The Destination

OB
Zaidan Chen; Martha Jeong; Ting Zhang (Harvard Business School)
People often want input from others but worry about interpersonal costs, such as seeming incompetent, burdening advisors, or feeling pressured to follow unwanted advice. In this paper, we propose that the type of advice request determines whether seekers can obtain helpful guidance without incurring these costs. We distinguish outcome advice requests, which focus on what decision to make (e.g., “Which job offer should I accept?”), from process advice requests, which focus on how to make the decision (e.g., “What should I consider when deciding between the offers?”). Although people naturally gravitate toward outcome advice (Study 1), our studies show that process advice requests yield significant advantages. Advice-givers perceive process advice seekers more positively, viewing them as having invested more effort (Studies 2–4). Process requests also elicit more factual, less opinion-driven information, producing advice that seekers find more helpful (Studies 2–3) and preserving greater decisional freedom (Study 2). Across four studies (Nadvisors = 1,905, Nadvisees = 1,189), our research demonstrates the dual benefits of asking for process advice: it improves impressions of seekers and enhances the usefulness of advice.
