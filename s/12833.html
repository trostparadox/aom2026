Session overview

Employees who feel connected to one another tend to be happier, healthier, and more engaged than those who do not. Yet many employees feel isolated in the workplace. The papers in this symposium explore psychological barriers that keep employees from conversing, connecting, and learning from one another at work. Apalkova, Wald, and Risen investigate the circumstances in which differences of opinion give rise to dialogue in which people intend to understand one another, or debate, in which people intend to persuade one another. They find that several situational factors, such as feeling certain of one’s position or discussing a moral issue, can incite debate rather than constructive dialogue. Vani, Abel, Schroeder, and Flynn investigate why people refrain from giving others unsolicited advice. They find that prospective advice givers underestimate how much their receivers would appreciate their unsolicited advice and how connected these receivers would feel to them as a result. Atir and Epley investigate why people’s overly pessimistic expectations about talking with strangers persist over time. They find that having a positive conversation with a stranger briefly produces more positive expectations about future conversations, but that this learning dissipates—a form of fleeting generalization that impedes stable learning from experience. Finally, Nguyen and Kardas investigate why people often struggle to expand their networks more widely during networking events. They find people are more reluctant to approach strangers who are engaged in conversation than those who are standing alone, but that a straightforward intervention—a coordinating device that prompts people to finish their conversations in a synchronized manner—enables people to meet strangers and thus expand their networks more widely. Together, these papers offer theoretical insights into why employees often fail to connect at work, as well as levers for managerial intervention.
