Circular Economy and Dynamic Capabilities: AI-Powered Topic-Modeling Systematic Review

ONE
Sarah Qianlin Wang (Toronto Metropolitan University); Naïma Cherchem (Toronto Metropolitan University); Rachel Dodds (Toronto Metropolitan University); Philip Walsh
The circular economy has emerged as a practical pathway to sustainable development, yet its conceptual ambiguity has raised growing criticism. From a dynamic capabilities perspective, firms’ meaningful engagement with circular transitions requires a clearer theoretical understanding of the organisational capabilities that enable such change. To clarify these foundations, a state-of-art AI-powered topic modeling technique is applied to 8,207 circular economy review article abstracts to identify the capability patterns that consistently underpin circular economy practices across the scholarly domain. The resulting structure comprises two sensing domains (foundations and frameworks, assessments and monitoring), four seizing strategic actions (waste management, product design management, supply chain management, and carbon management), and three transforming digital technologies (Industry 4.0, additive manufacturing, and blockchain). Together, these patterns articulate how firms detect circular opportunities, mobilise resources, and reconfigure operational architectures to embed circularity. Theoretically, the study advances understanding of the dynamic capabilities that shape circular economy transitions. Methodologically, it demonstrates an end-to-end, scalable, and reproducible AI-assisted approach for synthesizing large and complex research fields.

Rethinking the Approach to Circular Innovation

ONE
Francesca Zoccarato (Free University of Bozen-Bolzano); Alessandro Narduzzo (Free University of Bozen-Bolzano)
Despite its rapid diffusion in policy, industry, and academic discourse, the Circular Economy (CE) has struggled to deliver the transformative outcomes it promises. While extensive research has focused on circular strategies such as reducing, reusing, and recycling, less attention has been paid to the theoretical foundations that explain how circularity actually emerges through innovation. This article argues that the limited impact of the CE is not primarily an implementation failure, but a theoretical one: the CE has developed largely without a strong engagement with innovation theory. Drawing on classic and contemporary perspectives in innovation studies, including recombination, bricolage, translation, and exaptation, the article reframes circularity as an outcome of innovation processes that reinterpret and reconfigure existing artifacts rather than optimize predefined material loops. Focusing on repurposing as a paradigmatic but underexplored circular innovation strategy, the analysis shows how new functions and value can emerge from existing products through creative reinterpretation rather than restoration or recycling. By bridging CE research with theories of innovation, this article advances a new perspective on circular innovation, shifting the focus from waste management and material efficiency toward processes of functional redefinition and value creation. The paper concludes by outlining implications for research, policy, and practice, highlighting how embracing innovation theory can unlock more transformative pathways toward circularity.

Localizing Circular Economies as Lived Experiences of Organizing Sustainably

ONE
Andrea Beye (Copenhagen Business School)
The circular economy has become a central framework for sustainability transitions across policy, industry, and academia, promising a shift from linear “take–make–dispose” models toward closed loops of reuse, recycling, and regeneration. Despite its growing prominence, organization scholarship has engaged only selectively with circular economy, and existing research has predominantly approached circularity as a system to be measured, a governance project to be implemented, or a future to be envisioned. In this paper, I argue that these dominant approaches share an epistemic blind spot: they overlook how circular economies are lived and organized in everyday practice. Conceptualizing circular economy as a floating signifier, the paper shows how its semantic openness has enabled multiple “ways of knowing” to coexist. I organize this heterogeneous literature into three dominant epistemic orientations— calculative–systemic, governance–implementation, and visionary–strategic—and demonstrate how each brackets lived experience as residual or derivative. To address this limitation, I advance the concept of lived experiences of circular economies, understood as the situated, embodied, socio-material, and relational practices through which circularity is enacted, negotiated, and contested in everyday life. Building on this perspective, the article proposes a theoretical and methodological toolbox for studying circular economies as lived experiences of organizing, and outlines promising areas for future research, including the role of everyday practices, spatial and temporal rhythms, and informal or marginal forms of circular organizing in sustainability transitions.

Lifecycle Thinking for Sustainability: A Literature Review and Systems Research Agenda

ONE
Brooke A. Lahneman (Montana State University); Hyeonjin Cha (Bucknell University); Suzanne Gladys Tilleman
Though growing in scholarly and practical interest, global progress toward circularity, or closed-loop systems, remains slow. Lifecycle thinking aims to promote circularity in products, organizations, and economies by measuring environmental and social impacts across these levels. Our systematic review of 153 articles shows that lifecycle thinking research remains concentrated on sustainable supply chain and industrial ecology, particularly on innovating the design and use of lifecycle analysis tools at the product level. We also find a scarcity of theoretical frameworks and contributions. To provide an overview of patterns in the existing literature, we integrate the articles on lifecycle thinking at three levels of analysis – product, organization, and economies – and identify critical research gaps. The analysis reveals two major shortcomings. First, despite the potential to generate measurable impacts from products, organizations, and economies, the lifecycle research has focused on developing product level insights from designing and applying lifecycle analysis tools. Second, little attention has been given to why and how to utilize insights from lifecycle thinking at one level (product, organization, economy) across other levels to advance circularity. We argue that investigating the potential of lifecycle thinking for advancing circularity requires examining cross-level impacts of insights found at any given level, for which we propose leveraging systems theory. We also suggest a future research agenda that combines insights across levels to enable a holistic systems perspective of lifecycle thinking to advance circularity.

Circular Economy–SDG Crossover: Review, Synthesis, and Future Directions

ONE
Abdul Rahman Butt (University of Agder); Anushree Luukela-Tandon (University of Eastern Finland); Prasoon M. Tripathi; rsha ali alghafes (Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University)
Circular Economy (CE) and Sustainable development goals (SDGs) have recently received much attention due to their ability to address the circularity and sustainability of businesses. However, literature is still limited in adequately adopting a business-oriented perspective on the relationship between CE and SDGs and instead offers a fragmented view of this relationship. This study reviews existing research to focus on CE-SDG crossover in businesses and explicate possibilities to address the gap through a systematic literature review (SLR). We identified 45 studies from WoS and SCOPUS following stringent selection criteria and performed a thematic analysis to recognize extant research themes and specific gaps through which we propose potential avenues for future research. Additionally, we comprehensively synthesize our findings to develop a multi-layered conceptual framework, incorporating intra-and-inter-organizational perspective, towards the proposed concept of circular sustainable development (CSD) thereby presenting implications to further theoretical and practical advancement in the field.
