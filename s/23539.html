AI Adoption for Project Performance: The Mechanisms of Knowledge Sharing and Technologic Readiness

TIM
Muhammad Qamar Zia; Dr Muhammad Sufyan Ramish (Dow University of health sciences)
Numerous project-based organisations are employing Generative AI (GenAI) across a wide range of project activities, which can substantially enhance project performance. This unique study, based on the transactional theory of stress, examined the interrelationship between AI adoption, knowledge sharing, project performance, and the technological readiness of project members. Drawing on survey data from 271 respondents in the construction sector, the research reveals that AI adoption has a positive influence on project performance, both directly and indirectly, through enhanced knowledge sharing. Additionally, the technological readiness of project members also moderates the linkage between AI adoption and knowledge sharing. Interestingly, our findings did not moderate the indirect effect of AI adoption on project performance through knowledge sharing.

From Regulation to Co-Learning with AI Governance in Experimental Spaces

TIM
Nguyen Thao Khuat (EBS University of Business and Law); Katrin Burmeister-Lamp (EBS University of Business and Law)
Regulating AI is challenging because system behavior is probabilistic, context-dependent, and often partially opaque, limiting the effectiveness of rule-based, ex ante governance. While AI regulation research increasingly calls for adaptive and experimental approaches, we still lack a clear understanding of how regulators and innovators jointly generate compliance-relevant knowledge under uncertainty. This paper addresses that gap by theorizing AI regulation as a co-learning process and using AI regulatory sandboxes as an empirical lens into how such learning is organized in practice. Drawing on coactive vicarious learning (CVL) theory, we conceptualize regulator–firm interaction as a discursive process of experience sharing, joint analysis, and supportive conditions that enable candid disclosure and iterative interpretation. We conduct an inductive Gioia analysis of 59 AI-related regulatory sandbox documents across eight jurisdictions. The findings identify five mechanisms that structure co-learning in AI governance: protocolized communication infrastructures, institutionalized psychological safety for vulnerability disclosure, discursive practices for governing “black box” and ethical ambiguity, temporal architectures shaping learning diffusion, and governance designs that manage regulators’ dual role as collaborators and enforcers. The paper contributes to CVL theory by extending it to public–private experimental governance and demonstrating how co-learning can be institutionalized through routines, artefacts, and accountability safeguards. It contributes to AI regulation research by reframing effective governance as the design of learning infrastructures that transform uncertainty into auditable and collectively defensible regulatory judgment.

Governing Artificial Intelligence as an Institutional Design Challenge

TIM
Imen Ameur (Hult International Business School)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in organizational decision-making and innovation processes, yet governance failures persist despite the widespread adoption of ethical principles, regulatory initiatives, and technical safeguards. Existing approaches to AI governance often remain fragmented, treating governance as a matter of compliance or technical control rather than as a core organizational capability. This paper advances a conceptual framework that reframes AI governance as an institutional design challenge central to technology and innovation management. Drawing on governance, organizational, and innovation scholarship, the paper theorizes AI governance as the alignment of normative commitments, organizational processes, and technical controls. The framework explains how misalignment across these components produces persistent governance failures, particularly in innovation-intensive contexts and with the diffusion of generative AI. By centering human judgment, decision authority, and accountability, the paper contributes a human-centered governance perspective that clarifies why governance lags behind innovation and how organizations can sustain responsible AI-enabled innovation under conditions of uncertainty.

Silenced by the Machine: Firms’ Investment in AI and the Suppression of Employee Whistleblowing

TIM
Shunyi Lai (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Haibin Yang (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
Drawing on terror management theory (TMT), this study examines how firms’ AI investment affects employee whistleblowing and the moderating role of firm-specific knowledge. We extend TMT beyond biological death to the underexplored domain of social existential threat, grounded in Marxist and Weberian philosophical foundations that frame work as a core to social essence and identity. Our empirical analysis of U.S. public firms reveals that AI investment is negatively associated with employee whistleblowing, as an AI-induced existential threat motivates employees to prioritize organizational attachment over confrontational behaviors. This negative relationship is strengthened by firm-specific knowledge, which constrains employee mobility and intensifies reliance on the current organization for self-esteem. Theoretically, we advance TMT by illuminating that firms’ AI investment can trigger a mortality salience cue and identify firm-specific knowledge as a critical boundary condition shaping TMT-driven defensive behaviors. We also contribute to the AI literature by uncovering the existential threat-based mechanism linking firms’ AI investment to employee whistleblowing.

When Enforcement is Not Control: AI Model Providers and Platform Dependence (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Adrian Schadl (University of Siegen)
Digital platform ecosystems organize value creation through interdependence between platform owners and complementors. As platform owners institutionalize Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) as boundary resources (GenAI-BR), they place generative capability into interface-level tools that complementors increasingly rely on to contribute. This creates a dependence puzzle: enforcement is visible at the interface, yet AI model providers outside the ecosystem set the capability conditions that make GenAI-BR effective. Research on GenAI-BR acknowledges shifting dependence but typically attributes where dependence consolidates from dyadic owner-complementor arrangements. However, when model providers set model-level capability conditions, interface enforcement no longer locates where control resides. Building on Resource Dependence Theory and multi-source qualitative evidence from social media and innovation platforms, this study shows that GenAI-BR become critical as platform entry and competitiveness increasingly depend on platform-provided tools and as enforcement shapes which tool use and outputs remain viable. Yet, where dependence consolidates shifts with model control: high model control anchors dependence at the platform, low model control partially leases dependence upstream to model providers. These results imply a two-layer dependence architecture that links interface enforcement with model-layer capability control, revising dependence attribution and clarifying how bottlenecks can sit outside the focal platform ecosystem.
