Repair Versus Retribution: Social Perception of Managerial Responses to Workplace Misconduct

SIM
Warren Cook (University of Wisconsin-Oshkosh); Ken Butterfield (Washington State University)
How managers respond to workplace misconduct shapes not only outcomes for victims and offenders, but also how managers themselves are perceived. While punishment communicates rule enforcement and may deter future misconduct, it can also provoke anger and retaliation. In contrast, more restorative or reparative responses prioritize repairing harm, restoring damaged relationships, and reaffirming shared values. We examine observers’ perceptions of managers in the context of employee misconduct: (1) Why do observers differ in their perceptions of managerial responses to employee misconduct? (2) What can managers do to influence those perceptions? The results reveal that managers’ response type (restorative vs. punitive vs. combined restorative/punitive) shapes observer appraisals of managers along three core dimensions: fairness, warmth, and competence. Appraisals can also depend on misconduct intensity and observers’ justice orientation. We also found that observers view managers’ use of restorative responses in a broadly favorable manner in both high-intensity and low-intensity situations, whereas punitive responses were viewed more positively in high-intensity situations relative to low-intensity situations.

The Cycle of Erosion: How Relational Work Incongruence Drives Weaker Persistent Adversity Adjustment

SIM
Wenlin ZHOU (Fudan University); Yuting Zhang (Fudan University); Xiaoquan Zhou (Fudan University)
Employing an inductive, qualitative case study of a thirty-year-old Chinese catering firm, this research examines how small service firms navigate persistent adversity through relational work. Moving beyond existing literature that frequently examine customer-oriented and employee-oriented relational work as separate pathways, we induce a process model revealing how managerial cognition focused on external relational salience triggers a state of relational work incongruence. This incongruence precipitates a relational capital erosion cycle where internal failures eventually permeate the external service interface and undermine organizational adjustment. This study makes three theoretical contributions. First, we extend relational work theory by identifying domain incongruence as a critical antecedent of organizational failure. Second, we advance stakeholder theory by uncovering a sacrificial logic that leads to systemic value destruction under extreme resource constraints. Finally, we redefine resilience in the context of persistent adversity by identifying a paradox wherein the depletion of internal relational structures for short-term survival forfeits the capacity for long-term transformation.

Moral Consistency Versus Licensing: Examining the Impact of CSR Performance on Corporate Misconduct

SIM
Qingqi Tao (University of Bath School of Management); Tahiru Azaaviele Liedong (University of Bath); Chenjian Zhang (The University of Western Australia)
Although researchers have invested effort to understand the relationship between corporate social responsibility (CSR) and corporate misconduct, they have treated CSR performance as an aggregate construct despite its multifaceted nature, and this has led to contradictory findings. Drawing on moral self-regulation theory, we address this problem by distinguishing between two types of CSR performance, namely philanthropy performance (PP) and sustainable recognition (SR), and argue how they trigger different moral mechanisms to affect corporate financial misconduct (CFM). We predict that PP grants moral credits that can license financial misconduct while recognition embeds external scrutiny that sustains moral consistency. We also predict that board governance dynamics may further condition these effects, i.e., board political connections and co-opted supervisors amplify licensing and deter consistency. Using data from Chinese listed firms, we find support for our hypotheses. Our findings contribute to the CSR-misconduct literature by providing a disaggregated understanding of the impact of different types of CSR performance on corporate misconduct.

Seeing Green Twice: How Attention to the SDGs Conditions the Social Impact of GSCM (WITHDRAWN)

SIM
Hicham Nachit (ESCA Ecole de Management); Helena Barnard (University of Pretoria)
Global corporations increasingly claim that greener supply chains advance both firm outcomes and the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), yet evidence on how SDG engagement conditions these links remains mixed. Building on the Attention-Based View (ABV), we theorize SDG adoption as an attention amplifier that shapes how green supply-chain management (GSCM) translates into corporate social performance (CSP). Using a cross-section of 239 Africa-headquartered MNEs, we estimate a model with three SDG dimensions (i.e., environmental, social, economic). Results show, first, a strong positive association between GSCM and CSP. Second, environmental and economic SDG adoption exhibit positive level effects on CSP, while the social dimension is not significant in this sample. Third, the environmental dimension negatively moderates the GSCM–CSP relationship indicating that when environmental attention is already high, additional GSCM effort yields smaller incremental social-performance gains. These findings temper linear “doing-well-by-doing-good” assumptions and suggest diminishing returns or attention reallocation once environmental commitments become extensive. We contribute an IB-grounded account of how managerial attention and institutional cues interact with supply-chain practices, and we offer guidance on sequencing and calibrating SDG adoption so that it complements, rather than crowds out, the social value created by GSCM in African MNEs.
