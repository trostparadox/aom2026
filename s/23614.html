Session overview

This session foregrounds intersectionality as a lens for understanding leadership, recognition, and identity among marginalized groups. The papers examine women of color CEOs, Global South scholars, BIPOC women entrepreneurs, migrant women's identity reconstruction, and the broader theorization of work for marginalized populations. Together, they reveal how overlapping structures of inequality shape organizational life while also generating new forms of agency and resistance.

The Contrapower Tax on Women of Color CEOs: Micropolitics, Emotions, and Organizational Sensework

DEI
Samantha E. Erskine (University of Massachusetts Boston); Diana Bilimoria (Case Western Reserve University)
Formal authority at the organizational apex does not guarantee epistemic authority—the credibility to define problems, interpret evidence, and set agendas. Through critical grounded theory analysis of 24 interviews with 15 Black women and Latina nonprofit CEOs, we theorize how institutional hegemonies are reproduced and resisted through micropolitical sensework: the situated, power-laden coupling of meaning-making and emotion work that stabilizes or destabilizes legitimacy. We show how predominantly white stakeholders deploy sensegiving and sensebreaking tactics—euphemizing, tone-policing, agenda-resetting, evoking white fragility—to decouple Black women and Latina CEOs' formal authority from epistemic credibility, while those CEOs engage in counter-sensegiving to reclaim interpretive control and sensebreaking to repair sabotaged meaning systems. This process extracts what we term a contrapower tax: the excess emotional, temporal, and political labor women of color CEOs expend managing stakeholder fragility, re-credentialing their expertise, and restoring organizational focus—labor required precisely because they hold authority. We contribute by (1) theorizing micropolitical sensework as the meshing of sensegiving/sensebreaking with discursive emotion work, extending power scholarship beyond cognition to embodied, affective practice; (2) introducing the contrapower tax to explain how formal authority paradoxically increases regulation of women of color leaders; and (3) specifying how the coupling of meaning and emotion orients attention, procedures, and resources to reproduce or contest hegemony at the apex. Our findings advance intersectional leadership theory and reveal mechanisms through which inequality regimes persist even when women of color occupy the top office.

Stepping into the Matrix: A Typology of BIPOC Women Entrepreneurs’ Authentic Signaling

DEI
Tina Sharifi (MacEwan University)
This paper explores the challenges of constructing a legitimate entrepreneurial identity faced by Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC) women. Building on signaling theory, the study examines how BIPOC women entrepreneurs navigate the intersectionality of race, gender, and other identity markers, balancing authenticity with external expectations of legitimacy. Employing a qualitative approach that combines qualitative content analysis and reflexive thematic analysis of public narratives, the paper develops a four-part typology, namely, Storyteller, Architect, Negotiator, and Self-Actualizer, that captures how BIPOC women use authenticity and intersectional identities as strategic tools in signaling legitimacy. The typology contributes to entrepreneurship and signaling theory by extending existing work on authenticity and legitimacy through an intersectional lens and offers a framework for understanding how marginalized entrepreneurs, including BIPOC women, negotiate normative entrepreneurial expectations.

Who Am I Now? Adaptive Identity Capital and the Lives of Migrant Women

DEI
Oyovwe Akpoigbe (Not Associated)
In this paper, I develop the concept of Adaptive Identity Capital to explain how migrant women rebuild belonging and a sense of self amid disruption and inequality. Drawing on identity work, intersectionality, and social capital theories, I argue that belonging is not a fixed state, but a capacity cultivated through emotional resilience, relational reach, and reflective continuity. Existing research explains how people make sense of who they are but rarely how they sustain belonging when recognition is fragile or absent. Focusing on the lived experiences of migrant women, I propose that Adaptive Identity Capital enables individuals to transform displacement into renewal by linking inner meaning-making with external validation. This framework integrates psychological, structural, and relational dimensions of adaptation, extending current understandings of identity reconstruction. The study contributes to theory by conceptualizing belonging as a generative process that connects personal coherence with social recognition, and to practice by offering insights for organizations and communities that seek to foster inclusion as developmental work. Keywords: Adaptive Identity Capital, Identity Work, Intersectionality, Belonging, Social Capital, Migration, Inequality
