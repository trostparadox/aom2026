Session overview

Advice interactions are pivotal “pathways” through which employees access knowledge, build credibility, and accumulate informal influence, yet gender inequality research has focused more on “gateway” decisions (e.g., hiring, promotion) than on how everyday advice exchanges are psychologically interpreted. We examine advice evaluation—how useful individuals perceive the advice they received and the advice they gave—to identify a subtle mechanism through which gendered dynamics may persist. Drawing on social role perspectives, we theorize that advice roles create asymmetric identity implications: receiving advice can signal dependence (particularly threatening for men under agency norms), whereas giving advice can signal competence and influence (potentially costly for women under modesty and communion norms). We further argue that team climate cues shape these evaluations in role-specific ways. Using time-lagged survey data from 289 employees nested in 74 teams, we test whether gender predicts advice usefulness evaluations and whether psychological safety (a cue of interpersonal risk protection) and knowledge exchange norms (a cue that expertise sharing is valued) moderate these effects. Results show no overall gender difference in evaluations of advice received, but women evaluate the advice they give as less useful than men. Consistent with our contingency arguments, psychological safety strengthens men’s perceived usefulness of advice received, whereas knowledge exchange norms strengthen women’s perceived usefulness of advice given. These findings position advice evaluation as an underexamined psychological pathway through which gendered participation and influence may compound, and they highlight climate-focused interventions that legitimize advice receiving for men and validate advice giving for women.
