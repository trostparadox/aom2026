The Algorithmic Coordination of the Commons

SIM
Tobey Scharding (Rutgers Business School)
Organizations increasingly deploy algorithms to coordinate common resources such as roads, social networks, and knowledge infrastructures. While existing research on commons governance emphasizes restraining individual use to prevent depletion, algorithmic coordination of commons raises normative problems (e.g., allocating the advantages produced by algorithmic coordination) that prevailing frameworks do not fully address. Drawing on Locke’s principle of best common advantage, I argue that algorithmic design choices about allocating advantage generate systematic ethical risks of manipulation and exclusion. Applying this standard to GPS navigation, social media, and large language models illustrates how particular design logics (e.g., ranked news feeds, selective routing) can reduce the advantages users gain from commons and/or arbitrarily differentiate among users. The analysis reframes algorithmic ethics as a problem of organizational design and responsibility, extending commons governance theory to digitally mediated forms of coordination and opening new avenues for research on algorithmic institutions and digital governance.

Leveraging and Relationships in Deliberative Sensemaking of Materiality in ESG

SIM
Jamie Lesley Plaatjes (University of Exeter); Onna Malou van den Broek (Copenhagen Business School)
As ESG investing becomes central to attempts at “greening” capitalism and promoting more responsible forms of finance, understanding how materiality is constructed within these processes is critical. This paper asks the question: how do ESG consultants deliberatively make sense of materiality for investors? Drawing on immersive qualitative research within a South African ESG consultancy, we examine how consultants, as intermediaries in an emerging market context, interpret and mediate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) issues within responsible and green investment. Using a deliberative sensemaking lens, we explore how consultants engage in both prospective and retrospective meaning-making as they navigate tensions between impact and risk, manage client relationships, and influence corporate behaviour. We make three key contributions: first, we show that materiality is constructed. Second, we conceptualise leverage as both a cognitive framing device and a practical tool that enables a light-touch mode of intervention, reflecting depoliticising efforts. Third, we demonstrate how consultant-investor relationships shape how materiality is constructed and shared, elevating the usefulness of deliberative forms of sensemaking. Our study contributes to just sustainability transition scholarship by highlighting how ESG consultants can and do reconstruct materiality, providing evidence that the ‘business case’ for investment can be shaped and deconstructed by these intermediaries.

How Does Either/Or Become Both/And?——Tracing the Attention-Driven Process of ESG Profit Symbiosis

SIM
Hong Xianpei (Huazhong Agricultural University); Xiaojun Zhang (Huazhong Agricultural University); Xuhong Zhao (Huazhong Agricultural University); Yang Yu (Huazhong Agricultural University); Yiting Zeng (Huazhong Agricultural University); Dehua He (Huazhong Agricultural University)
This study explains how organizations transform the ESG–profitability tension from an either–or tradeoff into a both–and symbiosis and how organizational attention enables this shift. Drawing on a longitudinal single-case study of Industrial Bank Co., Ltd. (CIB) from 1988 to the present, we combine interviews and archival materials and apply process tracing and coding to uncover the mechanisms through which ESG enters the strategic agenda, attracts resources, and becomes institutionalized. We find that ESG–profit symbiosis is not a discrete strategic choice but a multi-stage process driven by attentional reconfiguration. Survival pressures initially lock attention onto profitability, relegating ESG to instrumental philanthropy. As institutional signals and opportunity structures intensify, attention is redirected toward green finance, which is then embedded in replicable routines. Subsequent structuring through governance and organizational carriers enables the parallel pursuit of ESG and profit, turning green finance into a validated business line. Finally, regulatory constraints and capital-market preferences institutionalize and culturally normalize ESG as routine practice. We synthesize these insights into an attention-driven paradox transformation model and show how institutional design and organizational arrangements stabilize ESG–profit symbiosis in financial institutions.
