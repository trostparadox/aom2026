Anti-Immigrant Hate Crimes and Cross-Border Marriage Markets: Evidence from a Marriage Platform

TIM
Eva Dziadula (University of Notre Dame); Anand Gopal (University of Maryland College Park); Sabari Rajan Karmegam (George Mason University); Madeline Zavodny (University of North Florida)
Digital platforms increasingly record real-time behavioral responses to salient social events, offering insight into preference shifts that are difficult to observe in traditional data. We examine how a high-profile anti-immigrant hate crime in the United States affected matrimonial preferences on a major Indian online matrimonial platform. Drawing on availability cascade theory, we argue that extensive media coverage of the February 2017 Kansas hate crime increased perceived risks associated with marriage-based relocation to the US. Using a differences-in-differences design that compares US-based Indian profiles to Canadian controls, we find that US-based Indian men experienced significant declines in matrimonial interest following the hate crime, including fewer profile views and expressions of interest, with effects intensifying over time. These effects are concentrated among non-citizens and highly educated men and are not observed for US-based Indian women. Our findings demonstrate how platform data can serve as early indicators of shifts in marriage and migration preferences and show how localized acts of violence can generate broader consequences for cross-border family formation.

Asymmetric Peer Effects on Digital Innovation in Hospitality and Tourism Industry

TIM
Xuerong Peng (Zhejiang Gongshang University); Tingyu Zhu (Fudan University); Seoki Lee (The Pennsylvania State University); xinhua ? (Zhejiang Gongshang University)
This study investigates how industry and geographic peers asymmetrically influence hospitality and tourism (H&T) firms' digital innovation through efficiency-seeking and legitimacy-seeking mechanisms, and how governance structures moderate these effects. Analyzing panel data of 899 firm-year observations from 66 listed Chinese H&T firms (2001-2022), we find that industry peers significantly drive focal H&T firms' digital innovation, while geographic peers show no significant effect. External (government digital orientation) and internal governance (state ownership, board independence) amplify industry peer effects through distinct mechanisms: government digital orientation heightens legitimacy pressures while enhancing peer visibility; state ownership intensifies executives' reliance on industry benchmarking to mitigate career risks; board independence strengthens firms' capacity to identify and learn from industry peers' digitalization. We reveal that digital innovation's primary efficiency-driven nature and codifiable knowledge make industry peers more influential than geographic peers in H&T's low-tech context, guiding H&T firms toward industry-centered benchmarking strategies for high-uncertainty digital investments.

Do Biomedical Researchers Study Topics Related to Their Own Ethnicity?: Evidence from the NIH

TIM
Wesley Greenblatt (Harvard University); Young Hou; Sukhun Kang (University of California Santa Barbara)
The scientific interests of researchers are fundamental to determining the direction of innovation, yet may be shaped by an investigator’s background, exposure to topics, and perceived problem importance. We study the role of ethnicity in shaping investigator research direction at the National Institutes of Health, analyzing over 100,000 R01 grants from more than 32,000 distinct investigators between 1980 and 2015. We combine image and name-based predictions with clerical review to identify the ethnicity of investigators. Across four measures of the ethnic orientation of research, including relative disease mortality, and keywords related to ethnicity, geographic location, and socioeconomic status and healthcare disparities, we find investigators are more likely to study topics related to their own ethnic background. We also examine if minority researchers have a disparity in the resources available to them for conducting research, which may contribute to distortions in the types of innovations produced. We find that while there is a disparity in the raw grant renewal rate of minority investigators, this disparity resolves after controlling for investigator, grant, and institutional characteristics. These findings suggest how frictions for minorities entering and remaining in research careers may impact the development of innovations that address the healthcare needs of minority populations.

Ethnic Singulars and Impactful Innovation in R&D Teams (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Thomas Yoon (University of Liverpool); Gina Dokko (University of California Davis)
In this paper, we bring a characteristic of group composition into research linking diverse inputs and innovation: ethnic singulars, which we define as individuals who are the sole members of an ethnicity on a team. Using a sample of patenting teams in the U.S. Information and Communications Technology industry, we hypothesize and find that the proportion of ethnic singulars is positively related to the impactfulness of a team’s patents, over and above any positive effects for ethnic diversity. Consistent with our theory, we find that the effect of singulars on impactfulness is strengthened when there is an improved ability to collaborate, specifically, when singulars have worked with other team members before or are geographically co-located with other team members.

Status, Structure, and Citation Disadvantage: How Niche Dynamics Shape Reverse AI Innovation in U.S.

TIM
Qiuming Zhang (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Amit Jain (National University of Singapore)
We investigate why Chinese reverse AI innovations filed in the United States attract substantially fewer external citations than comparable U.S. domestic AI innovations. Building on niche theory, we conceptualize external citations as niche-entry events and theorize that diffusion is shaped jointly by the status of niche occupants and the structure of the niche network. Using a matched sample of 4,274 AI patents (2,137 Chinese reverse innovations and 2,137 U.S. domestic innovations) and 23,531 external citation events, we employ nonparametric event history analysis to compare niche-entry dynamics across the two innovation sources. We find that the citation disadvantage of Chinese reverse innovations is, at its core, a status disadvantage: higher focal-firm status and association with high-status nonfocal firms significantly increase niche entry, with effects that are markedly stronger for Chinese reverse innovations than for U.S. domestic innovations. Network structure exerts asymmetric influences: direct ties (citations made by focal innovation) consistently promote entry for U.S. domestic innovations but hinder Chinese reverse innovations, whereas indirect ties (second-order overlaps) significantly suppress entry for Chinese reverse innovations while remaining negligible for U.S. domestic innovations, indicating structural lock-in under dense local closure. Our results advance theory by (1) reframing reverse-innovation diffusion barriers as status-based and structure-contingent mechanisms within technological niches; (2) distinguishing the diffusion roles of direct versus indirect ties; and (3) demonstrating source-dependent elasticities in niche responses.
