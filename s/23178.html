The Pursuit for Certain Signals: Uncertainty Reduction and Signaling in Online Job Search

CTO
Raka Bhattacharyya (University of Southern California); Marlon DeMarcie Twyman (University of Southern California)
As normative job search and networking procedures become digitized, employers and job seekers increasingly consult job search platforms such as LinkedIn, Indeed, and Glassdoor to most effectively identify and connect with one another. However, despite the flexibility and customizability of job search sites, beginning professionals must regulate feelings of uncertainty and maintain motivation throughout their job search processes within a vacillating workforce. Utilizing Uncertainty Reduction Theory and signal theory as the foundation of our inquiries, we interviewed aspiring data science professionals to further understand uncertainty regulation through the information applicants both share and receive during online job search. We discovered that the employers’ current online communication processes, which were considered to be asymmetric and lacking by the participants, increased applicants’ uncertainty and disillusioned their job search processes. Through this paper, we developed a comprehensive understanding of the communication and information beginning professionals require and expect to inform their job search processes. With disbalanced communication serving as a critical barrier between employers and promising candidates, we seek to inform organizations, professional counselors, and job search platforms hoping to support and connect with the next generation of professionals.

When Voice Backfires: Internal Employee Voice Channel and Declining Employee Satisfaction

CTO
zhao Xu (Renmin University of China); Jingmei Zhou; Kanliang Wang (Renmin University of China)
Employee voice plays a critical role in shaping employee satisfaction and organizational outcomes. With the rise of digital platforms, firms increasingly adopt platform-mediated employee voice channels that allow employees to publicly share work experiences and concerns, often accompanied by managerial responses. However, the causal impact of introducing such channel on employee satisfaction remains unclear. Exploiting a quasi-experiment on a large job search platform, where an employee voice channel is introduced for firm, we employ a difference-in-differences design to identify its causal effect on employee satisfaction. We find that the introduction of the voice channel leads to a significant decline in employee satisfaction. This negative effect is more pronounced in highly active firms and among senior employees, while it is attenuated for employees with longer tenure. Mechanism analyses grounded in affective events theory and expectation-disconfirmation theory indicate that the feedback frustration effect constitutes the dominant mechanism. When voiced concerns are not adequately addressed, unmet expectations trigger frustration and disappointment, leading to lower satisfaction. We further show that the voice channel is effective in alleviating surface-level operational issues but less effective in resolving deeper structural concerns. This study provides causal evidence of the unintended consequences of platform-mediated employee voice channel and offers practical implications for the design and governance of employee voice systems.

Why Do Subscribers Leave? How Fatigue and Social Pressure Drive Subscription Cancellation

CTO
John Sheng-Han Yang (National Taipei University of Business); Tzu-Chen Yen (National Taipei University of Business); Chien Miao Ting (National Taipei University of Business); Pei Yi Chung (National Taipei University of Business); Jun-Chun Zeng (National Taipei University of Business)
Digital subscriptions have become a part of modern consumer life, yet an alarming 39% of digital subscribers consider canceling at least one service due to overwhelming management demands and costs, according to Deloitte's 2025 report. Subscription-based business models have increased dramatically in digital services, but subscriber retention is still a challenge when users manage multiple recurring payments. This study focuses on Expectation– Disconfirmation Theory with the Stimulus–Organism–Response framework to examine unsubscribe intention among paying subscribers. Analysis of data from 311 subscribers reveals that subscription fatigue serves as a major mechanism linking contextual pressures to discontinuation propensity. Financial pressure increases subscription fatigue and indirectly affects unsubscribe intention. Social pressure demonstrates a dual role: it increases fatigue while simultaneously reducing unsubscribe intention through relational and reputational costs. Perceived confirmation is positively associated with unsubscribe intention, supporting a conditional interpretation in which favorable service evaluations may coexist with cancellation readiness. This research makes three theoretical contributions. This study makes three theoretical contributions. First, it establishes subscription fatigue as a strain-based mechanism that extends expectation–disconfirmation theory. Unlike classic satisfaction, which evaluates alignment with prior expectations, subscription fatigue highlights the emotional burden from managing subscriptions. Second, it reveals the dual effect of social pressure in subscription contexts. Third, it provides evidence for a conditional interpretation of discontinuation. For practitioners, reducing the burden of subscription management and designing features that balance social-embedding benefits with minimal impression-management demands may improve retention.

How Biopolitical Logics and Algorithmic Envelopment Reconfigure Consumer Behaviour

CTO
Isabel Bess Goddard (University of Oxford)
As AI takes on newly central roles in corporate life, recent research has documented how organizational processes are being adapted to support algorithmic integration through techniques including black-boxing, envelopment, and workplace jurisdiction (Anthony, 2021; Kim et al., 2025; Marti et al., 2024). While previous scholarship has explored these themes by investigating the relationship between algorithms and industry members or organizational employees, I propose that similar investigations are needed to evaluate the influences of algorithmic logic on consumer behavior. I explore this process by drawing on Floridi’s framework of envelopment and Foucault’s theorization of biopower to analyze how organizational leaders make the data environment surrounding their AI systems more conducive and legible for algorithmic functioning by encouraging users to alter their behavior in strategic ways, ultimately allowing for greater optimization, value extraction, and control over end-users (Floridi, 2019; Foucault, 2010; Gillespie, 2017). Thus, the question that guides this study is: how might algorithmic logics encourage organizations to coerce consumer behavior into forms more conducive to AI functionality? I explore this question in the context of dating apps. Specifically, I examine how dating app firms develop product features that aim to convert user behavior into more algorithm-friendly data sets. In brief, I find that dating app firms engage in strategies including drawing a narrow boundary around the algorithm by targeting niche and contained user groups; facilitating high quality input data through identity verification tools; and growing the quantity of data through in-person “brand activation” events, essential to supporting the accuracy of the algorithm’s predictions (Robbins, 2020). Through this study, I make two critical contributions to the algorithmic and organizational literature. First, I show that in order for AI innovations to permeate consumer behavior, previous processes must be adapted to facilitate conductive algorithmic environments. This study draws attention to the ways by which consumer actions must be made legible for AI through techniques that coerce end-user behavior into algorithmically digestible data. Second, in building on previous literature illustrating how the work of algorithmic envelopment modifies industry member behavior, I show that it also changes consumer actions in ways that allow for greater optimization, value extraction, and control (Foucault, 2020). In so doing, this study contributes a vital new perspective to research exploring emerging trends in AI and organizations.
