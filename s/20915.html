Automation, Technology, and Pressure: Examining Maladaptive Coping Mechanisms in Work Environments

Authors: Tera Webb University of Alabama at Birmingham; Philip Cendoma University of Alabama at Birmingham; Beata Buchelt Krakow University of Economics; Anthony C. Hood University of Alabama at Birmingham

Session overview

In high-throughput, technology-heavy healthcare environments, a significant gap often exists between work as imagined (formal protocols) and work as done (actual practice). This study investigates how frontline clinical laboratory professionals navigate this gap through maladaptive coping strategies, including workarounds, shadow knowledge (informal, undocumented practices), and role spillover (absorbing responsibilities outside formal boundaries). While these behaviors may sustain short-term performance, they can become normalized, potentially obscuring systemic issues and introducing safety risks. Drawing on Adaptive Structuration Theory, Sociotechnical Systems Theory, and Normalization Process Theory, this research frames these behaviors as structurally conditioned responses to system misalignments rather than individual noncompliance. We propose a conceptual model where perceived departmental support and psychological safety serve as critical organizational conditions that influence whether operational problems are surfaced or managed through informal, "silent" strategies. Specifically, the study hypothesizes that higher levels of support and safety will be negatively associated with the use of maladaptive strategies, with psychological safety acting as a key mediator. Using a cross-sectional survey design with laboratory professionals in acute care hospitals, this study will utilize validated scales to measure workplace climate and coping frequencies. The findings are expected to contribute to organizational behavior and theory by extending workaround research into the understudied context of the clinical laboratory. Furthermore, this work highlights the importance for leadership to treat these coping behaviors as signals of structural strain and opportunities for system redesign rather than mere rule-breaking.
