Supervisor-Subordinate Optimism Congruence on Turnover Intention Through Supervisor Approachability

OB
Huimiao Zheng; Scott Dust (University of Cincinnati); Minya Xu (Peking University); Sharmeen Merchant (University of Cincinnati)
Prior research has largely taken a leader-centric view of optimism, yielding mixed findings. We adopt a dyadic perspective, suggesting that subordinates evaluate relationship quality with their supervisors through their own sense of optimism. Drawing from social exchange theory, we hypothesize that as optimism levels between supervisors and subordinates become more congruent, subordinates’ perceptions of supervisor approachability increase. Moreover, we suggest that this perception is higher at higher levels of optimism congruence compared to lower levels. Additionally, we hypothesize that supervisor approachability is greater when subordinates have higher levels of optimism than supervisors, rather than when supervisors are more optimistic than subordinates. We further propose that approachability mediates the effect of supervisor-subordinate optimism congruence on turnover intention. Finally, we hypothesize that subordinate’s perceptions of organizational power distance neutralize the congruence effects. Using multi-wave data from full-time employees, we find support for our hypotheses and discuss implications for supervisor-subordinate relationships.

Impact of Self–Coworkers Emotion Regulation Congruence on Work Outcomes

OB
Ya Liu (Wuhan University); Russell Eric Johnson (Michigan State University)
Emotion regulation in the workplace has garnered substantial attention from organizational scholars over the past four decades, with research flourishing in understanding both intrapersonal and interpersonal processes of emotion regulation. Although prior research has illuminated how emotion regulation operates at both the individual and dyadic levels, social interactions between emotional laborers and interaction partners do not occur in a vacuum. Instead, employees qua third party observers are likely impacted by the emotional regulation strategies used by their coworkers. Drawing on person–environment fit theory, we offer a comprehensive and nuanced perspective on the interplay between a focal employee’s and coworkers’ emotion regulation, and in doing so address how perceived congruence and incongruence in emotion regulation influence the focal employee’s work outcomes. Through two experimental studies manipulating emotion regulation congruence and a field study utilizing polynomial regression analysis, we demonstrate that while emotion regulation congruence in general enhances organizational citizenship behavior and well-being via an improved sense of belongingness, sense of belongingness differs between congruence at higher versus lower levels of emotion regulation. Overall, our research provides both theoretical and practical implications for employees’ emotion regulation in the workplace.

Beyond Feeling Good: Emotional Fit and Daily Functioning at Work

OB
Leon Joshua Bach (University of Zurich); Jochen I. Menges (University of Zurich)
Positive emotions are widely regarded as beneficial for performance and well-being at work. We argue that this conclusion overlooks an important source of variation: employees differ in which positive emotions they want to experience. In this research, we built on a new concept, emotional fit, defined as the degree to which employees’ experienced emotions align with their personally preferred work emotions. We propose that emotional fit, rather than positive emotions in general, is a key mechanism through which emotions support daily functioning and longer-term work outcomes. We test these ideas in a two-week field study that combines daily experience sampling with a brief, preference-based intervention. Within-person analyses show that on days when employees experience higher emotional fit, they report greater flow and make more progress toward their work goals, above and beyond general positive emotions. We further examine whether emotional fit can be intentionally shaped through an intervention that helps employees plan for their preferred emotional experiences at work, and assess whether accumulated emotional fit relates to task performance and turnover intentions over time. Together, these findings suggest that the benefits of emotions at work depend not only on whether emotions are positive, but on whether they are personally meaningful. By shifting attention from emotional valence to emotional alignment, this research advances a more individualized perspective on emotions at work and identifies emotional fit as a promising target for theory and intervention.

How Leader-Follower Health Promotion Behavior (In)Congruence Shapes Follower Withdrawal Behavior

OB
Luyuan Jiang; Qing Liu (Aston University); Yuqing Gan; Yutong Ye
Employee health is foundational for both individuals and organizations, and prior research has demonstrated the benefits of leader health promotion behavior for employee health and well-being. However, little is known about how such behaviors are socially interpreted by followers and how these interpretations shape broader work-related outcomes. Drawing on social perception theory, we propose that followers evaluate a leader's health promotion behavior relationally by comparing it with their own health promotion practices, and that this comparison informs their judgments of the leader's warmth. We argue that congruence between leader and follower health promotion behavior signals alignment in values and situational understanding, fostering perceptions of leader warmth and reducing workplace withdrawal behavior, whereas incongruence undermines warmth and increases withdrawal. Results from a three-wave, multisource survey from 35 leaders and 181 followers support the proposed research model. By examining leader-follower congruence in health promotion behavior, this study extends leadership and workplace health literatures by demonstrating that incongruence between leaders’ and followers’ health promotion behavior can carry relational costs that extend beyond health outcomes to influence everyday organizational functioning.

Workplace Wellbeing from an Attachment Perspective: The Family-of-Origin Relational Environment

OB
Emma Lei Jing (NEOMA Business School); Elise Yu (NEOMA Business School); Jeffrey Yip (Simon Fraser University)
Drawing on attachment theory, we introduce the concept of family-of-origin relational environment to explain how a person’s relational experiences in different domains influence workplace wellbeing. We develop and test a theory of temporal relational (in)congruence—the extent to which workplace relational experiences confirm or disconfirm the relational expectations shaped in the family of origin. We propose that such (in)congruence influences workplace wellbeing through employees’ attachment bond with their workplace and that these effects are buffered when leaders provide secure-base attachment. Findings from a survey of 817 working adults provide partial support for our model. This study extends attachment theory into organizational settings by theorizing how cross-domain, temporally distant relational expectations (i.e., family of origin to workplace) shape workplace attachment processes and, in turn, employee wellbeing.
