Measuring Evidence-Based Decision-Making Behavior: A Scale Development Study

OB
Qing Ye (Tilburg University); Tina Sahakian (Tilburg University); Christine Yin Man Fong (Tilburg University); Brigitte Kroon (Tilburg University)
Evidence-based decision-making (EBDM) has long been claimed effective, but empirical support is limited in organizational settings, partly due to the lack of a reliable, valid scale to measure general EBDM behavior in a wider industrial context. This study addresses this gap by developing the Evidence-Based Decision-making Behavior Scale (EBDM-BS) to assess how often practitioners engage in EBDM behavior at work. In Study 1, we generated and refined items based on a systematic review of the EBDM literature, cognitive interviews with practitioners, and expert panels with EBDM researchers. Across study 2, 3 and 4, we provided empirical evidence to show the reliability, factorial structure, discriminant validity, nomological network as well as the criterion validity of the EBDM-BS. This scale development and validation study provides an assessment tool to measure EBDM behavior and will accordingly enable theoretical and empirical investigations of this construct in organizational settings across industries. This study can also help practitioners to assess their organizational and managerial EBDM behavior and further optimize their implementation of EBDM in the workplace, thereby improving organizational performance and achieving sustainable development in a long term. This study bears great significance to bridge the gap between research and practice.

Anticipating High Performance: The Double-Edged Effects of Leader Expectations on Followers

OB
Jaewook Lee (Kent State University); Cristiano Guarana (Indiana University Bloomington)
Drawing upon the approach-avoidance motivation framework and the perfectionism literature, this research examines how leader expectations for followers’ high performance can increase followers’ work engagement and withdrawal by triggering their adaptive and maladaptive forms of perfectionism. On the one hand, we suggest that leader expectations can enhance followers’ work engagement by triggering their excellence-seeking perfectionism. On the other hand, we suggest that leader expectations can also increase followers’ work withdrawal by inducing their failure-avoiding perfectionism. Additionally, we suggest that leader helping behaviors strengthen the effect of leader expectations on followers’ work engagement via excellence-seeking perfectionism and weaken the effect of leader expectations on followers’ work withdrawal via failure-avoiding perfectionism. We tested our model by conducting an experimental vignette study and a multiwave field study. The results of both studies demonstrate that leader expectations are positively related to followers’ excellence-seeking and failure-avoiding perfectionism. Also, excellence-seeking and failure-avoiding perfectionism mediate the positive effects of leader expectations on work engagement and work withdrawal, respectively. Furthermore, leader helping behaviors significantly strengthen the effect of leader expectations on followers’ excellence-seeking perfectionism and the indirect effect on their work engagement via excellence-seeking perfectionism. This research provides several theoretical and practical implications about leadership behaviors and perfectionism.

High Performance or Just Giving a Performance? The Effects of Performative Work on Promotability

OB
Savannah Asay (Indiana University)
The identification and recognition of high performance is a central objective for organizations, as both managers and organizations seek to distinguish top performers, while employees actively strive to be perceived as such. In spite of this, there may be incongruities between what managers know and what employees want them to know about their performance quality, leading employees to engage in performative work— highly visible work behaviors employees engage in with the intent of signaling high performance. In line with this, I draw upon signaling theory to propose that performative work serves as a costly signal of high performance, resulting in reputational rewards from managers. Further, I propose job performance as a moderator of these effects, such that low performers who engage in performative work incur penalty costs in the form of decreased perceptions of competence, commitment, and promotability. To test these ideas, in Study 1 I generate and validate a measure of performative work. In Study 2, I conduct an experimental test of the effects of performative work, finding that performative work results in increased managerial perceptions of work commitment, competence, and promotability. Furthermore, I found support for job performance as a moderator of these relations (albeit in the opposite direction than hypothesized), suggesting that low-performing employees receive greater (rather than lesser) reputational rewards upon engaging in performative work. I discuss the implications for theory and practice.

Effects of Newcomer Reputation for Creativity on Peer Learning and Covert Competition

OB
Min Li (University of Science and Technology of China); Qingxiong Weng; Yanfen Wang (University of Science and Technology of China); Lixin Chen (University of Science and Technology of China)
Companies often recruit creative employees to tackle complex external competitive environments. Prior research has shown that creative newcomers can reap significant benefits. However, it has overlooked the interpersonal reactions that peer newcomer may enact to compensate for their gap with the creative newcomer. Drawing on cognitive appraisal theory, we theorize that a creative newcomer's reputation for creativity influences their peer newcomer' learning behaviors and covert competition, via admiration and hostility, respectively. Moreover, when the peer newcomer's zero-sum mindset was lower, the positive indirect effect of newcomer reputation for creativity on learning behaviors was stronger, whereas the indirect effect of newcomer reputation for creativity on covert competition was weaker. The results of two experimental and two time-lagged studies support these hypotheses. These findings provide a nuanced explanation for the unintended consequences of newcomer reputation for creativity and offer important implications for organizations to enhance the effectiveness of newcomer socialization.
