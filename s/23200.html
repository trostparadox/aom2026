All Creatures Great and Small: Managerial Experience, Prior Firm Size, and Startup Performance

ENT
Rui Agostinho (Instituto Superior Técnico, Universidade de Lisboa, Lisbon, Portugal); Jolanda Hessels (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Hugo Castro Silva; Rui Baptista (Universidade de Lisboa)
We investigate how the career experiences of both founders and employees influence startup performance. In the empirical analysis we combine two rich Portuguese datasets with extensive information at the firm and worker levels: Quadros de Pessoal and Sistema de Contas Integradas de Empresas. Analysing firms created from 2010 to 2019 using correlated random effects models we find that startups whose founders and employees stem from small firms, initially perform better. Over time, however, startups with founders and employees from large firms, especially those with managerial experience, perform better.

Asking as Entrepreneurial Practice: How Experience Becomes Expertise

ENT
Tiago Ratinho; Saras Sarasvathy (University of Virginia)
Entrepreneurial expertise is often invoked but rarely specified: What, exactly, do entrepreneurs get better at, and how do they get better at it? We argue that expertise in entrepreneurship develops through repeated engagement in a particular behavior we call “the ask”: a direct request for a concrete commitment from a specific stakeholder, made under uncertainty, with the goal of moving the venture forward. We theorize that as entrepreneurs make these asks, they receive immediate feedback about who will agree to what, and on what terms. Over time, this feedback produces calibrated expectations - more accurate, stakeholder-specific judgments about what is realistically obtainable - and those calibrated expectations, in turn, support expert decision-making logics under uncertainty. We test this mechanism using survey data from 620 entrepreneurs in the United States and India. We find that ask experience predicts calibrated expectations, and calibrated expectations predict the use of expert decision-making logics, but ask experience per se does not directly do so. In other words, experience becomes expertise through cognitive calibration, not simple repetition. We discuss implications for theory, practice, and entrepreneurship education.

Barriers to Firm Innovation: Exploring the Moderating Role of Criminal Activity

ENT
George Saridakis (University of Kent); Anoosheh Rostamkalaei; Sandra Sookram (The University of the West Indies, St. Augustine Campus); Anne-Marie Mohammed (The University of the West Indies, St. Augustine Campus)
Employing Caribbean data from the Innovation, Firm Performance, and Gender survey to examine the relationship between barriers, crime, and firm innovation. We investigate how internal and external innovation barriers relate to the various forms of innovation and whether exposure to a crime-prone environment hinders innovation. Ultimately, we explore the interaction between barriers and crime to determine potential moderating effects. We found that barriers related to organizational capabilities and to costs and risks reduce the likelihood of innovation; conversely, Market conditions appear to be positively linked with increased innovation. Exposure to crime alone does not significantly reduce innovation; however, crime exacerbates the negative impact of barriers on innovation. Bribery reduced the propensity to innovate. These findings offer important policy implications for small and medium-sized firms, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to address both structural barriers and crime-related challenges to enhance firm competitiveness and innovation in the Caribbean.

Entrepreneurial Ecosystems Factors Shaping Community and Clean-Tech Ventures: A Longitudinal Study

ENT
Jason Roncancio (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Thomas Bauwens (Rotterdam School of Management)
Entrepreneurial ecosystem research often assumes a uniform payoff: stronger ecosystems produce more entrepreneurship. This view overlooks venture heterogeneity. In Europe’s energy transition, we contrast two venture forms sharing a technological domain but organized around distinct ownership and governance logics: community energy ventures and cleantech startups. We assemble a NUTS-2 panel (1990-2024) combining nearly 10,000 community initiatives, regional cleantech startup activity, indicators of civic capacity, entrepreneurial culture, innovation and finance, and the staggered adoption of feed-in tariffs and coordination regimes. Using Poisson models and event-study designs around policy shifts, we show that conventional ecosystem pillars do not raise all venture types equally. Entrepreneurial culture, finance, and innovation capacity exhibit asymmetric effects across forms. Community energy is more likely where civic coordination and delivery capacity align with policies and institutions, rather than where market resources alone dominate. These findings reposition entrepreneurial ecosystem quality as a driver of venture form, not just venture volume.

When Diversity Pays Off:Founder Experience, Capital, and Ecosystem Logics in Clean Energy Innovation

ENT
Xi Zhang (New Jersey Institute of Technology); Shanthi Gopalakrishnan (New Jersey Institute of Technology)
How do founding team characteristics translate into innovation outcomes in startups, and under what conditions does this relationship hold? Drawing on knowledge configuration theory and institutional logics, we examine how founding team experience heterogeneity influences innovation outcomes through capital acquisition, and how this process is moderated by firm size and regional entrepreneurial ecosystem capacity. Using a manually curated panel of 202 U.S. clean energy startups founded between 2009 and 2021, we find that heterogeneous founding teams enhance a venture’s intellectual property (IP) not directly, but indirectly by securing external capital. This mediating effect is significantly stronger in larger firms, which are better positioned to absorb and coordinate diverse experiences. Surprisingly, the relationship weakens in high-capacity ecosystems, where institutionalized support structures reduce the marginal value of capital as a driver of innovation. Our findings offer new insights into how experiential diversity is activated through resource orchestration and shaped by the prevailing logics of innovation across ecosystems. The study contributes to research on entrepreneurial strategy, innovation management, and the contextual dynamics of clean energy venturing.
