From Asset to Liability: When and Why Managers Penalize Employees’ Family Motivation

OB
Yali CHE (Zhejiang University); Fan Zhou; Chenxi HAN (Zhejiang University)
Research examining family motivation from the employee perspective has firmly established its role as a positive driver of effort and performance. Our study shifts the perspective to the managerial lens and reveals a key paradox: this positive driver can lead to adverse career outcomes. Specifically, drawing upon signaling theory and work-family literature, we propose that the observed employee family motivation is negatively related to perceived work–family centrality. Furthermore, the ideal worker norm—a prevailing belief in work primacy—amplifies the negative impact of this perception on key managerial outcomes (i.e., promotability evaluation, career mentoring support, and I-deal grants). Using a multi-method design (a controlled scenario experiment and a multi-wave field survey), we find robust support for our hypotheses. Our research advances theory by offering a contextualized signaling account of how normative environments shape the career consequences of non-work motives. It thereby makes important contributions to the literature on family motivation, the work–family interface, and managerial evaluation.

Not Just Between Boss and Employee: Spouse Reactions to Boundary-Blurring Leader Interactions

OB
Xueqi Wen (Tongji University); Zihan Liu (Singapore Management University); Feng Qiu (University of Massachusetts Amherst); Tammy D. Allen (University of South Florida); Sijia Zhao (Tongji University)
Leaders increasingly build relationships through after-hours messages and social invitations that extend the leader-employee tie into employees’ nonwork time and spaces. We argue that these boundary-blurring interactions are not interpreted only within the dyad: spouses actively appraise the same cues because leader access can reshape household outcomes. Drawing on interdependence theory, we propose a triadic, cross-domain model in which the *basis* of spouse dependence determines whether boundary blurring is construed as shared opportunity or relational encroachment. When spouses are financially dependent on the employee, leader boundary blurring is more likely to elicit spouse vicarious pride, which encourages employee relational closeness with the leader. When spouses are emotionally dependent on the employee, the same boundary blurring is more likely to elicit spouse jealousy, which discourages closeness. Three-wave, multi-informant data from 345 employee–spouse dyads in a Chinese manufacturing plant support this moderated mediation model. The findings reposition the household as an active meaning-making system that can sponsor or inhibit leader-employee relationship development.

Spread or Stop? How Employee Unethical Pro-Family Behavior Shapes Spouse’s Moral Response at Work

OB
PENGXIANG FAN (Beijing Jiaotong University); Ming Guo (Beijing Jiaotong University); songlin yang (Beijing Jiaotong University)
While existing research has examined the antecedents and employee outcomes of employee pro-family behavior (UPFB), its implications for the consequences of spouses remain largely unexplored. The present research provides an important extension to the UPFB literature by investigating the distinct optimal distinctiveness processes that shape spouses’ own psychological experiences and their subsequent behavior at work. We propose that, given its dual moral nature, employee UPFB simultaneously increases spouses’ differentiation motives (which trigger work moralization) and affiliation motives (which reduce moral efficacy). Importantly, trait mindfulness moderates these countervailing psychological experiences by strengthening work moralization and moral efficacy, which leads to spouse UPFB paradoxically. Results from a field survey study with matched employee-spouse data largely corroborate our propositions. This research provides a more fine-grained understanding of the complex experience of spouses as bystanders and advances the ethics of work–family interface literature.

Two Pathways from Leader Dominance and Prestige to Work–Family Conflict and Enrichment

OB
Matthew Ryan (London Business School); Garrett L. Brady (Bocconi University)
Work increasingly extends into employees’ personal lives, yet research on work–family outcomes has focused largely on job characteristics and individual differences, paying less attention to everyday social interactions at work. We examine leadership as a central interpersonal process through which work experiences spill over into the home domain. Drawing on dual-strategies theory and the work–home resources model, we propose that leaders’ dominance-and prestige-based influence shape employees’ work–family conflict and enrichment through two mechanisms: supervisor-directed exemplification (a behavioral pathway) and job satisfaction (an affective pathway). We test this model across two pre-registered field studies: a three-wave survey of full-time employees and a 10-day daily diary study with employee–partner dyads. Across both studies, leader dominance is associated with greater work–family conflict, primarily via increased exemplification, whereas leader prestige is associated with greater work–family enrichment, primarily via higher job satisfaction. These findings highlight leadership influence as a proximal social mechanism of work–family spillover and show that how leaders secure deference shape employees’ home lives in systematically different ways.
