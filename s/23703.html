Unraveling the Dynamic Imprinting: A Big Data Investigation of Economic Downturns and Career Mobility

CAR
Chen QIAN (University of Science and Technology Beijing); Mengke Wang (South China University of Technology)
The global economic downturns in recent years have significantly impacted the career development of highly educated graduates, challenging the traditional notion that higher education guarantees career success. This study examines how macroeconomic shocks affect long-term career mobility among highly educated graduates, with a focus on the dynamic mechanisms of imprinting. Utilizing a large-scale resume dataset from a professional social networking platform in China—covering 2,166,147 career mobility records from 2001 to 2016—this research evaluates the enduring effects of labor market shocks. The findings show that economic downturns at graduation significantly suppress subsequent career mobility, supporting the existence of a persistent “human capital depreciation imprint.” This imprint captures both external market devaluation and internal skill depletion or self-concept erosion, formed under unfavorable macroeconomic conditions during labor market entry. Importantly, these imprints are not static. Our analyses reveal that improvements in external market conditions (e.g., subsequent economic recovery) and internal factors such as personal achievements (e.g., job promotions) can gradually mitigate these constraints. Notably, the timing, continuity, and accumulation of such positive developments—especially early and sustained economic recovery or repeated promotions—are essential for attenuating or even reversing these negative effects, while delayed or sporadic improvements offer limited relief. Theoretically, this research advances imprinting theory by empirically validating a dynamic remediation model and specifying the temporal patterns through which negative career imprints are mitigated. Methodologically, it highlights the value of big data and longitudinal tracking for uncovering nuanced career dynamics. Practically, the findings underscore the importance of early, sustained interventions by individuals, organizations, and policymakers to help graduates overcome early setbacks and build adaptive, upwardly mobile careers.
