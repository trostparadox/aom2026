Orchestrating Socially Oriented Innovation Ecosystems: A Phased Approach to Integrating State Actors

SIM
Kyeyoung Shin (University of Oxford); Pinar Ozcan (University of Oxford)
This study investigates how orchestrators of socially oriented innovation ecosystems integrate state actors to enable large-scale impact when they lack structural power. We conduct a single-case study of the Mojaloop Foundation—a nonprofit that stewards Mojaloop, an open source digital payments platform designed to enable interoperable, low-cost, and inclusive financial systems in resource-constrained settings. Rather than simply promoting a technical solution, the foundation had to initiate and orchestrate an innovation ecosystem to secure state actors' participation and achieve its mission under conditions of limited authority. Drawing on a rich longitudinal dataset of interviews, field observations, and archival materials, we develop a process model of global-local ecosystem bridging, comprising three phases: mobilizing global ecosystem participants to leverage global legitimacy and offset structural power deficits; cultivating local ecosystem readiness through localized engagement; and demonstrating cross-ecosystem readiness to secure state actor commitment. Across these phases, the orchestrator addressed distinct challenges, including the liabilities of newness, smallness, and foreignness, as well as resource constraints and public sector risk aversion. This study contributes to research on ecosystem orchestration by theorizing how orchestrators without structural power integrate state actors through phased, relational processes of global-local ecosystem bridging, enabling innovation ecosystems to emerge and scale in institutionally complex domains.

University Entrepreneurial Ecosystems Under Dual Commitments: Global Configurations and Impact Logics

SIM
Jason Roncancio (Erasmus University Rotterdam)
University entrepreneurial ecosystem (UEE) research is largely shaped by evidence from North America and Western Europe and by principles that privilege individual instruments such as TTOs, incubators, or accelerators. As a result, relatively little is known about how UEEs are configured as whole systems across diverse geographies, and about how those configurations enable universities to pursue both economic and societal value. This article addresses the research question How do universities configure internal translation arrangements and external partner ecologies within university entrepreneurial ecosystems, and how do these configurations shape distinctive impact logics across places? And argues that variation in entrepreneurial outcomes and impact profiles is best explained at the level of system configuration. The study draws on systematic qualitative content analysis of publicly documented evidence from 240 research-intensive universities across Africa, the Americas, Asia, Europe, and Oceania. The findings suggest that cross-place variation is structured by two system properties, architectural coupling and external actor density, which combine into four recurring configurations and associated impact logics: scale-oriented venturing, portfolio commercialization, ecosystem brokerage, and capability building.

Unpacking the Mechanisms to Advance Partnerships for Sustainability

SIM
Vilja Lange (Dresden University of Technology); Elizabeth Embry (University of Kansas); Samanthi Dijkstra-Silva (Dresden University of Technology)
As the increasing environmental and social challenges are beyond the scope of a single organization to address, partnerships for sustainability are a continuously growing phenomenon. Partnerships for sustainability are intentional, voluntary collaborations between two or more organizations intended to advance social or environmental sustainability goals. While fostering great opportunities for the organizations involved, like reduced costs of sustainability initiatives and new market opportunities, these partnerships also require investments. Thus, it is critical to understand the mechanisms supporting the development and implementation of these partnerships. To explore this, we conducted a systematic literature review to specifically examine partnerships with a sustainability focus. We found that five key mechanisms advance partnerships for sustainability: trust, partner heterogeneity, a shared sustainability definition and goal alignment, coordination, as well as knowledge exchange. These mechanisms and their interplay are presented in a framework to guide managers and policy makers when designing and engaging in a partnership with a sustainability focus.

How Robust Action Guides Ecosystem Leadership Decision Making in Social Entrepreneurship Ecosystems

SIM
Bart Leyen (Vrije Universiteit Brussel); Nikolay Anguelov Dentchev (Vrije Universiteit Brussel)
Leadership in social entrepreneurship ecosystems (SEEs) is enacted in an environment characterized by competing institutional logics. Under these conditions, ecosystem leadership faces a set of challenges common to mobilizing collective action (participation, coordination, and momentum). Yet, existing research does not explain how leaders make decisions about managing these challenges. This gap cannot be explained by roles or practices alone but requires explaining how leaders exercise judgment in choosing whether, when, and how to intervene. Drawing on robust action, this study conceptualizes SEE leadership as decision orientations for mobilizing collective action under conditions of institutional plurality. The study is based on an inductive qualitative analysis of two mature SEEs (Belgium and the Netherlands). The findings identify three recurring leadership decision orientations: Engaging, Aligning, and Stimulating, and a Leadership configuration that helps ecosystem leaders choose how to involve actors, coordinate interdependent activities, and generate momentum. These decision orientations enact robust action by preserving flexibility, deferring closure, and enabling provisional coordination among autonomous yet interdependent actors. The study contributes by explaining how ecosystem leadership in SEEs operates as a set of decision orientations that enable leaders to mobilize collective action under conditions of institutional plurality, weak hierarchy, and persistent disagreement.

An Interaction-Based View of Ecosystem Emergence

SIM
Nayyer Khan (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Ashutosh Jha (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Priyatam Anurag (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow)
This paper presents an interaction-based perspective on the study of ecosystems. It aims to augment the conceptualisation of ecosystems’ existence as the realisation of the value proposition more than the minimisation of transaction costs. Unlike established business models, ecosystems are characterised by interpretive ambiguity, contested responsibilities, and evolving understandings of “how value is created and captured”, particularly salient in models that span organizational, professional, or sectoral boundaries, thereby, defining an emergent trajectory rather a pre-determined one. This conceptualisation extends the understanding of ecosystems beyond the traditional bilateral assessments required for decision-making in static contexts. Moreover, researchers have called for addressing the present limitations of the Transaction Cost Theory, particularly in analysing the emergence of new business models, alongside other TCT constraints. Hence, this study presents a complementary perspective centred on interaction as the unit of analysis for the white-spaces in ecosystem analysis. With a backdrop of the current research on the analysis of ecosystems, the paper has utilised an inductive case-based analysis juxtaposing TCT limitations with the unique characteristics of the ecosystems. To demonstrate the ongoing phenomenon, we have relied on the PingAn Good Doctor, a chinese healthcare platform, eventually proposing “interactions-based view” as a potential theory for understanding ecosystems.
