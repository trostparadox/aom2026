How U.S. Hospitals Manage Conflicting Missions and Goals: A Scoping Review

HCM
Huseyin Tanriverdi (The University of Texas at Austin); Annika Hesse (The University of Texas at Austin); Iris Xue Qian (The University of Texas at Austin); Mia Rodriguez (The University of Texas at Austin); Eshaan Sheth (McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin)
Background: Hospitals in the United States face increasing pressure to pursue multiple, often competing missions, including delivering high-quality patient care, maintaining financial viability, advancing education and research, fulfilling ethical or religious commitments, and providing community benefit. These competing objectives generate persistent mission–goal tensions that vary across hospital types. Although prior studies examine specific tradeoffs, a comprehensive synthesis of how such tensions manifest across U.S. hospitals has been lacking. Objective: To map the extent, nature, and framing of mission–goal tensions in U.S. hospitals and to identify governance, leadership, and technology mechanisms used to manage these tensions. Methods: This scoping review followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Searches of Web of Science and EBSCOhost identified peer-reviewed studies of U.S. hospitals examining organizational missions alongside competing goals. Search terms captured hospital context, mission- or values-based objectives, expressions of tension or tradeoff, and competing financial, operational, academic, or ethical goals. Eligible studies were charted by hospital type and synthesized descriptively and thematically. No formal quality appraisal was conducted. Results: A total of 66 studies were included, spanning for-profit, nonprofit, religiously affiliated, academic medical centers, and government-operated hospitals. Across hospital types, mission–goal tensions were structural rather than episodic and reflected tradeoffs between mission-driven objectives and financial performance, efficiency, or productivity pressures. Nonprofit hospitals balanced community benefit obligations against financial sustainability; for-profit hospitals faced tensions between profit maximization imperatives and expectations to provide accessible, high-quality, and community-oriented care; religious hospitals navigated conflicts between doctrinal commitments and service scope; academic medical centers managed tensions between education and research missions and clinical productivity; and government hospitals, particularly within the Veterans Health Administration, addressed competing mandates related to access and efficiency. Governance, leadership, and technology were identified as mechanisms for managing these tensions. Conclusions: Mission–goal tensions are a persistent feature of U.S. hospitals. Hospitals manage, rather than resolve, these tensions through organizational structures. This review provides a systematic map of mission–goal tensions and responses, informing future research and policy discussions.

When Governance Creates Complexity: Institutional Logics and Tensions in Swiss Hospitals (WITHDRAWN)

HCM
Benjamin Wyss (University of Bern); Seraina Frey (University of Bern); Carl Rudolf Blankart (University of Bern)
Hospitals face competing demands from professional norms, market pressures, and regulatory requirements – yet we know little about how governance structures generate these conflicting demands. This qualitative study examines how governance mechanisms in Switzerland’s mixed hospital system produce tensions and how they are perceived by hospital leaders. Drawing on expert interviews with eighteen senior hospital executives and cantonal health directors, we applied grounded theory and the Gioia methodology to inductively develop a typology of four governance-induced tensions. Horizontal tensions appear between hospitals under unequal conditions, vertical tensions between managerial autonomy and political control, organization internal tensions among actors representing divergent perspectives, and functional tensions between economic control and professional care missions. Connecting these empirical patterns to institutional theory, these tensions reveal how governance mechanisms embed competing institutional logics into the field’s institutional infrastructure. The study advances institutional theory by conceptualizing governance as an active source rather than mere context of institutional complexity. We identify different reform levers: harmonizing cantonal financing regimes, separating political oversight from operational management, strengthening integrative governance structures, and aligning financial incentives with care quality goals. Recognising governance as a structural source of institutional complexity reframes how health reforms should be designed and evaluated.

Delay and Displace: Managing Conflicting Logics and the Consequences

HCM
Alex Schulte (University of Miami)
Previous literature has emphasized how paradox management strategies enable organizational sustainability and peak performance. However, the external costs of these strategies remain underexplored empirically. I address this gap by examining the religious-medical paradox in U.S. Catholic hospitals, where Ethical and Religious Directives (ERDs) prohibiting abortion often conflict with medical standards of care. Using a quasi-experimental two-stage least squares instrumental variable (2SLS-IV) analysis of 1.1 million urgent pregnancy-related admissions (2010-2022) combined with 25 qualitative interviews, I provide the first large-scale causal evidence of how Catholic hospitals—which have a large and growing market share—manage paradoxical tensions and the consequences for external stakeholders. I find that going to an ERD-adherent hospital (vs. not) reduces abortion rates by 0.88 percentage points (137% relative to the mean, p < 0.05), primarily in urban settings. While aggregate complication rates appear similar across hospital types, this masks substantial heterogeneity: Hispanic patients experience 82% higher complication rates at ERD- adherent hospitals, and patients with medically complex pregnancies face significantly elevated morbidity (p < 0.05). By integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence, I identify two mechanisms through which hospitals navigate this paradox: temporal separation (delaying care until clinical deterioration meets doctrinal thresholds) and spatial sorting (transferring patients to non-Catholic facilities). While these strategies stabilize organizational identity, they externalize friction costs (e.g., delays, transfers, and complications), which fall disproportionately on vulnerable patients. Overall, I extend the literature by identifying negative externalities of paradox management and provide empirical evidence to inform ongoing policy debates related to Catholic hospital expansion and patient protections.

When Friends Turn Foes: Unsynchronized Institutional Logics in Healthcare Organizations

HCM
Piengnapa Pakuthai (Tilburg University)
Healthcare organizations are often seen as institutionally pluralistic, yet innovation implementation frequently generates persistent conflict even when actors share goals and values. Prior research highlights top management support but offers limited insight into why innovation struggles in professional settings where frontline staff have autonomy. This study explores how organizational attention flows across levels and how conflicts emerge despite shared beliefs. Using the attention-based view and institutional logics, we studied four innovation projects at a Dutch healthcare organization. Interviews with managers and frontline staff show that attention mainly stabilizes at the frontline, where control over resources and routines is concentrated. Despite shared commitments to care and innovation, actors prioritize these differently over time and roles. We propose "unsynchronized institutional logics" to explain conflicts not from opposing logics but from uneven, misaligned enactments across levels, causing friction and turning allies into foes. This research extends the attention-based view beyond top management and highlights how degree and timing differences in logics create tension within healthcare organizations.

Inhabited Decision-Making: Institutional Norms and Practical Concerns in High-Stakes Settings

HCM
Amelia Kathryn Hawbaker (University of Oxford)
High-stakes situations in organizations such as hospitals often require critical decision-making in highly pressurized contexts that have immediate life and death consequences. Drawing on a 19-month ethnographic study and 32 qualitative interviews in a labor and delivery unit in a Midwestern U.S. hospital, I examine how healthcare providers navigate high-stakes situations where institutional norms, organizational rules, and immediate practical concerns collide. Using an inhabited institutionalist lens, I find that through two relational processes – connecting care and disconnecting care – providers are able to creatively link and separate patient care to navigate three recurrent sources of tension: conflicting patient and provider priorities, the intersection of temporal and spatial constraints, and threats to hospital legitimacy. Connecting and disconnecting care are boundedly creative practices that enable providers to balance normative expectations with situational demands, illustrating how social relationships shape organizational decision-making in high-stakes and highly pressurized contexts. This study advances theory on inhabited institutionalism and high reliability organizations by revealing how interactional processes sustain organizational functioning when moral and practical pressures collide.
