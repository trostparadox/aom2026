The Making of a University President: Evolving Profiles, Favoritism, and Knowledge Production

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 8:30AM–9:30AM
Sheraton Philadelphia Downtown Hotel — Poster Wall 4

Author: Zerin Zilin Dong University of California Merced

Session overview

University presidents occupy one of the most powerful leadership positions in knowledge-producing organizations: they set hiring priorities, control resource allocation, and define long-run institutional strategy. Yet we know remarkably little about how leaders’ own career histories and intellectual identities shape what happens inside universities. This paper asks a simple but largely unexplored question: Do academic units perform better when the university president comes from their own disciplinary and career background? To answer this, I assemble the first large-scale historical database of over 2,000 presidents and chancellors at 146 U.S. R1 research universities from 1900 to 2025. For each leader, I reconstruct disciplinary training, academic and administrative career paths, exposure to non-academic organizations, gender, and race using a combination of large-language-model extraction, web scraping, and manual archival verification. This allows me to trace how individuals move through the academic labor market and how these pathways differ across fields and historical eras. I link these leadership data to department-level research output from OpenAlex to study how presidential identity shapes organizational outcomes. Using dynamic difference-in-differences and synthetic-control designs, I compare departments whose disciplinary field becomes represented in the presidency to otherwise similar units within the same institution. Beyond performance effects, I document how the presidential labor market has transformed over the past century—from clergy and scholar-administrators to leaders with increasingly managerial and external career profiles— altering which academic fields gain access to organizational power and resources. The findings contribute to research on leadership, internal labor markets, and organizational inequality by showing how elite career trajectories shape the production of knowledge itself.
