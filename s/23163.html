Who Is Watching? Privacy Disclosure Under Interpersonal and Institutional Gaze

CTO
Suzie Xi (University of Notre Dame); Heng Xu (University of Florida); Nicholas Berente (University of Notre Dame)
This study evaluates privacy disclosure through the lens of “gaze,” which refers to individuals’ perceptions of being observed. We distinguish between two types of gaze—the interpersonal (Sartrean) gaze and the institutional (Foucauldian) gaze. Drawing on a scenario-based experiment, we find that both interpersonal and institutional gazes significantly influence privacy disclosure. Further, gaze appears to moderate the relationship between privacy protection and privacy disclosure. Under low-protection conditions, gaze appears to substitute for privacy protection to an extent and increases the likelihood of privacy disclosure, but this effect diminishes when the protection level is high.

Human–IT Complexity and Cybersecurity Risk

CTO
Thorsten Schlüter; Thomas Schäper
Cybersecurity research has traditionally emphasized technical controls, yet growing evidence suggests that human behavior plays a central role in shaping organizational security risk. Drawing on complexity theory, we conceptualize human-IT complexity as an emergent, organization-level form of human-IT complexity arising from shadow IT use, workarounds, and improvised system interactions. We theorize a nonlinear relationship between human–IT complexity and cybersecurity risk attention, arguing that increasing human-IT complexity initially amplifies risk through heightened opacity and coordination challenges, but that salience-driven organizational adaptation can partially attenuate this effect beyond a critical threshold. To test this argument, we develop a scalable measure of human–IT complexity from large-scale employee-generated text and relate it to firms’ cybersecurity risk discourse in 10-K filings. Using firm-year panel data, we find robust evidence of an inverted U-shaped relationship between human–IT complexity and cybersecurity risk disclosure. Our findings extend cybersecurity research by integrating human-IT complexity into a sociotechnical framework and demonstrate a novel approach for measuring human-driven cybersecurity risk at scale.

Learning Together: How Social Interaction Shapes Employees Phishing Reporting

CTO
Biju B Varghese (Rochester Institute of Technology); Quang "Neo" Bui (Rochester Institute of Technology); Emi Moriuchi (Rochester Institute of Technology)
Employee reporting is critical for organizations to blunt phishing attacks, yet awareness-oriented training often improves only what people know about phishing more than what they do (i.e., reporting). We address this problem by theorizing and testing the mechanisms through which a workplace embedded anti-phishing training experience can shape employee’s intention to report phish. Using a sequential mixed methods design, we first conduct a qualitative case study of a workplace embedded training intervention in an IT services firm to inductively specify a training-to-intention mechanism. We then test the resulting model using a post-training field survey in a different organization. Results from PLS-SEM analysis indicate that training experience predicts intention indirectly through phishing reporting self-efficacy and organizational security commitment, with security-focused social interaction contributing to these internal states. This study opens the training black box by specifying how training experiences translate into reporting intention, thereby integrating knowledge learning, rational cognitive, and social cultural perspectives from the phishing reporting literature.

Imposed Hybridity: When Personal Social Media Become De Facto Enterprise Social Media

CTO
Jiarui Yin (Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya - BarcelonaTech); siyu zhou (Shantou University); Ariane Ollier-Malaterre (Université du Québec à Montréal (UQAM))
This study explores the phenomenon of imposed hybridity—a condition in which WeChat, a personal social media (PSM) platform, functions as the de facto enterprise social media (ESM) for millions of workers in China, blurring boundaries between work and personal life. Drawing on 28 in-depth interviews with Chinese workers, we reveal how structural forces, such as WeChat’s technological dominance, cultural norms like guanxi, and organizational practices, constrain individual agency, creating a hybrid space with high boundary permeability. Findings highlight persistent use of WeChat for work communication despite formal ESM availability, driven by convenience and external demands, leading to a sense of inevitability and resignation. Consequences include chronic stress, reduced authenticity, and ineffective micro-resistance strategies, with significant mental tolls. Theoretically, we extend boundary theory by introducing the concept of imposed hybridity as a distinctive boundary condition characterized by structurally constrained integration and enrich affordance theory by emphasizing how sociocultural constraints shape the actualization of technological affordances. While grounded in the Chinese context, the phenomenon of imposed hybridity, where structural forces override individual boundary preferences, may extend to other settings where platform monopolies, cultural norms of connectivity, or organizational practices similarly constrain worker agency.

Pages That Remember, Pages That Forget: Ethics of Public Memory After Corporate Crises

CTO
Agnieszka Kwapisz (Montana State University)
Public accountability depends on what the public can find and remember. We compare how the same corporate crises are recorded across two digital knowledge infrastructures with different governance logics: Wikipedia (governed) and firm-controlled websites archived by the Wayback Machine. Using a pre/post design for 36 incidents (2001–2023), we track event–organization coupling (incident mentions) and coverage depth (page length), and we assess framing shifts from generic labels (e.g., “data breach”) to episode-specific anchors (e.g., “Boeing 737 MAX groundings”). We find that, first, on Wikipedia, incidents are rapidly integrated and remain visible (higher post-event mentions, deeper descriptions, persistence within the first year); on corporate traces, changes are smaller, more variable, and often short-lived. Second, naming shifts toward episode-specific anchors on the governed platform, tightening the firm–event association. Third, effects are heterogeneous: strongest for named, high-salience controversies and weaker for routine legal actions. Conceptually, we shift focus from past-as-narrative to past-as-infrastructure, specifying the social and technical mechanisms (verification friction, version histories, redirects) that stabilize public memory. Normatively, we propose a baseline of balanced remembrance: preserve both the fact of harm and the record of remediation. Policy and design implications include retention norms, verifiable sourcing, and archival bridges that help keep repair as traceable as wrongdoing.
