Too Much, Too Soon? Early Funding, Technological Unconventionality, and Innovation Capabilities

STR
Harsh Ketkar (McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin); Maria Roche (Harvard University)
The availability of financial resources significantly shapes firm innovation outcomes, especially for early-stage, innovation-focused technology firms. However, prior research has provided conflicting findings about this relationship: On the one hand, resource availability may boost innovation outcomes; on the other hand, resource constraints also may do so. We hypothesize that the timing and size of resource availability in the early life stages of a firm may predict its propensity to generate more unconventional technology combinations compared to its competitors. We use a novel dataset constructed from PitchBook and BuiltWith that consists of nearly 800,000 observations and covers 11,853 firms founded and funded between 2010 and 2019. Our findings provide evidence suggesting that early-stage firms that take longer to obtain first funding are more likely to use unconventional technological combinations to build their product after the funding event. However, firms that receive a larger first funding round experience a decline in the unconventionality of their technology stack following the funding event. Thus, by focusing on the timing and size of first funding, we offer a way to reconcile conflicting findings from prior literature.

The Soldier as Steward: Ownership Context and the Impact of Ex-Military Executives on Innovation

STR
Tazeeb Rajwani (University of Surrey); Zibang Chen (The University of Manchester); Tao Chen (University College Cork)
This study examines how ex-military executives foster firm innovation under varying governance structures, emphasizing the interaction between ownership and stewardship. Drawing on stewardship theory, we argue that the intrinsic motivation and long-term orientation of ex-military executives align with their ability to drive innovation, but these effects are contingent on ownership structure. Using 12-year panel data of Chinese listed firms (2006 to 2017), we find that ex-military executives significantly enhance innovation in state-owned firms, where greater autonomy, resource backing, and stronger motivation for innovation development amplify their potential. Conversely, family firms’ restricted autonomy, limited resources, and weaker motivation for innovation advancement suppress their innovative capacity. By integrating stewardship theory with autonomy literature, this research highlights the nuanced relationship between leadership traits, governance contexts, and innovation, offering valuable insights for designing governance structures that optimize managerial potential and foster sustained organizational growth.

Framing, Paradigms and Technological Exploration in Chinese Pharmaceutical Firms

STR
Kaixian Mao (Renmin University of China); Bilian Ni Sullivan (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Jingxuan Lu (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology)
We investigate how framing differences shaped by positional proximity and technological paradigms affect firms’ innovation responses to legitimacy-enhancing shocks within an industry. In the context of Tu Youyou’s 2015 Nobel Prize, we examine how traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) firms which are positionally proximate and paradigmatically embedded in holistic traditions differ from those positionally distant from TCM’s origins and paradigmatically flexible in reductionist Western medicine (WM) paradigm in their pursuit of exploratory innovation in TCM-related domains. Building on the attention-based view (ABV), we infer that situated attention leads to differential event framing. Whereas TCM firms are more likely to frame the shock as confirmation of traditional schemas, directing attention toward exploitation of existing practices, WM firms tend to frame it as untapped opportunity, enabling attention for exploratory innovation. Using patent data from Chinese pharmaceutical firms before and after, we find that WM-oriented firms engage in greater exploratory TCM-related innovation than their TCM-oriented counterparts. This effect is moderated by R&D intensity (amplifying exploration), TMT tenure (reinforcing inertia), nationalism (heightening identity confirmation), and negative sentiment toward TCM (reducing opportunistic framing). These results reveal how attentional mechanisms sustain persistent paradigmatic divides in fields with co-existing paradigms. Implications for ABV, institutional change and innovation heterogeneity are discussed.

Misvaluation and Misallocation: How Analyst Errors Shape Firm Strategy and Long-Term Value

STR
Anand Nandkumar (University at Buffalo, School of Management); Arnab Choudhury (Columbia Business School)
This study examines how routine features of firms’ strategic communication shape analysts’ assessments of intangible returns and subsequently influence firms’ innovation portfolios and long-term value creation. We argue that divergence between the strategic priorities articulated in the Prepared Remarks (PR) section of earnings calls and the focal concerns raised by analysts in the Q&A segment generates systematic interpretive frictions that lead analysts to misvalue firms’ future potential. Using a large panel of U.S. public firms, we construct a novel market-anchored measure of analyst misvaluation based on the decomposition of tangible and intangible returns and show that conversational dissonance increases the likelihood of both undervaluation and overvaluation. The direction of misvaluation, however, depends on disclosure style: clarity amplifies undervaluation when divergence is high, whereas opacity amplifies overvaluation. These misvaluations have distinct strategic consequences. Overvaluation encourages wider exploratory search, whereas undervaluation induces a shift toward exploitation. Both forms of misvaluation ultimately reduce long-term investor value, as measured by LIVA, although overvaluation has the more detrimental effect. Taken together, our findings reveal how unintentional features of corporate communication, rather than only deliberate obfuscation, systematically shape external evaluations, distort the balance between exploration and exploitation, and undermine firms’ long-run competitive advantage.
