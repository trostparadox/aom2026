Visibility, Strength of Negative Signals, and Gender-Based Penalties

DEI
Peter D Sherer (University of Calgary); Ke Wang (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
Why do women sometimes face harsher penalties than men, yet at other times appear more immune to them? Research on the gender punishment gap shows that women are penalized more harshly for their actions or adverse events than men. By contrast, top management diversity research finds that women in high-level positions are treated leniently because firms benefit reputationally from the diversity signals they send. We argue that these divergent findings reflect differences in the visibility of penalties and the negative signals they emit. Drawing on signalling theory, we theorize that highly visible gender-based penalties (e.g., terminating a female executive) threaten firms’ reputations, whereas less visible penalties (e.g., limiting pay increases) are easier to impose without attracting attention. We test these arguments using multilevel piecewise regression and random-effects logistic regression on terminations and pay, comparing them before and after an industry downturn, drawing on a proprietary dataset of individuals in oil and gas firms (N = 276,282). We find that, amidst the downturn, women executives were not terminated at higher rates than male executives, given the high visibility of such actions. However, non-senior women included in mass layoffs and women executives in firms with more senior-level women had higher termination rates than their male counterparts, making instances of gender-based penalties less visible. Moreover, women executives received modestly lower pay increases than their male counterparts, sufficiently small as to avoid drawing attention. Thus, our study contributes to understanding how the visibility of punishments leads to differences in the penalties that women face.
