Global Gender Orders: Culture, Archetypes, and Institutional Contexts

Session overview

This session explores how national cultures, institutional environments, and enduring gender archetypes shape women's leadership and inequality across contexts. The papers span cross-national archetypes of womanhood, leadership in higher education, unconscious bias, gendered meritocracy in Nigeria, and literary-historical constructions of gender. Together, they show how global gender orders continue to regulate leadership legitimacy and women's participation in organizational life.

The “Bad Woman” Sycorax: a Reflection of Gender Inequality and Roles

DEI
Vittoria Sarti; Mercedes-Victoria Auqui-Caceres (University of Padua)
This study analyses how women’s identity is shaped by culture and society’s beliefs. This study examines the figure of Sycorax in William Shakespeare’s The Tempest through the lenses of gender in early modern England. Since Sycorax never appears on stage, her presence can be felt and constructed by some male characters, most notably Prospero, Ariel, and Caliban. Everyone frames her as a powerful witch and as a symbol of anti-patriarchal ideas. An important focus is given to the perception of white and black magic by comparing the female magic of Sycorax and the male magic of Prospero in relation to that society. Based on historical texts on witchcraft, witch hunts, and gender norms, this article contextualises Sycorax within the narratives that demonised women who transgressed patriarchal boundaries. Moreover, the differences between Sycorax and Miranda highlights how different women were perceived, focusing on the model of the angelical and perfect woman that Miranda represents, while Sycorax represents the foul witch, the woman condemned because of her illegitimate desire to break social norms. By intersecting historical and literary events, we conclude that the presence of systematic gender disparities and expected feminine behaviours in early modern England still influences our contemporary society.

Narrating Womanhood, Regulating Leadership: Archetypes Across National Contexts

DEI
Batia Ben Hador (Ariel University); Zuzanna Staniszewska (Kozminski University in Warsaw); Medha Bakhshi (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Can (Coco) HE (HEC Paris); Tracy Hopkins (The University of Notre Dame Australia); John Millea (Henley Business School, University of Reading, UK)
This paper examines cross-nationally dominant female archetypes and their implications for women's participation and leadership in the workplace. Six scholars from Australia, China, India, Poland, Germany, and Israel collaborated on the study, each describing how cultural ideals of womanhood remain influential today. Drawing on Social Role Theory and Role Congruity Theory, we argue that these archetypes are culturally grounded expressions of historically gendered divisions of labor and that they structure persistent tensions between communal expectations of femininity and agentic expectations of leadership. Using an interpretive, comparative qualitative approach, we presented six national archetype profiles: the Good Mother, Xian Qi Liang Mu, Sati Savitri, Matka Polka, Die große Mutti, and Eshet ?ayil, and analyzed how each elevates women’s moral value while simultaneously restricting their professional autonomy and leadership legitimacy. Across contexts, archetypes reward women for self-sacrifice, relational labor, and service to family or nation, but penalize self-advocacy, ambition, or deviation from traditional roles. Our analysis demonstrates that these archetypes serve as mechanisms of cultural regulation that reinforce conservative gender norms, influencing how organizations in different cultures evaluate female leaders and how women navigate their own identities at work.

Female Leadership Underrepresentation in Higher Education Institutions: A Systematic Review

DEI
Zhuo Wang (University of Leeds); Yingqi Wei (University of Leeds); Gideon Azumah (University of Leeds); Nafisat Olawunmi Olabisi; Mingchu Wang (University of Greenwich)
Despite increasing number of females entering academia, females remain underrepresented in leadership roles (FLU) in Higher Education Institutions (HEIs). This study aims to systematically review the phenomenon, antecedents, processes, impact, and solutions in addressing female leadership underrepresent ions in HEIs. Using a systematic literature review approach, 344 articles were identified through Web of Science and Scopus search using keywords related to FLU and HEIs. Following screening and sample selection process, 79 articles were retained for full-text analysis and coding. The review categorises findings across societal, organisational, and individual levels and synthesises key theoretical perspectives. This analysis identifies 17 major barriers, spanning six societal, seven organisational, and four individual dimensions, that impede female academics progression to leadership roles in HEIs. These include persistent gender norms, institutional resistance, policy silence, and psychosocial challenges such as limited mentorship and self-confidence. 64 evidence-based recommendations were extracted from the literature, highlighting the need for context-sensitive, multi-level interventions. This paper offers the first comprehensive synthesis of all aspects and dimensions of FLU in HEIs. The findings inform scholars, policy makers, and individuals by demonstrating that FLU in HEIs requires not only societal reform but also effective organisational implementation and individual transformation.

Unconscious Bias in Women's Progression to Leadership in HEIs

DEI
Ashraful Alam (University of Salford); Shobod Deba Nath (University of Dhaka); Dr Sajal Roy (Australian Catholic University); Angel Jimenez-Aranda (University of Salford); Mansura Akter (University of Dhaka); Aditi Shams (University of Dhaka); Katherine Rostron (University of Salford); Evelyn Oginni (University of Salford); Syed Sharaf Mustayin (University of Dhaka)
This study investigates the underlying causes of unconscious biases and negative stereotypes affecting women’s access to leadership positions in higher education institutions and explores strategies to overcome these barriers. A mixed-methods research design was adopted to generate comprehensive findings, structured into three interrelated phases. The first phase involved a survey of 275 academic professionals examining perceptions of how unconscious bias influences female leadership in HEIs. The second phase consisted of semi-structured interviews with 31 academic professionals to gain qualitative insights into the root causes of unconscious bias and possible mitigation strategies. The final phase employed focus group discussions to enable triangulation and enhance the credibility of the results. The analysis identified key root causes of unconscious bias, including gender stereotyping, internalized patriarchal norms, socio-cultural expectations, gendered language, and unequal educational investments. The study also highlights effective strategies to counter these biases, such as targeted institutional interventions, implementation of structural support systems, active male allyship, digital transformation initiatives, and identity negotiation by women leaders. The study contributes theoretically by extending the glass ceiling framework, proposing that discursive and developmental mechanisms shape leadership perceptions and career trajectories that normalize unconscious bias and perpetuate structural inequalities within higher education institutions globally today.

Gendered Meritocracy: Why Women Leaders Outperform Men in Nigeria's Patronage Bureaucracy

DEI
Michael Roll (German Institute of Development and Sustainability (IDOS), Bonn)
High-performing government agencies exist in seemingly weak states, but little is known about how they emerge. Drawing on literature that links women to positive political and managerial outcomes, I explore whether organizational performance differs under male and female agency leaders in a patronage state. Focusing on Nigeria, original data come from four sources: a unique performance survey of 120 federal government agencies (1999–2015); a hand-collected dataset of key information on all agency leaders during this period; 138 in-depth interviews with senior officials and politicians; and participant observation in selected organizations. The results show that, despite being severely underrepresented, agencies led by women are more than twice as likely to perform well as those led by men. Contrary to mechanisms favored in the literature, which draw on alleged behavioral gender differences, I argue that this pattern is explained by a process of “gender differential selection.” Inadvertently, women leaders are selected based on a narrower and more merit- and mission-oriented set of criteria than men, which I refer to as “gendered meritocracy.” My key contributions are to demonstrate that gender effects exist in non-Western bureaucracies and to offer new concepts for studying gender effects which may have theoretical purchase elsewhere.
