Author: Aria Yongting Huang University of Oxford

Session overview

Modern organizations increasingly rely on quantification—indicators, metrics, rankings, and analytics—to know, evaluate, and govern. Yet even in data-rich settings, quantified knowledge remains pervasively incomplete. Gaps, omissions, and unmeasured dimensions persist. The more organizations quantify, the more they encounter boundaries of what can be measured. Contrasting the methodological view which sees missing data as a methodological deviation from an ideal of complete observation and thus in need of statistical repair, we draw on the sociology quantification to reconceptualize missing data as an organizational phenomenon, defining the boundary of organizational knowing. We develop a process theory of organizational missingness that explains how absences are produced and institutionalized across four stages Counting defines eligible cases, generating boundary missingness through exclusion. Commensuration translates heterogeneous qualities into a common metric by discarding incommensurable dimensions, producing residual missingness. Authoritative infrastructures aggregate and edit data, absorbing uncertainty by treating partial representations as epistemically sufficient, sustaining infrastructural missingness. Reactivity generates behavioral and interpretive responses through which actors manage visibility, producing strategic missingness. Missingness is a constitutive component of quantified organizing that shapes what can be known, valued, and governed. We contribute to organization knowledge, sociology of quantification, and provide implications for studies on inequality. We call for a research agenda towards understanding missing data as an organizational phenomenon.
