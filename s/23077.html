From AI Literacy to AI Mastery: Behavioral Capability and Performance in AI-Enabled Work

HR
Guorong Zhu (Salem State University)
Despite widespread organizational investment in artificial intelligence (AI), employees often display large and persistent performance differences when working with the same AI tools under identical access and cost conditions. Existing explanations—such as AI literacy, technology adoption, experience, or usage frequency—account poorly for this divergence. Drawing on behavioral trace data from 336 employees completing a real-task AI assessment in a multinational organization, we reconceptualize AI-related capability as a processual, behavioral form of mastery enacted during human–AI collaboration. We introduce AI mastery as a behavioral capability reflected in how individuals structure tasks, translate intent into prompts, verify AI outputs, and iteratively refine collaboration under uncertainty. Across four AI-enabled work tasks, AI mastery strongly predicts task performance, above and beyond job level, function, tenure, and self-reported AI proficiency. Among its dimensions, framework construction emerges as the most consequential mechanism, indicating that AI amplifies structured cognition rather than substituting for it. Counterintuitively, job level and experience exhibit diminishing or negative associations with AI mastery. Exploratory analyses further identify three reproducible collaboration patterns—structure-building, high-frequency but unstructured, and minimal-engagement—associated with sharply different outcomes. Together, these findings advance HR theory by reframing AI capability from static traits to observable collaboration processes, and they inform new approaches to assessing and developing employee capability in AI-enabled work.

Exploring How Multi-Objective Optimization Reduces Group Differences in Automatic Essay Scores

HR
Louis Hickman (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Andrew Speer (Indiana University); Chelsea Song (Indiana University Bloomington); Siyi Liu (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Machine learning (ML) and natural language processing (NLP) are increasingly used to automatically score open-ended text for college admissions and pre-employment testing. When doing so, assessment score differences between protected demographic groups (e.g., race, gender) are a critical concern. Multi-objective optimization methods have been proposed to mitigate group differences for traditional and ML-powered assessments by reducing the weight assigned to predictors that contribute to group differences. However, the process by which this occurs on high dimensional data (e.g., using NLP) remains unclear. In this study, we examine how multi-objective optimization reduces Black-White score differences on NLP ML college admissions essay scores, focusing on three possible explanations: (1) adjusting the relative emphasis of the knowledge, skills, abilities, and other characteristics (KSAOs) measured, (2) reducing reliability, and (3) reducing cognitive saturation (related to #1). Using a large, naturalistic dataset across two modeling tasks, we find that multi-objective optimization reduces Black-White group differences on NLP ML essay scores primarily by adjusting the relative emphasis of the KSAOs measured. Reductions in cognitive saturation also contribute to reduced group differences but are not the primary factor. Furthermore, we demonstrate that in some contexts (e.g., open-ended responses), multi-objective optimization methods might weigh information indicative of protected group characteristics, raising potential concerns about this methodology. We discuss the theoretical, practical, and legal implications of these findings in the context of automatically scoring open-ended assessments and personnel selection more broadly.

No More “Tell Me About Yourself”: Generative AI for Scaling Structured Job Interview Guides

HR
Tobias Marc Härtel (Osnabrück University of Applied Sciences); Theresa Schmidt (FH Münster)
Structured job interviews are among the most valid selection methods, yet adoption remains limited because developing them requires substantial resources and expertise. This study examines whether generative AI (ChatGPT-4o mini) can overcome these barriers by supporting competency assessment and interview question design. We conducted a multi-stage validation study involving 129 subject matter experts and 138 positions from a large multinational organization. Using a 36-competency taxonomy, AI-based competency ratings showed high reliability across independent runs (mean r = .90/.78 within jobs/competencies) and converged with expert ratings (mean r = .47/.31 within jobs/competencies) at levels comparable to expert-expert agreement. Building on these competencies, experts evaluated AI-generated interview questions more favorably than commonly used interview questions on overall quality (M = 3.76 vs. 2.76 on a 5-point scale, d = 1.20), including psychometric, normative, and practical criteria. This advantage was largely consistent across structured question types, unaffected by disclosure of AI authorship, and moderated by attitudes toward AI such that favorable attitudes amplified AI advantages. Importantly, AI-generated questions were rated favorably even by experts skeptical of AI. The findings position generative AI as decision-support infrastructure that can scale evidence-based interviewing while preserving human judgment, offering a practical path to narrowing the scientist-practitioner gap.

From Replication to Reasoning: Advancing Automated Scoring of Constructed-Response SJTs

HR
Yuxin Wang (University of Science and Technology of China); Liyan Xi (Singapore Management University); Qingxiong Weng
Constructed-response situational judgment tests (CR-SJTs) offer higher response fidelity and a better validity–diversity balance than multiple-choice formats, but adoption is limited by the cost, inconsistency, and low traceability of manual scoring. Prior work on automated scoring in personnel selection typically treats holistic subject matter experts (SMEs) ratings as ground truth and optimizes human–machine agreement, overlooking process evidence in open-ended responses. Grounded in the recognition-primed decision model, we shift the goal of automated scoring from replicating SMEs scores to rule-guided inference from observable behavioral evidence. We decompose each response into an ordered sequence of actionable behaviors, derive interpretable rule features capturing response quality, priority (order), and within-item stability, and construct a rule-based score. We then train a RoBERTa model with pairwise ranking to learn this rule-based standard at scale. Using high-stakes hospital recruitment data (selection sample N = 2,845; performance subsample N = 410), we compare SMEs scoring, rule-based scoring, and automated scoring on agreement, criterion-related and incremental validity, and subgroup differences. Rule-based scoring shows the strongest criterion-related validity and adds incremental predictive value beyond SMEs scores, whereas automated scoring closely follows the rule design but does not provide incremental validity.
