When HR Practices Feel Personal: The Effect of Individualized HR Practices on Employee Behavior

HR
Mengwei Li (Tilburg University); Kyoung Yong Kim (Villanova University); Zhongxing Su (Renmin University of China); Niannian Dong (University of Science and Technology Beijing)
Individualized HR practices (I-HRM) represent tailored arrangements made between employees and their line managers to address individual needs. This study explores how employees’ perception of I-HRM influences their emotions toward line managers and coworkers, shaping work behaviors in the team, including knowledge sharing and helping behaviors. Drawing upon positive psychology literature, social exchange theory, and social comparison theory, we hypothesize that the perception of receiving I-HRM enhances employees’ sense of pride when comparing themselves to a focal coworker, leading to more knowledge sharing and helping behaviors for self-enhancing interpersonally. On the other hand, employees’ I-HRM perception increases the feeling of gratitude toward line managers, motivating employees to reciprocate with knowledge sharing and helping behaviors, particularly when they perceive their line managers as highly embodying the team. Using two survey samples with multiple time points, we found some supports for our hypotheses.

When Climate and Resources Matter: A Meta Analysis of HRM System Strength and Employee Outcomes

HR
Timothy Colin Bednall (Swinburne University of Technology); Karin Sanders (UNSW Sydney); Huadong Yang (University of Liverpool)
Previous research shows that perceived human resource management (HRM) system strength, defined as the process through which employees understand and interpret HR practices, influencing employee outcomes. However, little is known about how these relationships vary across national contexts. Drawing on climate-affluence theory, we argue that a nation’s climatic and economic resources contexts jointly exert a top-down influence on the relationship between perceived HRM system strength and employee outcomes (employee attitudes, discretionary behaviors and wellbeing). Results from a meta-analysis —comprising 33,948 unique participants, 78 samples, and 53 studies, in 21 countries—show that the effect of perceived HRM system strength on the employee outcomes is influenced by the climatic demands of a country. A harsh climate strengthens the positive effect of perceived HRM system strength on employee attitudes and wellbeing more than a mild climate. Furthermore, climatic demands and economic resources jointly influence the effect of perceived HRM system strength on discretionary behaviors: In wealthy nations, the moderating effect of harsh climate on the positive effect of HRM system strength tends to be mitigated. Finally, we discuss theoretical and practical implications of these results.

Empowered or Marginalized? The Impact of Smart HRM Technology on HR Department Influence

HR
Shijun Huang (Nanjing University); Jieyu Hu (Nanjing University Business School); Youqing FAN (Western Sydney University)
This study examines the impact of Smart HRM technology on HR department influence. Using data from line managers and HR managers from 56 companies in China, the results show that Smart HRM technology has a positive indirect effect on HR department influence through HR organizational capital. Conversely, it has a negative indirect effect on HR department influence through HR socialization. Additionally, HR symbolic competence strengthens the relationship between HRM organizational capital and HR influence. Specifically, the effect of HR organizational capital on HR department influence is stronger when HR symbolic competence is high. This study contributes to the strategic HRM and digital HRM literature by offering new theoretical perspectives and practical insights on how HR departments can reconstruct their influence in a digitalized environment.

Balancing the Inside and Out: How Firms Navigate Trade-Offs Between External CSR and HIHRS

HR
Joonyoung Kim (University of Missouri-Columbia); Hyesook Chung (The University of Texas at Dallas); Julia Stevenson-Street (University of Missouri-Columbia)
Integrating insights from strategic human resource management (SHRM) and stakeholder salience theory, we investigate how firms reallocate internal resources toward employees in light of their externally-directed corporate social responsibility (CSR) orientation. We focus on changes in high-investment human resource systems (HIHRS) as a key form of internal stakeholder investment and theorize that firms with stronger external CSR orientation may reduce discretionary HIHRS investments, reflecting trade-offs between external legitimacy and internal resource deployment. Using a 22-year panel of 21,934 firm-year observations from 2,176 publicly traded U.S. firms, we find that external CSR orientation is negatively associated with year-over-year changes in HIHRS. This relationship is moderated by contextual factors that shape the urgency (crises and collective turnover), legitimacy (practice-level and firm-level legitimacy), and power of stakeholders (human capital intensity and B2B vs. B2C classification). Our findings contribute to SHRM, resource management, and CSR literatures by illuminating how firms navigate resource allocation trade-offs in response to shifting stakeholder salience across organizational and industry contexts.

Energizing the Underground: An Integrated Human Energy Perspective on IHRM Practices and Bootlegging

HR
Jiaojiao Qu (Huaqiao University); Hao Zeng (Jiangxi Science and Technology Normal University); yan feng (Huaqiao University); Rong Fang (Huaqiao University)
As an indispensable part of independent and open innovation, bootlegging plays an important role in promoting and supporting the development of new quality productivity. Previous studies have explored the impacts of various factors on bootlegging at organizational, leadership, job characteristics, and individual levels. However, theoretical research focusing on the antecedents of bootlegging at the organizational level is relatively scarce, and the impact mechanism of specific human resource management practices on bootlegging is not yet clear. Based on the integrated model of human energy, this paper analyzes the triggering mechanism of inclusive human resource management practices on bootlegging from an energy perspective while considering the dual paths of authorization (job autonomy) and empowerment (thriving at work), and examines the boundary role of leader secure-base support. Using three waves of data collection from 1,095 individuals at 179 small- and medium-sized enterprises, this study finds that: (1) Inclusive human resource management practices have a positive impact on bootlegging; (2) The relationship between inclusive human resource management practices and bootlegging is mediated by both job autonomy and thriving at work, with inclusive human resource management practices promoting bootlegging by enhancing employees’ job autonomy and thriving at work; (3) Leader secure-base support has a positive moderating effect on the indirect relationship between inclusive human resource management practices, thriving at work, and bootlegging. Put otherwise, the higher the level of leader secure-base support, the stronger the positive impact of inclusive human resource management practices on bootlegging through thriving at work. Meanwhile, the moderating effect of leader secure-base support on the indirect relationship between inclusive human resource management practices, job autonomy, and bootlegging is not significant. These results confirm the promotional mechanism of inclusive human resource management practices on bootlegging from an energy perspective, not only enriching our understanding of the impact of inclusive human resource management practices on bootlegging, particularly at the organizational level, but also expanding the research on inclusive human resource management practice outcomes. It furthermore expands the literature regarding the boundary conditions of leader secure-base support and its interactions between human resource management systems and leadership. Ultimately, the results of this study expand the theoretical basis for enterprises to implement inclusive management strategies to stimulate employee bootlegging.
