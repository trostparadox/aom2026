Integrating Goal Conflict in Innovation Strategy: The Role of R&D and Industry Growth

TIM
Ngoc Quynh Dao (University of Neuchatel)
This paper examines how firms balance economic and sustainability-related objectives in their innovation strategies and identifies the conditions under which firms allocate greater attention to sustainability. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm and the attention-based view, I analyze how financial performance shortfalls relative to aspirations—both social (peer-based) and historical (firm-based)—interact with industry growth to shape innovation goal orientation. Using panel data from the KOF Swiss Innovation and Digitalization Surveys (1996–2011), I measure innovation goals from managers’ reported importance of multiple objectives and distinguish an economic goal index from a cost efficiency/environment (sustainability-related) goal index; results are robust to SEM-based latent measures. Empirically, social performance shortfalls are associated with higher R&D intensity, consistent with problemistic search, and R&D intensity is positively related to attention to both economic and cost efficiency/environment goals, suggesting that R&D can serve as a multifinal means supporting multiple objectives simultaneously. In contrast, the association between performance shortfalls and goal orientation is conditional on long-run industry growth. Historical performance shortfalls are linked to stronger emphasis on cost efficiency and sustainability-related goals, but this relationship weakens in industries characterized by positive and stable growth. Social performance shortfalls, by comparison, are associated with lower emphasis on cost efficiency/environment goals in declining industries; however, in growing industries the interaction offsets this pattern, shifting attention back toward cost efficiency and sustainability. The findings contribute to behavioral strategy by clarifying boundary conditions under which firms respond to aspiration shortfalls by reallocating attention across economic and sustainability-related innovation goals.

Knowing What Not to Do: R&D Experiments and Technological Trajectories

TIM
Topi Taipale (Tampere University); Mirva Peltoniemi (Tampere University); Sandeep Pillai (Tulane University); Antti Sihvonen
How do R&D experiments shape technological trajectories? Much research has examined how the selection environment and the organization of R&D units within firms influence the evolution of technological trajectories but the internal mechanisms through which R&D experimentation contributes to this evolution remain less understood. We open the black box of R&D and analyze the relation between technical possibilities and technological trajectories in a unique context that does not interact with the market. Using historical methods and grounded theory techniques, we study 437 research project reports from Central Laboratory, which operated as the joint R&D lab of the Finnish forest industry companies from 1916 to 2009. Our findings suggest that in addition to generating actionable knowledge that increases technical variation, R&D experiments also produce restraining knowledge about “what not to do”. This type of knowledge stabilizes and directs technological trajectories, and it is not visible to the market in the form of patent records. We contribute to the literature by identifying and conceptualizing the types of knowledge that firms produce through R&D. Moreover, these knowledge types offer a possible explanation for why absorptive capacity varies between firms despite the similar level in investments in R&D and why do firms invest in R&D even under low appropriability regimes.

Knowledge Use And Reuse In Orphan Drug Development: A Knowledge-Based Perspective

TIM
Yelin Hu (The University of Manchester); Dimitri Gagliardi (The University of Manchester); Shukhrat Nasirov (The University of Manchester)
Commercial organizations that operate under uncertainty and resource constraints increasingly draw on knowledge reuse. However, dominant perspectives frame it as a product-level shortcut rather than a strategic innovation logic. In this research, we recast orphan drug development as a knowledge-driven innovation process, where commercial organizations manage the exploration and exploitation of three interrelated knowledge domains – scientific, medical, and product – to create and reuse knowledge across innovation activities. This perspective explains when knowledge reuse can rival de novo discovery and why it can be attractive when market payoffs are uncertain. Our conceptual model connects distinct exploration-exploitation pathways to their outcomes and to orphan-drug types. It reveals three knowledge-enabled strategic benefits: branching development directions, building pipeline backbones, and establishing research networks. Therefore, we shift the conversation from a market-centric perspective to knowledge reuse for innovation under uncertainty, demonstrating how organizational learning generates transformative outcomes within the orphan drug context.

Navigating Uncertainty? Systems Integration Capability and Firm Innovation Persistence

TIM
Guangpei Chen (University of Electronic Science and Technology of China); Jiang Wei (Zhejiang University of Finance and Economics); Qing Wang (University of Warwick); Yansong Hu (University of Warwick)
The heightened uncertainty in R&D activities caused by the evolving nature of technology and external institutional shocks has significantly undermine a firm’s ability to sustain innovation engagement and continuously generate innovative outcomes. This paper focuses on systems integration capability, a higher-order dynamic capability that enables firms to form a coherent product architecture by linking and synthesizing both internal and external resource and competences, and investigates its role in fostering firms’ innovation persistence. Drawing on an unbalanced panel dataset of 1,735 Chinese manufacturing firms during the period 2005-2024, we find that systems integration capability, measured by the relative property of a firm’s knowledge breadth vis-à-vis its immediate production demands, can significantly enhance firm innovation persistence. Moreover, this positive effect is more salient in sub-industries where the technology is fast-moving (i.e. high technological dynamism), or is highly reliant on foreign sources (i.e. high technological dependence). Our study provides both theoretical and practical implications for persistent innovation in uncertain environments.

Negative Transfer in Utilizing Generational Products

TIM
Naoki Yasuda (Rikkyo University)
Although effective utilization of innovation is crucial for sustaining firm growth, existing research has not fully addressed its complexities. Prior studies emphasize the benefits of accumulated experience, showing that experience increases post-adoption utilization by shortening learning curves. Yet organizational learning research highlights the potential liability of experience, as firms may misapply prior knowledge across contexts. We examine this tension in the context of generational products, where firms frequently rely on accumulated knowledge to adopt new versions. We argue that firms with extensive experience in earlier, weakly related generations are less effective in deploying new products. Using data on airlines’ adoption of Boeing 737 NG aircraft, we find strong evidence for this view. We further extend the literature by showing that negative transfers are more pronounced when firms fail to recognize generational differences, abandon obsolete knowledge, and allocate sufficient managerial attention during adoption.
