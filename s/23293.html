A Theory of Temporal Effectuation

ENT
Christopher Wynn (University of Victoria)
A key challenge for entrepreneurs is to unite stakeholders with divergent temporal perceptions by configuring narrative time as temporal work. In early-stage ventures, uncertainty is temporal. Stakeholders lack a shared basis for judging pace and horizons as they often disagree about when action and value realization should occur. Entrepreneurs therefore perform temporal work, a relevant theoretical lens that studies how entrepreneurs can garner stakeholder commitments for their ideas amid uncertainty by using narratives to bridge different time horizons to construct a workable timeline that enables commitment. Relatedly, effectuation theory explains how entrepreneurs act without reliable forecasts, yet effectuation scholarship largely treats time as an implicit, backgrounded construct. This paper develops a theory of temporal effectuation, foregrounding time, and proposes a narrative process through which entrepreneurs manipulate the past, present, and future during uncertainty. I identify two time related mechanisms in entrepreneurial storytelling that facilitate the narrative process. Temporal conflation blends distinct temporal perspectives to produce a single coherent sequence that renders ventures plausible for heterogeneous audiences. Temporal elasticity stretches or compresses temporal windows to pair immediacy with long-term credibility as narratives evolve. Together, these mechanisms show how narrative time functions as an important effectuation related resource for garnering stakeholder commitment through entrepreneurial narratives. The theory links effectuation to temporal work scholarship and offers actionable guidance for researchers and practitioners seeking to synchronize stakeholders’ divergent temporal perspectives, thereby sustaining coordinated commitment and venture momentum when outcomes and timelines are uncertain.

Beyond the Checklist: A Process-Based Model of Learning Effectuation

ENT
Cynthia Maria Katharina Zabel (ESCP Business School); Rene Mauer (ESCP Business School)
In an era of economic volatility, traditional predictive management models are often insufficient. While effectuation offers a critical logic for navigating uncertainty, understanding how professionals actually acquire it remains limited. This paper bridges effectuation theory with adult learning science to investigate how participants perceive key factors for effectively learning effectuation. Adopting a qualitative single-case design, we examined a ‘train-the-trainer’ program for professionals in the DACH region. Data collection involved 50 semi-structured interviews with 29 participants across six training cycles. Using iterative analysis, we identified overarching themes regarding the learning process. Our analysis reveals that participants perceive effectuation learning not as a static checklist, but as a dynamic cycle of ‘Action,’ ‘Sensemaking,’ ‘Reflection,’ and ‘Commitment.’ This challenges passive pedagogies, suggesting effective learning requires learner-centered, experiential methods. The study contributes a process-oriented theoretical model connecting effectual principles with adult learning theories. This offers educators an actionable framework for designing curricula based on immersive cycles of action and reflection. By illuminating the cognitive shift required, this research makes effectuation more accessible and practical for the global entrepreneurship community. Key Words: Effectuation, Adult Learning, Entrepreneurship Education, Process Model, Qualitative Research

Entrepreneurship Under Constraint How Bricolage Transitions into Effectuation in Family Businesses

ENT
Swapnil Sahoo (Great Lakes Institute of Management, Gurgaon); Munish Kumar Thakur
How do entrepreneurs in resource-constrained environments (RCEs) innovate despite limited resources and weak institutions? This study examines the sequential relationship between bricolage and effectuation in Indian RCEs. We argue that innovation often begins with improvised, individual bricolage and evolves into effectual action as social legitimacy and access to mainstream resources increase. Drawing on ERG theory and sociomateriality, we show that entrepreneurial behaviour is shaped by existence needs and material engagement. The study confirms that effectuation mediates the link between bricolage and performance, and that socioemotional wealth (SEW), though helpful for legitimacy, negatively moderates the bricolage–effectuation relationship.

Expanding Effectuation Through Imagination and Spiritual Means

ENT
Juan Manuel Gil (Butler University)
Effectuation theory explains how entrepreneurs navigate uncertainty through available means rather than predetermined goals, yet its explanatory architecture privileges cognitive-rational framings that treat entrepreneurial knowing as primarily explicit, codifiable, and amenable to conscious deliberation. This paper extends effectuation by theorizing imagination means and spiritual means as transversal resources that operate pre-reflectively throughout the effectual process. We argue that these means do not function as additional inputs to decision-making but constitute the perceptual field within which cognitive analysis subsequently unfolds, shaping what entrepreneurs perceive as possible and worthy before explicit deliberation commences. The Embodied Effectuation Model offers a reconceptualization of effectuation’s central questions by highlighting the embodied and spiritual dimensions that influence entrepreneurial action, regardless of whether entrepreneurs consciously recognize them. Our framework advances effectuation theory by specifying how entrepreneurs orient action when cognitive analysis provides insufficient foundation for choice, identifying boundary conditions under which pre-reflective knowing becomes most theoretically relevant, and differentiating entrepreneurship from managerial activities that presume cognitively tractable environments. These contributions open new research directions examining how imagination and spiritual capacities develop across entrepreneurial careers and how they interact with cognitive means across diverse contexts.

Toward a Venture Diffusion Theory of Entrepreneurial Performance in Disequilibrium

ENT
Patrick J. Murphy (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Parul Manocha (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Christopher Crawford (University of Tampa)
Successful entrepreneurial ventures routinely manifest as performance outliers, systematically violating the normality and equilibrium assumptions of conventional industrial-organization frameworks. Although entrepreneurship research has increasingly embraced power law and complexity perspectives to address these empirical regularities, most applications remain macro-oriented, with limited insight into individual venture dynamics. In this conceptual paper, we integrate epidemiological principles with general systems theory and complexity science to extend power law reasoning to the venture level. We reconceptualize venture growth with diffusion logic, tunable parameters, phase transitions, and the harmonic nature of feedback. Our contribution bridges external disequilibrium and endogenous operations, providing a unified foundation for theorizing population-level skewness and idiosyncratic entrepreneurial performance.
