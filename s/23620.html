Power, Inequality, and Inclusion Across Difference

Session overview

This session brings together research on how inequality is reproduced, negotiated, and sometimes challenged across lines of race, caste, gender, and organizational difference. The papers examine decoupling in gender equality efforts, caste-based favoritism, leadership pathways for researchers of color, whiteness in teams, and the liberating potential of cross-difference work relationships. Together, they illuminate how power operates in organizational life while also identifying relational and structural possibilities for more meaningful inclusion.

The Last Mile: Decoupling Dynamics and Gender Equality in Finance

DEI
Carol T. Kulik (Adelaide University); Isabel Metz (Melbourne Business School, University of Melbourne)
Despite decades of equal employment opportunity legislation and gender diversity initiatives, progress toward gender equality in private sector organizations has been frustratingly slow. Responding to calls in the decoupling literature for more empirical work in uncertain business environments, we conducted a case study of a business unit in Australia’s finance sector. We found evidence of both policy-practice decoupling (symbolic adoption of policies) and means-ends decoupling (symbolic implementation of practices), characterized by three organizational elements: an exclusive focus on a single gender equality indicator, a chief executive’s blind spot, and a risk-averse organizational culture. Our findings demonstrate that unchecked policy-practice decoupling creates the conditions for means-ends decoupling: when the business unit’s initial diversity practices failed to achieve its gender diversity goals, decision-makers adopted a wider portfolio of practices that were even less effective. The chief executive’s vocal support for gender diversity and a “best practice” portfolio isolated diversity activities from the unit’s core business and diverted employees’ attention away from the lack of substantive progress. We recommend that organizations adopt an experimental approach with customized practices to avoid becoming mired in the “last mile” of their diversity journey.

Outgroup Favoritism and Inequality Reproduction In the Workplace: The Case of Caste in India

DEI
Anurag Gupta (City University of Hong Kong); Marko Pitesa (Singapore Management University)
Organizations remain sites where group inequalities persist even as overt discrimination is increasingly regulated. We advance a supply side account of hierarchy reproduction: when some members of disadvantaged groups come to endorse group based inequality, they channel discretionary help upward, quietly compounding advantage. India’s caste system—a rigid, ascribed, and culturally legitimized order—provides a most likely case to observe such subordinate participation. Integrating Social Dominance and System Justification perspectives, we predict—and, in a preregistered attitudinal study and a multi source field study in mixed caste teams, show—that lower caste employees report higher social dominance orientation than higher caste employees and, guided by this endorsement, preferentially help higher caste coworkers in ways that elevate recipients’ performance and promotability; these effects are specific to caste and are not mirrored by social class background. We complement the dominant demand side lens that treats inequality as something done to subordinates by elites and demonstrate how a celebrated practice—helping—can function as an unobtrusive mechanism of hierarchy maintenance in formally egalitarian settings. For Social Dominance Theory and System Justification Theory, our results provide a rare, real world demonstration of explicit subordinate endorsement exceeding dominant group endorsement on a strong measure—offering a rare, real world demonstration of the theories’ most controversial implication.

The Invisible Infrastructure: Why Leading Researchers of Color Become Research Leaders

DEI
Daphne C. Watkins (University of Michigan)
This qualitative study reports findings from an investigation of the complex journeys of faculty of color who lead research institutes, centers, labs, and programs in North America. Drawing on Crenshaw's intersectionality frameworks and Critical Race Theory, we examine how social identities shape leadership pathways within academic environments. Through semi-structured, in-depth interviews with 15 research leaders of color (RLOC) at research-intensive institutions, this study examined why these researchers became research leaders. Thematic analysis using the rigorous and accelerated data reduction (RADaR) technique uncovered patterns of resilience and strategic navigation of academic structures, while highlighting the added complexities and diminished recognition often experienced by RLOC. Findings reveal that research leadership development among scholars of color occurs primarily through observational learning, trial-and-error, intentional mentorship-seeking, and leveraging lived experiences, rather than through formal research leadership training. Participants described unique challenges in team building and resource acquisition, directly connected to their social identities, and often navigated institutional environments where their authority and expertise faced heightened scrutiny. This study offers vital insights for institutions seeking to diversify research leadership and inspires emerging scholars of color to pursue research leadership for social impact in academic environments.

The Team Was Like Sweden: A Discursive-Psychodynamic Analysis of Whiteness in an All-White Team

DEI
Erica Gabrielle Foldy (New York University); Mica Baum-Tuccillo (City University of New York Graduate Center)
This paper examines the dynamics of an all-white team that was consulting with a predominantly Black and Latino organization. Because of this racial difference, team members were anxious that their whiteness would lead them to do or say something offensive. We use discursive analysis to investigate team members’ speech and explicit sensemaking – for example, joking that their team was like Sweden. We deploy systems psychodynamics to explore underground psychological defenses that stemmed from their anxiety but ultimately reinforced it, because these processes diverted the team from directly addressing its racialized fears. We develop a model that shows how the team is influenced by its context, including common societal discourses related to whiteness. The team’s internalization of these discourses spurs their anxiety about being offensive which then prompts them to bond tightly together as well as split off concerns about themselves and identify their client as the problem. Ultimately, this interlock of discourses and psychological defenses becomes an amplifying loop. Moreover, by integrating discursive and psychodynamic lenses, we show how whiteness functions not merely as ideology or identity but as a defensive, affectively charged system of meaning that impacts team functioning and possibilities for cross-racial collaboration.

Different, Together: The Liberating Potential of Cross-Difference Work Relationships

DEI
Jennifer Wiseman; Amelia Stillwell (The University of Utah); Jared Mitchell Poole (University of Massachusetts Boston)
This study examines how positive workplace relationships across race and gender differences create spaces for partners’ mutual liberation from constraining stereotypes and hierarchical expectations— relational microemancipations. While prior research conceptualizes microemancipations as individual acts of resistance, we demonstrate their relational dimension through a qualitative study of diverse partnerships in collegiate athletics administration. Drawing on interviews, embedded observation, and archival data of NCAA athletics administrators in such self-identified positive partnerships, we identify interrelated perceptions, dyadic interactions, and public-facing partner support practices that sustain and strengthen relationships across differences, enabling both dominant and minoritized partners to transcend constraining expectations. We outline a process model of how positive relationships can develop when organizational support and equal status are ambiguous. Our findings offer insights into how positive workplace relationships across differences develop and sustain amid the constraints of hierarchical organizational contexts.
