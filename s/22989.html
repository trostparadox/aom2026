From Pride to Peril? The Impact of MNE Foreign Divestments on Home-Country Employee Sentiment

IM
Chenjing Wang (Peking University); Vladislav Maksimov (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Li Tong (Peking University)
Foreign divestment has emerged as a critical component of multinational enterprises’ (MNEs) strategic decisions. While prior research has examined the antecedents of foreign divestments, we shift the focus to the internal consequences, specifically the impact on their home-country employee sentiment. Drawing on psychological contract theory, we propose that foreign divestments may be perceived by employees as a breach of the implicit promises associated with the prestige and global opportunities of working for an MNE, thereby eliciting negative sentiment. We further theorize that this effect is shaped by attribution processes, guided by two key cues: the MNEs’ exposure to geopolitical tensions (external cue) and their environmental, social, and governance reputation (internal cue). To test our arguments, we constructed a panel dataset of 1,007 U.S. publicly listed firms, comprising 7,586 firm-year observations from 2008 to 2023, matched with 341,390 Glassdoor ratings submitted by these firms’ home-country employees. Our study contributes to the foreign divestment literature by highlighting its internal ramifications, extending traditional internationalization perspectives through a psychological lens, and offering new insights for international human resource management research.

Configuring International Collaboration: A Modular Microfoundations Approach

IM
Gina Aprilitasari (King's College London); Johann Fortwengel (King's College London); Crawford Spence (King's College London)
This paper applies a modular microfoundations perspective to examine how internal decision-making processes shape firms’ non-equity international collaboration strategies. Drawing on an inductive, comparative case study of twelve professional service firms (PSFs), the study explores how variation in interpretive mechanisms gives rise to different strategic configurations of cross-border collaboration. Two micro-level dimensions emerged through iterative analysis—organizational identity commitment and interpretive simplicity—which interact to produce four types of collaborative strategies: Symbolic Guardians, Alignment Strategists, Opportunistic Brokers, and Growth Architects. These patterns explain why firms differ in degree of formality, scope of engagement, and partner selection. Anchored in the B–C–D segment of Coleman’s (1990) micro–macro framework, the study provides a partial modular explanation that links decision-team sensemaking to firm-level strategic outcomes. By shifting attention from external contingencies to internal meaning-making processes, the paper advances microfoundational perspectives on alliance strategy in international business. While grounded in the PSF context, the findings offer broader relevance for organizational settings—such as family firms, start-ups, and founder-led businesses—where strategic direction is shaped less by formal governance routines and more by managerial influences, professional norms, and value-based reasoning.

The Cultural Individualism Contingency of Trust and Opportunism in Cross-Border B2B Data Sharing (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Nicolas Oliver Gilgen (University of St. Gallen); Shelby Meek (Kennesaw State University); Matthias Tietz (University of St. Gallen)
This study explores how cultural individualism moderates the relationship between different types of trust (interpersonal vs. interorganizational) and opportunism (known vs. assumed, active vs. passive) and managers’ willingness to share data. Our conjoint experiment with 349 managers across four continents – from Tunisia, Thailand, the USA, and DACH region (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) totaling 2,792 data-sharing decisions – reveals cross-cultural variations: in individualistic contexts, interorganizational trust mitigates active opportunism. Collectivists prefer clarity about specific known opportunistic risks over assumed opportunism. We identify substitution effects and extend the ‘cushion hypothesis’ to individualists relying on organizational commitments and exit options as safety nets.

I See Omens: Irrationality of MNE CEOs in International Risk Taking

IM
Yi Yang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Longwei Tian
Individuals often resort to irrational behaviors in circumstances characterized by high uncertainty, high stakes, and a lack of control. Such characteristics are prevalent in decision-making of internationalization. In this study, we examine the impact Chinese zodiac on CEOs’ risk-taking in internationalization. We propose that CEOs become more risk taking when zodiac shows auspicious omens and less aggressive when zodiac shows ominous omens. We also investigate two moderating factors —social movement and CEO board power. Empirically, we compiled a database of 1,102 CEOs from 673 Chinese MNEs from 2006 to 2017. We found supportive evidence for our hypotheses by integrating fixed-effect models and a sharp regression discontinuity design (RDD). This research adds a new dimension to the study of CEOs’ decision making in international business.
