The Optimal Measure of Distinctiveness: Aligning Theory of Evaluation with Practice of Measurement

STR
Aria Yongting Huang (University of Oxford); Eric Zhao (University of Oxford); Alexander Vossen (Tilburg University)
Distinctiveness is a central concept in strategy and organization research. Organizations strive to stand out, yet in ways that others can recognize and value. While the notion of distinctiveness is prevalent—whether framed as differentiation, innovation, identity, or category—we rarely ask whether the measures of distinctiveness align with theories of how audiences evaluate it. This potential misalignment underpins empirical fragmentation that pervades optimal distinctiveness (OD) research—a growing literature that explores how organizations balance differentiation and conformity. We argue that empirical inconsistencies in OD are not a signal of weak theory but stem from a lack of alignment between theory and measures. Seemingly similar measures of distinctiveness encode different evaluative logics about how audiences perceive, compare, and value difference. We demonstrate this through a systematic review of 78 OD studies and an empirical comparison of 42 distinctiveness measures, showing that measurement variance mirrors theoretical variance, as each measure models a distinct way that audiences perceive and judge difference. To resolve this misalignment, we develop a theory-method alignment framework, linking the theory of evaluation to the practice of measurement. The framework reframes measurement as a theoretical act, transforming methodological diversity from a source of fragmentation into a foundation for cumulative theorizing about distinctiveness in organizations.

Capturing Industry Cluster Dynamics: A Reproduction, Replication, and Extension of Kim et al. (2022)

STR
Andreas Schwab (Iowa State University); Xavier Martin (Tilburg University); Tess Beck (Florida Atlantic University); Kalan Giovonni Horton (Rochester Institute of Technology); Azucena Grady (Texas Tech University); Aditya Sai Phutane (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); YI YING (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Jasmine Nicole Kelley (University of Tennessee at Chattanooga); Wuraola Ayomide Okuwobi; Kelvin Memeh (The Ohio State University); Vitaliy Skorodziyevskiy (University of Louisville); Michael D Ryall (Florida Atlantic University); Yuxi (Lucy) Zhao (Concordia University); Alanna Hirshman (Texas Tech University); Justin Tumlinson (University of Exeter); Vishal K. Gupta (University of Alabama); Athina Skiadopoulou (University of Alabama)
This study presents a systematic replication of Kim, Shaver, and Funk's (2022) novel framework for measuring the dynamics of industry clusters. Nine independent research teams comprising doctoral students and faculty mentors conducted independent reproductions, exact replications, and constructive replications across multiple industries and an extended timeframe (1974-2022). Our reproductions and exact replications validate the original findings reported by Kim et al. (2022) for the semiconductor industry, confirming the reliability of the measurement framework. Constructive replications, applying the agglomeration measurement framework to nine different industries—automotive, aerospace, venture capital, shipbuilding, entertainment, real estate, video games, steel, and wind power—help identify both strengths and limitations of this framework. Based on these insights, we present best practices for applying these measures, including addressing U.S. Census data quality issues, assessing structural break validity and reliability, and other guidelines. Overall, our findings strongly support the validity and broader applicability of Kim et al.'s (2022) framework, while also highlighting boundary conditions and opportunities for methodological refinements. Furthermore, this project contributes not only to the agglomeration dynamics research but also demonstrates the value of collaborative multi-team replication efforts in advancing scientific knowledge.

Mature Industry and Optimal Distinctiveness

STR
Huajie Shen; Stan X. Li (City University of Hong Kong)
Optimal distinctiveness refers to the approach where a firm maintains a moderate level of differentiation from the industry standard to balance the conflicting demands of being unique while also conforming. This concept has recently gained popularity in research and is viewed as a broad positioning strategy. However, the understanding of optimal distinctiveness throughout various industry stages and the suitable criteria for differentiation remains unclear. To fill this gap, we suggest that companies in a mature industry phase should use the prototypes of their specific strategic groups as benchmarks instead of the overall industry prototype. This strategy is beneficial because group prototypes help firms identify the diverse competitive landscape and the legitimacy challenges within their strategic group, making it easier to manage the tension between conformity and differentiation. Our analysis is based on a comprehensive dataset from the Canadian property and casualty insurance sector covering the years 2000 to 2020, which provides strong support for this perspective.

Does Second-Generation Succession Drive New Industry Entry?

STR
Baoxuan Yan (Sun Yat-Sen University); Yingxian Zeng (Jinan University); Likai Zou (Shantou University); Xinchun Li (Sun Yat-Sen University)
Family firms generally opt for conservative diversification strategies to preserve family control. However, owing to the second generation's lack of legitimacy, weaker emotional attachment to the family firm, and the high transfer cost of the first generation's social network, they are motivated to expand their careers, leading to entering new industries after succession. Empirical analysis of data from Chinese listed family firms reveals that second-generation succession is positively correlated with the family firm's expansion into new industries. While family directors' involvement weakens this positive effect, competition in the main business industry strengthens it. This study demonstrates that when family firms enter the inheritance stage, their goal is not solely to maintain family control and retain their original business territory, but also to expand their business. From an intergenerational inheritance perspective, this study enhances our understanding of the motives behind family firms' diversification expansion strategies and provides insights for adjusting diversification strategies during the inheritance period.

Structural Drivers of Clustering: Insights from a Simulation Model

STR
Jovan Grahovac; John Christopher Butler (The University of Texas at Austin)
We build a simulation model of an emerging industry to study conditions under which transportation costs, early mover advantages, and heterogenous entrepreneurial capabilities lead to the formation of industrial clusters. Focusing on the situation in which resources necessary to start firms are available in all parts of a geographic space, we find that low transportation costs favor the formation of one central industry cluster, moderate transportation costs lead to the breakup of this cluster into four that move toward the corners of the geographic area, and high or extremely low transportation costs lead to the disappearance of clusters. Timing and capability advantages are conducive to agglomeration in the presence of spillovers and resource mobility but result in highly dispersed industry configurations in their absence. Moreover, resource mobility tends to partially offset the clustering effects of spillovers. We also find that the initial local supply of resources and the ability of firms to accumulate them are also conducive to the formation of industrial clusters.
