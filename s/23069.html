Multiple Pathways to Financial Distress: A Set-Theoretic Analysis of Hospital Performance (WITHDRAWN)

HCM
Benjamin Wyss (University of Bern)
Hospitals absorb one third of Swiss healthcare expenditure. Yet, many acute care providers operate under persistent financial pressure, with the majority reporting negative net profits in recent years. Existing research is dominated by US evidence and correlational designs that struggle to capture causal complexity and equifinality. This study applies crisp-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis to identify configurations of conditions associated with financial performance in Swiss acute care hospitals. Using national data for 2022-2023, we analyze 97 hospitals across three subgroups (large public, small public, and private hospitals), complemented with eighteen expert interviews. We find no necessary conditions for sustainable or unsustainable financial performance, indicating that no single factor consistently determines outcomes. Instead, we identify twelve configurations that are sufficient for financial distress. The same factor can lead to success or failure depending on its combination with other conditions. For instance, high case complexity leads to distress when combined with below average insurance mix but not when paired with operational efficiency. This configurational complexity explains why prior correlational studies produced inconsistent findings. The study provides empirical evidence of equifinality and conjunctural causation in hospital finance and demonstrates the value of configurational approaches for analyzing financial performance.

“A Done Deal to be Made”: Post-Merger Decision-Making for a Rural Hospital in a Multihospital System

HCM
Alison Coates (Clarkson University); Suhaib Riaz (University of Ottawa); Janice Probst (University of South Carolina); Agnes Grudniewicz (University of Ottawa)
Hospitals and health systems in the United States are merging at historic rates, yet little is known about what happens afterwards regarding strategic decision-making. Following a merger, hospital-network decision-making environment becomes more structurally complex due to multiple interdependent governance structures and multilevel decision-making authority and more socially complex due to goal conflicts, cultural differences, and shifting power relations. To better understand strategic decision-making in these structurally and socially complex contexts, we explored the case of a rural hospital that had recently become part of a larger health network and executed several strategic decisions. We describe how different decision-making groups (hospital and network executives and governing boards) engaged in an interactive and inclusive process of information exchange to align their individual understandings of the hospital’s problems and the solutions, and to mobilize the decision. Rather than a classical model of decision-making, we discovered that, in this context, decision-making resembled a process of frame alignment that resulted in a collective decision frame. Viewing the post-merger social environment through the lens of social movement theory may help to improve post-merger integration and increase the potential for successful strategic decision-making.

Nursing Staff Turnover Mediation between Rural–Urban Location and U.S. Nursing Home Quality

HCM
Shivani Gupta (UHCL); Akbar Ghiasi (University of the Incarnate Word); Devdutt Upadhye (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Robert Weech-Maldonado (University of Alabama at Birmingham)
Rural nursing homes consistently exhibit lower quality of care than urban facilities, but the mechanisms underlying it remain insufficiently understood. Nursing staff turnover (RN, LPN, and CNA) is a plausible pathway because workforce instability can disrupt continuity and coordination of care. The purpose of this study is to test whether nursing staff turnover mediates the association between rural location and nursing home quality. The data for 2021 to 2023 were derived from PBJ, Care Compare, USDA, LTCFocus, and AHRF. The primary independent variable was location (rural = 1, urban = 0). The dependent variable was the quality measures star rating. Nursing staff turnover rates were modeled as parallel mediator variables between location and quality. SEM was done to examine the direct, indirect and total effects of location on quality. We controlled for facility and environmental characteristics influencing nursing homes’ quality of care. Rural location was associated with lower quality star ratings (negative direct effect). Higher nurse staffing turnover was also associated with lower quality. However, rural location was associated with lower nursing staff turnover, producing positive indirect effects. Despite this offsetting pathway, the total effect of rural location on quality remained negative. Lower turnover in rural facilities partially attenuates the rural quality disadvantage, suggesting that other structural constraints (e.g., resource limitations and payer mix) may play a larger role in driving lower rural performance. Therefore, policies that strengthen the capacity of rural and Medicaid-heavy nursing homes—alongside targeted workforce stabilization —may be most effective for improving rural nursing home quality.

Staying to Care: Coping, Commitment, and Professional Identity in Resource-Constrained Health System

HCM
Gregory N. Orewa (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Nero Edevbie (Sam Houston State University); Juliet Osuji (North Carolina Agricultural and Technical State University); Emmanuella Osih (The University of British Columbia); Desiet Stifanos (The University of Texas at San Antonio)
Although physician migration from low-resourced health systems has been widely studied, little is known about how physicians who remain cope with the chronic institutional strain that shapes their everyday work. Drawing on in-depth qualitative data from physicians practicing in a resource-constrained healthcare system, this study examines how doctors navigate persistent resource shortages, weak institutions, inadequate compensation, and widespread patient poverty. We identify four interconnected coping strategies – resigned acceptance, emotional resilience, relational dependency, and strategic adaptability – that enable physicians to continue practicing despite sustained ethical, psychological, and organizational strain. These strategies extend beyond traditional problem-focused or emotion-focused responses, operating instead as a relational and organizationally embedded process shaped by professional values and structural constraints. Physicians rely on informal networks, ethical improvisation, and shared practices to compensate for institutional breakdown while sustaining professional identity and commitment to patient care. Together, these findings extend coping theory beyond individualistic assumptions and highlight the ethical and relational dimensions of adaptation in fragile contexts. The study offers important implications for physician retention, suggesting that effective policy and organizational interventions must move beyond financial incentives to address institutional strengthening and physicians’ psychosocial well-being.

Routine Dynamics Under Crisis: How Japanese Nursing Care Organizations Responded to the Pandemic

HCM
Shinsuke Tahara
This study examines how Japanese nursing care organizations reconfigured their organizational routines in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and how these adaptations influenced their long-term sustainability. Employing the theoretical lens of routine dynamics, the research identifies which routines were altered, which remained stable, and how these choices impacted organizational resilience. Utilizing a case study approach, the study draws on interviews with 30 individuals—including managers and frontline staff—from four nursing care organizations, alongside video-based interaction analysis of caregiving practices. The findings indicate that organizations with lower sustainability tended to adopt mechanical, low-engagement routines that prioritized staff convenience at the expense of user well-being. In contrast, organizations demonstrating stronger sustainability preserved user-centered care and communication routines throughout the pandemic. Many also continued implementing learning therapy, a cognitively engaging program that functioned as a mechanism for routine transformation and the maintenance of meaningful interactions. These practices enhanced organizational capabilities and adaptability. This study contributes to the literature on routine dynamics by illustrating how external crises like COVID-19 can act as catalysts for routine change, which in turn can either undermine or reinforce the long-term sustainability of human service organizations.
