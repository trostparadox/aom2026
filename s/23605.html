Anchored in Noise: The Impact of Dominant Team Members on Idea Relevance in Brainstorming

TIM
Felix Mosner (University of Cologne); Fabian J. Sting (University of Cologne); Christoph Fuchs (University of Vienna)
Organizations increasingly rely on brainstorming to harness the creative potential of teams, yet collective ideation often falls short of its promise. While prior research has identified structural barriers such as production blocking and evaluation apprehension, little is known about how individual-level dominance shapes the dynamics linking individual and group ideation in real organizational settings. Using data from 180 naturally occurring brainstorming sessions involving 30 teams in a manufacturing firm, we examine how dominant group members impact idea productivity across sequential ideation stages. Contrary to conventional expectations, sessions with dominant members do not produce fewer ideas overall. Instead, we find systematic differences in the distribution and relevance of ideas across stages. Dominant members generate disproportionately many off-topic ideas during the individual ideation phase. Importantly, their early and assertive inputs significantly reduce both the quantity and relevance of ideas generated during subsequent group ideation, the main brainstorming activity. Our findings extend classic brainstorming theory by showing that production blocking is not merely a mechanical turn-taking constraint but is socially amplified by dominance dynamics. The same may hold for evaluation apprehension. We offer important insights for organizations aiming to optimize their creative group work initiatives.

Poison or Stimulant? Digital Transformation in Family Firms: A Socioemotional Wealth Perspective

TIM
Shisong Jiang (Wuhan University); Shuo ZHANG (Wuhan University); Luwei Li (Wuhan University); Linqing Liu (Wuhan University)
Extant research on digital transformation (DT) largely emphasizes its technological and performance implications. However, DT is also a complex sociocultural process, and firms are deeply concerned with its social consequences. Prior studies have largely overlooked this dimension, limiting our understanding of why firms choose to (non)engage in DT. Given that family members in family firms tend to prioritize socioemotional wealth (SEW) over purely economic objectives, this study focuses on family firms to examine the social characteristics embedded in the DT process. Building on Miller and Le Breton-Miller’s (2014) typology of SEW, we investigate the effects of Restricted SEW and Extended SEW on DT. Using an unbalanced panel dataset comprising 7,494 firm-year observations from 1,399 Chinese family firms between 2009 and 2022, we employ negative binomial regression analyses. The results show that, to avoid external financing and the introduction of external human resources during DT—and in light of the high risks associated with such transformation—family ownership suppresses DT in family firms. In contrast, motivated by concerns over the legitimacy of technology adoption and the firm’s long-term development, family identity promotes DT. Furthermore, we examine the moderating role of the introduction of state capital. The introduction of state capital strengthens the positive effect of family ownership on DT and weakens the inhibiting effect of family identity on DT. By highlighting the socialized nature of DT, this study advances a more comprehensive understanding of DT and extends the scope of research on the two dimensions of SEW.

How Senior Leaders’ Entrepreneurial and Leadership Roles Shape Innovation Efficiency

TIM
Arabella Maddalena Nigg-Stock (University of Liechtenstein); Djordje Zivkovic (University of Liechtenstein); Leo Brecht (University of Liechtenstein)
Research on innovation efficiency, the transformation of a limited set of innovation resources into innovation outputs has gained increased attention in recent years. While studies show that innovation-efficient firms achieve superior long-term performance, the roles of senior leaders for being innovation-efficient is less examined. In this paper we address this research gap and explore how senior managers’ entrepreneurial and leadership roles, defined as identifying and advancing new opportunities and articulating strategic direction, aligning members, and motivating coordinated action, across innovation phases reveal success-promising innovation-efficiency patterns. By examining 24 firms in the DACH–LI region through case study research, our findings suggest that senior managers’ leadership and entrepreneurial roles both contribute to innovation efficiency, yet they do so through different types of activities. Across the four phases of the innovation process (i.e., search, select, implement, and capture), the two roles operate concurrently but encompass distinct responsibilities that together shape how firms advance opportunities and realize innovation outcomes efficiently. With these interesting and novel findings, we provide empirical evidence of distinct patterns throughout four innovation stages and contribute to the ongoing debate of dynamic managerial capabilities in the innovation-efficiency context.

The Consensus Filter: How Inter-Firm Collaboration Shapes Novelty and Impact

TIM
Fabian Sommer (Universität Innsbruck); Johann Fueller; Julian Just (Universität Innsbruck)
The innovation literature widely views inter-firm collaborations as a mean to generate novel and impactful solutions: partners pool distinct knowledge and reach beyond the capabilities of a single firm. Yet, large-scale evidence frequently shows the opposite—collaboration is often linked to conventional outcomes. We resolve this puzzle by viewing the inter-firm boundary not merely as a source of diverse knowledge, but as a 'consensus filter': shared ownership requires agreement on rights and responsibilities, which screens out novelty—especially when technologies are interdependent and fields are mature. Leveraging patent data from (1990–2020), we track firms over time to compare their solo versus inter-firm patents using semantic and combinatorial novelty measures. We find that, on average, collaboration narrows the novelty distribution, filtering moderately unconventional ideas. Yet in complex domains requiring specialized expertise, collaboration can be necessary to produce rare system-level breakthroughs that a single firm is unlikely to achieve alone. We also show why firms keep collaborating: it brings a valuation premium. Joint governance provides a “credibility floor” that helps inventions get recognized and diffuse, even when novelty is constrained. Overall, firms trade novelty variety for more reliable impact and diffusion.
