AI-Technology Coupling Coordination and Regional Technological Path Dependence: Evidence from China

TIM
Noland Y. Ding (Chinese Academy of Science); Yifan Yang (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences); Kaihua Chen (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences); Ling Tang (Institutes of Science and Development, Chinese Academy of Sciences)
The evolution of regional technologies is typically path dependent, as new activities build upon related knowledge bases that restrict the extent of breakthrough diversification. However, the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) may alter these constraints. This study examines how such general-purpose technology (GPT) reshapes these evolutionary constraints. We develop the concept of AI-technology coupling coordination (AI-CCD) to capture the structural embedding and proportional coordination between AI development and regional technological portfolios. Using patent data from China, we analyse how AI integration mitigates path dependence and fosters path-breaking renewal in regional technological evolution. The results show that stronger AI-technology coordination reduces technological lock-in and promotes path-breaking development, revealing that AI can help regions escape entrenched trajectories. These findings provide new insights into how transversal technologies reconfigure regional technological trajectories and advance our understanding of path-dependent and path-breaking dynamics in technological evolution.

Innovation at What Cost? Electricity Demand in Computing Power Development

TIM
Tian Wei (NEOMA Business School); Han WU (Dublin City University)
The development of computing power has become a core innovation activity through which firms build digital capabilities in artificial intelligence, cloud services, and data-intensive applications. Advancing computing power requires continuous innovation in speed, reliability, and scale, yet these performance-oriented choices substantially increase electricity demand. While prior research documents the energy implications of large-scale computing systems, less is known about how managerial decision processes during computing power innovation shape attention to electricity costs. Drawing on salience theory, this study examines how performance-emphasis framing influences electricity cost salience in innovation evaluation. Using an onsite experiment with 783 developers and managers from computing power firms in China, we show that emphasizing computing speed significantly reduces electricity cost salience, while scale-oriented framing has a weaker but still significant effect; reliability-oriented framing shows no systematic impact. These findings are robust across perceptual measures, behavioral choices, and transformer-based language model analyses of open-ended responses. The results reveal a cognitive mechanism through which performance-driven innovation can systematically background resource costs, with implications for sustainable digital innovation.

Technological Concentration and AI Diffusion Versus Specialization Across Countries

TIM
Fernando Sanchez Henriquez (Universidad de Chile); Andres Velez-Calle (EAFIT University); Jessica Salmon; Salma Zaman; Emine Beyza Satoglu
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly conceived of as a General-Purpose Technology (GPT), yet countries differ in whether AI innovation evolves as a broadly diffused technological layer or as a concentrated specialization. Drawing on the GPT literature and research on national innovation systems, we examine how countries’ historical technological concentration shapes AI innovation specialization, and how this relationship is affected by digital access and digital capability maturity. Using an AI patent dataset covering 75 countries from 1998 to 2023 and estimating fractional-response generalized estimating equations (GEE), we find that higher technological concentration is associated with lower AI specialization, consistent with GPT diffusion dynamics rather than path-dependent specialization. However, digital readiness reshapes this relationship: internet access dampens the diffusion effect, whereas secure digital infrastructure flips it toward specialization in technologically concentrated countries. Overall, the findings show that GPT-driven AI diffusion is contingent on country-level digital capabilities, highlighting how different forms of digital readiness channel national AI trajectories toward either broad diffusion or concentrated technological development, with important implications for GPT research and innovation policy.

The Local Economic Effects of AI Data Center Entry

TIM
Yiyang Zeng (Georgia Institute of Technology); Daniel Yue (Not Associated)
The explosive growth of artificial intelligence has triggered an unprecedented wave of data center investment, yet systematic evidence on how these facilities affect local economies remains scarce. We study the local economic effects of data center entry in the United States using comprehensive facility-level data merged with county-level economic outcomes. Employing a Callaway-Sant'Anna difference-in-differences framework that accounts for staggered treatment timing, we find that data center activation increases local employment by 3.5%, total wages by 5.0%, business establishments by 4.7%, and median household income by 1.9%, while reducing unemployment rates and stimulating building permit activity. These effects are concentrated in metropolitan areas, where agglomeration economies amplify multiplier effects, while non-metro counties experience negligible gains. Big Tech facilities generate larger effects than third-party data centers, and counties with early first-entry timing experienced stronger effects than those with recent first-entry timing. We also examine electricity price effects, finding that identification is complicated by utility service territories spanning multiple counties. In areas with cleaner identification (fewer shared utilities), we detect positive price effects, suggesting data center demand raises costs for local ratepayers. Our findings inform ongoing policy debates about data center expansion and its local economic consequences.

The Lost Equilibrium: Geopolitical Turbulence and the Dynamics of Technological Distinctiveness

TIM
Si Zhang (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences); Shirong Gu (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences); Yimei Hu (Aalborg University)
Firms consistently face the challenge of navigating through conformity-differentiation paradox. This tension is particularly pronounced in an era marked by global geopolitical instability. In this bifurcated conditions, firms in decoupled economies need strategic determination of whether to conform with the mainstream technological trajectories or to pursue differentiated technological pathways. Drawing on the theory of optimal distinctiveness, this paper investigates how firms adjust their innovation strategies and the subsequent effects on their market performance amid a changing global landscape. By measuring the cross-market technology distinctiveness and adopting a panel dataset of integrated circuit firms from 2012 to 2022, we find an inverted U-shape pattern between the technological distinctiveness of Chinese firms relative to U.S. firms and firms’ market performance. However, this relationship converts to a U-shape after decoupling. Meanwhile, the domestic technological distinctiveness among Chinese firms consistently follows a U-shaped relationship. Our finding suggest that firms in decoupled countries require either greater technological conformity or differentiation to sustain their market performance. The varying patterns of the distinctiveness - performance relationship across markets arise from differing level of market technology and whether the benefits derived from institutional support outweigh the drawbacks posed by institutional pressures.
