The Embrace of Ambiguity: An Existentialist Reframing of Moral Expansion in Organizations

SIM
Lauri Pietinalho (NYU Stern School of Business); Matt Statler (New York University)
What happens when people in organizations experience moral ambiguity, where established norms, rules, or values no longer suffice, and come to experience their moral responsibilities more expansively? Existing organizational research has approached this phenomenon through concepts such as moral imagination, moral insight, and moral leadership. While this work highlights engagement with moral ambiguity, it has examined moral expansion primarily at a representational or attributional level, rather than as a lived experience. Drawing on the existentialist ethics of Søren Kierkegaard and Simone de Beauvoir, who placed the experience of embracing ambiguity at the center of moral life, this paper theorizes moral expansion as an experiential phenomenon structured through three intertwined relations: to the self, to the world, and to others. Building on their philosophy, we argue how meaningful moral expansion is predicated on the internal orientation with which individuals act under ambiguity, rather than on any specific external outcome of action. The paper develops this existentialist account in detail, illustrates it through a narrative example, and discusses how it reframes moral expansion in organizational ethics, with implications for future research and practice.

Exploring the Emotional-Spiritual Pathway of Responsible Leadership: A Case Study

SIM
Xueting Jiang (New York Institute of Technology); Bogdan Prokopovych (University of Massachusetts Amherst)
Organizations increasingly confront complex ecological and societal challenges that demand collaboration across sectors. Yet, research remains limited to leadership practices that effectively engage diverse stakeholders in advancing social and environmental responsibility, particularly to meta-organizations that operate cultivate stakeholder engagement and responsibility in the absence of formal authority. In this study, we employ an extended case study methodology to examine the Planetary Health Alliance (PHA), a meta-organization leading the planetary health movement. Notably, PHA’s leadership team draws on emotional and spiritual foundations to cultivate a broader community of care in response to the global environmental crisis. Based on our data analysis, we propose an emotional and spiritual pathway through which leaders can meaningfully engage diverse stakeholder groups. This research contributes to responsible leadership literature by highlighting the value of integrating emotional and spiritual components into leadership practices.

To Be Good, Feel Good, or Look Good? A Theoretical Model of Moral Advice Seeking and Taking

SIM
Joseph P. Gaspar (Quinnipiac University); Redona Methasani (The University of Connecticut)
We develop a theoretical model to understand why individuals seek and take moral advice and the consequences of these decisions. Whereas prior research has assumed that individuals seek and take moral advice to inform their moral judgments and make more ethical decisions (authentic moral advice seeking and taking), we argue that, in practice, people often seek and take moral advice to manage their moral impressions, their relationships, and their moral self-concept (strategic moral advice seeking and taking). We further propose that moral advice seeking and taking influence moral perceptions (e.g., perceptions of moral character), interpersonal relationships (e.g., interpersonal trust), and ethical decision-making (including ethical blind spots, moral hypocrisy, and unethical behavior). Our theory advances a new, generative framework to understand moral advice and ethical decision making in organizations.

The Right Thing to Do: Understanding the Influence of Multiple Identities in Ethical Decision-Making

SIM
Anasha Kannan Poyil (Case Western Reserve University)
The research examines how multiple identities shape ethical decisions at workplace and how employees navigate tensions between personal values and organizational demands in the context of ethical decisions. Employing a qualitative, interpretive approach, the study uses semi-structured interviews with experienced managers and leaders across industries to explore lived experiences of managing identity-based ethical tensions. The study employed grounded theory analysis to identify themes from participant stories. The research demonstrates that multiple identities interact and act as a lens in the ethical decision-making process. People deal with identity conflicts employing different strategies like compartmentalization or disengagement. Findings also indicate that organizational culture and leadership practices either create barriers or promote ethical integrity within business organizations. These findings are relevant in the identity and ethics literature as well as for organizations to create identity-informed ethics training systems.

Leaders’ Sensemaking of Adoption of Sufficiency Ethic in Business. A Qualitative Investigation

SIM
Shivinder Kaur; Amandeep Dhir; Anushree Luukela-Tandon
In the global economy today, a ‘growth ethic’ dominates such that humanity is living beyond the current matter-energetic limits, This outrightly questions the conventional business growth-driven models and market-driven solutions, calling for sufficiency in business production. In this paper, we seek to explore leaders’ conceptualisation sufficiency in production, the ethical dilemmas that they associate with it and their engagement in ethical sensemaking of these ethical tensions. We adopt a multistage qualitative approach to answer these questions and engage in emergent and inductive coding. We find that they associate ethical tensions arising from personal constraints, situational constraints, environmental constraints and temporality of benefits. We also discover that they engage in prospective sensemaking triggered by expectations, commitment and rarely based in their identity. We also find that besides sensemaking, they engage in sense-specification and sense-breaking. We propose important theoretical and practical implications.
