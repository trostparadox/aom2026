At Face Value: A Relational Mechanism Behind the Beauty Premium at Work

OB
Alessandro Iorio (Bocconi University); Ekaterina Netchaeva (HEC Paris); Giuseppe Soda (Bocconi University)
Attractive employees consistently receive higher pay, more promotions, and better performance evaluations, yet the behavioral processes behind these advantages remain unclear. Because these outcomes often depend on how employees engage with and are engaged by colleagues, we investigate whether an overlooked interpersonal mechanism – individuals’ positions in workplace instrumental networks – helps explain the beauty premium. We argue that facial attractiveness creates a social-capital advantage: coworkers are more likely to seek out attractive colleagues for task-related advice, drawing them into more frequent and higher-quality exchanges that broaden access to work-relevant information. These interactions, in turn, increase instrumental network prominence and enhance performance. Using a multimethod design that pairs a preregistered online experiment with complete workplace network data from a multinational organization, we find consistent support for this relational pathway. In the experiment, facial attractiveness causally increases others’ willingness to select attractive targets as advice partners, thereby increasing their centrality, and to provide them with richer assistance. The field study shows that facially attractive employees are sought out more often for task advice and consequently occupy more prominent positions in instrumental networks, which are associated with better performance outcomes. Together, these findings identify a concrete interpersonal mechanism through which physical appearance generates social capital and shapes career outcomes.

Beauty at Work: A Two-Century Analysis of Attractiveness and Occupational Stereotypes

OB
Kelly Nault (IE University); Khwan Kim (UCL School of Management); Marko Pitesa (Singapore Management University); Stefan Thau (INSEAD)
We investigate how attractiveness stereotypes have evolved over 200 years of English-language texts using historical word embeddings from the Google Ngram corpus. Beautiful and handsome are consistently aligned with positive traits (e.g., generous, brilliant), while ugly is linked to more unstable and broadly negative associations. Over time, occupations become increasingly semantically aligned with attractiveness, particularly those associated with social capital. These results suggest that while the core stereotype of beauty is stable, its occupational relevance intensifies under service-driven economic conditions. Our findings reveal the enduring and adaptive nature of beauty stereotypes and highlight the value of historical NLP methods in social psychological research.

Activating Close Ties at Work: Bonding Social Capital, Support Mobilization, and Coping Orientation

OB
Tien Quyet Nguyen (FPT University); Thijs Alexander Velema (National Taipei University); Shin-I Shih (National Sun Yat-Sen University)
Bonding social capital has been widely assumed to benefit individuals by facilitating access to social resources, yet empirical evidence remains mixed. To reconcile these inconsistencies, this research proposes a conditional process model in which bonding social capital influences task performance through two forms of social support mobilization: support seeking and support provision. We argue that support seeking generally enhances performance by granting access to instrumental and emotional resources, whereas support provision can hinder performance by depleting personal resources. Furthermore, the extent to which bonding social capital translates into support mobilization is contingent on individual factors. Specifically, internal locus of coping is proposed to weaken the positive effects of bonding social capital on support seeking and support provision. To test this model, we employ an intensive longitudinal field study designed to capture enacted social support mobilization in employees’ everyday work analyzed with structural equation modeling. This research aims to advance theory by clarifying the mechanisms and boundary conditions under which bonding social capital helps—or hinders— individual performance in the workplace.

Signal or Noise? The Asymmetric Impact of Employee Cross-Level Ties on Direct Leader Ambidexterity

OB
Lufeng He (Southeast University); Hongjiang LV
Existing research on leader ambidexterity has largely overlooked the informal networks in which leaders are embedded, particularly the effects of cross-level relationships within teams. Drawing on relationship signaling theory, we propose a triadic model that examines how employees’ cross-level ties with indirect leaders shape direct leaders’ ambidexterity through leader-leader (L-L) ties, and how gender dissimilarity between leaders moderates this process. Using two experiments (N1A = 199; N1B = 260) and a multi-source field social network study of indirect leader - direct leader - employees (N = 106), we find that cross-level friendship ties facilitate direct leader ambidexterity by activating L-L friendship ties, whereas cross-level advice ties undermine direct leader ambidexterity by activating L-L advice ties. Moreover, gender-dissimilar leader dyads amplify the positive effect of the friendship pathway while attenuating the negative effect of the advice pathway. This research reframes leader ambidexterity as relationally embedded and demonstrates how cross-level informal networks shape leader ambidexterity through the filtering of relational signals, offering novel theoretical insights and practical implications for social capital allocation and leadership development in complex organizations.

Echoes of Exit: Social Ties to Former Coworkers and Turnover

OB
Chunyan Peng (University of Missouri-Columbia); John Schaubroeck (University of Missouri-Columbia); Dongchul Kim; Wei Zeng (Hunan University)
Scholars have argued that turnover can spread within the organization due to employee observational learning. Our work extends this view by developing a theory identifying employees who are more vulnerable to turnover contagion and the timing of their departure decision. Drawing from social capital theory and turnover events theory, we argue that individuals having expressive ties to departed coworkers tend to leave very near to the parting time of their coworkers (i.e., ‘immediate turnover’), whereas those with instrumental ties to departed coworkers experience a decline in job performance, resulting in their leaving in a later period (i.e., subsequent turnover). We tested our hypotheses in two field studies. Study 1 surveyed a sample of insurance salespersons and measured performance using performance-based pay data. Study 2 recruited employees working in the headquarters of a large airline. The results largely supported the postulated differential effects of expressive and instrumental ties to departed coworkers on when and why employees leave their organization. Study 2 also showed that a higher quality relationship with the leader buffered the effect of losing instrumental ties on performance decline and indirectly reduced subsequent turnover. Our research identifies employees who are more vulnerable to social capital losses triggered by coworker departure and offers insights into theory and practices concerning the consequences of employee turnover.
