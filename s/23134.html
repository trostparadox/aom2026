The Illusion of Family Motivation: Why the Drive to Work Hard for Your Family Can Hurt Them

MOC
Hsuan-Che (Brad) Huang (The University of British Columbia); Lieke L. Ten Brummelhuis (Simon Fraser University, Beedie School of Business)
Family motivation is important in both organizational and family life, as it drives employees to work hard to support their family. A common assumption is that family motivation inherently benefits the family. Our research challenges this assumption by providing an alternative perspective on how family motivation can also backfire. Drawing on identity theory and insights from Rokeach’s value system (terminal vs. instrumental values), we develop a dual-pathway model in which family motivation acts as a double-edged sword impacting family satisfaction through countervailing mechanisms. Specifically, we propose the terminal pathway highlights how employees’ family motivation heightens family role salience (the end goal of family motivation), reduces workaholism, and ultimately promotes their spouse’s family satisfaction. On the other hand, the instrumental pathway predicts how family motivation simultaneously heightens work role salience (the means to achieve the end goal) and increases workaholism, thereby undermining family satisfaction. Findings from two pre-registered experiments and a field survey using dyadic (employee–spouse pairs) and time-lagged designs reveal that family motivation enhances the employee’s family and work role salience. However, we find consistent support for the instrumental pathway, whereby family motivation impairs family satisfaction via enhanced work role salience and workaholism, but not for the terminal pathway. In the field study, we further extend our model by showing that the positive effect of family motivation on family role salience is stronger when intrinsic work motivation is lower rather than higher. This research underscores the motivation to work hard for one’s family can in fact undermine family life.

The Road to Independence: A Role Transition System to Combat Ableism Toward Neurodiverse Workers

MOC
Christine Anna Mihelcic (University of Richmond); Dane Cannon (The University of Utah); Devin Rapp (San Diego State University); Glen E. Kreiner (The University of Utah)
Among the numerous challenges facing neurodiverse workers, their macro role transition from school to the workplace remains woefully understudied in the management literature. Drawing upon interviews, field observations, and secondary data from a postsecondary transition program for students with intellectual and developmental disabilities, we examine how such programs can reconfigure the path from school (high dependence) to work (greater independence). We find that these programs operate as a structured liminal system, designing the “in-between” space between school and work. Specifically, this system supports the transition from student to worker by addressing the image- and identity-related barriers associated with being a non-prototypical role incumbent. The system addresses these barriers via four strategies: (1) building belonging through space and lateral mentoring; (2) raising expectations and reframing others’ roles; (3) balancing empowerment and interdependence; and (4) emphasizing economic value. Our findings reveal how systems can address ableism by redesigning elements of transition to change both the focal neurodistinct student as well as other stakeholders within the system (e.g., parents, mentors, employers). We discuss implications of our work for theorizing on role transitions and ableism within organizations.

How and When Frontline Employees Experiencing Social Undermining Displace Aggression to Customers

MOC
Rongsong Wang (East China Normal University); Zhenyuan Wang (East China Normal University); Duy An Nguyen
Service sabotage is a widespread phenomenon that causes substantial financial and reputational losses in the service industry. While research has begun to examine the role of organizational factors in fostering service sabotage, limited attention has been devoted to understanding how and when social undermining drives service sabotage as a form of displaced aggression. To address this gap, we integrate social exchange theory (SET) and social cognitive theory (SCT) to develop a moderated chain-mediation model. Analyzing three-wave survey data collected from 298 frontline employees in service companies using SPSS and Mplus, we demonstrate that social undermining significantly increases employees’ psychological contract breach. This breach, in turn, fosters moral disengagement, which ultimately leads to service sabotage. Additionally, traditionality weakens both the positive effect of social undermining on psychological contract breach and the chain-mediating role of psychological contract breach and moral disengagement in linking social undermining to service sabotage. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms and boundary conditions underlying service sabotage, offering practical insights for reducing unethical practices in service organizations.

Employee Liberalism and Issue Support Behavior in a Hostile Political Environment

MOC
Carolyn Thi Dang (The Pennsylvania State University); Steven Kardel (The Pennsylvania State University); Gabriela Rivera (The Pennsylvania State University)
We engage in full-cycle research to examine how employees in a hostile political environment can nonetheless become motivated to engage in issue support behavior on behalf of their political ideology. In Study 1, we first observe whether liberal (vs. conservative) employees engage in less issue support behavior following a U.S. Supreme Court ruling that overturned Roe v. Wade (Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization in June 2022). After establishing that the workplace post-Dobbs is hostile (as exemplified by liberals vs. conservatives engaging in less issue support behavior), we draw from motivated cognition to explore why and under what conditions liberals can re-engage in issue support behavior after the Dobbs ruling. We theorize that liberals have a heightened desire for their organization to take stands on political issues, and that this is a key mechanism that can explain how liberals, in a hostile political environment, may still be motivated to engage in issue support behavior. We suggest contingencies that matter as well, including the perceived liberalism of the organization and satisfaction with organizations’ stands on political issues. Following the full-cycle approach, Studies 2 through 4 test our proposed mechanism and moderators, and we return to Study 1’s field study to test our proposed model in full.

Family-Support Differentiation and Relative Deprivation: The Role of Workplace Competition

MOC
Peiran Wen (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Peilin Zuo (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
As the number of organizations that employ algorithmic methods increases, employees are increasingly reliant on these methods for assignments and performance evaluations. However, the long-term consequences of algorithmic transparency are still unknown. Based on Self-Expectation Theory and Future Work Self, this research develops a dual-path framework to investigate how algorithmic transparency affects employee career success through underdogs and favored expectations. Through a study of both waves and an experiment, the results demonstrate a consistent favor of this framework. Algorithmic transparency is linked to disparate career paths via different expectations of success. Additionally, future work that is self-paced has a asymmetric role of moderateing, it increases the path associated with negative expectations of future earnings, but not the path associated with positive expectations. These findings demonstrate the dual nature of algorithmic transparency and the increased understanding of how algorithmic management affects employee careers.
