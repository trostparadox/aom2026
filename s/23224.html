Control Dilemma and Resource Dependence: The U-Shaped Logic of Founder Defense

ENT
Yi-Chi Hsiao (National Central University); Chun-Ping Yeh (National Taiwan University of Science and Technology)
This study investigates the dynamic behavioral responses of startup founders to board power imbalances, addressing a critical gap regarding post-investment control strategies. Integrating Resource Dependence Theory and the Control Dilemma framework, we hypothesize a non-linear, U-shaped relationship where founders initially acquiesce to dilution for resource acquisition but trigger defensive equity reconcentration once a power imbalance threshold is breached. Using a longitudinal dataset of 192 Taiwanese startups (2017–2023), fixed-effects regression analysis confirms this U-shaped trajectory. Furthermore, results indicate that Specific Human Capital (SHC) significantly moderates this phenomenon; founders possessing high technical expertise and social status exhibit a steeper defensive response, reacting more aggressively to regain sovereignty than their lower-SHC counterparts. These findings challenge static governance models, highlighting the strategic elasticity of founder power and offering implications for designing sustainable founder-investor partnerships.

Engineering Business Model Scalability: A Process Model

ENT
Erkko Autio (Imperial College London); Llewellyn D W Thomas (University of Sydney Business School)
Despite numerous recent treatments, the definitions of scaling remain outcome-oriented and lack tangibility to inform managerial practice. Several influential definitions define scaling as an outcome, e.g., ‘exponential growth’, but do not elaborate how this outcome can be achieved. We develop a process model that opens the black box of scaling—scalability and its determinants. Our process model defines scaling as a demand-driven process of rapid expansion of the firm’s outputs that drives increasing returns to scale. This process drives the need for adjustments in the firm’s operational and technological architectures. Our model also connects scaling with validation-focused entrepreneurship heuristics by highlighting product-market fit as the trigger of scaling. We illustrate our process model with examples.

Navigating Algorithmic Routine for Scalability: A Case Study on KuteSmart’s Mass Customization

ENT
Zhikai Wang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Mingchao Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
Our study focuses on a particular aspect of digital scaling firms’ internal organizing, namely, the role of algorithmic routines. By means of an in-depth case study on KuteSmart’s mass customization, we develop a model of dynamic algorithmic routines to understand how digital firms foster scalability. In the context of efficiently replicating customized businesses cross regions, we find that a scalable automation is composed with three types algorithmic routines: algorithmic platformism, generativity, and management. Based on the idea that the core tension of designing a scalable automation is the well-balance of routine stability and changeability, we identify platform managers’ reflexivity on routines as a conflict between replicability and entrepreneurship, and internal workers’ reflexivity on routines as a conflict between organization- and market-orientated responses. The study provides an analysis of socio-material enactment on scalability and contributes to the emerging literature on scaling.

Towards an Error-Driven Theory of Ownership Competences: Evidence from First-Time Founders

ENT
Domenico Appio (Politecnico di Milano); Francesco Paolo Appio (Paris School of Business); Josip Kotlar (Politecnico di Milano)
When navigating the early-stages of startup creation, first-time founders are confronted with extreme uncertainty, scarce resources, and time pressure; in this context, errors are unavoidable yet potentially generative. We study how they interpret and learn from errors and how these processes build ownership competences (OCs). Using a grounded-theory approach, we investigate 72 first-time founders who made the decision to incorporate their startup. Our study enriches the literature on entrepreneurial errors by developing a process model that treats errors as generative antecedents for OCs. We show that cognitive approaches—shaped by psychological distance—govern the depth and trajectory of learning. We extend competence-based theory by demonstrating how OCs emerge through error-driven interdependencies and synergies, outlining the contours of an error-driven theory of OCs.

When the Apple Falls Far: A Process Model of Founders’ Post-Exit Routines in Early Venture

ENT
Yolanda Christophe (University of Notre Dame)
Most entrepreneurs enter entrepreneurship after leaving established organizations, carrying stocks and imprints from prior employment. Although these imprints shape early venture behavior, we know little about how founders manage inherited routines after exit. Drawing on organizational learning and routine dynamics, we develop a process model of how founders enact, intensify, and revise operational routines after leaving employment. Using longitudinal field data from 4,739 founders documenting their transition from employment to entrepreneurship, complemented by 40 in-depth interviews, we trace how inherited operational routines unfold over time. We find that 1) founders initially activate and intensify enactment routines, 2) experimentation emerges only after breakdown, and 3) routine stabilization occurs only when signals emerge. Building on these findings, we challenge the stickiness of imprints (routines) and suggest that they evolve during early venture development.
