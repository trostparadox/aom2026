Relational Crescendo: Towards Understanding Leader Development Through Relational Co-Presence

Author: Ellina Watanabe ESADE Business School - Ramon Llull University

Session overview

Leadership development is often understood as “the process of growth individuals go through to become more effective in leadership roles” (Birkinshaw & Gudka, 2022, p.71, emphasis added). The process traditionally focuses on leaders in formal roles wishing to motivate, inspire, engage and “generally get more out of their ‘followers’” (Reitz, 2017). In the recent years, however, leadership development literature experienced a growing delineation between leader and leadership development (Carroll & Simpson, 2012; Day, 2001; McCauley & Palus, 2020; Petriglieri & Petriglieri, 2015), with former traditionally focusing on development of individual skills and capacities, and later – on social capital, relational practices and “the development of the collective as a whole” (McCauley & Palus, 2020, p. 3). Furthermore, a relational turn in the last two decades cast an increased attention towards collective meaning-making (going beyond leaders in formal roles), relational capacities and collaboration as scholars explore fresh perspectives to teach and develop relational leadership. Carroll and Simpson (2012) concur that intentionally different strategies are needed to facilitate process and relational orientation in leadership. Most notably, relational work and strengthening sociality (Carroll & Simpson, 2012) become main focus for leadership development to happen differently. While importance of personalization of management learning through personal exploration of life trajectories and their effect on students’ world of work has been previously discussed (Kets de Vries & Korotov, 2007; Petriglieri et al., 2011; Petriglieri & Petriglieri, 2015), the focus remains largely on the individual and their relation to difficult experiences, interactions, peers, and situations. This paper, in turn, starts with the relational work and co-presence where leadership roles are suspended and new ways of being human within leadership are explored. Thus, unlike traditional leadership development logic directed at getting better at one’s role as a leader, quite the opposite, individuals drop their leadership profiles and lean into human connection that, in turn, transforms their leadership trajectories. The question then becomes not how to enable leader development but how can being more relationally human better support leader growth and transformation?
