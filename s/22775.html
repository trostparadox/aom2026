Authentic Believers Instead of Emotional Masqueraders: Examining the Dual Effects of SMX

OB
Yanjing Li (University of Chicago); ZHONGXU LIU (Fudan University); Ruizhe Hou (University of Amsterdam)
Previous research has predominantly regarded high-level leadership as a distal strategic force with uniform positive impacts. We challenge this simplified view and demonstrate the dual effects of skip-level leader-member exchange (SMX) on employee emotional labor strategies. Drawing upon conservation of resources theory, we develop a nuanced model that simultaneously examines SMX’s beneficial effects on deep acting and its inhibitory effects on surface acting via unique psychological mechanisms. Our analysis of three-wave, multisource data reveals that SMX has an indirect positive effect on deep acting via increased role efficacy and an indirect negative effect on surface acting via enhanced perceptions of interpersonal justice. Furthermore, leader–leader exchange (LLX) moderates these indirect effects, highlighting a complex interplay where distal support acts as both a synergistic resource for professional validation and a compensatory resource for relational justice. Taken together, our research challenges the assumption that immediate supervision is the sole driver of employee emotional regulation and calls for further attention to be paid to the integrated impact of vertical leadership relationships in organizations.

Unpacking the Effects of Supervisor Unfounded Confidence via Supervisor Meddling and Emotional Labor

OB
Joohyung (Jenny) Kim (City University of Hong Kong); Soohyun Yoon (Hong Kong Baptist University); Jeffery LePine (Arizona State University); Wu Wei (Wuhan University); Danni Wang (Rutgers University); Haeyoung Koo (City University of Hong Kong); David A. Waldman (Arizona State University)
Supervisors benefit from the social advantages of confidence, even when that confidence is unjustified. However, there are reasons to believe that supervisors’ unfounded confidence—or supervisors’ beliefs in their abilities or knowledge despite lacking corresponding evidence—can have complex implications for employees within supervisor–employee relationships. Drawing on relational models theory and emotional labor research, we argue that supervisors’ unfounded confidence engenders employees’ emotional labor and downstream consequences because it manifests in misguided actions. Specifically, we propose supervisor unfounded confidence may result in supervisor meddling, whereby supervisors interpose obtrusively into their employees’ work-related affairs. We further propose that supervisor meddling may, in turn, elicit employees’ surface and deep acting of positive affect toward the supervisor, which exert opposing influences on employee task performance, supervisor-directed citizenship and counterproductive behaviors, and voice. Finally, we theorize that the implications of supervisor meddling to surface and deep acting, and in turn, the mixed downstream consequences, may be more pronounced when there are heightened expectations, or emotional display rules, to show positive emotions towards the supervisor. Results largely support our hypotheses, albeit the moderating effects of positive affect display rule functioned differently than we predicted. Implications to theory and practice are discussed.

A Natural Quasi-Experiment Examining the Affective Consequences of the Joining of Layoff Survivors

OB
Olivia Zhishuang Guan (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Ming Fang (Jinan University); Hua Liu (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Mass layoffs have become an increasingly common organizational response to economic turbulence and rapid technological transformation. Yet nowadays, organizations do not simply reduce headcount; they selectively retain employees deemed high-value human capital and redeploy them into existing teams. Although prior research has devoted substantial attention to layoff victims and survivors, little is known about how these practices affect a greatly impacted yet understudied group: incumbent employees in teams that receive redeployed layoff survivors. Integrating affective events theory with uncertainty-identity theory and the newcomer socialization literature, we develop competing hypotheses regarding whether the joining of layoff survivors increases or reduces incumbents’ fear. We further theorize that incumbents’ within-team status moderates these affective responses. We conducted a natural quasi-experiment field study based on an exceptionally rich panel dataset. Leveraging a facial-recognition access-control system, we analyzed 76,617 daily photo records of 2,728 incumbent team members pre- and post-a mass layoff, and matched these data to archival performance records. Difference-in-differences analyses reveal that the joining of layoff survivors significantly reduces incumbents’ fear, particularly among high-status team members, with the effect persisting for up to three months. Mediation analyses further reveal that fear serves as the key mechanism between the joining of layoff survivors and incumbents’ performance. Together, these findings extend research on layoffs by illuminating how layoff-related practices generate ripple effects that shape the affective experiences of employees beyond those directly involved. Methodologically, this study demonstrates a scalable and non-intrusive approach to examining how employee emotions unfold over time in natural organizational settings.

How Workplace Anxiety Reshapes State Goal Orientations to Influence Proactivity and Proficiency

OB
Yahua Cai; Zhihang Li (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Weili Zheng; Kan Ouyang (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Sun Xiuli (Capital University of Economics and Business); Qingyun Luo (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics)
Drawing on self-regulation and goal orientation theories, this study proposes that anxiety redirects motivational efforts, shifting employees from a focus on learning to a preoccupation with managing external perceptions. Using a daily diary study (Experience Sampling Method, ESM) of 101 employees in an administrative department in mainland China over seven workdays, we revealed that workplace anxiety is positively related to state proving goal orientation (PGO) and avoiding goal orientation (AGO), but is negatively related to state learning goal orientation (LGO). The results suggest a nuanced trade-off in performance: PGO positively relates to task proactivity while negatively relating to task proficiency; conversely, AGO positively relates to task proficiency but negatively to task proactivity; while LGO, despite its positive link to task proactivity, shows no significant relationship with task proficiency. Additionally, our findings reveal that competitive climate, in its role as a key contextual factor shaping the standards for self-regulation, strengthens the positive relationship between workplace anxiety and both PGO and AGO. Theoretical and practical implications were also discussed.

Biohacking Anxiety? An Experimental ESM Study of Non-Invasive tVNS and Anxiety-Mediated Outcomes

OB
Keith Leavitt (Oregon State University); Luke Zhu (York University); Trevor Foulk (University of Florida); Dustin L. Hegland (University of Florida); Christopher Barnes (University of Washington)
Anxiety represents a pervasive mental health concern within the United States, and negatively impacts myriad workplace outcomes. But curiously, while significant work has investigated sources of workplace stress that may result in anxiety, little extant work has tested interventions aimed at attenuating workplace anxiety in-situ. Drawing from the neurovisceral integration model (Thayer & Lane, 2009) and the transactional model of stress (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), we deployed a four-week experimental experience sampling (ESM) study to test the impact of non-invasive transcutaneous vagal nerve stimulation (tVNS) on daily workplace anxiety and subsequent daily workplace outcomes. On days when the device was used in active (versus placebic) mode, midday workplace anxiety was reduced for individuals high in dispositional stress reactivity. This in turn mediated reductions in end-of-workday anxiety and physical symptoms, and increased job engagement and job performance. However, contrary to expectations, we find that using the device increased daily workplace anxiety for those low in stress reactivity (likely as a function of irritation for individuals with healthy vagal tone). Taken together, we find evidence that the relatively low-cost, non-disruptive use of tVNS can meaningfully improve workplace outcomes by reducing anxiety for those high in dispositional stress reactivity.
