Guidelines for Vigilantes:The Influence of Punishment Altruism and Gender on Bystander Behavior

OB
Yilin Wang (Nankai University); ?? ? (Lanzhou University)
The concept of workplace vigilante originates from individual’s perception of threats to the normative order, referring to those who monitor and punish deviants without formal authority. Although recent descriptive research has systematically established the formation of the role of workplace vigilante, the outcomes of their behavior remain unclear. Based on the cognitive appraisal theory of emotion and gender role theory, we conducted three experiments to investigate how the punishment altruism and gender of workplace vigilante influence bystander behavior. The results indicate that (1) The punishment altruism of workplace vigilante significantly affects bystander behavior; (2) Moral appraisal and moral emotion mediate the relationship between the punishment altruism and bystander behavior; (3) The gender of workplace vigilante moderates the chain-mediating effect of moral appraisal and moral emotion. These findings enrich research on the outcomes of workplace vigilante in organizations and provide practical implications for female workplace vigilante on how to act to gain support.

Dark Knights and Shadow Governance: How Employee Vigilantism Shapes Leader Empowerment

OB
Yahong Xie (Wuhan University of Technology); Mingze Li (Wuhan University of Technology)
Workplace vigilantism not only has important consequences for vigilantes and their targets, but also shapes third parties’ subsequent reactions. Prior research has paid limited attention to the potential effects of employee vigilantism on leaders as critical third parties. Drawing on trust theory and adopting a third-party perspective, we propose that leaders develop higher levels of competence-based trust in vigilante employees, which in turn leads leaders to grant vigilante employees a higher level of empowerment. In addition, we examine the critical boundary role of leaders’ power distance orientation in this process. Using data from a scenario-based experiment (Study 1, N = 94) and a three-wave survey (Study 2, N = 249), the results reveal that employee vigilantism promotes leader empowerment by fostering leaders’ competence-based trust. Moreover, when leaders’ power distance orientation is lower, leaders develop higher levels of competence-based trust in vigilante employees, which further enhances leader empowerment. These findings offer informative theoretical, empirical, and practical implications for the scientific governance of employee vigilantism in organizations.

From Vigilante Fire to Vitality: Social and Work Consequences of Employee Workplace Vigilantism

OB
Mingze Li (Wuhan University of Technology); Huaying Wang
Studies of workplace vigilantism has been primarily focused on investigating its antecedents, while overlooking its consequences for the vigilante employees’ work-related outcomes. Based on conservation of resources theory, we argue that workplace vigilantism can serve as a resource-generating act, motivating employee key work-related behaviors (performance, creativity, proactive behaviors) through a heightened experience of relational energy. We also propose that perceived organizational support serves as a critical individual difference in organizational context perception. A multi-wave and multi-source field survey comprising of 338 employees and 80 matched supervisors shows that workplace vigilantism has positive relationship with relational energy, and it affects employee work outcomes of job performance, creativity, and proactive behavior through the mediating effect of relational energy. Our empirical findings also support the moderating effects of perceived organizational support, such that it moderates the relationship between employee workplace vigilantism and relational energy, with high levels of perceived organizational support magnifies the energy-generating process. Moreover, perceived organizational support also moderates the indirect relationship between workplace vigilantism and work outcomes via relational energy, such that the indirect relationship is enhanced via heightened levels of relational energy. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

The Implications of Formal and Informal Power for Apology Avoidance After a Transgression

OB
Elena Hayoung Lee (USC Marshall School of Business, University Of Southern California); Peter H. Kim (University of Southern California)
Despite the potential benefits of apologies for the repair of trust, relationship maintenance, and conflict resolution, past research suggests that powerholders avoid apologizing. Yet few studies have directly tested this assumption, let alone considered whether that relationship depends on the type of power one might hold. Our research addresses this limitation by investigating the implications of formal and informal power for apology avoidance in organizational contexts where each type of power can be found. Across a multi-method investigation combining field and experimental studies, results reveal that power does not uniformly inhibit apologies. Instead, informal power heightens apology avoidance, whereas formal power tends to reduce it, suggesting that structural authority may often enable rather than impede reparative efforts. We also identify a previously overlooked mechanism, dominance concern, as the central mediator of this relationship and find that this mechanism drives apology avoidance more strongly and more consistently than any of the alternative explanations that have been identified by the literature. These findings challenge the prevailing assumption that power would simply increase apology avoidance, provide deeper theoretical insight into this relationship, and offer practical implications for both organizational design and leadership development.

Ethical Perspectives on the Impact of Vicarious Exploitation on Employees’ Knowledge Hiding

OB
Jinyu Liu (Southwest Jiaotong University); Chunyong Tang (Southwest Jiaotong University)
Exploitative leadership is a negative leadership style that directly harms victims and indirectly impacts bystanders. Drawing on SCT, this study examines how exploitative leadership influences bystanders’ knowledge hiding behavior and introduces the concept of vicarious exploitation. The research comprises two empirical studies: a scenario-based online experiment and a three-phase survey involving 230 employees in China. The findings indicate that vicarious exploitation significantly increases knowledge hiding behavior, with moral disengagement acting as a mediator. Additionally, power distance moderates the relationship, with individuals perceiving higher power distance experiencing stronger effects. This study contributes to exploitative leadership research by adopting a bystander perspective and offering a theoretical framework to explain how and when vicarious exploitation triggers knowledge hiding behaviors among employees.
