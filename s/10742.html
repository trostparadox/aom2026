Digital Respite: A Study on Cyberloafing, Recovery, Job Performance and End-of-Work Fatigue

Friday, Jul 31 2026 · 4:00PM–5:00PM
Pennsylvania Convention Center — Poster Wall 2
HR

People

Authors: Shu Zhang Hangzhou Normal University; Vivien Lim National University of Singapore; Shidie Cao East China Normal University; Thompson Teo National University of Singapore; Hui Meng East China Normal University

Session overview

Cyberloafing is typically viewed as counterproductive behavior, but emerging research suggests that it may also serve recovery-related functions. Drawing on the effort–recovery model, we conceptualize cyberloafing as a form of micro-breaks and examines its daily relationships with job performance and end-of-work fatigue via recovery experiences (psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, and control). Using a ten-workday experience sampling design with 125 full-time employees (1,086 day-level observations), we tested a multilevel mediation model. Results showed that morning cyberloafing was positively related with relaxation, mastery, and control experiences during work hours, but not significantly related with psychological detachment experiences. Moreover, cyberloafing had positive indirect effects on job performance through mastery and control experiences, and a negative indirect effect on end-of-work fatigue through relaxation experiences. Notably, the within-person correlation between cyberloafing and job performance was nonsignificant; however, after accounting for recovery-related pathways, cyberloafing was marginally and negatively related with job performance, suggesting a double-edged sword effect. This study advances cyberloafing research by adopting a within-person approach to highlight that cyberloafing impacts daily functioning through distinct recovery experiences. Our findings also provide practical insights for managing cyberloafing at work by highlighting the importance of distinguishing recovery-oriented cyberloafing from counterproductive cyberloafing.
