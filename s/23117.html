Behind the Smile: How Emotional Labor and Job Fit Shape Satisfaction and Turnover

HR
Abdollah Zeraatpisheh
Purpose Emotional labor is fundamental to public service work, yet its implications for employee well-being and retention remain contested. While surface acting is widely assumed to produce strain, deep acting is often positioned as a more adaptive regulatory strategy. Less understood is whether personality– job fit moderates these effects. This study investigates how emotional labor strategies influence job satisfaction and turnover intention among U.S. federal employees and whether job fit alters these relationships. Theoretical Background Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, emotional labor is conceptualized as a resource-intensive process that can deplete psychological reserves. Surface acting requires suppressing or faking emotions, producing emotional dissonance, and resource loss. Deep acting involves modifying internal feelings to match display rules, but this process may also be demanding in emotionally complex public sector environments. Person–job fit theory suggests that alignment between individual traits and job demands enhances satisfaction and may buffer strain. However, emerging research indicates that emotional display rules may be strong enough to override individual dispositions, leaving open the question of whether fit meaningfully alters emotional labor outcomes in federal work. Research Questions How do surface acting, deep acting, and personality–job fit predict job satisfaction and turnover intention? Does job satisfaction mediate the relationship between surface acting and turnover intention? Does personality–job fit moderate the relationship between emotional labor and satisfaction, and its indirect effect on turnover? Design/Methodology This study analyzes data from the 2016 Merit Principles Survey (N = 13,716), one of the largest federal workforce datasets that includes measures of emotional labor. A structural equation model is estimated using maximum likelihood with missing values. Surface acting, deep acting, personality–job fit, job satisfaction, and turnover intention are included as focal variables. Emotional labor is assessed with validated multi-item scales, while satisfaction and turnover intention are measured with established global indicators commonly used in turnover research. Moderation and mediation are tested using centered variables and nonlinear combinations of parameters. Findings Several important findings emerge. Both surface acting and deep acting reduce job satisfaction and increase turnover intention. Deep acting, often assumed to be adaptive, is negatively associated with satisfaction in this federal context. The emotional intensity and bureaucratic constraints of public service roles may make deep acting particularly taxing, similar to surface acting. Job satisfaction mediates the relationship between surface acting and turnover intention. Employees who engage more in surface acting report lower satisfaction, which in turn predicts stronger turnover intention. This mediated pathway appears robust, demonstrating that emotional strain translates into withdrawal primarily through declines in job satisfaction. Personality–job fit strongly predicts satisfaction but does not buffer emotional labor’s negative consequences. Although employees who perceive strong job fit report higher overall satisfaction and lower turnover intention, the negative slope between surface acting and satisfaction remains unchanged across fit levels. High-fit employees generally experience higher satisfaction, but they are not protected from declines in satisfaction when emotional regulation demands intensify. Originality/Value This study advances emotional labor and public HRM scholarship in four ways: It provides one of the largest empirical tests of emotional labor processes within the U.S. federal workforce. It demonstrates that deep acting can be detrimental in emotionally demanding government contexts, challenging assumptions about its adaptiveness. It evaluates personality–job fit as a boundary condition and finds no buffering effect, suggesting that emotional labor demands may overwhelm the advantages of individual dispositions. It identifies job satisfaction as the central pathway linking emotional labor to withdrawal, reinforcing its importance in organizational retention strategies. Implications The findings suggest that emotional labor is not simply an individual-level coping resource but a structural job demand embedded in public service roles. Enhancing job fit can raise overall satisfaction, but does not shield employees from emotional strain. Agencies should therefore treat emotional labor as a formal HRM concern. Key implications include: Training supervisors to recognize emotional strain; Normalizing conversations about emotional demands; Providing recovery opportunities and psychological support; Distributing emotionally intense tasks more equitably; And redesigning roles to limit unnecessary requirements for emotional display. Conclusion Emotional labor, job fit, and job satisfaction jointly shape turnover intention in government. Even well-matched employees experience strain when emotional demands are chronic, underscoring the importance of directly addressing emotional labor through job design, supervisory practices, and organizational support.

Performing Platform Work Under Pressure: The Roles of Job Insecurity and Work–Life Balance

HR
Katarzyna Piwowar-Sulej (Wroclaw University of Economics and Business); Qaisar Iqbal (Al Yamamah University); Mariusz Soltysik
This study examines both the direct and indirect effects of job insecurity on job performance among platform workers, with work–life balance acting as a mediating mechanism. The analysis additionally explores whether proactive personality moderates these relationships. The research draws on time-lagged data obtained through computer-assisted telephone interviews with 280 on-demand platform workers engaged in physical service provision, such as delivery. The proposed framework was tested using PLS-SEM. The results demonstrate that job insecurity influences job performance directly, as well as indirectly via its impact on work–life balance. However, proactive personality does not play a significant moderating role. These findings highlight the need for platform organizations to reduce job insecurity and support employees’ work–life balance as strategic drivers of enhanced performance. Recommended actions include implementing minimum income protections, ensuring transparent deactivation processes, and providing psychological support.

Restoring Balance: The Effect of Work Hour Limits on Employee Health

HR
Kyeonggook Park (City University of Hong Kong); Junhyok Yim (City University of Hong Kong); You Jin (YJ) Kim (City University of Hong Kong); Tae-Youn Park (Sungkyunkwan University); Mijeong Kim (Pusan National University)
Overwork threatens employee health across industrialized economies, yet evidence on whether statutory work hour limits can mitigate this threat remains limited. By exploiting South Korea’s labor reform that reduced the maximum workweek from 68 to 52 hours, we analyze over 1.26 million employee-year observations to estimate the effect of reduced working hours on employee health using a difference-in-differences design. Results show that the 52-hour policy significantly improved employee health indicated by objective health markers such as BMI, blood glucose level, and liver function. These improvements operate partly through lifestyle changes, with increased exercise and reduced drinking among employees who have been influenced by the 52- hour policy. However, such health benefits are pronounced among higher-income employees, while lower-income employees experience minimal benefits or even adverse effects. Overall, we provide empirical evidence that work hour regulations improve employee health while highlighting critical equity concerns.

Decoding Remote Work Intensity-Intention: A Configurational Analysis of Conditions Shaping Intention

HR
Beatrice Schuldt (University of Münster)
As major corporations mandate a return to office, a critical tension has emerged between institutional directives and employee preferences. This paper investigates the underlying factors influencing employees’ preferences for remote work and their intended usage. Grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), we explore how various factors shape Remote Work Intensi-ty-Intention (RWI-I). Using a two-step methodological approach, we first conducted 35 semi-structured interviews to identify key drivers for RWI-I, followed by a fuzzy-set Qualitative Com-parative Analysis (fsQCA) based on 167 employee data to determine how these factors collective-ly influence high RWI-I. Our findings reveal eight pivotal conditions, including interpersonal constraints, job context constraints, family life, care work contributions, leadership attitude, workplace availability, distance, and digitization. Moreover, we reveal three distinct configura-tions shaping RWI-I: Prepared Remote Parents, Efficiency-driven Commuters, and Care-bound Balancers. By demonstrating that multiple pathways lead to RWI-I, this study advances a config-urational understanding of TPB and offers nuanced insights into how individual attributes, sub-jective norms, and perceived behavioral control in modern work arrangements shape employees’ intentions for remote work.
