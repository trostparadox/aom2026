Session overview

What are firms, and why do they exist? This foundational question has generated rich theoretical perspectives from transaction cost economics to resource-based views, knowledge-based theories, and platform economics. What unites these diverse frameworks is a shared recognition that technological change persistently challenges our theories of the firm, creating new coordination problems that existing organizational forms cannot adequately manage. This panel symposium examines how the emergence of generative machines — AI systems that create meaningful outputs through probabilistic recombination in high-dimensional vector spaces — may pose fundamental challenges to existing theories of the firm. Unlike physical machines that transformed matter or smart machines that processed symbols, generative machines produce context-specific, semantically relevant outputs that cannot be anticipated at design time. When AI agents bring together distributed capabilities and resources from heterogeneous sources at runtime, they create systematic value spillovers that escape traditional coordination mechanisms. Neither hierarchies that assume value is determined at production time nor platforms that rely on architectural control fully address this phenomenon. The symposium brings together scholars who have shaped our understanding of firms and technology across multiple generations: David Teece (complementary assets and dynamic capabilities), Carliss Baldwin (modularity and design rules), Geoffrey Parker (platform ecosystem and network economy), Cristina Alaimo (data objects), and Youngjin Yoo (digital innovation and layered modular architecture). The panel will address two fundamental questions: What new forms of coordination failure does the emergence of generative machines create? How must our diverse theoretical frameworks evolve to address these coordination problems and capture the value that generative externalities generate? The symposium advances a research agenda that integrates insights across theoretical traditions while proposing temporal unbundling and generative externalities as new dimensions for understanding organizational design in the age of AI.
