Microfoundations of Design Practice: Six Identity-Based Orientations

Author: Michelle Pannone Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University

Session overview

Design thinking has become a widely adopted approach for innovation in organizations and entrepreneurship, yet research has primarily emphasized processes, tools, and macro-level outcomes while overlooking the individual-level mechanisms through which design practices are enacted. Consequently, we lack a clear theoretical explanation for why individuals engage differently with core design activities such as problem framing, collaboration, prototyping, and implementation, even when operating within similar processes, teams, and organizational contexts. This conceptual work introduces design identity as a microfoundational construct for understanding how individuals engage with design practice. Building on identity theory and microfoundations research, design identity is defined as an individual’s self-perception of who they are when designing, informed by the skills, attributes, and roles they associate with design work. Design identity functions as a constellation of self-meanings that orient individuals toward particular design behaviors, preferences, and contributions. This work theorizes six elements of design identity, Visionary, Connector, Design Process Manager, Design Leader, Implementor, and Technical Contributor, each representing a distinct way individuals enact design through framing possibilities, integrating stakeholders, orchestrating processes, aligning narratives, driving execution, and materializing ideas through making. By foregrounding identity, this framework shifts attention from design thinking as a universal process to design as a heterogeneous, lived practice shaped by individual self-concepts. Design identity is positioned as a microfoundational link between individual cognition and collective design outcomes, offering an explanation for variation in how design work unfolds within teams and organizations. Conceptually, this work extends identity theory into the design domain and offers a microfoundational lens for entrepreneurship research.
