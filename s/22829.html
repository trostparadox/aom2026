Propellant Organizing: A Process Model of Simultaneously Managing Grand-Challenges and Legitimacy

SIM
Anjan Ghosh (Nazarbayev University); Indranil Biswas (NEOMA Business School); Shahzad Ansari (University of Cambridge); Sougata Ray (Indian School of Business)
Grand challenges demand continuous experimentation and recombination, yet social development organizations often face restrictive institutional environments where visible deviation from institutional templates triggers scrutiny, stigmatization, and sanction. How, then, do such organizations keep reconfiguring their organizing to address evolving grand challenge complexity while maintaining legitimacy? Drawing on a 13-year longitudinal qualitative study of a leading Indian social development organization and its temporary spinoff, we develop an inductive process model of propellant organizing. We show how organizations break legitimacy vs mission deadlocks through four mechanisms: negotiated grafting, creative bonding (a temporal connect– disconnect choreography across parent and spinoff), legitimized heterogeneity for grand challenge mitigation, and exiting the spinoff as legitimacy scaling. Our model advances theory on grand challenge organizing by explaining legitimacy-gated reconfiguration, re-theorizes spinoffs as temporary propellants enabling interorganizational synchronization, and reveals how evolving meanings of “being social” sustain both identity coherence and external legitimacy.

Understanding the Microprocesses of Purpose Implementation: The Case of MiBanco

SIM
Alvaro Lleo (University of Navarra); Diego Arias Padilla (University of Detroit Mercy); María Paula Florez-Jimenez (Universidad de Navarra)
The increasing prominence of corporate purpose in management research highlights its potential to enhance performance, engagement, and resilience. Yet we know relatively little about how purpose is translated from an official declaration into a shared, lived reality within organizations. This study addresses that gap by examining the microprocesses of purpose implementation at MiBanco, a Peruvian microfinance institution whose stated purpose is to “transform lives” through financial inclusion. Taking as base the Purpose Strength Framework (PSF) we, combine a quantitative assessment of purpose implementation with in-depth qualitative interviews across hierarchical levels and functions. Our analysis identifies four microprocesses—cultural embedding, structural flattening, converging, and homogenizing—that activate the levers of strategy, leadership, systems, and communication in everyday practice. These microprocesses jointly generate two dimensions —purpose authenticity and purpose identification—which in turn enable three interrelated processes of implementation: purpose knowledge, internalization, and contribution. The study advances purpose implementation perspectives and reinforces the literature about purpose implementation by offering an empirically grounded, micro-level account of dimensions, and provides practical guidance for organizations seeking to durably enact and widely share corporate purpose.

There is More Than One Way to Persist: Organizational Identity as a Precursor to Social Impact Str

SIM
Habib Ashraful Islam (Wayne State University); Miranda J. Welbourne Eleazar (University of Iowa)
Firms increasingly adopt social impact strategies, yet many struggle to sustain these commitments over time. While prior research explains adoption and diffusion, not much is known about why some firms persist while others disengage. We examine persistence in social impact strategies among Certified B Corporations (B Corps), which must repeatedly reaffirm their commitments through recertification. Drawing on organizational identity and framing perspectives, we argue that persistence depends on how social impact activities are combined into internally coherent systems. Using fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) on longitudinal data from 777 B Corps across 14 countries, we identify multiple, equifinal configurations associated with persistence and non-persistence. Persistent configurations consistently feature worker-focused practices as a core element, while other domains vary. Non-persistent configurations emphasize externally oriented practices without comparable internal substitutes. Our findings shift focus from adoption to persistence, demonstrating the value of configurational approaches for explaining sustained social impact strategies.

Corporate Sustainability Mainstreaming: Toward a Dynamic Conceptualization

SIM
François Maon (IESEG School of Management); Susana Esper (IESEG School of Management); Valérie Swaen (Université catholique de Louvain); Adam Lindgreen (Copenhagen Business School)
This article introduces a composite framework of corporate sustainability (CS) mainstreaming at the organizational level that builds on a combination of ideal-type theories of change. The result is a quad-motor theory of CS mainstreaming in which life cycle, teleological, dialectical, and evolutionary motors of change operate interdependently, depending on the unit of change considered and the level of integration of CS principles into the company’s culture and strategy. This conceptual study supports more comprehensive accounts of the context-based processes of CS organizational mainstreaming; it also helps bridge the gap between research into what prompts corporate engagement in CS and the actual design and implementation of CS initiatives. Overall, the framework we propose provides a basis for moving stakeholder-based and other corporate sustainability development models beyond their relatively static or fixed formulations toward more dynamic, time-dependent, and contextually situated understandings of corporate sustainability mainstreaming.

Building a Values-Based, Purpose-Driven Ecosystem: The Case of “We Live Healthfully”

SIM
Merve Vuslat Aksu (Marmara University); Elif Hasret Kumcu (Aksaray University)
How do values-based, purpose-driven initiatives scale into complex ecosystems in the absence of formal authority, monetization, or hierarchical control? Drawing on an in-depth qualitative case study of Saglikli Yasiyoruz (We Live Healthfully - SY), a large-scale digital health ecosystem originating from the founders’ personal health transformation, this study examines how a values-based ecosystem emerges, evolves, and sustains coordination over time. Based on interviews with founders and key stakeholders, archival data, and longitudinal observations, we show that SY developed through three intertwined phases: informing, networking, and orchestrating, while pursuing societal impact at micro, meso, and macro levels. We theorize how purpose provides directional orientation, shared values enable relational alignment across heterogeneous actors, and trust operates as the central mechanism translating purpose-driven values into coordinated action and distributed governance. By conceptualizing trust as a generative mechanism rather than an outcome, this study contributes to research on values-based management, ecosystem organizing, and purpose-driven organizing by explaining how large-scale ecosystems can be built and governed through normative rather than contractual means.
