Boardrooms and Bias: Women’s Legitimacy in Corporate Governance

Session overview

These papers investigate women's access to influence and legitimacy in corporate governance. Examining board committees, director stigma, crisis appointments, and the consequences of women CEOs exits, the session shows that gains in representation do not necessarily translate into equal authority or taken-for-granted legitimacy. The session highlights the subtle and persistent ways gendered expectations shape governance outcomes in boardrooms around the world.

Papers (4)

From Token to Straw Woman? Women’s Inclusion in Major Board Committees After Gender Quotas

DEI
Daniela Cardenas Huerta (SKEMA Business School); Michel Ferrary
Gender quotas have increased the presence of women on boards, but their access to substantive power remains uncertain. This study examines horizontal and vertical segregation in French listed companies seven years after the Copé-Zimmermann law came into effect. Using an original dataset manually collected from 1,433 directors of SBF120 French companies, we analyse how gender, symbolic capital and organisational status influence appointments to the major board committees (MBCs) and chair positions. Our findings show a persistent exclusion of women from MBC positions in blue-chip firms, although this pattern is moderated by elite educational credentials. Therefore, symbolic capital functions as a conditional access mechanism in a company's environment orientated towards status and legitimacy. Conversely, vertical segregation remains pronounced in non-blue-chip firms, where women’s likelihood of obtaining chair roles remains significantly lower, and symbolic capital does not offset this disadvantage. These findings suggest that quotas have reshaped board composition but have not fully altered the symbolic and organisational processes structuring access for women to powerful instances: the MBCs.

Proven But Not Taken for Granted: Women Directors and the Limits of Legitimacy in Mexican Boardrooms

DEI
Mayra Ruiz-Castro (Queen Mary University of London); Martha Rivera-Pesquera (IPADE Business School); Renu Gupta (Heriot-Watt University)
Women’s underrepresentation on corporate boards has been extensively researched to understand both the structural barriers they face and strategies they adopt to navigate male-dominated boardrooms. However, there is little understanding about how women construct and maintain their legitimacy in these spaces. Adapting Suchman’s legitimacy framework to individual actors, we argue that unlike men, cognitive legitimacy- being taken-for-granted for board roles- is not automatically attributed to women by credentials or appointments. Drawing on interviews with 33 women serving on boards in Mexico, we expand Suchman’s framework by conceptualizing anticipatory legitimacy work and compensatory legitimacy work as additional labor that women undertake to maintain their place on the boards. Our findings suggest a multi-level operation of legitimacy whereby individual women may achieve success but the collective normalization of women’s presence and value in boardrooms remains unaccomplished.

Women Directors and the Stigma of Incompetence

DEI
Rita Goyal (Coventry University); Nada Kakabadse (University of Reading); Panagiotis Andrikopoulos (Coventry University); Pratishtha Nikam (King's College London)
Several policy efforts – mandated and voluntary - have increased women’s representation on corporate boards. However, diversity initiatives often have an unintended consequence. Women may face subtle and persistent stigma - an insinuation that they are less competent or appointed primarily to satisfy targets. Prior research reports women directors’ ambivalence toward board diversity initiatives; however, as women's representation on boards grows, their interpretations, negotiations, and responses to the stigma of incompetence require fresh theorization. Drawing on Social Cognition Theory and Stereotype Threat Theory, this study examines women directors’ temporal adjustments and relational responsibilities in the boardroom. Through 33 elite interviews with women directors and a reflexive thematic analysis, the findings show that stigma management varies along two dimensions. Women with collective leadership roles (e.g., Chair and CEO) tend to employ problem-focused strategies of empathetic reframing, which diffuse stigma while reasserting competence. Conversely, directors with shorter adjustment periods and roles with limited collective responsibility often rely on emotion-focused response of challenge and rationalization to protect their professional legitimacy. Additionally, in boardrooms context the verbalization of stigma from male peers is sophisticated and manifested as helplessness and resentment. The study advances scholarship on stigma, gendered power dynamics, and the social psychology of elite work by demonstrating the relevance of role responsibility in cognition and emotion, which underpin stigma responses. The findings offer implications for boards and policymakers seeking to build cultures of genuine equity and belonging within corporate governance.

Consequences of Her Exit: Media Coverage and Diversity Initiatives as Proximal and Distal Outcomes

DEI
Asmaa Belal (Waseda University); Yoshio Yanadori (Waseda University)
CEO departures attract significant academic and public attention, yet little is known about how firms react to these events especially when the departing CEO is a woman. Using a matched sample of female and male CEO departures from U.S. public firms (1994-2022), this study examines media coverage and subsequent non-financial organizational responses. We find that female CEO departures attract significantly greater media attention than male departures and are associated with changes in diversity-related initiatives, particularly policy actions and diversity-related disclosure. These effects are moderated by the reason for departure. By conceptualizing media coverage as a proximal outcome and focusing on post-departure organizational actions beyond financial outcomes, this study advances research on gendered leadership, media coverage, and organizational responses to strategic events.
