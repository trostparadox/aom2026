From Ancient Wisdom to Contemporary Organizations: A Theory of Power from Epictetus's Discourses

MSR
Dharm Prakash Sharma Bhawuk; Jin Oh Bae (University of Hawaii at Mänoa)
This paper extends organizational scholarship on power by bringing Epictetus's Discourses into conversation with contemporary perspectives on power. While research largely conceptualizes power as external—arising from resource control, formal authority, status, or structural advantage—we examine how power may be exercised from positions of marginality. Using inductive, grounded textual analysis of The Discourses, we identified 92 passages across four books in which Epictetus explicitly discusses power. From our analysis of the text, we derived five themes: reasoning faculty as power; power to assign meaning to events; exercise of power; power, character, and virtue; and power and freedom. These themes form Epictetus's theory of power, which views power as an internal capacity rather than a function of external circumstance. We compare Epictetus's theory of power with contemporary perspectives on power in organizations and show that ancient wisdom texts such as The Discourses can serve as a beneficial and viable source for developing and extending theoretical insight.

Navigating Perceptions of Stigma in British Workspaces: A Study of Skilled Muslim Refugees

MSR
Dulini Fernando (Aston University)
I examine how 32 skilled refugees account for being Muslims in the British workspace and show how individuals navigate perceptions of religious stigma by means of simultaneous engagement and disengagement. I draw attention to the potential implications of individuals’ stigma navigation strategies to depict religious stigma as a contested social process, and highlight how skilled Muslim refugees operate as a reflexive group of agents as they attempt to diversify the representations attached to them.

An Exploratory Study of Self-Spirituality Adherents' Innovation at Work

MSR
Nurit Zaidman; Kathryn Pavlovich
This paper examines the role of ‘bottom-up’ employee everyday practices and routines that may be risky, unseen and contested that create innovation in organizations. Our context is New Age spirituality because of its continued stigmatization in the workplace. The qualitative research found that innovation occurred through planned, planned and emergent and emergent self-spiritual practices. The planned practice was holism – that is, the deliberate inclusion of a wider scope of practices; planned and emergent practices included generative talk – the deliberate introduction of radical ideas - and the harmonizing of the context by keeping positive and an energetically clean environment. Practices of humanism were more emergent and included listening, empathy and recognition. The activities were generally secretly organized, mostly alone, and sometimes conflicted with the social norms prevailing in the organization. We believe that the study opens a pathway for exploring innovation within organizations through domains traditionally overlooked in scholarly research—such as religion.

The Temporalization and Spatialization of Everyday Epistemic Resistance by Marginalized Artists

MSR
Tathagata Bhowmik (Masters' Union School of Business); Diana Bilimoria (Case Western Reserve University)
This study examines how marginalized Baul performers in India enact non-discursive forms of everyday epistemic resistance to counter the caste- and class-driven structures that shape their precarious working lives. Using Charmaz’s Constructivist Grounded Theory, we revisit earlier findings on relational precarity and shift toward a community-level analysis that foregrounds how alternative ways of knowing and living emerge through collective practice. The study finds that Baul performers reconfigure the use of time and the spaces they inhabit to construct an alternative moral and ethical economy grounded in communal care, interdependence, and spiritual devotion. Through temporal practices, such as valuing distribution over accumulation, offering durational care to the community, privileging communal over market time, and preserving artistic sovereignty, and spatial practices, such as rejecting exclusionary urban venues, ritualizing marginal spaces, negotiating institutional boundaries, and embodying resistance through sartorial expressions, artists cultivate a temporo-spatial epistemology that destabilizes dominant notions of social class, occupational hierarchy, caste identity, and capitalist value. This study extends understandings of everyday resistance by highlighting subtle, culturally embedded practices that are non-confrontational, and advances scholarship on epistemic resistance by demonstrating how marginalized communities generate alternative ethical and social realities through spiritual practices and critical collective memory.
