When Being There Matters:Mobility as a Legitimacy Signaling Mechanism for Internationalization

IM
Rajarshi Mukherjee (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
Emerging economy firms often internationalize from institutionally weak environments, facing legitimacy deficits in developed markets. While prior research emphasizes asset-seeking expansion or governance upgrades, we know less about how resource-constrained born globals construct legitimacy through everyday strategic actions. Drawing on legitimacy theory, we examine the geographic mobility of human capital—the onsite deployment of employees to client locations—as a relational mechanism that enables internationalization. Using a longitudinal panel of Indian information technology outsourcing firms merged from CMIE Prowess and World Governance Indicators (1989–2022), we find that geographic mobility is positively associated with export intensity. However, this relationship is attenuated as home-country institutional quality improves, suggesting that geographic mobility substitutes for weak formal institutions. The study contributes by theorizing geographic mobility as a contingent, low-cost legitimacy-building mechanism for emerging economy born globals, with implications for how firms strategically deploy onsite resources across institutional contexts

Well-Being Regulation Through Identity Work in Self-Initiated Expatriation

IM
Dennis Gleissner (HHL Leipzig Graduate School of Management); Lin Chen (Aalto University)
Expatriate well-being is an emerging focus in expatriation research, but it is still commonly framed as a downstream adjustment outcome shaped by external conditions. We take an identity-based perspective and examine how self-initiated expatriates in Japan encounter identity threats and resources across national identity, professional identity, and personal expatriate life, and how they regulate these experiences through coping and identity work. Based on 29 semi-structured interviews, we find that well-being is tightly connected to experiences of foreignness, legitimacy, and belonging. Participants rely on strategies such as selective self-presentation, strategic communication, and career recalibration to manage identity strain. Our contribution is to connect identity work to expatriate well-being and to specify the mechanisms through which well-being is produced and managed across work and non-work domains.

Learning to Leave: How Embeddedness Both Anchors and Enables Global Mobility

IM
Yuheng Guo (University of Göttingen); Fabian Jintae Froese (Georg August University Göttingen); Samuel Davies; Tobias Blay (Georg August University Göttingen)
In today’s global talent market, self-initiated expatriates (SIEs), i.e., individuals who independently secure employment abroad, play an increasingly important role to fill skill gaps as organizations reduce expensive traditional expatriate programs. Yet the mechanisms shaping their international relocation decisions remain underexplored. Building on embeddedness theory, we develop a model that highlights a double-edged-sword effect: embeddedness can simultaneously constrain global mobility by lowering SIEs’ job search activity and facilitate global mobility by fostering job-specific knowledge acquisition enabling them to relocate more easily. We further distinguish between community and career embeddedness to capture the multifaceted nature of these dynamics. Evidence from a three-wave survey of 499 SIEs across 27 host countries largely supports our hypotheses. Our findings advance international business research by elucidating how different forms of embeddedness both inhibit and promote expatriate mobility, and by identifying job-specific knowledge acquisition as a critical boundary condition that influences the relationship between job search and relocation. In addition, findings reveal that only the links dimension (personal connections) of career embeddedness facilitates job knowledge acquisition. We discuss the implications for theory and practice.

Status Matters: How Social Status Affects Self-Initiated Expatriates’ Embeddedness

IM
Anoop Adhur Kutty (Aalto University School of Business); Alexei Koveshnikov (Aalto University); Miikka J. Lehtonen (The University of Tokyo)
In this paper, we explore the role of self-initiated expatriates’ (SIE) status in influencing SIEs’ professional and social embeddedness. Importantly, we differentiate between the influences of two types of status - one objective measured as SIEs’ socioeconomic status and one subjective measured as a perception of SIEs’ own relative social standing vis-à-vis host country nationals. We draw from social identity theory to argue that a higher SIEs’ status results in a higher degree of both professional and social embeddedness because higher status SIEs are likely to experience lower degrees of deprivation and higher insider status in the host organization. Our analysis of 251 SIEs in Japan shows that only objective socioeconomic status predicts SIEs’ embeddedness via lower deprivation (due to higher income and job position) and higher insider status (due to higher job position). The relative social standing has only indirect effect on embeddedness via lower relative deprivation but no effects via insider status. Our study is among the first ones to delve into the influences of different indicators of SIEs’ status. It advances theoretical discussion around identity, inequality, and mobility in global work and calls for more attention to status-related barriers and enablers of the SIE expatriation experience.
