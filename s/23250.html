A Multi-Level Attention Framework for Market Reactions to Alliance Announcements

STR
Noushi Rahman (Pace University); James Rawlins (Pace University)
Drawing on behavioral finance and investor attention research, we examine how different dimensions of surprise in alliance announcements affect partnering firms’ cumulative abnormal returns (CAR). We distinguish between surprise magnitude, based on a firm's alliance frequency, and surprise experience, based on investors’ attention capacity given industry-level alliance activity. We find that both surprise magnitude and surprise experience are positively associated with CAR, and that surprise experience partially mediates the relationship between surprise magnitude and CAR. These findings suggest that the timing and context of alliance announcements can be strategically managed to shape market reactions. This research contributes to the alliance literature and to work on investor attention by demonstrating that surprise effects are not uniform across announcements and that industry-level attention conditions how firm-level events are perceived and priced by capital markets.

An Inductive Reassessment of Acquisition Premiums and Their Performance Consequences

STR
Joonas Kalle Juhani Kauto (University of St.Gallen); Tomi MM Laamanen (University of St. Gallen)
Acquisition premiums are commonly interpreted as evidence of managerial overpayment, yet prior research suggests they may reflect informed assessments under valuation uncertainty. We revisit acquisition pricing by applying a machine-learning–based inductive framework to 1,833 North American acquisitions (2000–2024), triangulating insights through traditional econometric regression models and long-horizon total shareholder returns (TSR). The analysis uncovers nonlinear patterns indicating that acquirers systematically pay higher premiums for targets that markets may undervalue—specifically distressed firms, low Tobin’s Q firms, and R&D-intensive firms. Crucially, TSR analyses reveal that these premiums are statistically unrelated to long-run value destruction, suggesting the market eventually validates the price paid. These findings challenge the overpayment narrative, positing that premiums often represent rational, context-dependent strategic judgments rather than behavioral bias.

Make Numbers Count: The Effect of Quantification on Investor Response to Acquisition Announcements (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Florian Karl Rues (University of Mannheim); Constantin Wespatat (University of Mannheim)
Extant M&A research has identified a myriad of firm- and deal-related factors that shape investor response to acquisition announcements. More recent work has also examined the effects of acquirers’ communication, primarily focusing on communicative tactics executives can employ to influence investor response. However, there is less evidence on how investors respond to the content of acquirers’ communication surrounding the announcement. Drawing on insights from the “Sociology of Quantification”, we theorize that quantification, i.e., the production and, in particular, the communication of numbers in deal presentations, aids executives in convincing investors of the merits of an announced acquisition. We further propose that disciplining numbers, i.e., numbers that serve investors as benchmarks for monitoring the performance of acquirer executives, are more effective than non-disciplining numbers. Moreover, we argue that disciplining numbers are particularly effective for acquirers with a reputation of adhering to provided numerical targets and under conditions of high information asymmetry. An analysis of hand-coded data from 720 conference calls hosted by U.S. public acquirers between 2013 and 2018 supports most of our predictions. Our main results show that a one standard deviation increase in quantification translates into a sizable change in acquirer market value of $90.89 million.

Growth Strategies and the Valuation of Conglomerates

STR
Deborah Dulex (University of Lausanne); Daniel Oyon (University of Lausanne)
This paper examines whether the valuation of conglomerates depends on how firms grow. Using a panel of U.S. public firms from 2002 to 2022, we decompose firm growth into organic and acquisition-driven components measured over a three-year horizon and relate them to excess value. We confirm the presence of a diversification discount on average but show that it is not static. Growth is positively valued at moderate levels yet exhibits clear non-linear effects. Conglomerates benefit more from incremental growth than specialized firms at low levels but experience sharper diminishing returns as growth intensifies, particularly when driven by acquisitions. Organic growth displays similar valuation patterns across organizational forms. Propensity score matching results indicate that residual heterogeneity in valuation is primarily driven by short-term, acquisition-based growth. Overall, our findings show that the diversification discount depends on firms’ growth paths rather than scope alone.

Wired to the Deal: How Structural Embeddedness Shapes Rival Returns to Competitors’ Acquisitions (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Kamyar Goudarzi (College of Charleston); Dimitrija Kalanoski (The University of Manchester)
Acquisitions often reshape competitive dynamics beyond the acquirer–target dyad, yet we know little about why uninvolved rivals’ market reactions differ and when competitors’ acquisitions benefit rather than harm them. Drawing on social embeddedness theory, we argue that rivals are not evaluated in isolation; they differ in their structural embeddedness within the market’s information infrastructure. We focus on shared security analysts linking acquirers and uninvolved rivals and theorize that these ties facilitate comparative interpretation of deal implications, predicting positive rival announcement returns, especially when expected acquirer value creation is high. We further argue and show that this effect depends on the information environment, weakening when rival-side information is abundant or noisy and strengthening when acquirer-side coverage and disagreement increase the value of shared-analyst benchmarking. Using a large sample of publicly listed acquirers and their publicly traded rivals, we find support for these predictions.
