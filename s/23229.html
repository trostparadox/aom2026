Managing Artificial Intelligence with Human Capital: Toward a Strategic Entrepreneurship Theory

STR
Chien-Chi Tseng (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Organizations increasingly rely on artificial intelligence (AI) to identify opportunities, allocate resources, and scale strategic initiatives. Yet despite substantial investments, many firms struggle to translate AI adoption into sustained entrepreneurial advantage. We argue that this challenge reflects a deeper theoretical problem: prevailing research conceptualizes AI either as a technological resource or as a labor substitute/complement, rather than as a component embedded within the firm’s human capital system. We challenge the assumption that entrepreneurial cognition is exclusively human. We develop a theory of strategic entrepreneurship for AI–human capital co-orchestration that explains how firms simultaneously pursue opportunity-seeking and advantage-seeking when cognition, judgment, and learning are distributed across humans and machines. Integrating strategic entrepreneurship, human capital theory, and bounded rationality, we introduce the construct of hybrid advantage—entrepreneurial rents generated by hybrid cognitive systems in which AI contributes machine-embedded rationality, while human capital contributes judgment. We theorize a four-stage process—opportunity construction, strategic judgment and commitment, capability reconfiguration, and value capture and renewal—governed by organizational mechanisms that calibrate trust, allocate accountability, and preserve exploration under uncertainty. Our model extends strategic entrepreneurship beyond human-only cognition, reframes human capital as relational and recomposable, and provides a generative agenda for research on AI-enabled strategy.

AI-Washing or AI-Embedding? How Negative Attainment Discrepancy Shapes Firms' Divergent AI Strategies

STR
Lingli Luo (Zhejiang University); Lei Cao (Zhejiang University)
Drawing on the Behavioral Theory of the Firm and threat-rigidity theory, we explain why and under what conditions negative attainment discrepancy shapes firms’ divergent substantive and symbolic AI investment strategies in the era of Industry 4.0. We theorize that performance shortfalls trigger problemistic search and heightened risk tolerance, prompting firms to increase substantive AI investments aimed at performance recovery. Simultaneously, these shortalls induce threat-based cognitive constriction and communication withdrawal, suppressing symbolic AI investments oriented toward external legitimation. We further argue that these effects are contingent on both internal and external contexts. Organizational slack and industry digitalization facilitates problem-solving responses, while alleviating perceived threat and rigidity, thereby amplifying the positive effect of performance shortfalls on substantive AI investment and attenuating the negative effect on symbolic AI investment. In contrast, environmental dynamism dampening managerial risk-taking while heightening uncertainty and threat rigidity, weakening the positive relationship between performance shortfalls and substantive AI investment and strengthening the negative relationship with symbolic AI investment. Using a large-scale longitudinal dataset of Chinese listed firms from 2010 to 2022, we find empirical support for our hypotheses. By distinguishing between substantive and symbolic AI investments and identifying their distinct behavioral antecedents, this study advances research on AI strategy, performance feedback, and organizational decoupling in the context of emerging digital technologies.

Is AI Disruptive or Enabling in an Ecosystem? Empirical Evidence from a Digital Platform

STR
Shiva Agarwal (The University of Texas at Austin); Harsh Ketkar (McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin); Ram Ranganathan (The University of Texas at Austin)
Is AI a disruptive or enabling technology? By analyzing adoption patterns in a major digital platform ecosystem, we explore whether AI fosters entry by new competitors or strengthens incumbents. We exploit an exogenous demand shock—the announcement of ChatGPT—to examine shifts in competitive positions. We find that, before the shock, new entrants were more likely to introduce AI-enabled products; however, afterward, incumbents responded to increased user demand for AI products at higher rates. Furthermore, post-shock, users favored AI-enabled products offered by incumbent firms despite new entrants introducing more novel AI-enabled products. Our abductive analysis reveals that this advantage stems not from incumbents' existing complementary assets or resources, but from their established technological capabilities. These findings challenge conventional views on AI, suggesting that AI may not be competence-destroying in digital industries. We discuss how these findings contribute to understanding entry-exit patterns, innovation strategy, and ecosystem evolution in general-purpose technologies.

The Generation and Diffusion of Artificial Intelligence (AI) Around the World

STR
Heather Berry (Georgetown University); Róisín Donnelly (Tilburg University)
We analyse the generation and diffusion of AI innovation around the world, exploring whether AI technologies and applications are more localized in their search and development compared to non-AI inventions in the same-technology field, country and year. We use machine learning to parse the text of patent documents to capture AI-related inventions (regardless of the technology code) in Patstat Global – a database that includes patent families from over one hundred patent offices in both industrialized and developing countries. Our descriptive statistics show variations in AI technology focus across countries and over time while our regression results confirm that AI patents are less likely to build on foreign knowledge than non-AI patents, highlighting the important role of local ecosystems, data and users in AI innovations.

Talking AI vs. Doing AI: How Short-Termism Shapes Symbolic and Substantive AI Adoption

STR
SAMI ASSAF (IE Business School); Carl J. Kock (IE Business School)
Corporate references to artificial intelligence (AI) have surged, creating the appearance of broad technological transformation. Yet substantive AI adoption is costly and multi-period, requiring infrastructure, talent, integration, and organizational learning—raising the possibility that much observed AI disclosure reflects symbolic alignment with field-level expectations rather than implementation-oriented commitment. We argue that managerial short-termism helps explain this divergence: short-term-oriented firms face strong incentives to appear technologically responsive while avoiding investments with delayed and uncertain payoffs, making “AI talk” easier than “AI build.” Using textual analysis of 6,278 10-K filings from 715 S&P 500 firms over 2015–2024, we develop scalable measures that distinguish symbolic AI references from substantive, implementation-oriented AI disclosure, alongside firm-level measures of managerial short-termism. Consistent with our theory, short-termism is associated with weaker substantive AI disclosure within firms over time, while showing no comparable relationship with symbolic AI language—suggesting that the main imprint of short-termism is reduced follow-through rather than inflated rhetoric. Following the post-2022 generative-AI shock, substantive AI disclosure rises on average, yet the short-termism penalty for substantive engagement becomes more pronounced. An ancillary timing analysis further indicates that short-term-oriented firms escalate into more salient AI disclosure later. Overall, the study explains why AI discourse diffuses rapidly even as implementation remains uneven and identifies managerial temporal orientation as a key constraint on substantive technological transformation.
