Session overview

Highly skilled human capital with advanced scientific and engineering knowledge is a critical engine for innovation, entrepreneurship, and industry growth. Recent work has focused on the role of star scientists and educated engineers as a critical source of innovation; scientists and STEM-educated graduates may also become science- or technology-based entrepreneurs; and the industry evolution literature also discusses the role that science and engineering human capital plays in shaping the early stage of industries. While this growing scholarly attention has established the static importance of human capital, it often treats the supply of talent as a fixed endowment. What is often missed is the dynamic co-evolutionary process where academic institutions and industries mutually shape one another over time. This process is relevant not only in developing countries where the supply of skilled human capital is scarce and expanding access to university education is a major political concern, but also in developed countries when universities and academic research are evolving around new technological domains. For instance, many top-tier US universities have recently begun implementing AI-related programs and colleges, which significantly alter the supply of AI-related expertise in industries. However, we still lack a clear understanding of how these macro-level shifts in the demand and supply of human capital create new strategic imperatives for firms, as well as the 'micro-foundational' mechanisms that determine who successfully translates this new academic knowledge and skills into innovation and industrial leadership, and how. This gap is critical, as understanding how academic talent is allocated across sectors and industries has significant political implications for innovation and economic growth, and how to effectively hire and retain talent is also a key managerial concern for firms. This presenter symposium brings together junior strategy and innovation scholars who are actively engaged in studying the co-evolution of academia and industry, as well as the role of highly skilled human capital in innovation, entrepreneurship, and industry evolution. While the first two studies examine how exogenous shocks to demand (i.e., import suspension during WWI in Japan) and supply (i.e., higher education expansion in Vietnam) reshape industrial landscapes, the latter two studies zoom in on the micro-level mechanisms related to academic talent—firms’ hiring decisions and external and internal mobility—that enable firms to adapt to these shifts. By highlighting the co-evolutionary nature of academic research (or university education) and industry, this symposium aims to develop our understanding of how individuals and firms should adapt to changing technological environments and human-capital supply conditions. Additionally, by integrating four empirical papers that examine distinct contexts —including developing economies, historical industrialization periods, and advanced economies—this symposium offers attendees opportunities to learn how diverse settings can be leveraged to advance strategy and innovation theory.
