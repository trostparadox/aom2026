The Agency Gap in Task Assignments: The Unintended Consequences of Task Autonomy

OMT
Lenard Strahringer (Stanford Graduate School of Business)
Modern organizations increasingly let workers allocate work autonomously instead of prescribing it to them to boost motivation, innovation, and productivity. While such autonomy is assumed to free workers from both structural rigidity and managerial bias, I argue that it can inadvertently create an agency premium for men. Drawing on legitimation theory, I posit that agentic behaviors, such as task claims, must be legitimized to oneself and others. Legitimacy is often granted based on perceived competence, a resource less available to women due to pervasive gender stereotypes, particularly in male-typed fields. Faced with this higher bar to legitimate their task claims, women might rely on an alternative source of legitimacy: formal managerial authority. I test this theory using data from over 100,000 task assignments in a large software development company. The findings are clear-cut: despite a gender-neutral norm of self-assignment, men assign themselves significantly more and more desirable tasks than women. Women, in contrast, claim unclaimed leftover work. Notably, tasks assigned by male managers are similarly gendered, but female managers assign work more equitably, with tasks of similar importance assigned to men and women. These results caution that practices promoting worker agency can inadvertently reinforce inequality by allowing men to claim more desirable, and potentially career-enhancing work, thereby challenging the meritocratic ideal of autonomous organizational design.

Task Allocation in the Division of Labor: How Specialized Roles Shape Professional–Client Matching

OMT
Jillian Chown (Kellogg School of Management, Northwestern University); Carlos Inoue (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
A central challenge in organizations is ensuring that expertise is deployed where it creates the most value. In professional settings, this takes the form of matching clients with the professionals best equipped to serve them. As professionals become more specialized, their expertise deepens but their scope narrows, creating a tension that makes effective matching both more valuable and more difficult. We argue that specialized roles help resolve this tension by codifying expertise into formal positions that provide allocative infrastructure to channel work to appropriate professionals. We examine whether such roles improve professional–client matching and how organizational conditions shape their effectiveness. We test these ideas in Brazilian maternity wards that introduced obstetric (OB) nurses as a new specialized role. Using data on more than 15 million births (2012–2022) and a difference-in-differences design, we find that OB nurse adoption is associated with improved professional–client matching—measured as the alignment between a patient’s risk profile and a provider’s prior experience with similar cases. The positive association is stronger when client demand is higher, workflows are more predictable, and organizational experience is greater. Post-hoc analyses show that improved matching is associated with better maternal and newborn health outcomes. Our findings advance organization theory by extending research on task allocation, conceptualizing roles as allocative infrastructure, and identifying the organizational conditions that shape their effectiveness. These insights can extend beyond healthcare to other expertise-driven industries where matching specialized workers to clients is critical for performance.

Occupational Gender Composition and Task Segregation in Professional Work

OMT
Jillian Chown (Kellogg School of Management, Northwestern University); Sarah Kaplan (University of Toronto)
Tasks are a central arena through which inequality is enacted in organizations. Even when men and women hold the same occupational titles, they often perform systematically different kinds of work—a form of intra-occupational task segregation that shapes both material and symbolic outcomes. We theorize that the extent of task segregation depends on an occupation’s degree of gender integration. In historically male-dominated occupations, the presence of women can heighten rather than diminish the symbolic boundaries around who performs which kinds of work. Using administrative billing records from over 20,000 physicians in 16 more or less gender-integrated specialties (occupations) in Ontario, Canada’s universal healthcare system, we analyze more than 700 medical procedures (tasks) over 11 years. Confirming previous research, we find that men disproportionately perform technically complex tasks, while women are concentrated in communicative and relational work. Yet, surprisingly, these divisions are most pronounced in specialties with greater female representation where symbolic boundary activation may reassert distinctions between masculine and feminine forms of expertise. Our findings position tasks as a central mechanism through which demographic composition and symbolic hierarchies interact to reproduce inequality, revealing how gender integration can intensify rather than erode divisions of labor within professional work.

How Moral Enactments Shape Professional Segmentation

OMT
Søren Lund Frandsen (Copenhagen Business School); Jane Bjørn Vedel (Copenhagen Business School)
Professions are expected to carry shared standards that make “who does what” look broadly similar across comparable workplaces. Yet under the same external pressures, similar professionals in similar organizations can nevertheless reorganize their work in sharply different ways. We examine this puzzle through an inductive, comparative study of five life- science departments in one national university system (95 interviews) facing the same environmental change. Across cases, some units split professional work into distinct bundles—protecting a subset of members for professional core work while shifting collective obligations onto others— whereas other units keep core and collective tasks broadly bundled across most jobs; two cases fall in between. These differences are not well explained by technology adoption, demographics, or client differentiation. Instead, we show that they hinge on local moral positions about what counts as an appropriate professional contribution and what successful members owe the collective. We identify an entrepreneurial moral position that treats external individual performance as a warrant for relief, priority, and control, and a communal moral position that treats core obligations as broadly shared and only loosely tied to individual performance. We trace how these moral positions are enacted through routine allocation decisions that configure task capacity (who is resourced to do which work) and task ownership (where tasks “live” and who can claim the associated benefits). Over time, these enactments crystallize into divergent patterns of within-profession differentiation under similar environmental pressures.

Jurisdictional Reinforcement in New Institutionalism: Evidence from Organizational Sustainability

OMT
Janna Z. Huang (University of California Berkeley); Adair Morse (University of California Berkeley); Katherine Baird (University of California Berkeley)
A key dimension of institutionalization is the emergence of professional groups that become increasingly isomorphic across organizations. Yet recent institutional perspectives emphasize multiple, divergent pathways of institutionalization. This paper introduces a theory of jurisdictional reinforcement as both an alternative to and a complement to professionalization. Under jurisdictional reinforcement, institutionalization occurs within organizational jurisdictions where practices are already embedded, rather than through separate professional units. We test jurisdictional reinforcement alongside professionalization using original survey data from 149 firms, measuring both where sustainability work is already embedded across organizational units and where firms prioritize sustainability hiring across jurisdictions of work. We document isomorphic institutionalization and professionalization of sustainability, but largely confined to reporting and strategy functions. Most critically for jurisdictional reinforcement, when sustainability tasks already exist in particular organizational units such as operations, finance, and data, firms respond by deepening hiring in the corresponding functional jurisdictions of work, rather than implementing activities through sustainability units. Finally, when we examine jurisdictional reinforcement and professionalization together within the same domain, we find that they operate in tandem, reconciling existing accounts of isomorphic professionalization with new institutionalist accounts of organizational heterogeneity.
