Closing the Loop: A Systems Theory of Entrepreneurial Action and Time

ENT
Bernd Wurth (University of Glasgow); Georges Romme (Eindhoven University of Technology); Bob Walrave (Eindhoven University of Technology); Erik Stam (Utrecht University)
Entrepreneurship research has long recognised that entrepreneurship unfolds through time, yet dominant theoretical frameworks remain largely static. This paper develops a dynamic systems theory of entrepreneurial action that is built on four core principles, namely feedback loops that connect goals/beliefs and decisions/actions to results and environmental responses; temporal delays that separate cause from observable effect; stock–flow accumulation processes; and non-linear dynamics including thresholds and tipping points. We demonstrate how this perspective provides a foundation for previously only loosely connected theoretical streams such as judgment-based approaches, effectuation, the theory-based view, and (entrepreneurial) ecosystems by revealing them as complementary descriptions of nested feedback systems operating at different timescales and levels of aggregation. The framework shows that entrepreneurs operate within learning loops whose effectiveness depends on delay structures; that ventures exhibit characteristic dynamic patterns emerging from underlying feedback architectures; and that ecosystems shape individual action through parameter modification. We reconceptualise external enablers as interventions that modify feedback structure (e.g., reducing delays, lowering thresholds, restructuring loops, or building stocks). Our primary contribution is demonstrating that dynamic systems thinking constitutes the missing theoretical architecture for cumulative theory building about entrepreneurship as process.

Emotional Pathways of Pivoting: Interplay Between Passion & Fear in Response to Negative Feedback

ENT
Md Kamrul Hasan (Washington State University); Benjamin Warnick (Washington State University)
Entrepreneurs routinely receive negative feedback that challenges the viability of their venture ideas. Although feedback is often theorized as informational input triggering cognitive reassessment and strategic realignment, we argue that it also operates through an emotional pathway. Integrating research on entrepreneurial feedback, emotion, and decision-making in lifestyle entrepreneurship, we test a model where negative feedback elicits fear of failure that can encourage pivoting rather than solely impeding persistence. Bridging streams that typically treat passion and fear as opposing forces, we theorize that they can co-occur as emotional ambivalence shaping responses to evaluative threat, such that product passion—a defining feature of lifestyle entrepreneurship —amplifies both fear in response to negative feedback and the translation of fear into pivoting intentions. We test this model across two studies. Study 1 employs field data from 66 nascent entrepreneurs in business pitch competitions and shows that negative feedback increases fear of failure, thereby predicting pivoting and persistence intentions. Study 2 uses a two-wave preregistered experiment with 158 nascent entrepreneurs, replicating the fear-based pathway and demonstrating that passion increases fear responses and strengthens fear-driven pivoting. Our findings highlight an emotional pathway of entrepreneurial decision-making and clarify how lifestyle entrepreneurs experience and respond to negative feedback.

Opportunity or Threat? How Entrepreneurs Interpret Customer Feedback When Pivoting

ENT
Ralf Wilden (Macquarie University); Nidthida Lin (Macquarie University); Melissa Akaka (University of Denver); John Sebesta (University of Denver); Johann Nils Foege
Using an abductive, mixed-methods research design, we investigate when and how entrepreneurs incorporate customer feedback to inform their pivoting decision making. We conducted interviews with 21 early-stage entrepreneurs in two phases to explore the value of customer feedback in entrepreneurial journeys and, more specifically, in pivoting decisions. Drawing on this qualitative investigation, we theorize that entrepreneurs differently weight customer feedback when exposed to opportunity-based versus threat-based information. We identify three feedback characteristics – source of feedback (existing vs. potential customer), degree of customer engagement (high vs. low), and feedback type (direct vs. indirect) – that shape pivoting decisions. We test our theory using a discrete choice experiment with 418 entrepreneurs across both opportunity-based and threat-based information scenarios. This study bridges entrepreneurship and marketing theory to show how entrepreneurs selectively attend to different dimensions of customer feedback when making pivoting decisions, offering both theoretical insights into entrepreneurial pivots and practical guidance for entrepreneurs with new ventures designing and navigating business model transformation.

Risky Play: How Entrepreneurial Firms Transform Negative into Positive Unintended Consequences

ENT
Mark Shilong Jia (Tsinghua University); Pengxiang Zhang (Peking University)
Unintended consequences are a key interest in organization and strategy research, yet it remains unclear how some ventures successfully transform negative into positive unintended consequences, while others fail. With a deep dive into five SaaS ventures for seven years, our data reveal the successful ventures followed (1) a threat reappraisal process, and then engaged in (2) a serendipitous learning process. By contrast, failed ventures continued the original product-market fit with an unaddressed threat. Primarily, we contribute a process theory of risky play that bridges the gap between negative and positive unintended consequences. Broadly, we contribute to organization theory by clarifying how executives regulate emotion and cognition with a threat reappraisal. We also contribute to strategy literature by introducing how executives use serendipitous learning to trigger positive unintended consequences. Further, we provide a solution for executives navigating product user resistance.

When Motives Clash: Gendered Consequences of Motivation for Entrepreneurial Fear of Failure

ENT
Viva Nsair (Western Michigan University)
Scholars and practitioners often assume that extrinsic incentives fortify nascent entrepreneurs against doubt. We challenge this intuition by theorizing and testing how intrinsic and extrinsic motivation jointly shape entrepreneurial fear of failure over time, and how these effects differ by gender. Integrating self-determination theory with role congruity theory, we argue that extrinsic motivation heightens fear of failure because external contingencies increase performance pressure and contingent self-worth, whereas intrinsic motivation can either buffer or exacerbate this fear depending on gendered role demands. Using a two-wave panel of adult nascent entrepreneurs (N = 153; 50% women), we find that extrinsic motivation at Time 1 positively predicts fear of failure at Time 2. Critically, a three-way interaction shows that intrinsic and extrinsic motivation combine to reduce fear for women but amplify fear for men: the extrinsic-to-fear slope is weakest for women at high intrinsic motivation and strongest for men at high intrinsic motivation. These results, which remain robust when adding risk aversion and perceived social support as covariates, extend research on motivational antecedents of fear by specifying when motivational composition alleviates versus exacerbates threat appraisals. We contribute a gender-contingent account of how autonomous and controlled motives translate into fear, offering implications for the motivational design of incubators, accelerators, and investment processes.
