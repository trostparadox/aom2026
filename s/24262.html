A Nagging Feeling: Workplace Surveillance and Employee Function Creep Concerns

OB
Ariane Ollier-Malaterre (Université du Québec à Montréal (UQAM)); Yanick Provost Savard; Xavier Parent-Rocheleau (HEC Montréal)
Reflecting the quantification of management as well as the digitalization of work processes and organizational control practices, employee surveillance technologies have become more sophisticated, relying on algorithmic software collecting large amounts of data on employees’ behaviors, performance, attitudes, and emotions. Like other technologies, employee surveillance technologies are subject to function creep, i.e. gradual expansion beyond original purposes. This article analyzes employees’ function creep concerns, i.e. their worries about potential uses of surveillance data over time. We draw on workplace surveillance research as well as the adjacent fields of privacy and surveillance studies to examine the relationship between employees’ function creep concerns, invasiveness concerns, psychological contract breach and subjective stress. Across two time-lagged studies (n = 175 and n = 475), employees’ function creep concerns were associated with invasiveness concerns. In turn, invasiveness concerns were associated with psychological contract breach and subjective stress. Moreover, function creep concerns were directly associated with psychological contract breach (both studies) and subjective stress (study 2). In study 1 on unionized workers, preferences for segmenting work and nonwork domains acted as a moderator, strengthening the relationship between invasiveness concerns and psychological contract breach, but not subjective stress. This study extends organizational control research by identifying function creep as a key driver of employees’ negative reactions to surveillance, and invasiveness as the underlying mechanism. It contributes to organizational behavior theories by showing how the digitalization and quantification of work can undermine important employee attitudes at work.
