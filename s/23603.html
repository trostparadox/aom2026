Neurodivergence, Disability, and Organizational Design

Session overview

This session addresses how organizations can better understand and support neurodivergent and disabled employees through leadership, communication, work design, and support structures. The papers explore masking among neurodivergent women leaders, autistic professionals' moral injury, ADHD employees' experiences of feedback, remote inclusion for employees with disabilities, and the roles of self-stigma and supervisory support. Together, they offer actionable insight into how organizational design and managerial practice shape inclusion.

Neurodivergent Female Leaders: Masking, Adaptation, and Relational Leadership

DEI
Joanna Szulc (Gdansk University of Technology); Zuzanna Staniszewska (Kozminski University in Warsaw)
Many neurodivergent women lead in workplaces never designed with their realities or needs in mind, making their contributions both essential and often invisible. To function in environments that reward conformity and social fluency, they frequently adopt strategies such as social masking - practices largely absent from academic research and managerial discourse. In this qualitative, phenomenon-driven research, we examine the lived experiences of neurodistinct women leaders to understand how masking shapes relational leadership. We introduce the Masked Leadership Development (MLD), a developmental process through which women move from survival, to awareness of their adaptive strategies, and ultimately to connection with others. Our findings show that masking, often seen as limiting, can be reinterpreted over time as a source of relational competence, and that navigating workplaces not built with their needs in mind gives rise to distinctive ways of relating to others. By revealing these everyday, often invisible processes, this research moves beyond deficit framings to illuminate the strengths neurodivergent women bring to leadership development. Our study provides a phenomenon-driven account that advances theory and underscores the importance of organizational environments that allow diverse relational strengths to emerge.

How Autistic Professionals Navigate Persistent Moral Injury in Organizations

DEI
Michelle Blood Checketts (University of Wyoming); Teresa Cardador; Denise Lewin Loyd (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
Scholars have typically conceptualized moral injury as the result of discrete, high-stakes events; however, the reality of contemporary organization life is that individuals in many occupations encounter recurring situations that challenge their sense of moral integrity. Research has rarely examined moral injury as a frequent, sometimes daily phenomenon, leaving important questions about how repeated moral disruptions shape ongoing employee well-being. Recent theory suggests Autistic individuals experience heightened moral distress more readily than neurotypical peers due to a rigid need for rule following, leading to frequent experiences of intense moral injury that persist over long periods of time. Over 48 semi-structured interviews with Autistic professionals, the findings suggest that what we term an Autistic need for justice produces frequent and intense perceptions of moral transgression within organizations, leading to an embodied experience of moral injury. The findings highlight that due to the persistent nature of this experience, Autistic professionals enact various response strategies to navigate moral injury, including proactive, reactive, and enduring. The findings also demonstrate the importance of organizational supports to aid in navigating moral injury, suggesting novel supports related to culture and communication as keys for facilitating beneficial response strategies. By understanding the Autistic experience of moral injury, this research highlights how the daily occurrence of perceived morally injurious events is experienced by individuals and how it necessitates additional response strategies and supports beyond those typically emphasized in moral injury literature. This research also broadens our understanding of the Autistic lived experience of working in organizations.

Rethinking Feedback and Research Norms Through the Lived Experiences of ADHD Employees

DEI
Joanna Szulc (Gdansk University of Technology); Tomasz Balicki (Gdansk University of Technology); Maria Eulalia Witoslawska-Piotrowska (University of Lodz)
Feedback is widely recognized as a cornerstone of learning, performance, and inclusion at work. Yet dominant models of “good feedback” in management studies often rely on implicit neurotypical assumptions, privileging indirectness, emotional cushioning, and ambiguity as markers of care and professionalism. Drawing on a qualitative study with 37 formally diagnosed white-collar employees with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), this paper challenges these assumptions by foregrounding how feedback is experienced, interpreted, and evaluated by a marginalized employee group. Empirically, the study is based on semi-structured interviews combined with a visual Pictor technique, enabling participants to represent feedback relationships and dynamics beyond linear verbal narratives. The research team itself was neurodiverse, and the fieldwork required significant methodological adaptation, including flexible scheduling, repeated reminders, and rescheduled interviews. Rather than treating these features as methodological shortcomings, we analyze them as constitutive elements of inclusive research practice. Our findings show that employees with ADHD consistently value feedback that is direct, honest, and development-oriented, while expressing frustration with indirect communication, “sugarcoating,” and implicit expectations. Feedback framed as polite but ambiguous often increased cognitive load, delayed sensemaking, and strained workplace relationships. At the same time, participants emphasized that direct feedback was not experienced as uncaring when it was constructive, dialogical, and oriented toward growth over time. Building on these insights, we develop a reflexive argument that links feedback practices and research practices as parallel sites of exclusion. We show how the same norms - politeness, control, predictability, and linearity - shape both organizational feedback models and dominant qualitative research designs, often to the detriment of neurodivergent participants. The paper contributes to inclusive and creative management research by reframing care away from communicative softening and methodological control, and toward clarity, dialogue, and relational accountability.

Inclusion Among Remote Employees With Disabilities: A Social Presence Perspective

DEI
Yuting Chen (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Xiji Zhu (Hitotsubashi University); David C. Baldridge (Oregon State University); Xiao-Yun Xie (Zhejiang University)
Remote work has expanded labor market access for people with disabilities (PWD) but may also intensify social isolation, thereby undermining workplace inclusion. Drawing on social presence theory, we examine when leaders’ digital communication media richness alleviates loneliness and enhances inclusion for PWD working remotely. We argue that richer leader communication media, such as synchronous voice or video and rapid feedback, increases perceived social presence, reducing loneliness and fostering inclusion. However, these benefits depend on employees’ social avoidance motivation. We further theorize that two disability attributes, disability severity and perceived disability related physical unattractiveness, heighten avoidance and attenuate the effectiveness of rich media. We test our model using a multi-method design including qualitative interviews, an experience sampling study of 142 employees with disabilities in China, and a two-wave survey of 444 remote employees with disabilities worldwide. Findings demonstrate that leader communication media richness enhances workplace inclusion, yet this impact is contingent upon disability attributes and social avoidance motivation. Overall, this research advances the literature on disability inclusion and remote work by identifying leader communication as a critical mechanism for fostering inclusion while clarifying the boundary conditions under which such communication is most effective.

Too Unique for Inclusion? Self-Stigma, Supervisory Support and Inclusion of People With Disabilities

DEI
Hilla Back (Aalto University); Daria Kautto (Laurea University of Applied Sciences); Seonyoung Hwang (Royal Holloway, University of London); Marjo-Riitta Diehl
Our understanding of the inclusion experiences of the world’s largest minority group – people with disabilities (PWDs) – remains fragmented and under-theorized. In this study, we examine how self-stigma and supervisory support shape the inclusion experiences of PWDs at their workplace. Using a mixed-methods design, we draw on a survey (n = 255) and interviews (n = 36) of employees at a university in Finland. The results of the study show that PWDs, especially those with mental disabilities, experience significant lower levels of inclusion. Intrapersonal psychological mechanism of disability self-stigma is identified to play a substantial role in perception of inclusion by PWDs. Type of disability (particularly mental illness), the level of disruptiveness and visibility of disability increase PWD’s self-stigma. Our study offers nuanced insights into the mechanisms underpinning these relationships, highlighting the crucial role of emotional support from the supervisor in lowering self-stigma and fostering inclusion as employees disclose their uniqueness. Our study contributes to inclusion scholarship by emphasizing an employee-centered lens on inclusion and highlighting the complex dynamics of self-stigma and supervisory support. It offers actionable insights for creating inclusive organizational cultures that empower employees to express their unique identities.
