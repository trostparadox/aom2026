Facing Ambiguity: Making Sense of Multiple Leader Role Models in Management Consultancies

MC
Franziska Weindel; Björn Schäfer; Paul Goldmann
Leadership development is widely understood to unfold through formal programs and structured learning approaches. However, such methods do not fully capture how leadership learning unfolds in dynamic and ambiguous environments, such as project-based organizations. By applying an abductive research approach, we examine leadership learning by prioritizing individuals' sensemaking of the multiple leader role models they observe. Based on 36 interviews with mid-level managers in management consultancies, this study identifies three reflective processing modes that offer a nuanced understanding of leadership development within these firms. We further introduce a leadership learning cycle that extends beyond this specific context and offers a generalizable framework, highlighting the pivotal role of observation, reflection, and identity work in leadership learning. Our findings connect the leadership development literature to the specific context of management consultancies and provide implications for advancing theory and informing practice.

Conceptualising Leader Role Models: Observational Leadership Development in Management Consultancies

MC
Franziska Weindel
Management consultancies are distinguished by their unique organizational structures and career dynamics, where individuals are frequently exposed to multiple role models from whom they develop leadership skills through observation. Although role models are widely acknowledged as important in leadership development, little is known about the precise nature of role models in this domain - a leader role model. Drawing on a multiple case study design, this study aims to address this gap by the development an overarching definition of a leader role model as the conceptual basis for a cross-organizational framework of core characteristics. The findings underscore observational learning as a central mechanism, highlighting that leadership growth in professional organizations occurs predominantly through everyday interactions with diverse leader role models across projects, and that these interactions play a crucial role in shaping emerging leader identities. The study contributes to the extant literature on professional organizations, identity construction and observational leadership development, offering valuable practical insights on improving leadership development programs embedded within everyday professional practice.

Holding Environment In Psychodynamic Coaching: An Integrative Review And Construct Clarification

MC
Daniel Minis (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Svetlana Khapova (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Konstantin Korotov (ESMT - European School of Management and Technology - Berlin)
The holding environment is widely referenced in executive coaching as a means of providing psychological safety and emotional containment. While frequently cited as a key mechanism for facilitating developmental insight and behavioral change, its theoretical adaptation from psychoanalytic practice has not been critically examined. Originally conceptualized by Winnicott as a therapeutic space for processing unconscious material, the holding environment has been incorporated into coaching without sufficient attention to contextual differences, including relational dynamics, objectives, and ethical considerations. This paper systematically analyzes the construct’s integration into executive coaching, identifying theoretical ambiguities and areas of misalignment. Through an integrative literature review, we trace its conceptual evolution, examine its current application, and propose a refined definition tailored to the coaching context. By clarifying the construct’s theoretical foundations, this study contributes to both scholarly discourse and professional practice, ensuring its appropriate and effective use within executive coaching.

Dysfunctional Leaders: Only the Tip of the Iceberg, System Function and Leadership Decay

MC
Anezka Viskova-Robertson (Western Michigan University)
Why do organizations repeatedly retain leaders whose actions undermine long-term viability despite widespread recognition of dysfunction? This paper presents a theory-building synthesis that traces organizational and leadership theory to examine how dysfunction and resistance to change becomes normalized within organizational systems. Drawing on a systematic literature review spanning the early twentieth century to the present, the analysis traces six theoretical periods and shows how dominant theories shaped the conceptualization of leadership, accountability, and legitimate intervention. Paradoxically, as explanations for dysfunction became more sophisticated, the capacity to act on them narrowed. Across periods, scholarship generated increasingly sophisticated explanations for dysfunction, yet corrective insights were selectively enacted in ways that preserved existing decision-making structures. Synthesizing systems thinking with insights on political dynamics and stakeholder coordination, the analysis proposes that systems capable of rejecting decay require resource finality as binding constraint and evolutionary mechanisms linking accountability to long-term viability. The paper contributes a system-level explanation for persistent leadership dysfunction and highlights underutilized implications of systems thinking for organizational accountability under conditions of genuine constraint.

Toward a Balanced Perspective on Compassionate Leadership in Organizations.

MC
Bahriye Goren (Cornell University)
Compassionate leadership has gained increasing attention as organizations confront rising employee distress and uncertainty. However, existing research is concentrated largely in healthcare and educational settings, with relatively little examination of compassionate leadership in broader organizational and management contexts. As a result, the construct remains conceptually fragmented, unevenly measured, and weakly integrated into mainstream leadership theory. To address this gap, we conduct a systematic literature review of 89 peer-reviewed studies across management, psychology, healthcare, and education journals. We identify five dominant conceptualizations of compassionate leadership: as a dispositional trait, an action-oriented response process, a relational leadership practice, a systemic or organizational capability, and a dimension embedded within established leadership theories. We show how the dominance of care-oriented contexts and conceptual pluralism has limited theoretical clarity and measurement portability beyond caring professions. Building on this synthesis, we argue that compassionate leadership is inherently paradoxical, requiring leaders to balance care and performance, empathy and authority, and individual and organizational interests. We advance a Balanced Compassionate Leadership framework that integrates empathy with emotional regulation, self-compassion, and intentional action. We conclude by outlining a research agenda that prioritizes theory development, measurement, and the roles of gender and culture in shaping compassionate leadership in general organizational contexts.
