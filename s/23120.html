The Impact of Political Differences on Perceptions of Co-Worker Trustworthiness

CM
Edward C. Tomlinson (University of South Florida)
Political polarization has become a significant workplace issue. Prior research has established that differences in political affiliation, beliefs, and activities damage interpersonal trust. However, no prior work has explored which dimensions of a co-worker’s trustworthiness are most implicated in the context of a political dispute, which must be understood in any attempt to repair trust. In Study 1, participants described a political conflict they encountered with a co-worker and provided ratings of co-worker trustworthiness. In Study 2, participants were presented with a hypothetical scenario that involved evaluating a resume for a newly hired co-worker who differed (or not) from them politically. Study 3 extended this approach by including comparisons with politically similar co-workers. As predicted, across all studies ratings of values congruence and benevolence are lower than ratings of ability and behavioral integrity. Studies 2 and 3 also demonstrated that in the context of political differences, all forms of trustworthiness decrease but values congruence and benevolence suffer the most. This suggests that interventions aimed at repairing trust in polarized workplaces should focus on these latter two dimensions.

How We Get to Yes Matters! The Influence of Negotiation Tactics on Interpersonal Trust

CM
Seola Kim (University of Minnesota); Pri Pradhan Shah (University of Minnesota); Stephen L. Jones
Trust is widely recognized as essential for effective negotiation processes and outcomes, yet most negotiation research has examined trust primarily as an antecedent rather than as an outcome of negotiation. We shift this perspective by conceptualizing trust as an emergent outcome shaped by negotiation interactions and behaviors. Using a longitudinal field experiment with pre- and post-negotiation trust assessments toward negotiation partners and non-negotiating classmates, we employ a difference-in-differences (DiD) approach to isolate the causal effects of engaging in negotiation and of distinct negotiation behaviors on post-negotiation trust. We examine overall perceived trustworthiness as well as its core dimensions—ability, benevolence, and integrity. Extending beyond the traditional focus on integrative and distributive tactics, we introduce interpersonal behaviors as a third category of negotiation behavior. Results show that engaging in negotiation increases overall trust perceptions of one’s partner. Integrative behaviors are positively associated with perceived ability, distributive behaviors are negatively associated with perceived integrity, and interpersonal behaviors are positively associated with perceived benevolence and integrity. By leveraging a DiD methodology to strengthen causal inference in a naturalistic setting, this study clarifies how specific negotiation behaviors differentially shape distinct facets of trust, offering both theoretical insight and practical guidance for navigating trust development when creating and claiming value simultaneously.

Distant and Distrusting: Dominance, Trust, and Distance in Leader-Follower Relationships

CM
Laurel Detert (University of Michigan); Richard Ronay; Charleen R. Case (HEC Paris)
Leaders vary significantly in the physical distance they enact from their followers, a choice which has significant consequences for their reputation and relationships. Leaders’ physical distance choices are often guided by their orientation towards gaining and maintaining their leadership position, like their dominance orientation. Although a dominance-oriented leader may want to use physical proximity to demonstrate the characteristic physical aggression and formidability of this leadership orientation, we argue that dominance-oriented leaders may be more fearful of potential opposition from dissatisfied followers and instead proactively employ physical distance as a mitigating tactic for this anticipated threat. Thus, we argue and show that dominance-oriented leaders, in anticipation of potential opposition from followers, enact greater physical distance from their followers, in part due to their low trust in their followers that stems from a chronic state of anticipated threat. We also propose that these distance choices have important consequences, leading followers to, in turn, have low trust towards their leader and less interest in a close leader-follower relationship. Across three empirical studies, we unpack a novel antecedent of leader-follower physical distance that stems from the psychological underpinnings of dominance-oriented leaders and directly link leaders’ distance choices to follower reaction by integrating dual-strategies theory into the leadership distance literature.

The Trickle-Down Effect of Supervisors’ Felt Trust: Passing Down Benefits or Relieving Burdens?

CM
Krisna Bonilla (Toulouse I Capitole University)
This study investigates supervisors’ felt trust from managers and examines whether and why it trickles into their trusting behaviors toward subordinates. Drawing on a qualitative study in a high-risk gold mining and processing company in the Philippines, we analyzed 30 semi-structured interviews with supervisors, complemented by non-participant observations and archival materials. Our findings show that the trickle-down process is enabled by an interacting configuration of mechanisms - role efficacy, leader role legitimacy, challenge and hindrance demands, and role model influence from manager - that operate in combination, rather than as independent patterns. This study advances trust, felt trust, leadership, and trickle-down research by foregrounding supervisors’ dual role as both leader and follower, as well as trustee and trustor. It explains how felt trust from managers is translated into trusting behavior toward subordinates, offering a more holistic account of trust dynamics in contexts where risk-taking is essential at work.
