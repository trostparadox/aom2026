The Architecture of Ideas: An Integrative Model of Ideation Processes for Problem-Solving

MOC
Wieland Freyer (RWTH Aachen University)
This paper integrates diverse perspectives into a cognitive microfoundational framework that explains how ideators generate creative ideas, how they form meaningful linkages between personal needs and available solutions, and why some ideators produce more creative outcomes than others under identical conditions. While existing creativity research emphasizes who is creative (traits, motivation, expertise), this paper illuminates how creative ideas take shape through the cognitive processes by which ideators navigate their subjective need and solution landscapes. I propose that creative advantage stems not only from inherent attributes but from need-solution anthropization, the active modification of ideators’ mental landscapes through vertical adjustments (shaping) and horizontal reconfigurations (shifting). Shaping recalibrates perceived cost-benefit ratios, enhancing usefulness; shifting brings distant information into cognitive proximity, fostering novelty. I integrate two fundamental ideation pathways, structured problem-solving (Type I) and unstructured, intuitive linking (Type II), within a unified landscape model, demonstrating that both paths benefit from complementary anthropization mechanisms. By explicating the cognitive architecture underlying knowledge recombination, I advance the understanding of ‘innovation architecture’ and reveal serendipity not as fortuitous chance but as an optimizable outcome of deliberate landscape modification. This framework offers organizations and individuals strategic leverage to systematically enhance creative ideation beyond conventional design thinking approaches.

Negative Framing and Payment Compliance for Public Services: Evidence from a Field Experiment

MOC
Chelsea Tianyi Che (UCLA Anderson School of Management); Juanjuan Meng (Peking University); Rui Zhou (Pennsylvania State U); PENG CHANG (YUCHANG REAL ESTATE GROUP.)
This study investigates how positive and negative framings of objectively equivalent peer information regarding payment history influence household payment behavior concerning property management fees. It employs a field experiment involving 2,669 households in China within the context of payment compliance for public services. The findings indicate that negative framing of information about the non-payment rate is more effective, while positive framing of the payment rate shows no significant impact. A heterogeneity analysis of payment status reveals that negative framing exerts a stronger motivating effect on both early and late payers, but it tends to undermine compliance behavior among on-time payers. Overall, the considerable potential for improving the behavior of late payers makes negative framing more effective across the entire population.

Performance Resets Reduce Social Comparisons and Cheating in Competitive Environments

MOC
Boyuan Ju (The Ohio State University); Sarah Doyle (The University of Arizona); Robert B. Lount (The Ohio State University)
Competitive environments can motivate individuals, but they also can increase unethical behavior. In this paper, we examine social comparison concerns as a key mechanism and investigate how altering the structure of performance feedback can help to mitigate cheating. Drawing on social comparison theory and emerging research on performance resets, we hypothesize that in highly competitive settings, performance resets reduce social comparison concerns, thereby decreasing individuals’ downstream cheating behavior. We test our hypotheses across two experiments, each employing distinct manipulations of competition and behavioral measures of cheating. In both studies, performance resets attenuated the effect of heightened competition on cheating by reducing social comparison concerns. This work highlights the importance of temporal feedback design in shaping unethical behavior while offering a novel approach to mitigating the pressures of competition.

Too Much of a Good Thing: How Ventures Navigate Advice Overload in an Accelerator

MOC
Elana Feldman (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Gerardo Okhuysen (University of California Irvine)
Nascent ventures face a range of challenges, including uncertainty, novelty, and inexperience. Although advice is often critical to overcoming these challenges, large amounts of advice can be difficult for those on the receiving end. Through an ethnographic study of an accelerator – a relatively new organizational form that, by design, exposes participating ventures to an unusually high volume of guidance – we examine how ventures navigate advice overload. Our inductive analysis of over 400 hours of observation, 57 semi-structured interviews, and more than 70 documents revealed two distinct patterns of advice use. One group of ventures exhibited an effective pattern, advice evaluation and integration, that allowed them to adopt helpful guidance iteratively during the accelerator. We found that this pattern was enabled by three advice overload mitigation practices: continual priority (re)anchoring, selective activity engagement, and purposeful advice targeting. In contrast, the second group displayed a pattern of ineffective advice use, automatic advice rejection or acceptance, wherein ventures either discarded or incorporated advice without thoughtful deliberation. While the first group made considerable progress during the accelerator by incorporating advice, the second group made minimal progress. Our findings contribute to scholarship on advice overload, advice as a social process, and venture-level dynamics in accelerators.

The Unintended Moral Consequences of Servant Leadership

MOC
Yu Rong (Grand Valley State University); Jinyu Hu; Kathryn A. Coll; Haoying Xu (Stevens Institute of Technology); Garret Ridinger (University of Nevada, Reno); James Leonhardt (University of Nevada, Reno)
In this paper, we examine why followers engage in unethical pro-organizational behavior (UPOB) while led by servant leaders. Challenging the assumption that unethical conduct stems solely from self-interest, this paper highlights how morally oriented leadership can unintentionally foster ethical transgressions. Drawing on leadership moralization theory, we propose that servant leadership influences followers’ UPOB through two pathways: an individual-focused path and a group-focused path. Followers’ endorsement of specific moral foundations shapes how they moralize leaders’ behaviors, which in turn motivates value-consistent behaviors even when such behaviors are ethically questionable. Evidence from a multi-wave study supports the group-focused pathway while revealing unexpected patterns in the individual-focused pathway. The findings advance follower-centric theory by elucidating cognitive mechanisms underlying UPOB, empirically testing leadership moralization theory, and revealing unintended moral perils of servant leadership.
