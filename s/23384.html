When Failure Lingers: Accumulated Aspiration Shortfalls and Financial Misreporting in Family Firms

STR
Xiayan Dai (Waseda University)
Financial misreporting is often preceded by prolonged periods of underperformance, yet existing research largely explains financial misreporting through contemporaneous incentives or isolated performance shortfalls. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm and the fraud triangle, this study conceptualizes failure as an accumulated and temporally weighted experience and introduces accumulated aspiration shortfall as a mechanism linking persistent underperformance to financial misreporting. Using a longitudinal panel of Japanese family firms, I find that accumulated aspiration shortfalls increase the likelihood of financial misreporting. However, this relationship varies systematically with how families are positioned within the firm. Family influence through management weakens the effect of accumulated failure, whereas ownership-based family influence strengthens it. By highlighting the joint roles of temporal performance feedback and governance structure, this study offers a novel perspective on the ethical consequences of lingering failure in family firms.

From Trigger to Trajectory: Cumulative Events and the Temporal Dynamics of Decision-Making

STR
Lonneke Weterings (Rotterdam School of Management); Luca Berchicci
Organizations routinely face negative performance feedback that prompts decision-makers’ attention and action. Responses to performance feedback vary depending on constraints of bounded rationality, the presence of multiple underlying goals, and the locus of search. Despite extensive research on performance feedback, the theory remains agnostic about the underlying causes of performance feedback deviations. While existing research implicitly emphasizes punctuated events as triggers, we argue that cumulative events, which unfold gradually and exert reoccurring pressure, are equally critical yet underexplored. We introduce time-based theorizing into the Behavioral Theory of the Firm to show how cumulative events shape adaptive responses and how they can lock organizations into recurring feedback cycles. We develop a typology illustrating how decision-makers’ temporal orientation and organizational goal prioritization jointly shape responses to cumulative events, potentially leading to self-reactivating feedback loops. By focusing on the impact of cumulative events, our model explains how organizations become locked into recurring cycles of feedback and respond, offering new insights into the dynamics of organizational adaptation.

Revisiting Uncertainty Avoidance in the Behavioral Theory of the Firm

STR
Feifei Jiang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Zhi Cao (The University of Arizona); Donghan Wang (Communication University of China)
Behavioral theory of the firm suggests that firms avoid environmental uncertainty by using short-term responses to short-term feedback. We extend this logic by suggesting that, paradoxically, the very process firms adopt responses to performance feedback to avoid uncertainty is shaped by that same uncertainty. When the uncertainty is high, the outcomes of the chosen response become less predictable, raising concerns about whether it will restore firm performance. We argue that firms manage these outcome uncertainties of the chosen response by adjusting how they implement it. Focusing on the smartphone industry, we examine how firms implement new product introductions (NPI) in response to performance shortfalls as the industry evolves from early to mature stages. We find that, in the early stage of the industry (high uncertainty), firms use an experimental approach to implement NPI, reducing the novelty rather than the number of new products. As the industry matures and uncertainty declines, firms shift to a focused-differentiation approach, reducing the number of products rather than their innovativeness. These findings illuminate how boundedly rational firms manage uncertainty while using responses to feedback to avoid uncertainty. They also highlight implementation as a critical aspect of addressing performance shortfalls.

Adapting to Paradigm Shifts: Performance Feedback and Innovation of Transformative Technologies

STR
Bilian Ni Sullivan (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Jingxuan Lu (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology)
We examine the role of paradigm stage in organizational adaptation to transformative technologies. Integrating technological paradigm theory with the behavioral theory of the firm, we develop a hierarchical contingency framework in which technological paradigm stage serves as the primary scope condition on performance-feedback dynamics, while public discourse acts as a second-order moderator. We argue that in the rapidly evolving field of artificial intelligence (AI) as am exemplar of transformative technology, performance shortfalls consistently suppress AI innovation, but paradigm maturity progressively attenuates this deterrent. Moreover, both the valence (positive vs. negative sentiment) and content of public discourse moderate such relationships. Positive sentiment amplifies AI innovation among low performers only in early, ferment stages, whereas negative sentiment exerts its strongest suppressing influence when the paradigm has matured. Further, the complexity of discourse content exerts the strongest impact in the initial ferment stage. Analyses of all U.S. publicly listed firms from 2001-2019 confirm our predictions. This study makes significant contributions to the behavioral theory of the firm by establishing paradigm stage as a foundational factor that affects multiple canonical predictions of the behavioral theory of the firm and illustrating organizational adaptation to transformative technologies as fundamentally contingent on the underlying technological regime and the evolving public discourse that surrounds it.
