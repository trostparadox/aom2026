Session overview

This session examines how gender inequality is sustained through everyday organizational processes that shape women’s advancement, safety, and access to opportunity. The papers explore gendered self-regulation in professional networking, organizational responses to sexual harassment, and the career consequences of seeking family support. Together, they show how informal norms, support structures, and workplace practices influence whether women can build connections, remain protected, and access career development on equitable terms.

Through the Glass Network: Gendered Self-Regulation in Professional Networking

DEI
Ruth Mateos De Cabo (Universidad San Pablo CEU); Ricardo Gimeno; Gabriela Contreras (Radboud University Nijmegen); Miryam Martinez-Martinez (CEU San Pablo University)
Purpose. This study examines how professional networking reproduces gender inequality. Drawing on Feminist Institutional Theory, it investigates how informal institutions shape the behavioral consequences of internal orientations via gendered self-regulation, constraining women’s engagement in career-enhancing networks. Design. Using a sequential mixed-methods design, the study combines a multigroup structural equation model on a matched sample with in-depth interviews with women managers. Networking is analyzed across four dimensions: maintaining contacts, socializing, professional activities, and internal visibility. Findings. Extroversion facilitates networking across all dimensions and genders. There is no gender difference in modesty or moral conflict, yet they operate as gender-contingent and domain-specific constraints. Moral conflict limits women’s maintaining contacts and internal visibility, while modesty constrains maintaining contacts and participation in professional activities. Socializing remains largely gender-neutral. These effects are result of anticipatory self-regulation, including backlash avoidance, meritocratic ideals, authenticity concerns, and self-efficacy doubts, reflecting responses to gendered legitimacy and reputational costs. Originality. The study moves beyond deficit-based explanations of women’s networking behavior by disentangling internal orientations from self-regulatory mechanisms through which they become behaviorally consequential. By demonstrating equality of inputs alongside inequality of effects and integrating Feminist Institutional Theory with a multidimensional mixed-methods analysis, it offers a domain-sensitive explanation of how informal institutions reproduce inequality through gendered self-regulation in career-enhancing networks.

How Organizations in Brazil Deal with Sexual Harassment Against Female Employees

DEI
Alice Oleto (Fundação Dom Cabral); Jussara Pereira (UNIVERSIDADE FEDERAL DE MINAS GERAIS - UFMG); Carlos Eduardo de Lima
Although research on sexual harassment at work has increased in recent years, limited attention has been given to how organizations themselves handle such cases. This paper addresses this gap by asking: How do Brazilian organizations deal with cases of sexual harassment experienced by their female employees? The study adopts a qualitative and exploratory research design grounded in a constructivist Grounded Theory approach. Data were collected over through in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 43 women active in the Brazilian labor market, working in diverse organizational contexts and hierarchical positions. The findings indicate that many organizations lack clear, transparent, and trustworthy mechanisms for handling harassment cases. Reporting channels are often poorly communicated or perceived as unsafe, which discourages reporting. Organizational responses frequently involve denial, victim-blaming, symbolic actions, or solutions that displace victims rather than address perpetrators. Even in organizations perceived as professional or egalitarian, sexual harassment tends to be treated as a normalized element of organizational life.

Beyond Stigma: How Family Support Seeking Shapes Gendered Access to Career Development

DEI
Hongsong Guo (Central University of Finance and Economics); Hong Deng (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Haiyang Liu (Nanyang Technological University, Singapore); Yating Gao (China University of Geosciences Beijing)
Prior research has predominantly attributed family-related career penalties to cognitive stigma. Although such overt stigma has become increasingly constrained, accumulating evidence suggests that career disadvantages associated with family involvement persist in more subtle forms. Integrating the theory of gendered organizations with social role theory, we conceptualize family support seeking as a prevalent, informal, and subtle expression of family involvement, which triggers decreased promotability through supervisor assignment of work opportunities and organizational visibility. Results from archival data (Study 1) and field survey study (Study 2) indicated that family support seeking is associated with reduced assignment of work opportunities (conditionally supported in Study 1 and fully supported in Study 2), which subsequently diminishes organizational visibility and promotability. Importantly, these negative career consequences are significantly stronger for women. Implications for career penalties and family-supportive supervisor behaviors were discussed.
