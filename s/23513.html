Architecture in Blockchain Systems: A Formal Model of Blockchain Design Paradoxes

TIM
Rui Yang (Tongji University)
Blockchain systems are routinely described as confronting “paradoxes” or “trilemmas,” most prominently security versus efficiency and interoperability versus usability. Existing accounts often treat these tensions as intrinsic tradeoffs, offering limited theoretical leverage over when particular architectural responses emerge and why hybrid architectures (e.g., layered systems) sometimes dominate. We develop a formal theory that explains blockchain architecture as an endogenous solution to paradoxical demands. Building on systems theory, we conceptualize two orthogonal architectural levers— separation (temporal vs. spatial) and decoupling (vertical vs. horizontal)—as foundational design principles for blockchain infrastructures. We model a protocol designer who selects an architecture to attract heterogeneous users and developers whose adoption decisions interact through cross-side complementarities. The model yields novel, testable propositions: (1) security primacy induces temporal separation, whereas efficiency primacy induces spatial separation; (2) interoperability primacy induces horizontal decoupling, whereas usability primacy induces vertical decoupling; (3) paradox primacy can arise endogenously from ecosystem complementarities, producing counterintuitive design choices; and (4) hybrid, layered architectures emerge as equilibrium responses when paradoxical demands are jointly high. We conclude by deriving boundary conditions and welfare implications, outlining a research agenda for organizational design and platform strategy in decentralized infrastructures.

Cultivating Match Quality in Platform Ecosystems: Balancing Openness and Curation

TIM
Joe N. Ploog (IE Business School)
Platform ecosystems face a common governance challenge: sustaining high-quality matches between end users and complement(or)s as they evolve. We theorize how two strategic choices jointly determine the curation a platform sponsor deploys. The first one is a fixed design choice—complement type— whether complements persist (stock) or expire (flow). The second one is an adjustable choice—platform openness—which affects the platform ecosystem’s user base and heterogeneity. Combining these choices into a two-by-two typology yields four distinct platform ideal types, each with its own properties and curation challenges. Taking an evolutionary view, we argue that as user numbers and heterogeneity increase, curation becomes more important to sustain high-quality matches. Further, the effective mix of curation strategies shifts from manual to algorithmic. The timing and extent of this shift differ: it happens earlier in open platform ecosystems, where variety increases more quickly, and it is more pronounced in flow platform ecosystems, where complements’ fleeting nature requires stronger reliance on algorithmic curation. We also derive asymmetric governance implications: in stock platform ecosystems, reducing openness yields larger gains in match quality; in flow platform ecosystems, increasing openness leads to smaller losses in match quality. Conceptually, we separate diffusion (network effects) from retention (match quality) and show how appropriate curation increases retention and depends on diffusion.

Narrative as Platform Governance:How Platform Owners Build Trustworthiness When Entering Complement

TIM
Wen-Long Wang (National Cheng Kung University); TSENG YU-SHAN
This study draws on NVIDIA’s entry into the graphics card market to examine how a platform owner constructs trustworthiness through annual-report narrative strategies before and after entering a complementor market. The findings show that platform owners dynamically reconfigure their narratives as governance conditions change. Specifically, prior to entering the graphics card market, NVIDIA primarily adopted a calculative-trust perspective, disclosing foreseeable and verifiable information—such as technology roadmaps, product delivery cadence, and competitive rules—to reduce complementors’ uncertainty about returns and risks. After entering the market, NVIDIA shifted toward a relational-trust perspective, emphasizing a long-term partnership vision, value co-creation, and partner support to sustain interdependence and stabilize ecosystem functioning. This study argues that trust should be understood as a form of social capital within platform governance that can be deliberately allocated and recalibrated. By doing so, it addresses the relative neglect of trust mechanisms in the literature on platform owner entry into complementor markets, highlighting how trust can help maintain cooperative boundaries and governance effectiveness under heightened coopetitive tensions and uncertainty, thereby extending existing governance theory’s understanding of the functions and roles of trust.

Pathways to Performance: Configurations for Integration in Decentralized Autonomous Organization

TIM
Ying-Ying Hsieh (Imperial Business School, Imperial London); Giorgia Sampo' (University of Southern Denmark); Oliver Baumann (University of Southern Denmark); Oliver Alexy (Technical University of Munich)
As a novel kind of decentralized online form of organizing (DOFO), DAOs – decentralized autonomous organizations – seek to further extend the opportunities for participation that members have. Classic DOFOs such as open source, Wikipedia, or Anonymous often centralize key aspects of governance to ensure easier integration of the efforts of their distributed members. In contrast, DAOs allow members to generate, filter, and vote on proposals that may actively redesign the participation architecture in which governance rules are enshrined—with winning proposals being executed automatically on the blockchain. We seek to study to what degree these crucial decisions about participation in governance are truly decentralized and how DAOs may overcome potentially resulting integration challenges to still attain high performance. Following an abductive logic, we deploy fsQCA on a major dataset of DAOs to study which configurations of governance options on proposal generation, filtering, and voting allow DAOs to perform well. Surprisingly, we find that most high-performing DAOs still show high centralization, in that members either self-select into predefined participation architectures or in that those architectures restrict participation. We also show that even more permissive designs see less member interaction over time, and that highly permissive designs underperform. To the burgeoning literature on DOFOs, we suggest that DAOs may represent efficiency gains in governance, rather than a truly decentral, innovation-producing organization design.

The Privacy Trap: How Apple’s App Tracking Transparency Policy Prevents Developers from Multihoming

TIM
Tianxi Yang (Tsinghua University); Pengxiang Zhang (Peking University); Huiyi Litan (Renmin University of China); Ke Rong (Tsinghua University); Jiachen Liu (Tsinghua University)
Prior research on platform governance emphasizes that stricter control typically drives complementors away, while open, incentive-based governance attracts participation. We challenge this view by proposing that tighter control can also lock in complementors, plausibly by preventing them from porting platform-specific capabilities to rival platforms. Using Apple’s App Tracking Transparency (ATT) policy as a quasi-natural experiment, we analyze the multihoming activities of 9,694 app developers who previously focused solely on the iOS platform. The results show that ATT significantly reduced the likelihood for single-homing iOS developers to enter into the Android platform, reinforcing platform lock-in. Moreover, the lock in effect is stronger among developers lacking platform-agnostic capabilities, particularly those generating limited direct revenues from end users and those have few experiences in leveraging boundary resources. Additional tests reveal that lock-in makes developers more passive and reduces their subsequent innovation output over time. The study contributes to research on platform governance by showing how privacy-based data control unexpectedly traps complementors by preventing capability transfer.
