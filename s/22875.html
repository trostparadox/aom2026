Working in a State of Time Scarcity: Daily Time Poverty and its Behavioral Consequences

OB
Jun Yang (Tongji University); Bin Wang (Monash University); Jian Liang (Tongji University); Feifan Yang (Soochow University); Baihe Song (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Qian Jing (Beijing Normal University)
The escalating volatility and fragmented nature of modern work have made daily time poverty an increasingly pervasive threat to organizational effectiveness. While the toll of chronic scarcity is well-documented, we lack a precise understanding of how these daily temporal deficits dynamically impair task execution and who can effectively buffer such strains. Drawing on scarcity theory, we propose that daily time poverty heightens cognitive weariness, which in turn fosters procrastination and shortcut-taking at work. We also examine the moderating role of two work planning styles, namely, time management planning (TMP) and contingency planning (CP). Across two daily diary studies with independent samples, multilevel path analyses supported our model. Results from Study 1 (N between = 143, N within = 1259) indicated that daily time poverty predicted higher cognitive weariness, which heightened subsequent shortcut behavior and procrastination. Study 2 (N between = 210, N within = 998) replicated these findings and further revealed cross-level moderation, such that TMP strengthened but CP weakened the link between time poverty and cognitive weariness. Finally, theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

Beyond Stress Reduction: The Unique Role of Psychological Flexibility in Maintaining Performance

OB
Shaline Kulcsar (University of Mannheim); Torsten Biemann (University of Mannheim); Silja Bellingrath
Employees increasingly face acute, unavoidable stressors in modern work environments, yet existing psychological resources such as emotion regulation, mindfulness, and resilience primarily focus on buffering acute stress reactivity. This study proposes psychological flexibility (PsyFlex) as a complementary mechanism that enables individuals to persist in effective task engagement aligned with personally meaningful goals despite ongoing stress, thereby extending beyond established resources, whereas its counterpart, psychological inflexibility (PsyInflex), is hypothesized to undermine functioning under stress. Drawing on a dual-path stress framework, we distinguish (1) mechanisms that buffer stressor-induced strain (stressor -> stress) and (2) mechanisms that pre-serve performance under strain (stress -> performance). We tested these pathways in a laboratory experiment (N = 281) using a between-subjects design with a stress induction versus control condition, a multicomponent social-evaluative stressor, and a time-pressured performance task, integrating physiological indicators of acute stress (heart rate variability; RMSSD). Results show limited support for buffering effects on acute stress reactivity, with only reappraisal moderating physiological stress responses (H1). In contrast, PsyFlex uniquely preserved task performance under physiological stress (H2): under high stress, performance declined for low-flexibility individuals whereas it maintained or tended to improve among highly flexible individuals. No significant effect emerged for PsyInflex, nor for emotion regulation, mindfulness, or resilience. These findings position PsyFlex as a distinct resource by reframing performance under stress as a matter of sustained task engagement rather than internal state regulation, notably in high-pressure work contexts, with key theoretical and practical implications for organizations.

A Double-Edged Sword: How Workaholism Shapes Observers’ Motivation and Effectiveness

OB
Yingni Cai (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen); Xiaomin Xu (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen); Xuanyi Liu (University of California Los Angeles); Xinxin Chen (Macao University of Tourism); Yatian Zhou (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen)
Although previous research has focused on workaholism as an individual trait or work habit, little is known about how exposure to coworkers’ workaholic behavior affects employees’ motivation and subsequent work outcomes. Drawing on social information processing theory, we conceptualize coworker workaholism as a social information cue that shapes work passion via shallow and deep processing, linking to psychological and behavioral outcomes. Using an online scenario experiment and a multi-source, multi-wave field survey, we find that coworker workaholism increases mental fatigue and undermines task performance via obsessive passion, but decreases mental fatigue and enhances task performance via harmonious passion. These effects depend on employees’ attributions: when coworker workaholism is interpreted as driven by impression management motives, observers are more likely to develop obsessive passion; when interpreted as driven by intrinsic motives, observers are more likely to develop harmonious passion. By revealing the motivational mechanisms and attributional boundaries of coworker workaholism, our research advances theory on workaholism and social information processing, and underscores the importance of considering observers’ responses.

The Resilience Trap: How a Celebrated Capability Masks Precarity in the UK Music Industry

OB
Olena Khlystova (University of Reading); Yelena Kalyuzhnova (Henley Business School); Anna De Amicis (Henley Business School, University of Reading, UK)
This study challenges dominant assumptions about organizational resilience by examining how music practitioners navigated the COVID-19 crisis in the United Kingdom. While the music industry is often portrayed as inherently resilient due to its creative and flexible workforce, our qualitative case study of 28 music professionals reveals a more complex picture. We aim to resolve the paradox between the theoretical portrayal of creative industries representatives as inherently resilient and their actual vulnerability and fragility during crises. Drawing on an interpretive framework of cognitive, behavioral, and contextual resilience capabilities, we perform our analysis in an abductive mode, allowing surprising accounts to guide us as we identify three novel interpretive layers of organizational resilience not known before, namely, affirmed, challenged, and resisted resilience which portrays it as a new phenomenon. We find that resilience, often interpreted as adaptive, frequently functions as a moral imperative that obscures systemic precarity. Musicians recounted resilience not only as a coping strategy, but also as a burden, framing it as a form of “individualized responsibility” masking policy failure. By interrogating the “dark side” of resilience in a sector presumed to embody and live it, we offer a new understanding of resilience as narrative, performance, and resistance.
