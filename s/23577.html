Alignment Without Agreement: Materializing Values in Innovation Ecosystems Facing Grand Challenges

TIM
Luca Vadacca (University of Twente); Gijsbert van de Waerdt (University of Twente)
Innovation ecosystems are commonly defined as alignment structures organized around a focal value proposition. This view, however, underspecifies coordination in grand-challenge settings where actors mobilize multiple, legitimate, and partly incommensurable values. We address this gap by asking how ecosystem actors negotiate, align, and materialize overlapping value propositions over time without converging on a single notion of value. We develop a process theory of value proposition materialization based on a longitudinal qualitative study of a Dutch public–private innovation program for flood-protection infrastructure. Drawing on 29 interviews and extensive documentary data, we conceptualize materialization as trace-based value qualification: the cumulative work through which actors qualify and re-qualify value claims into propositions that become decision-consequential at successive commitment thresholds while remaining open to later contestation. We identify four mechanisms (stacking, braiding, rebinding, and redistributing) that explain how ecosystems progress under persistent value pluralism. Our findings reconceptualize alignment as temporary compatibility among commitments rather than convergence on a focal proposition, and theorize materialization as an ongoing, contested accomplishment. We advance innovation ecosystem theory by explaining how coordination remains possible when evaluative disagreement is durable, and we offer practical guidance for orchestrators and policymakers working in ecosystems operating under public scrutiny.

Collaborative Governance in Hybrid Organizations: Managing Multiple Logics for Sustainability

TIM
Xiaohua Li (Northwest University); Niraj Kumar (University of Essex); Lun Li (Beijing Normal University)
As societal grand challenges intensify, organizations anchored in a single institutional logic increasingly struggle to maintain stakeholder legitimacy. While research has primarily explored the governance of dual institutional logics, the mechanisms for managing complex institutional pluralism remain under-theorized. This study investigates how hybrid organizations balance multiple institutional logics through collaborative governance. Drawing on a longitudinal case study of an Electronic Incubator in China, we track the evolution of five logics: state, academic, professional, business, and community. Our findings reveal that such organizations encounter escalating governance tension arising from organizational positioning, bilateral relationships, and multilateral dynamics. To cope with these challenges, the incubator adopted a collaborative governance approach that integrates internal structural adaptation, paired institutional innovation, and the alignment of collective goals among diverse actors. This study extends hybrid organization research by moving beyond dual-logics frameworks and demonstrating how collaborative governance enables organizations to navigate pluralistic institutional environments.

Diversity-Performance Paradox in Mission-Oriented Grand Challenges

TIM
Lihong Qian (Portland State University); I-Chen Wang (Suffolk University)
This study examines how the initial design of inter-organizational partnerships shapes their ability to progress technology in mission-oriented grand challenges, revealing a core diversity–performance paradox. Drawing on the micro-foundations of dynamic capabilities, we argue that technology progression depends on a partnership’s capacity to sense opportunities, mobilize resources, and reconfigure its asset base. We theorize that five ex-ante design characteristics influence these capabilities asymmetrically. Using data from the EU Clean Hydrogen Partnership, we show that knowledge-based diversity -- differences in market maturity and technological experience -- enhances progression, while governance-related diversity -- institutional and financial contribution heterogeneity -- significantly undermines it. Ecosystem experience diversity has no effect. These findings demonstrate that partnerships benefit from diverse knowledge inputs but require homogeneous governance structures to convert diversity into technological advancement.

How Hybrid Logics Shape and Constraint Social Innovation in the Field of Energy Communities

TIM
Silvia Rita Sedita; Giuseppe Danese (University of Padua); Amir Maghssudipour (University of Padua)
This paper advances research on hybrid organizing and social innovation by examining how institutional complexity structures the trajectories of grassroots initiatives. Drawing on a systematic review of 172 peer-reviewed studies on energy communities (ECs)— a domain in which the tensions of hybrid organizing for social innovation are particularly visible—we synthesize dispersed critical insights into a framework that identifies three structural tensions that shape hybrid social innovations: structural rigidity rooted in path-dependent infrastructures and regulatory regimes; organizational vulnerability stemming from financial precarity and limited managerial capacity; and asymmetric inclusion emerging from persistent knowledge–power hierarchies. These tensions give rise to two broader counter-narratives—reproduction and symbolic accommodation—that challenge optimistic depictions of grassroots participation within socio-technical transitions. By integrating social innovation and hybrid organizing perspectives, the paper advances theory on how hybrid initiatives are simultaneously enabled and constrained by dominant institutional logics. The framework offers implications for understanding organizational responses to grand challenges and for designing more transformative and equitable innovation pathways.
