Cultural Pathways to Sustainability: How Organizational Cultures Shape Firms’ ESG Performance

ONE
Marianna Delegach (Sapir Academic College); Tal Katz-Navon (Reichman University); Dana Rachel Vashdi (University of Haifa)
Understanding how organizational culture shapes firms’ environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance is essential for advancing effective sustainability management. Culture reflects shared values and norms that shape how firms enact ESG principles. Drawing on the Competing Values Framework and Stakeholder Theory, we argue that organizational cultural orientations guide how organizations interpret and prioritize stakeholder expectations, thereby shaping performance across ESG domains. Using a unique multi-source dataset of 468 Fortune 500 firms (2015–2023), integrating over 500K Glassdoor reviews with external ESG ratings, we find that distinct cultural profiles foster different ESG outcomes. Environmental performance improves under hierarchical cultures when supported by financial resources, whereas market cultures are associated with negative environmental outcomes. Social performance is strengthened by adhocracy and hierarchy cultures, and governance performance improves under adhocracy and market cultures. Implications for firms’ balanced cultural orientations for superior ESG performance are discussed.

How Do Firms Respond to ESG Risk Exposure? Evidence from Chinese Firms

ONE
Junyi Feng (Central South University); Shinwari Riazullah (Central South University)
This study examines how firms respond to ESG risk exposure using a sample of Chinese A-share listed firms from 2009 to 2024. Departing from the prevailing assumption in the literature that firms primarily engage in substantive adjustments, we distinguish between substantive and symbolic responses when firms face legitimacy pressure arising from ESG risk exposure. Using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) machine learning models to identify the key determinants of firms’ response choices and to support causal identification, we find that ESG risk exposure significantly increases firms’ symbolic responses. Furthermore, our moderation analysis reveals counterintuitive findings: firms with stronger green innovation capabilities and those led by CEOs with stronger early-life Confucian cultural imprints exhibit a greater tendency toward symbolic responses. This study sheds light on firms’ actual responses to ESG pressure in the Chinese context and reveals that, under heightened institutional pressure, organizational resources and cultural values may be redirected toward legitimacy repair rather than substantive adjustment, offering practical implications for firms and policymakers in emerging economies.

Institutional Openness and Corporate ESG: Evidence from a Staggered Reform in China

ONE
LI WEN (Renmin University of China); Yinghan Zhao (Renmin University of China)
This study examines whether place-based liberalization can reshape corporate sustainability behavior. We exploit the staggered rollout of China’s Pilot Free Trade Zones (FTZs) as a quasi-natural experiment and link policy adoption to a firm-year panel of publicly listed companies from 2009 to 2024. Using difference-in-differences designs with rich fixed effects and matching-based validation, we find that FTZ designation is associated with a significant improvement in firms’ ESG performance, with effects that strengthen over time. Event-time analyses show no differential pre-trends and reveal a delayed but persistent post-adoption trajectory, consistent with gradual institutional and organizational adjustment. We further explore two channels through which liberalization influences ESG upgrading. First, FTZs increase firms’ exposure to foreign capital and international governance norms, strengthening external monitoring and encouraging ESG improvements. Second, FTZ reforms ease financing frictions, enabling firms to undertake sustainability investments that are otherwise constrained by limited access to capital. Heterogeneity analyses suggest stronger effects among firms with greater absorptive capacity and market-based incentives. Overall, the findings highlight an underexplored pathway through which openness-oriented institutional reforms can generate non-financial returns by changing corporate behavior.

AMC Configurations, Causal Asymmetry, and ESG Improvement in Heavily Polluting Firms

ONE
Siying Chen (Nanjing University Business School); Jie Zhang
Improving ESG performance in heavily polluting industries is pivotal for a credible green transi-tion, yet prior research seldom explains how external pressures, managerial motives, and organiza-tional resources jointly shape ESG outcomes. Drawing on the Awareness–Motivation–Capability (AMC) framework, we theorize ESG improvement as a strategic response process and test it in 703 Chinese A-share listed heavily polluting firms (2021). Combining Necessary Condition Anal-ysis (NCA) with fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), we find that no single an-tecedent is necessary for high ESG performance. Instead, high ESG emerges through three equifi-nal configurations: (1) high regulatory and stakeholder awareness coupled with gender-diverse leadership; (2) strong organizational capabilities (profitability, scale, and internal control) that am-plify the effect of external scrutiny; and (3) clear strategic motivation under powerful CEOs com-plemented by balanced awareness and capability conditions. Configurations producing non-high ESG differ systematically, revealing causal asymmetry. Further analyses indicate meaningful heter-ogeneity by ownership, region, and policy environment. These findings extend AMC to corporate sustainability, clarify when and how multiple drivers substitute or complement one another, and inform differentiated governance approaches for hard-to-abate sectors.

Does Policy Uncertainty Amplify the Effect of ESG Rating Uncertainty on Corporate Debt Maturity?

ONE
Jiangtao Ma
This study examines how ESG rating uncertainty affects the maturity structure of corporate debt among Chinese listed firms from 2018 to 2023. The results indicate that ESG rating uncertainty significantly reduces the proportion of long-term debt. Moreover, Economic Policy Uncertainty and Climate Policy Uncertainty amplify this negative effect on debt maturity, highlighting the importance of policy stability in shaping debt financing. Pillar-level analyses reveal that the environmental and governance components of ESG uncertainty are the main drivers of the reduction in debt maturity, while the social pillar is not statistically significant. Further analyses reveal that information asymmetry and default risk are the primary channels through which ESG rating uncertainty undermines firms’ ability to access long-term financing. Our findings provide novel evidence on how ESG evaluations interact with corporate financing decisions under uncertain policy environments.
