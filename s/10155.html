How Consumers Make Sense of Greenwashing: A Multi-Method Examination of Counterfactual Appeal

Authors: Shan-Huei Wang Tunghai University; Jung-Hua Chang National Sun Yat-Sen University; Hui-Ju Lai National Sun Yat-Sen University; Meng Chun Syu Tunghai University

Session overview

Greenwashing has become increasingly prevalent as firms adopt sustainability-oriented communication without corresponding actions. However, little is known about how consumers interpret such mixed sustainability signals. This research integrates counterfactual thinking theory and signaling theory through a four-study, multi-method design to examine how counterfactual messaging, brand signals, information source credibility, and ESG beliefs jointly shape consumer responses. Study 1 uses a randomized experiment (N = 421) to test whether upward counterfactual messages—such as regret-based appeals highlighting environmental harm—alter attitudes toward fast fashion. Results show significant declines in environmental attitude, satisfaction, recommendation, and purchase intention after exposure to counterfactual greenwashing. Study 2 (N = 421) examines whether brand signal strength (boutique, well-known, unbranded) moderates reactions. Greenwashing triggered the strongest negative shift for unbranded items, diminishing recommendation and purchase intention, even for boutique brands, revealing the boundary conditions of brand signaling. Study 3 (N = 423) investigates the role of information sources (advertiser, friend/family, NGO) and identifies significant brand × source interactions. Messages attributed to firms and advertisers intensified negative reactions, while peer and NGO sources buffered them. To enhance external validity, Study 4 uses structural equation modeling (SEM) with 559 consumers in Taiwan’s retail sector. Results show that governance and social ESG beliefs significantly increase purchase intention via trust and perceived value. Together, these studies contribute to sustainability communication and consumer decision-making by revealing how emotional regret, brand signaling, and source credibility jointly influence responses to both deceptive and authentic sustainability claims.
