Environmental Innovation amid Physical Climate Impacts: The Moderating Role of Climate Governance

TIM
Benedikt Hummel (University of Bonn); Christina Dienhart
Businesses face rising exposure to physical climate impacts while simultaneously pursuing environmental innovation to achieve sustainability and performance objectives. Prior research offers conflicting findings regarding whether – and under which conditions – heightened physical climate impacts constrain or stimulate environmental innovation, creating ambiguity for firms’ strategic responses. Drawing on the attention-based view, we reconcile these perspectives by theorizing a non-linear relationship between physical climate impact intensity and environmental innovation, moderated by firms’ climate governance. Using longitudinal data on S&P 500 firms from 2000 to 2022, combined with over 3,000 firm-year observations on climate-related disasters, we show that climate governance critically shapes this relationship. Firms with weak climate governance exhibit an inverse U-shaped relationship, whereby moderate physical climate impact intensity stimulates environmental innovation, but extreme intensity suppresses it. In contrast, strong climate governance transforms this relationship into a U-shape, enabling firms to sustain environmental innovation even under extreme intensity. Our study challenges prior assumptions by theorizing and documenting a non-linear, climate governance–contingent relationship between physical climate impact intensity and environmental innovation. In doing so, we emphasize attention-directing structures as a mechanism for sustaining vulnerable strategic objectives in discontinuous environments.

From Battery-Fire Fear to EV Loyalty in the Long Run: A Serial Mediation Model of Continuance

TIM
Sohaib Mustafa (Beijing University of Technology); Lin Han (Beijing University of Technology); Haoran Liu (Beijing University of Technology); Khalid Jamil (Beijing University of Technology)
Electric vehicles (EVs) are scaling rapidly, yet sustained use depends on how owners continuously evaluate safety and value. Drawing on the Value-Based Adoption Model (VAM), this study develops and tests an EV-specific continuance framework in which perceived risk (PR) and perceived fear of battery fires (PF) operate as cost-side safety perceptions that undermine trust in EV technology (TR), while perceived green value (PGV), perceived environmental responsibility (PER), and perceived self-efficacy (PSE) act as benefit-side beliefs that strengthen perceived value (PV). Using survey data from 905 Chinese EV owners for six months in three lags and covariance-based SEM with 10,000-resample bootstrapping, the results show that PR and PF strongly erode trust, PGV, PER, and PSE increase perceived value, and trust further enhances value. Perceived value is the dominant predictor of continued usage intention, while trust retains an additional direct effect. Critically, the study validates the core process contribution; both PR and PF reduce continuance through serial mediation, explaining substantial variance in continuance with excellent model fit. The findings reposition battery-fire fear as a structurally consequential driver of EV loyalty and clarify how safety perceptions translate into post-adoption disengagement through trust and value.

Green Innovation and Institutional Resilience in China’s Firms Under the COVID-19 Shock

TIM
Aqsa Bilal Hussain (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Jingchun SUN (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Dr. Muhammad Shaique (Sukkur Institute of Business Administration); Bilal HUSSAIN (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Green innovation is pivotal to China’s “ecological civilization” agenda, yet its role in fostering organizational resilience during systemic shocks remains underexplored. This study examines how directive legitimacy (alignment with state sustainability goals), indigenous cultural values of harmony and long-term orientation, and compositional innovation capabilities interact to shape firm responses in a state-guided economy. Using a balanced panel of 834 Chinese A-share firms (8,340 firm-year observations, 2015–2022) linked to 59,989 quality-adjusted green patents, we apply staggered difference-in-differences models and machine learning methods to capture non-linear effects of provincial lockdowns. Results reveal that pre-pandemic green innovation mitigates COVID-19-related declines in return on assets and Tobin’s Q, particularly in environmentally sensitive industries. The study advances legitimacy and dynamic capabilities theories by conceptualizing directive legitimacy and compositional capabilities as enablers of resilience in state-guided contexts. Practically, the findings inform the design of green industrial policies and highlight the value of integrating traditional cultural principles and green innovation strategies to enhance resilience during exogenous shocks.

Scientific Impact of Interdisciplinary Research During Crises

TIM
Murat Tarakci (IMD - International Institute for Management Development); Swagatam Sinha (Tilburg University); Juan Pablo Madiedo Montanez
Prior studies demonstrate that interdisciplinary research achieves higher scientific impact. We investigate whether interdisciplinary research maintains this acclaimed scientific impact during crisis periods. We argue that while interdisciplinary research often enhances scientific impact, crises create conditions of urgency, cognitive overload, and normative pressures that diminish this benefit during a crisis. Focusing on the academic papers published on coronaviruses prior to the COVID-19 pandemic and their scientific impact before and during the pandemic, we find that interdisciplinary research saw reduced citations during the pandemic, mainly due to a narrowed attention that reflects immediate need for specialized, actionable insights. We contribute toward understanding the dynamic relationship between interdisciplinary research and crises. This understanding emphasizes policies that balance crisis-specific with long-term scientific needs.

The Divergent Effects of Natural Versus Technological Disasters on Green Innovation

TIM
Trang Luong (University of Sydney Business School); Jarrod Paul Vassallo (University of Cambridge); Yeonji Seo (The University of Sydney)
Green innovation is increasingly recognized as an essential mechanism for mitigating the risks posed by complex natural and societal challenges. With the rising frequency of natural and technological disasters, it is crucial to understand how these shocks influence national innovation systems and how policies can enhance resilience. Existing research remains fragmented, often focusing on single events or specific disaster sub-categories with narrow temporal or geographic coverage. Addressing these gaps, this study investigates how natural versus technological disasters affect national-level green innovation. Specifically, it asks: (1) Do repeated shocks stimulate or suppress national green innovation outputs? (2) Are there nonlinear thresholds beyond which disaster frequency triggers tipping points in innovation outputs? Furthermore, (3) Do these effects differ between natural and technological disasters? The results reveal contrasting curvilinear patterns: natural disasters exhibit an inverted-U-shaped relationship with green innovation (i.e., a concave relationship), whereas technological disasters exhibit a consistently negative but gradually flattening nonlinear association (i.e., a convex relationship). Theoretically, the study advances event-oriented research by comparing shock types across institutional contexts and emphasizes the role of attribution in determining national green innovation. Practically, it offers insights for policymakers and business leaders to foster green innovation amid growing environmental and technological disruptions.
