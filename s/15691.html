Session overview

In recent years, major advances have been made in the field of biomedical sciences, such as CRISPR gene editing, personalized medicine, and gene therapies. These innovations are potentially groundbreaking not only in terms of addressing key challenges in treating prevalent diseases, but also in offering solutions for historically underrepresented medical conditions (e.g. rare diseases), as well as expanding our life expectancy in aggregate. Nevertheless, challenges abound in terms of unlocking the potential value of such technologies, such as disputes over intellectual property (IP) ownership of these technologies, uncertainties in the supplies of necessary complementary assets, as well as the broader questions relating to equity and resource allocation, e.g. which demographic groups may be benefitting or left behind from such innovations, cross-country disparities in terms of access to knowledge, etc. This symposium, now on its second iteration (having initially debuted in the AOM Annual Meetings last year), will assemble five studies that offer diverse perspectives in relation to this phenomenon. These studies are primarily co-authored by young scholars with interests and expertise in investigating questions surrounding life science innovations. The topics range from patent litigation in the CRISPR and PCR technologies (Zyontz), uncertainties in the supply for the markets for technology (Ceccagnoli, Higgins, and Hofmeister), NIH funding of rare disease research and impacts on the value of innovation and subsequent spillovers (Chandra), impacts of Medicare policy changes on the representation of elderly patients in clinical trials and pharmaceutical innovation (Kao and Oostrom), and the tracing of ‘paper trails’ from centuries-old knowledge on medicinal plants to contemporary biomedical innovation (Ryu and Yao). A primary objective of this symposium is for the audience to better discern the commonalities among the presenters’ diverse settings of study, which relate to the strategies by firms and scientists in managing the obstacles in commercializing promising research, resource constraints, as well as addressing the issues of representativeness and equity in terms of who get(s) to benefit from such innovation.
