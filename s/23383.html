How External Uncertainty Influences Women's Entry into Corporate Boards: A Real Options Perspective

STR
Qi Yu (University of Liverpool); Shubin Wu; Jordi Surroca (University of Liverpool)
Research relied on different theories to explain the appointment of women to boards of directors, but some evidence still escapes their predictions. Complementing past approaches, in this study we introduce the real options theory to explain how external uncertainty about gender equality movement trends influences firms’ decisions to appoint female directors. Using an international panel dataset of 8,054 firms across 66 countries from 2000 to 2022, our study renders support to our real options-based argument. Results show that firms are more likely to appoint female directors when external gender-equality trends are highly uncertain. This positive effect diminishes as existing female representation increases, indicating that uncertainty primarily drives initial, low-commitment appointments rather than large-scale structural reforms. Moreover, higher uncertainty encourages firms to appoint female directors through board expansion, rather than the replacement of other board members. Overall, the findings highlight female board appointments as a flexible strategic response to uncertainty, consistent with the logic of real options theory.

When Humor Misses the Goal: Contextual Boundaries of Executive Humor in Media Approval

STR
Sebastian Vetter (University of Passau); Tarek Bakali (University of Passau); Andreas Sebastian Konig (University of Passau)
We theorize and test the contextualized effects of executive humor on media approval—the favorability of journalists’ affective coverage of an organization. Integrating humor research, affect-as-information theory, and sociological accounts of journalism, we develop competing hypotheses: executive humor may increase approval by triggering positive emotions and emotional coping among journalists; however, it may also reduce approval, as journalists may perceive humor as a status threat and norm-violation and, in turn, experience negative emotions. We test our ideas using a novel machine learning approach to detect humor in 4,590 press conferences held by head coaches of German Bundesliga soccer teams and measuring media approval in 223,728 newspaper articles. Our data primarily supports the notion of executive humor as a status-threat against journalists: executive humor is associated with lower subsequent media approval, especially, when humor is more malign and when journalists-organization proximity is low. Yet, partially, our data also support views of humor as a coping mechanism, with positive marginal effects of executive humor under very poor organizational performance. Our study informs the emerging contextualized view of executive humor, especially by considering journalists’ unique evaluative context and by introducing a multimodal approach for studying executive humor in real-life settings.

The Attention Effects of Environmental Shareholder Activism on Green Corporate Venture Capital

STR
Jonas Jung (TU Dortmund University)
Climate change intensifies pressure on firms to adopt more sustainable practices, and shareholder activism is a prominent mechanism through which investors seek to influence corporate environmental behavior. Yet environmental shareholder proposals typically receive low voting support, raising questions about whether such activism drives meaningful strategic responses. While prior research documents effects on informational outcomes, evidence on firms’ strategic actions remains limited. We address this gap by examining how environmental shareholder activism affects firms’ engagement in green corporate venture capital (CVC), a substantive vehicle for accessing external environmental innovation. Drawing on the attention-based view of the firm, we argue that environmental shareholder activism increases green CVC investments and investigate how technological turbulence and top management team (TMT) size moderate this relationship. Using a longitudinal sample of U.S. public firms and zero-inflated negative binomial models, we find that environmental shareholder activism positively predicts green CVC investments. Technological turbulence strengthens this relationship, while TMT size diminishes it. Our study contributes to research on shareholder activism, green CVC, and the attention-based view by demonstrating how external stakeholder pressure shapes firms’ environmental innovation strategies and by specifying boundary conditions that influence responsiveness to activism.

How Does Performance Feedback Affect Corporate Prosocial Claims as a Novel Marketing Strategy?

STR
Yinghao Zhu (Tongji University); Jieru MIAO (Tongji University); Xincheng Wang (Tongji University)
Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm, this study investigates when firms strategically deploy prosocial claims—statements about their commitment to social goals beyond profit—as a marketing tool. Our findings reveal that firms that fall short of performance goals are more likely to use prosocial claims to attract socially conscious customers and boost sales. Conversely, firms meeting performance goals may limit such claims to avoid consequential risks, such as potential investor discontent and skepticism of a novel marketing strategy of this kind. We examine the moderating effects of the top management team’s marketing background and prior corporate social responsibility ratings. Using a longitudinal sample of 3,682 Chinese public firms from 2012 to 2019, we find that performance feedback and contextual factors influence firms’ prosocial claims. This research enriches the corporate social responsibility (CSR) literature and contributes to the financial performance–CSR debate.

Perceived Organizational Purpose and Pay Satisfaction: A Global Employee Panel Study

STR
Leonie Brill (Georg August University Göttingen); Michelle Lang (Georg August University Göttingen); Michael Wolff (Georg August University Göttingen)
Using a large-scale unique international panel dataset from a single-firm setting, we find a posi-tive association between perceived organizational purpose and employees’ satisfaction with pay. Drawing on social exchange and equity theory, we conceptualize purpose as an intangible bene-fit that heightens perceived fairness in employees’ input–output evaluations. The association between perceived organizational purpose and pay satisfaction is stronger when purpose is more relevant to employees (measured through reversed age and prosocial tendencies). However, the association is weaker when employees face larger negative deviations from market benchmark pay. Our findings extend equity-based models of pay satisfaction by incorporating purpose as a non-financial component of total rewards and by identifying when and for whom purpose shapes pay evaluations most. Thus, we contribute to the growing purpose literature by providing a large-scale quantitative study on employee-level effects in a global context and by advancing emerging debates on whether purpose complements or substitutes monetary compensation by specifying the conditions under which purpose can (and cannot) shape pay evaluations.
