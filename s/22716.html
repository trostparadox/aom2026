Labor Practices and Worker Retention in U.S. Dairies: Bringing Farmworkers’ Voices to the Front

OSCM
Veronica Haydee Villena (Arizona State University); Kathleen Sexsmith; Adrian A Barragan (DVM, MS, PhD, -)
The current farm labor shortage, mass deportation threat, and high employee turnover in the U.S. dairy sector pose a serious concern for producers. To run their 24/7 labor-intensive operations, dairy farmers rely heavily on a workforce that is increasing comprised of undocumented immigrant workers. While some scholars have identified how worker deportability, precarious employment conditions, and agricultural industry exemptions from basic labor rights shape these employment concerns, our research identifies a less visible, farm-specific factor: (mis)alignment between producers’ and farmworkers’ expectations regarding labor practices in the workplace. We conducted a two-stage qualitative study including both producers (n = 8) and workers from their farms (n = 136) to compare and contrast their perspectives. The results show that producers and farmworkers coincide in their views on (a) the absence of key basic human resources practices on dairy farms, (b) the struggles of managing a mixed workforce (U.S.-born and immigrant workers) in an equitable manner, and (c) the impact of language barriers. The results also reveal a few differences. Farmworkers’ top workplace concerns were the lack of periodic, bilateral communication as well as not feeling appreciated for their labor, whereas producers’ top concern was the uniqueness of dynamics within the immigrant workforce. Surprisingly, we found many similarities between U.S. and immigrant workers—both rank periodic, bilateral communication; worker appreciation; performance evaluation; training; and tool and machinery maintenance as the most pressing issues to be addressed to improve their work environment. The primary difference was that immigrant (compared to U.S.) workers add pay increases to their list of top concerns and enjoy fewer benefits. By including farmworkers’ voices in the identification and ranking of labor areas for improvement, we suggest that producers and relevant stakeholders (e.g., dairy brands, cooperatives and extension educators) collaborate and develop tailored programs to tackle the identified areas that are recognized by both producers and farmworkers as important for improving the work environment. We provide several avenues for a future research agenda as well as implications for producers, cooperatives, downstream buyers, and policy makers.

Beyond Physical Augmentation: The Mental and Performance Effects of Exoskeleton Use in Heavy Work

OSCM
Jannick Fiedler (ETH Zurich); Torbjørn H. Netland
Technologies that assist workers are increasingly integrated into manufacturing, including systems designed to augment workers on demanding tasks. While recent research has focused largely on cognitive augmentation technologies, less attention has been given to how physical augmentation affects workers’ behavior and performance. Our study examines how exoskeletons, a prototypical physical augmentation technology, influence physiological strain, stress, self-efficacy, and performance in a field experiment conducted in a real manufacturing environment. Our ex ante theorization draws on the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping and Self-efficacy Theory. We designed a pre-registered field experiment in which frontline workers in an industrial firm completed a total of 26 hours of heavy work tasks, with and without occupational exoskeletons. To study the effects of exoskeletons on workers, we combined task-level performance data with physiological measurements, survey responses, and on-site observations. The results show that using exoskeletons significantly reduces physiological strain and perceived stress. However, we do not find a significant effect on self-efficacy or short-term performance improvements. Together, these findings suggest that exoskeletons primarily function as an additional coping resource by reducing physical strain and alleviating perceived stress, thereby promoting worker well-being during demanding work tasks, even in the absence of immediate productivity gains.

A New Life Overseas: Understanding Labor Supply Chains Through Refugees’ Inclusion

OSCM
Maryna Klymchuk (University of Manitoba, Asper School of Business); Keysa Mascena (University of Fortaleza); Minelle E. Silva (University of Manitoba)
Amidst global challenges around workforce dynamics over the last years, understanding labor shortage in supply chains as well as the inclusion of refugees in the job market became crucial. Therefore, grounded on acculturation theory, this study aims to answer: (1) Which tactics are used by construction supply chain actors to manage labor shortage? and (2) How can workers’ acculturation build operational culture in the supply chain? Through a qualitative case study in the Canadian construction industry, 29 semi-structured interviews were conducted combined with various documents for our content analysis. Findings show a bidirectional perspective among supply chain actors, which is related to SCM procedures adoption and adaptation based on acculturation. For instance, towards inclusion firms have adapted routines (e.g., translating some documents) while workers have adopted new behaviors (e.g., reframing their skills). This study contributes to theory by explaining the multicultural supply chain management concept which is central to connect both structure and agency. In addition, we show how refugees’ work can address skilled labor gaps in supply chains. Practical and societal implications are also identified as we have refugees as central actors in the debate of labor supply chain.
