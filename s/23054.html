Exporting Better for Less: FDI and the Export Price-Quality Divergence (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Thi Hang Banh (National University of Singapore); Ammu George (Queen's University Belfast); Bowen Yan (National University of Singapore); Huanhuan Zheng (National University of Singapore)
This study examines the impact of foreign direct investment (FDI) inflows on export performance, with a particular focus on the quality and price of exports. We find that FDI inflows improve export quality while exerting a negative effect on export unit values, consistent with FDI-related technology diffusion that enhances production efficiency and reduces costs. We interpret this pattern as reflecting the transfer of process, product, and organisational technologies embedded in FDI, which enable firms to enhance product quality while pricing more competitively. The magnitude of these effects is moderated by the technological context of exported products. For technologically mature products with high initial technological endowments, FDI results in larger quality gains and stronger unit value declines. In contrast, for products closer to the technological frontier, quality improvements remain strong, but price effects are muted, reflecting higher entry barriers and limits to technology diffusion.

When Output Is Not Productivity: Re-Aligning FDI Spillover Tests with the Productivity Construct

IM
Guangrui Guo (University of Windsor); Tianjian Guo (Indiana University Bloomington); Zhenzhong Ma (University of Windsor); Yongheng Yao (Wilfrid Laurier University)
A substantial stream of empirical international business (IB) and development research on FDI spillovers estimates a log production equation with deflated sales or value added on the left-hand side. It interprets the foreign-presence coefficient as a ‘productivity’ effect. Yet many influential spillover studies treat productivity as a distinct estimand, first estimating firm-level total factor productivity (TFP) using modern production-function methods (OP/LP/ACF) and then testing spillovers on that productivity measure. This paper shows that these two workflows answer different questions. We identify the specific identification step in the canonical Aitken–Harrison derivation that embeds a demand-side equilibrium outcome into a supply-side ‘TFP’ term, and we clarify when a one-step output regression can (and cannot) be interpreted as technical efficiency (TFPQ). We provide a constructive correction for IB scholars: an estimand-first, two-stage workflow that aligns theory, measurement, and identification, supported by a Monte Carlo illustration showing how market-stealing can masquerade as ‘negative productivity’ in output regressions even when true technology spillovers are positive.

The Co-Evolution of Two-Way FDI and Regional Industrial Systems in China

IM
Sha Xi (Tianjin Chengjian University); Meijie Xu (Tianjin Chengjian University)
Departing from the traditional focus on spillover effects, we argue that multinational enterprises (MNEs) fundamentally reshape regional industrial systems through integrated investment. We adopt a strategic coupling framework, treating inward and outward foreign direct investment (FDI) as complementary knowledge transfer channels. Our core proposition holds that successful regional industrial upgrading hinges not only on MNEs’ dual embedding in global and local networks, but also on the host region’s inherent capacity to absorb and strategically leverage the resultant knowledge flows. We conduct empirical tests using longitudinal data of Jiangsu Province of China from 2004 to 2023. The results confirm that the collaborative coupling of two-way FDI drives industrial upgrading, primarily by strengthening technology and human capital transmission channels. Beyond this, our analysis identifies prominent regional heterogeneities, highlighting how local institutional and industrial environments critically determine upgrading outcomes. Our findings advance understanding of MNEs’ dual role as both knowledge importers and exporters in regional development. They also reveal that the effectiveness of strategic coupling depends on local institutional complexity which is often overlooked in mainstream analytical frameworks. These insights offer timely guidance for enterprises and policymakers to address deglobalization pressures and build sustainable regional resilience.

Antecedent, Moderator and Consequence of Intra (Inter)-Regional Strategies

IM
Pham Hoanh Son Nguyen (Clermont School of Business)
In an internalization perspective, our research contributes to the debate on the link between performance and intra(inter)-regional strategies by examining three unexplored questions: (i) whether intra-regionalization yields higher global efficiency than inter-regionalization, (ii) whether and how intra-regionalization, inter-regionalization, and degree of international involvement interact to shape firms’ global efficiency over time, and (iii) whether and how domestic Firm Specific Advantages (FSAs) of the enterprise shape its choice of internationalization configuration (intra-regionalization vs inter-regionalization). Our results demonstrate for the first time that intra-regionalization leads to higher global efficiency than inter-regionalization. The results also reveal that this superiority becomes more pronounced as firms increase their involvements in international activities. Moreover, the findings also confirm that the greater the strength of a firm's domestic FSAs, the more it prefers intra-regionalization over inter-regionalization as a choice of internationalization configuration. We also discuss the contributions and implications of our research for the debate in question as well as for the literature of regionalization.
