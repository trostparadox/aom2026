Crafting Desirable Futures Through Narratives: How Entrepreneurs Innovate Business Models for Good

ENT
Marcelo Francisco De La Cruz (Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich); Susanne Ritter von Marx
Entrepreneurs are increasingly expected to contribute to socially desirable futures, yet commercial ventures often pursue business model innovation amid market pressure and radical uncertainty. Prior research shows that entrepreneurs use narratives to weave past, present, and imagined futures into meanings that make visions credible, enabling coordination and commitment. However, we know little about how such narratives encode guidance and sustain action for business model innovation for good across short- and long-time horizons. To address this gap, we ask how entrepreneurial narratives bridge temporal elasticity to sustain business model innovation aimed at crafting desirable futures. Drawing on Conviction Narrative Theory (CNT) and a grounded theory approach, we analyze interviews, pitch decks, and field notes from 21 European startups in an impact-oriented accelerator. We develop an explanatory model of four narrative elements that sustain future making under radical uncertainty: transformative agency, impact identity, value first, and ecosystem embeddedness. Transformative agency frames venture as change lever; impact identity anchors team purpose; value first grounds ambition in tangible offerings; embeddedness provides validation. The study advances narrative entrepreneurship by specifying what these future oriented narratives must contain and extends CNT into entrepreneurial decision making, offering actionable guidance for founders, educators, and accelerators.

Entrepreneurial Strategies and Frugal Innovation in Resource-Scarce Environments

ENT
Masoud Karami (Manchester Metropolitan University); Afsaneh Bagheri; Muthu De Silva (Birkbeck - University of London)
This study explores how small business entrepreneurs operating under conditions of resource scarcity and heightened uncertainty strategically combine adaptive (i.e., that reactively introduce changes in response to challenges) and transformative (i.e., that proactively take actions to transform existing resources into new future ones) strategies to generate different frugal innovations. Drawing from 39 in-depth interviews with entrepreneurs, the findings offer a nuanced understanding of how perceived short-term uncertainty (i.e., uncertainty that immediately affects business operations) and long-term uncertainty (i.e. uncertainty that influences business operations in the long run) trigger the adoption of specific combinations of adaptive and transformative strategies. The adoption of these hybrid strategies yields distinct forms of frugal innovation: output-focused frugal innovation (i.e., improving output to be affordable, customer-friendly, and reliable), and business model-focused frugal innovation (i.e., improving the business model to be cost-effective and environmentally and socially sustainable). This article makes original contributions to the literature on entrepreneurial strategy, frugal innovation, and their interplay—specifically in the context of entrepreneurs operating in uncertain, resource-constrained environments.

Franchising as an Institutional Architecture of Opportunity: A Four-Decade Bibliometric

ENT
Elizabeth J. Muniz (University of North Texas at Dallas); Banu A. Goktan (University of North Texas at Dallas)
This study synthesizes four decades of franchising research in entrepreneurship and small-business scholarship using a bibliometric, science-mapping approach. Analyzing 271 Scopus-indexed journal articles published between 1985 and 2025, we apply bibliographic coupling and keyword co-occurrence techniques to map the field’s contemporary intellectual structure and thematic evolution. Interpreted through the Institutionalized Capital Transfer (ICT) framework, the analysis identifies five coherent clusters: franchisee development, financial risks, innovation and international expansion, business growth, and social franchising. Together, these clusters illuminate the mechanisms through which franchising systems structure the transfer of human, social, and organizational capital to entrepreneurs via training, governance, network ties, and codified routines. At the same time, the results qualify the widely cited claim that franchising represents a lower-risk entry into entrepreneurship by showing that outcomes depend on financing conditions, governance quality, and institutional context. By linking each cluster to observable outcomes such as survival, time to break-even, and progression, the study reframes franchising as a contingent and testable institutional architecture rather than a uniformly protective business model. The paper concludes by offering a structured research agenda that clarifies how institutional design shapes entrepreneurial opportunity, performance, and inclusion.

Salmon, Ants and Buffalo: A Process View on Entrepreneurial Strategy Across the Taiwan Strait (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Irene Chu (Newcastle University); Mingchu Wang (Newcastle University); Kweku Adams
This study investigates how entrepreneurs from the Taiwan region adapt business strategy under prolonged cross-Strait geopolitical tension. Drawing on a social capital lens, we analyse 50 semi-structured interviews with 31 returnee and cross-Strait entrepreneurs collected in two waves across 20 months. Our inductive analysis reveals three recurring strategic paths. Returning Salmon selectively reallocate functions back to the Taiwan region while preserving carefully governed external ties and shifting toward higher specification activities. Embedded Ants rely on trusted collective arrangements that mobilise shared resources, bridge networks, and circulate knowledge to keep pace with larger rivals. Silent Buffalo emphasise discretion, incremental innovation, and disciplined execution to sustain niche leadership grounded in cultural and commercial legacies. We further develop a process model linking geopolitical and economic triggers to sense-making and low risk probing, and then to the enactment of these paths. The model connects market, social, and hierarchical relations with the opportunity-motivation-ability mechanism and clarifies the role of complementary capabilities. The resulting outcomes include continuity and legitimacy, risk dispersion, upgrading, and durable niche competitiveness. We extend social capital theory into a geopolitical setting by offering an archetype-based, process-oriented account of SME adaptation across the Chinese mainland, the Taiwan region, and adjacent markets.
