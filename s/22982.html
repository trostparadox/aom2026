Simulating Virtual Organizational Actors for Research: Introducing Synthetic Field Studies

RM
Anand Prema Aschwin Van Zelderen (SKEMA Business School - Grand Paris Campus)
Technological advances in both generative AI (GenAI) and extended reality (XR) now allow researchers to simulate virtual organizations and their actors— such as managers, coworkers, and customers—in ways that are interactive and immersive. This paper introduces synthetic field studies: a methodological framework that combines GenAI and XR to study organizational phenomena in ecologically valid working contexts with interactive, lifelike organizational actors. Through these technologies, researchers can move from static scenarios measuring imagined responses towards simulations capturing real-time behaviors and reactions. We define the core design features of effective virtual organizational actors, discuss early empirical applications, and outline best practices for enhancing the reliability, validity, and reproducibility of organizational research. We also outline ethical considerations and provide illustrative applications across domains including organizational behavior, strategic management, leadership, and marketing. We argue that synthetic field studies offer a powerful and novel approach to generate scientific insights that bridge the gap between laboratory and field studies.

Using Large Language Models to Construct Virtual Top Managers: A Method for Organizational Research

RM
Antonio Garzon-Vico; Krithika Komalapati; Arsalan Shahid (University College Dublin); Jan A. Rosier (University College Dublin)
This study introduces a methodological framework that uses large language models to create virtual personas of real top managers. Drawing on authentic CEO communications and Moral Foundations Theory, we construct LLM-based participants that simulate the decision-making of individual leaders. Across three phases, we assess construct validity, reliability, and behavioral fidelity by benchmarking these virtual CEOs against human participants. Our results indicate that theoretically scaffolded personas approximate the moral judgements observed in human samples, suggesting that LLM-based personas can serve as credible and complementary tools for organizational research in contexts where direct access to executives is limited. We conclude by outlining implications for future research using LLM-based personas in organizational settings.

Large Language Models as Surrogate Respondents in Capturing Tacit, Experience-Embedded Knowledge

RM
Chang Liu (University of Wisconsin-Madison)
Large language models are increasingly used as surrogate respondents to generate synthetic survey data. This study tests a critical boundary condition: whether such models can replicate not only observable attitudes but also deep, experience-embedded knowledge. Using survey data from 704 undergraduate business students, I compare human responses with synthetic responses generated under four model configurations for two outcomes: entrepreneurial intention and tacit entrepreneurial knowledge (TEK). TEK is measured using scenario-based judgments benchmarked against expert response profiles. Replicability is evaluated using mean differences and distributional comparisons. The results show that models approximate entrepreneurial intention distributions relatively well but systematically overstate intentions relative to human respondents. In contrast, models substantially underpredict TEK and fail to reproduce its dispersion. Scenario-level analyses indicate better alignment in analytically oriented tasks such as opportunity evaluation and strategy, but pronounced failures in social-reasoning-intensive contexts such as hiring, leadership, and investor relations. Adding codified external knowledge provides limited improvement, whereas conditioning on observed human exemplars yields the largest gains, suggesting that performance improvements mainly reflect pattern matching rather than the reconstruction of experience-based reasoning. Overall, the findings caution against treating synthetic respondents as interchangeable with human data when constructs depend on tacit, socially embedded judgment.

Can Large Language Models Simulate Ethical Decision-Making in Accounting? A Replication-Based Test

RM
Zhiyi Chen (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Nan Hu (University of Tulsa)
This study examines whether and to what extent large language models (LLMs) can simulate ethical decision-making as documented in the accounting ethics literature. Treating LLMs as synthetic professional accountants, we assess their performance by replicating three well-established studies that capture key dimensions of ethical decision-making. Model-generated responses are systematically compared with corresponding human evidence. The results indicate that LLMs reproduce theoretically consistent, average-level patterns in ethical judgments and intentions associated with salient individual characteristics and situational features. At the same time, model-generated responses exhibit substantially compressed dispersion relative to human samples, limiting their ability to capture response heterogeneity and extreme ethical positions. Moreover, although role-based prompting induces systematic shifts toward professional value orientations, alignment with documented human value hierarchies remains incomplete. Taken together, these findings delineate the capabilities and boundaries of LLM-based simulations, highlighting their value as complementary tools for early-stage inquiry and research design refinement in accounting ethics research.

Who Governs Theory Building from Literature? RAG, Human Judgment, and Systematic Review Rigor

RM
Dr. Faisal Saeed Malik (Karlsruhe Institute of Technology); Orestis Terzidis (Karlsruhe Institute of Technology)
Systematic literature reviews (SLRs) are a cornerstone of theory development in management and organizational research, yet their epistemic foundations are increasingly strained by rapid growth in publication, rising interdisciplinarity, and the expanding use of generative artificial intelligence (AI). Recent advances in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) promise scalability and improved evidentiary grounding by conditioning generation on retrieved sources. At the same time, they raise fundamental methodological questions concerning transparency, reliability, validity, and scholarly responsibility. In this paper, we reconceptualize RAG not as a technical efficiency tool but as a methodologically consequential research infrastructure that intervenes directly in epistemically sensitive stages of research review. Building on established standards for rigorous literature reviewing and extending epistemically grounded work on AI-augmented SLRs, we articulate five core epistemic principles, transparency, validity, reliability, comprehensiveness, and reflective agency, and translate them into a replicable methodological blueprint for RAG-enabled reviews. The framework specifies how corpus governance, retrieval design, prompting, validation regimes, and human adjudication must be explicitly configured and documented to preserve epistemic accountability. We argue that rigor in AI-augmented reviews does not arise from automation or model sophistication, but from the deliberate governance of human–AI interaction throughout the review lifecycle. By positioning human judgment as the commanding element within iterative RAG workflows, the paper provides a principled foundation for integrating generative AI into systematic review research while safeguarding theory-driven inference, contestability, and methodological legitimacy.
