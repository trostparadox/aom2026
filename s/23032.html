Ethical Governance Through Sustainability-Linked Incentives in Multinational Firms

IM
Jorge Ochaita González (Universidad Rey Juan Carlos); Guus Hendriks (University of Amsterdam); Jose Angel Zuniga Vicente (King Juan Carlos University); Diana Benito-Osorio (King Juan Carlos University)
Organizations increasingly use sustainability-linked executive compensation to encourage leaders to advance societal and environmental goals. Yet little is known about when these formal governance practices improve sustainability outcomes. We examine sustainability-linked pay as a mechanism that embeds societal expectations into executive decision-making, while theorizing that its effectiveness depends on the broader context in which leaders operate. Drawing on an alignment-under-complexity perspective, we argue that two forms of complexity, namely external institutional complexity stemming from internationalization and internal governance complexity reflected in board gender diversity, shape whether incentives provide clear guidance, motivation, and accountability. Using panel data on multinational firms from 20 countries (2015–2022), we find that sustainability-linked pay is positively associated with ESG performance, indicating that ethically oriented compensation design can yield meaningful improvements in sustainability. However, this effect weakens as internationalization (i.e., external complexity) increases, consistent with the challenge of aligning standardized incentive metrics with diverse stakeholder expectations across markets. Board gender diversity (i.e., internal complexity) also dilutes the effect of incentives, likely because more diverse boards expand the range of salient sustainability priorities. Our findings highlight the boundary conditions of sustainability-linked pay and underscore alignment as central to effective ethical governance.

Social Ties that Bind or Loosen: Business Group Embeddedness and CSR in Emerging Economies (WITHDRAWN) (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Pooja Thakur-Wernz (Washington and Lee University)
This study examines how business group (BG) ownership shapes corporate social responsibility (CSR) spending of their affiliate firms in emerging economies. Drawing on Social Network Theory (SNT), we conceptualize CSR as a network-provisioned club good, a collective reputational resource sustained through multiplex ownership ties, monitoring, and reputational ties within BGs. This shared good benefits all affiliate firms but requires coordination to prevent free riding, duplication, and misallocation. Using a panel of 477 publicly listed affiliate firms from 64 Indian BGs (2015–2024), we find that higher levels of BG equity ownership are associated with higher CSR spending, as ownership ties embed affiliate firms more deeply in networks of monitoring and reputational interdependence. This binding effect weakens under two conditions. At the BG level, product diversification, particularly into unrelated industries, reduces the capacity of BG ownership ties to enforce collective provision. At the affiliate level, international diversification shifts firms’ legitimacy anchors outward, loosening their embeddedness within the BG. By theorizing embeddedness as conditional rather than uniform, our study reconciles contradictory findings in the BG–CSR literature, extends understanding of CSR in emerging economies as a network- provisioned collective good, and contributes to SNT by specifying when ties bind and when they loosen.

Legitimacy and Value: Market Reactions to Activism-Driven Corporate Divestments

IM
Shubham Singh (Rutgers University); Shyamala Sethuram (Baruch College, CUNY); Ajai Singh Gaur (Rutgers University)
This study examines how social activism shapes the market performance of corporate divestments. While prior research largely conceptualizes divestitures as efficiency-enhancing strategic actions, it offers limited insight into divestments undertaken under conditions of social contestation. We develop a sociologically informed perspective, conceptualizing activism-affected divestments as legitimacy-restoring actions through which firms simultaneously address economic and moral evaluations. Using a large-scale event-study analysis of over 39,000 divestment announcements across 68 countries, we find that divestments generally generate positive abnormal returns, consistent with efficiency-based explanations. Importantly, divestments undertaken amid heightened social activism elicit significantly stronger positive market reactions. This effect is amplified when activism is frequent, geographically dispersed, and led by prominent NGOs, suggesting that investors interpret divestment as a credible response to elevated legitimacy risk. We further demonstrate that these valuation effects are institutionally contingent: in countries with open political environments, activism-driven divestments receive substantially higher market rewards compared to politically closed environments. By integrating social movement theory with research on corporate restructuring and international business, our study advances a socially embedded understanding of divestment performance, highlighting how market value is co-produced through strategic action, legitimacy considerations, and institutional context.

Sovereign Wealth Fund Acquisitions and CSR: The Role of Institutional Distance

IM
Guowei Qiu (University of Liverpool); Jordi Surroca (University of Liverpool); Yuxi Cheng (The University of Texas at San Antonio)
Sovereign wealth funds (SWFs) have become increasingly active in cross-border acquisitions, yet their state-linked nature often raises legitimacy concerns in host countries. While prior research has largely examined the investment strategies, financial outcomes, and political implications of SWF investments, less is known about how SWF acquisitions affect the corporate social responsibility (CSR) behaviors of target firms and how institutional context shapes these effects. Drawing on legitimacy theory and the liability of foreignness perspective, this study examines whether and how SWF cross-border acquisitions influence firms’ CSR behaviors, and whether institutional distance between the SWF home country and the host country moderates this relationship. Using a difference-in-differences design and a panel of publicly listed firms from 2003 to 2023, we find that firms acquired by foreign SWFs exhibit a significant increase in CSR behaviors following acquisition. This finding suggests that CSR serves as an important response to the legitimacy pressures associated with foreign state ownership. We further show that the moderating role of institutional distance varies across dimensions. Economic and cultural distance strengthen the positive effect of SWF acquisition on CSR behaviors, whereas demographic and knowledge distance weaken it. Our findings highlight the importance of considering institutional context when examining how firms respond to foreign state ownership. This study contributes to research on sovereign wealth funds, CSR, and institutional theory by showing that SWF acquisitions are associated with changes in firms’ CSR behaviors, and that these effects vary across institutional settings.

Laying Down the Law: Assessing Ethical Codes and Legalism on MNE Monitoring of Foreign Subsidiaries

IM
George O. White; Tazeeb Rajwani (University of Surrey); Thomas Lawton (University College Cork); Mirko Benischke (Rotterdam School of Management)
In drawing on neo-institutional theory, we develop a framework and test the relationships between the convergence of ethical codes of conduct, legalism, and monitoring of MNE foreign subsidiary chain partners. Our results show that greater enforcement of ethical codes of conduct positively influence a foreign subsidiary’s propensity to enhance the monitoring of supply chain partners. These relationships grow stronger when executive perceptions of legal environment unpredictability and the presence of a legal affairs officer(s) are considered. Our normative model also establishes that fit between MNE enforcement of ethical codes of conduct and the monitoring of foreign subsidiary supply chain partners, as predicted by legal institutional considerations, have better performance outcomes (greater satisfaction with reputation) than those not predicted by legal institutions oriented considerations. We find support for our hypotheses by employing data derived from 352 foreign subsidiaries operating in the Philippines and Thailand.

Why Firms Leave? From Economic Efficiency to Social Legitimacy

IM
Chansoo Park (Memorial University of Newfoundland); Magdalyn Adel Knopp; Ilan Vertinsky (The University of British Columbia); Sungjin J. Hong (Yeungnam University)
Research on the exit strategies of multinational enterprises (MNEs) has increased in importance and prevalence since 1980. Particularly in the last ten years, many papers have explored the subject, highlighting the need for review papers on the topic and the identification of future research priorities. This study examines the evolving perspectives of scholarly researchers on three major de-internationalization, foreign exit, and divestment (DFED) themes. First, to identify macro-level patterns and research trends, we used a Pre-defined Keyword Text Mining (PKTM) approach—a big data-driven methodology—to analyze themes and their evolution in both academic journals and reputable press since 1980. Specifically, we analyzed 210 articles on DFED topics, alongside coverage from seven major media outlets. Second, using a systematic review methodology, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P), we highlight the dynamic interplay between economic shifts and strategic decision-making in MNEs. The review also includes 210 journal articles, published from 1980 to 2025. We identify five distinct research eras defined by major global shocks and paradigm shifts, suggesting a theoretically grounded criteria for historical periodization. This study transcends prior literature reviews by synthesizing 45 years of scholarly articles into in integrative taxonomy of DFED motives, offering a strategic lens for the social and institutional complexities. Finally, we propose six research directions: digitalization, geopolitical volatility, sustainability, longitudinal studies, integrative models, and MNE exit strategies in emerging markets. By combining big data-driven keyword analysis and the structured insights from PRISMA-P, this study contributes a critical evaluation of the progression of thought within the field and uncovers gaps for future research.
