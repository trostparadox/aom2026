Gender Disparities in CEO Compensation Among Federally Qualified Health Centers

HCM
Dilorom Zuparova (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Ganisher K. Davlyatov; Nancy Borkowski (University of Alabama at Birmingham)
Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) serve as critical safety-net providers, yet little is known about gender disparities in executive compensation within these organizations. Guided by Role Congruity Theory, this study examines the relationship between CEO gender and compensation among FQHCs using a cross-sectional analysis of 2022 data from IRS Form 990 and the Uniform Data System (UDS). The analytical sample included 1,070 FQHCs with complete data on CEO compensation, gender, and organizational characteristics. Generalized linear models with gamma distribution and log link were used to assess the association between CEO gender and total compensation, controlling for organizational size, geographic location, grant dependency, CEO tenure, and succession type. Female CEOs comprised 51.1% of the sample, with a mean compensation of $265,799 compared to $335,733 for male CEOs. After adjusting for organizational and contextual factors, female CEOs earned approximately 15% less than male counterparts, representing an annual difference of approximately $49,000. This gender pay gap persisted across sensitivity analyses, including state fixed effects. External hire was associated with 20% lower compensation than stable leadership, whereas internal succession showed no significant difference. These findings suggest that gender-based compensation disparities exist within FQHCs despite their community governance structures and mission-driven focus, underscoring the need for greater transparency and equity in executive compensation practices across safety-net providers.

Determinants and Consequences of CEO-to-Worker Pay Dispersion in US Nonprofit Hospitals

HCM
Neeraj Puro; Reena Joseph Kelly (University of New Haven); Aizhan Karabukayeva (University of Oklahoma-Health Sciences Center)
There is evidence of wage inequalities in nonprofit hospital workers, who represent a significant portion of the US health care workforce. We investigate whether organizational factors, using Moulton’s (2009) realized publicness, and county factors, using social capital theory influence tournament incentives and wage inequality between the chief executive officer (CEO) and median worker in nonprofit hospitals. Along with certain organizational factors, we find that hospitals in US counties with higher social capital have lower tournament and wage inequality. We further explore the consequences of higher wage inequality and find it to have a positive association with hospital profitability in the short term. The results vary with community social capital, suggesting that local social norms influence wage inequality and hospital financial performance.

Markets, Ownership, and Pay: Understanding Wage Variation in U.S. Nursing Homes

HCM
Gregory N. Orewa (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Rohit Pradhan (Texas State University); Akbar Ghiasi (University of the Incarnate Word); Shivani Gupta (UHCL); Robert Weech-Maldonado (University of Alabama at Birmingham)
Nursing homes in the U.S. face persistent challenges in recruiting and retaining nursing staff: Registered Nurses (RNs), Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs), and Certified Nursing Assistants (CNAs). An important factor that may influence the recruitment and retention of nursing staff is wages. Guided by Resource Dependence Theory, this study examined how organizational and environmental factors are associated with facility-level wages for nursing staff in U.S. nursing homes. We used four secondary data sources: Payroll Based Journal, Medicare Cost Reports, LTCFocus.org, and Area Health Resource File for 2020–2022 yielding 37,936 facility-year observations. Multivariable linear regression models were estimated separately for inflation-adjusted RN, LPN, and CNA hourly wages with state and year fixed effects. Our findings suggest that greater organizational slack (size, occupancy rate, payer mix) were associated with higher wages: larger facilities and a higher share of private-pay residents were positively related to wages across nursing staff categories (ß = 0.02, 0.02, and 0.01, respectively; all p < 0.001). Higher share of Medicare residents was positively associated with RN (ß = 0.01, p < 0.001) and LPN (ß = 0.01, p < 0.01) wages, but not CNA wages. Not-for-profit independent (NFPI) facilities paid the highest wages (RNs: ß = 1.05; LPNs: ß = 0.71; CNAs: ß = 0.93; all p < 0.001), while for-profit independent (FPI) facilities paid the lowest (RNs: ß = -0.72; LPNs: ß = -0.59; CNAs: ß = -0.39; all p < 0.001). Among community variables, county-level household income was positively associated with wages ((RNs: ß = 0.50; LPNs: ß = 0.61; CNAs: ß = 0.45; all p < 0.001). Market competition as represented by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index was negatively associated with wages (RN ß = -0.001, p < 0.01; LPN ß = -0.02, p < 0.001; CNA ß = -0.01, p < 0.001). Wages increased by approximately $2.22, $2.37, and $2.12 per hour for RNs, LPNs, and CNAs, respectively, in the post–COVID-19 period (all p < 0.001). Overall, these findings highlight the importance of organizational and environmental factors in influencing wage-setting in nursing homes.

How Do Nursing Staff Wages Affect Turnover? Evidence from U.S. Nursing Homes

HCM
Rohit Pradhan (Texas State University); Akbar Ghiasi (University of the Incarnate Word); Ganisher K. Davlyatov
Background and Objectives Turnover among nursing staff [registered nurses (RNs), licensed practical nurses (LPNs), and certified nursing assistants (CNAs)] is a long-standing challenge in nursing homes with significant implications for quality of care. This study aimed to examine the relationship between nursing staff wages and turnover. Research Design and Methods We used national data from 2021 to 2023, linking multiple datasets including the Payroll-Based Journal and Medicare Cost Reports (n = 37,254). Turnover was modeled separately for each nursing staff type as a fractional outcome bounded between 0 and 1. The primary predictor variable was facility-level average hourly wage for each staff type. To address potential endogeneity and reverse causality, we used a two-stage residual inclusion IV approach with county-level average wages (excluding the index facility) as the instrument. Models controlled for facility-level organizational and county-level market characteristics, with fixed effects for county and year and cluster-robust standard errors at the facility level. Results Higher CNA wages were significantly associated with lower turnover (ß = -0.35, 95% CI [-0.59, -0.11], p = 0.005), indicating a 0.35 percentage point reduction in turnover per $1 increase in hourly wage. Wages were not significantly associated with turnover among licensed nurses. Discussion and Implications Findings suggest that wage increases may be most effective for CNAs, while retaining licensed nurses likely requires complementary organizational and non-monetary strategies. While nursing homes should strive to offer competitive wages to their staff, targeted reimbursement reforms may be necessary to overcome financial constraints.

Exploring the Economic Outcomes for Providers of Variable Reimbursement Rates in a Healthcare Value

HCM
Devdutt Shriram Upadhye (Mississippi State University); Mary Atkinson Smith (Mississippi State University); W. Paul Spurlin (Mississippi State University)
As healthcare providers face value-based payment (VBP) models, it is critical to understand how these programs affect providers' caring for vulnerable communities. Medicare’s Quality Payment Program (QPP) aims to reward high-quality, efficient care, but its design may unintentionally penalize providers who serve rural and poorer communities. Therefore, this study examined whether the QPP adversely affects providers who serve these populations. We analyzed how QPP payment adjustments relate to patient mix and consistently found that, regardless of quality measures, providers who serve poorer patients (higher proportion eligible for Medicaid) tend to receive smaller Medicare reimbursements. Moreover, while rural location alone did not significantly impact a providers' reimbursement adjustment, having a rural geographic classification status is associated with providers who serve poorer patients receiving even lower reimbursements. Therefore, providers in rural and underserved areas may be facing a compounded financial disadvantage, which may widen gaps in access if these providers exit these areas due to financial strain. Our findings offer important implications for both regulators and managers and leaders of healthcare provider organizations. Policymakers should consider the design of VBP systems to ensure they provide equity-sensitive payment adjustments and protections for rural practices and providers serving vulnerable communities. Healthcare administrators should consider forming strategic partnerships that help navigate various VBP programs to mitigate threats to financial stability and invest in building digital capabilities. These may be integral to consistent performance reporting and may help shield providers, especially those working at rural or smaller practices, from financial challenges. Keywords: Medicare, Value based payment, Equitable Reimbursement, Rural Access to Care, Organizational sustainability
