Motivate or Backfire? Value - Versus Control-Based Green Pay and Reward and Employee PEB

HR
Wenjing Guo (University of Leeds); Kerrie Unsworth (University of Leeds); FENG NI NI (emlyon business school)
Extensive research has examined how organizational environmental practices promote employee pro-environmental behavior (PEB), with green human resource management (GHRM) receiving particular attention. However, prior studies largely assume that GHRM practices operate uniformly across organizations, overlooking the ideology embedded in their design and implementation. Drawing on self-determination theory, we categorize GHRM into control-based and value-based forms according to the degree of control imposed and the extent to which environmental value is recognized and communicated. Focusing on green pay and reward (GPR), we distinguish between control-based and value-based approaches and examine how these types interact with implementation strength to influences employee PEB through green self-determination, with gender as a moderator. Multilevel data from 374 employees and 54 HR professionals from 54 organizations show that strong implementation of control-based GPR undermines green self-determination and reduces PEB, whereas strong implementation of value-based GPR enhances green self-determination and promotes PEB. These interactive indirect effects are stronger for males than females. This study advances GHRM research by highlighting the importance of ideological variation in practice implementation.

Green Human Resource Management on the Organization’s: Compliance and Voluntary Green Behavior

HR
Carlos Gabriel Colin Flores (Tecnologico de Monterrey); Juan Ignacio Sanchez (EGADE Business School)
The effects of Green Human Resource Management (GHRM) programs are seemingly predicated on self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000), which predicts that, once employees internalize the pro-environmental beliefs promulgated by GHRM, these beliefs drive employees to choose green behavior in a volitional rather than forced or controlled by external pressure manner. We propose a dual model that supplements the self-determination explanation with an extrinsic one, specifically that GHRM practices create conditions that increase the external pressure to engage in green behavior, even when employees had not yet internalized the pro-environmental beliefs promoted by GHRM. We tested this dual model by studying the effects of GHRM on the CO2 footprint left by light-truck drivers in Mexico City measured four times over 12 months. Our longitudinal, multi-method quasi-experiment involved 160 drivers assigned to either the treatment or the control group. The results supported the extrinsic hypothesis of our dual model because GHRM almost immediately reduced both CO2 and engine idle time, whereas the absence of effects in the employees’ private life suggested that GHRM did not yet win the drivers’ hearts. Support for the self-determination hypothesis of our dual model began to appear after eight months. In accordance with Socioemotional Selectivity Theory (SST), the external pressure to engage in green behavior exerted by GHRM was exacerbated among older drivers, who enjoyed a shorter future on the job. Theoretical and practical implications of the dual model were discussed.

Not All Green Practices Are Equal: A Micro-Foundational Analysis of Green HRM

HR
Hassan Imam (Roehampton University)
Despite the growing prominence of green human resource management, persistent theoretical tensions remain regarding how organisational intent translates into employee action. Most scholarship relies on the aggregation assumption, treating human resource systems as homogeneous bundles and masking the unequal influence of specific practices. This study addresses this limitation by examining the micro-foundational architecture of green human resource management and responsible leadership in the hospitality sector, a context characterised by high task standardisation and behavioural visibility. Using three-wave dyadic data from 323 employees and supervisors, the study employs psychological network analysis to map the item-level interconnections among HR practices, leadership cues, environmental identity, and pro-environmental behaviour. The findings reveal a concentrated architecture in which a small number of high-salience practices, most notably green training and promotion criteria, ocupy structural influential positions that anchor the translation of organisational intent into discretionary behaviour. The results further indicate that leadership influence is filtered through stable human resource signals rather than operating primarily through direct interpersonal pathways. Shifting the analytical lens from system averages to structural connectivity allows the study to elaborate Ability-Motivation-Opportunity and identity-based perspective and provides a more precise account of how greening operates as a configuration-dependent process. The findings offering a diagnostic framework for navigating the micro-foundational black box of sustainable work in resource-intensive service environments.

Green HRM and Responsible Leadership: JointEffects on Employee Environmenta lPerformance Across Cultures

HR
Ruilin Lyu (Newcastle University); Heidi Wechtler
This study examines how green human resource management (GHRM) and responsible leadership (RL) shape employee environmental performance (EP) through organizational citizenship behavior for the environment (OCBE) in Australia and China. Drawing on social exchange theory (SET), we investigate whether employees respond more strongly to system-based HR practices or to relational leadership cues when engaging in discretionary environmental behavior. Using survey data from employees working in ISO 14001–certified high-technology firms (Australia n = 200; China n = 200), we test a structural equation model across both contexts. The findings indicate that GHRM is positively associated with employee environmental performance in both countries, whereas the effects of responsible leadership vary by national context. In particular, formalized HR practices carry greater weight in the high power-distance setting of China, while relational leadership cues are more influential in Australia. These results underscore the importance of accounting for cultural context when designing and implementing sustainability-oriented HR and leadership practices.

From GHRM to Green Behaviors: Moderated Mediation Evidence Through Green Climate and Values

HR
Anjali Bansal (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow)
Despite mounting institutional pressures for sustainability, organisations grapple with translating green human resource management (GHRM) into enduring employee attitudes and behaviors. Borrowing from psychological ownership and institutional theories, this study illuminates the pivotal role of green organisational climate (GOC) as a mediator and individual green values (IGV) as a second-stage moderator, providing insight into employees’ psychological ownership for sustainability and their environmental behaviour. Using a two-wave cross-sectional data set of 403 participants, the study predicted a moderated mediation model using structural equation modelling. While theoretically this study makes a strong contribution to the literature on sustainability, sensemaking theory, and supply-value-fit theory, practically, it guides several HRM processes, such as recruitment, training, and the effective adoption of green behaviours.
