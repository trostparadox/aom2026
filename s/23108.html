Who Benefits from New Work? Employee Perceptions and Justice Evaluations in Family Businesses

HR
David Fischer (Technical University of Munich); Nevena Toporova (Technical University of Munich); Alwine Mohnen (Technical University of Munich)
The ongoing transformation towards “new work” presents opportunities and challenges, with challenges being particularly pronounced in industries employing both blue- and white-collar employees. Due to the differing nature of their roles, not all employees benefit equally from these practices. Employing a multiple case study of three large family businesses and one non-family business, this study explores the varying levels of awareness and differing perceptions of new work practices across the two employee groups, as well as the respective impact on organizational justice. This study provides a nuanced understanding of the intra-organizational dynamics at play in the adoption of new work practices. Our findings reveal a paradox: while blue-collar workers face unequal access to new work measures, they do not perceive distributive injustice regarding flexibility limitations, attributing these to occupational constraints rather than organizational inequity. However, concerns regarding interpersonal justice arise through reduced accessibility of white-collar decision-makers, while procedural justice issues emerge within white-collar teams through inconsistent policy application. Informational justice deficits exist but are largely accepted as role-inherent constraints. This study advances family business and human resource management scholarship by demonstrating that employee acceptance of unequal benefits depends critically on perceived legitimacy of role-based constraints and quality of cross-functional interactions, challenging assumptions about uniform fairness perceptions in contemporary workplace transformations.

Bounded Talent: Effectuation and Bricolage as Strategic Responses in Legacy Firms

HR
Aditya Moses (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Vishit Trivedi
The world of work is undergoing immense change. This has led to organizations rethinking about who is talent. While most organizations would be able to change their talent management strategy, legacy firms face a unique challenge. They have available human capital that may not fit the new definition of talent but cannot be displaced due to existing legacy; additionally, they need to bring in talent that meets the external requirements. In this study we focus on Indian legacy firms and identify the challenges and barriers that firms face when changing their talent management strategies. We interviewed 45 CXOs from 42 legacy firms to propose a new process model of talent management in legacy firms. We find that decision making in these firms is bounded by legacy and managerial cognition. We find that legacy firms adopt either a talent strategy of effectuation or bricolage depending on the barrier that they face. Furthermore, we identify the key elements that legacy firms employ in each of these strategies. This study contributes to literature by showing how the need for stability and the requirement of agility are balanced by organizations.

Institutional Voids and the Distortion of Stakeholder Relationships: Evidence from Benin

HR
Hakeem Ajonbadi (University of Doha for Science & Technology); Francine Ayebo Laourou (Coventry University); Olatunji David Adekoya
In this article, we explore how institutional voids shape stakeholder relationships by distorting stakeholder salience under conditions of institutional ambiguity. Based on qualitative interviews with farmers, agribusiness workers, and intermediaries in Benin’s agribusiness value chains, we develop a process framework that explains how weak enforcement, fragmented authority, and informal governance condition the enactment of power, legitimacy, and urgency. Our findings show that stakeholder salience becomes decoupled from material contribution and exposure to harm, systematically marginalising farmers despite their formal inclusion in cooperatives and contractual arrangements. By theorising institutional voids as salience-configuring conditions, the study advances stakeholder theory by demonstrating how institutional environments shape whose claims become actionable in practice. The analysis further contributes to institutional voids research by foregrounding its relational and processual consequences for stakeholder engagement in contexts marked by institutional ambiguity.

Why is HR Failing the Big Data Challenge? A Mechanism-Based Account of Practice (De)Coupling in HR

HR
Jaejin Lee (University of Oxford); Andrew Charlwood (University of Leeds); Emma Hughes (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester); Xanthe Whittaker (University of Leeds)
Despite securing symbolic legitimacy and gaining traction across organisations, we know little theoretically about why HR analytics (HRA) practice typically remains loosely coupled with everyday HR practice, or how tighter coupling between analytics and HR could occur. To address these omissions, this study extends classic ‘institutional myth/loose coupling’ arguments by specifying the multi-level mechanisms through which symbolic adoption is reproduced or reversed in day-to-day HR activities. Drawing on a two-year ethnography of a newly formed HRA team, the study identifies three mechanisms that reproduce loose coupling (Defensive Translation, Jurisdictional Distancing, and Identity Ambiguity) and three that support tighter coupling (Bridging Translation, Reconfiguring Artefacts, and Symbolic Reordering). The findings advance HR theory and institutional theory by reconceptualising HRA as a contested subfield of HRM shaped by competing institutional logics, identity tensions and relational vulnerabilities. HRA underperformance is therefore reframed as a contested institutional process rather than failure of technical implementation.

When New CEO Doesn’t Stick: Successor Tenure, Strategic Signaling, & Dynamics of Firm Value Recovery

HR
Vaibhav Mishra; Siddharth Kanakrai Upadhyay (University of Illinois at Springfield); Jerry Haar
CEO succession is a highly visible governance event, yet research has largely treated it as a discrete occurrence rather than a dynamic process. We shift attention from the turnover event to the tenure of the successor CEO and examine how successor tenure shapes firm value recovery following leadership transitions. Drawing on signaling theory and theory of collective forgetting, we argue that very short successor tenure signals weak succession planning and governance instability, prolonging uncertainty and depressing firm value, whereas longer successor tenure provides stabilizing cues and allows firm to shift the narrative, assisting valuation recovery. We further theorize that these effects depend on firm performance under the prior CEO. Using a longitudinal panel of 508 U.S. firms from 1993–2023, we find a U-shaped relationship between successor CEO tenure and firm value in the following year. Moreover, strong prior performance steepens this relationship, producing sharper initial penalties but stronger recoveries. Our study reconceptualizes successor tenure as a dynamic governance signal and advances a process-based view of post-succession value trajectories.
