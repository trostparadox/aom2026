Human Resource Management Governance in the Public Sector: An Exploratory OECD Perspective

PNP
Nicola Capolupo (San Raffaele Roma University); Emilia Romeo (San Raffaele Roma University); Gandolfo Dominci (University of Palermo); Valerio Giampaola (eCampus University)
This study examines how Human Resource Management (HRM) governance is structured within the central governments of OECD countries, drawing on a Strategic Human Resource Management (SHRM) framework adapted to the public sector context. Rather than relying on variable-centered approaches, the analysis conceptualizes HRM governance as an integrated system of delegated authority spanning key functional domains. Using original data from the OECD 2024 Survey on Public Service Leadership and Capability, the study explores cross-national variation in administrative autonomy across compensation management, recruitment and onboarding, and workforce planning. The objective is to identify recurring configurations of HRM governance that capture differing balances between central oversight and managerial discretion. The results point to the coexistence of distinct delegation patterns, reflecting alternative models of coordination, strategic steering, and operational flexibility within public employment systems. Overall, the study advances a more differentiated understanding of how governments structure HRM authority to enhance administrative capacity and strategic alignment in complex institutional settings.

Institutional Capacity Index – ICI: Model, Indicators, and Index (WITHDRAWN)

PNP
Humberto Falcao Martins (Fundação Dom Cabral); Ettore de Carvalho Oriol (Fundacao Dom Cabral); Paulro Roberto Guerra (Fundação Dom Cabral)
The academic and public policy debate on development widely recognizes the role of institutions, but lacks integrated models that systematically articulate the economic, political, social, and managerial dimensions in constructs that address Institutional Quality, Public Management Quality, and Business Environment Quality. The existing literature is fragmented, often sectoral and methodologically tautological, neglecting the dimension of public management as institutional capacity. Given this gap, this article proposes and tests a nomological model of Institutional Capacities, culminating in the construction of the Institutional Capacity Index (ICI). The research question that guides the study is: "Which model could test the relationship between the level of Development of the country, the quality of Institutions, the quality of Public Management, and the quality of Business Environment?" Methodologically, a post-positivist approach is adopted, based on international secondary data, with validation by SEM-PLS, applied to a sample of 146 countries. The results statistically confirm that Institutional Quality, Quality of Public Management, and Business Environment directly and indirectly influence development, explaining more than 79% of its variation. The main theoretical contribution lies in the formulation of an integrated and empirically validated model of institutional capacities. On a practical level, the ICI offers a robust tool for comparative diagnosis between countries and the formulation of public policies aimed at overcoming institutional bottlenecks and promoting the well-being of populations.

From Farm Loan Waivers to Zero Interest Credit Institutional and Behavioral Pathways

PNP
Prof Prashant Salwan (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Shiva Goyal (Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee); Bhaskar Shandilya (Indian Institute of Management Sambalpur)
Farm credit policies in India increasingly emphasise interest subsidies and zero-interest lending as a preventive alternative to politically salient farm loan waivers. Yet, there is limited beneficiary-level evidence on why the impacts of Zero-Interest Loan Schemes (ZILS) vary across farmers and whether social improvements precede economic gains or the reverse. Using primary survey data from 196 ZILS beneficiaries in Madhya Pradesh, this study examines two research questions: (RQ1) which institutional, behavioural, and demographic factors explain variation in economic and social outcomes, and (RQ2) whether social outcomes precede economic outcomes in shaping scheme impact. Authors construct latent indices for institutional accessibility, scheme awareness, credit discipline, risk protection, and perceived economic and social impacts using PCA-based scores, and identify beneficiary segments through K-means clustering. Robust regression results show that institutional accessibility and credit discipline are significant predictors of economic impact (ß = 0.171, p = 0.026; ß = 0.239, p = 0.033) and social impact (ß = 0.169, p = 0.038; ß = 0.442, p = 0.002), while awareness and risk protection are not consistently significant. Clustering reveals meaningful heterogeneity: a large “integrated” group with positive outcomes (n = 127), a “constrained” group with weaker access and awareness and negative social impact despite marginal economic gains (n = 62), and a small “high-risk” group with severe outcome deficits (n = 7). Demographic profiling indicates that disadvantaged groups, especially Scheduled Tribe households and smallholders, are disproportionately represented in weaker-performing clusters. Finally, bidirectional mediation analyses using bootstrap inference provide stronger support for a social-first pathway, where institutional and behavioral factors influence economic impact primarily through improvements in social outcomes. The findings imply that ZILS effectiveness depends on reducing last-mile access frictions and strengthening repayment-related behavior, and that monitoring social indicators is essential for achieving sustained economic improvements.

Creative Hubs as an Institutional Perspective: A Systematic Literature Review and Theoretical View

PNP
Eduardo Guilherme Nuncio (Universidade do Vale do Itajaí - Univali); Rodrigo Boeing Althof; Clarissa Stefani Teixeira; Deoclécio Junior Cardoso da Silva (Pontifícia Universidade Católica do Paraná); Danisson Luiz dos Santos Reis; Anete Alberton
Creative hubs operate as hybrid organizations that bridge cultural production, collaborative innovation, and public policy. Although their importance is increasing in urban and regional contexts, the concept remains fragmented and inadequately theorized from a governance perspective. This study systematically reviews 71 peer-reviewed articles published between 2005 and 2024, analyzing definitions, organizational structures, and analytical approaches to creative hubs. Building on public governance and socio-territorial research, the study proposes a framework that classifies hubs by organizational models, governance structures, and institutional roles. The findings delineate five principal governance roles for creative hubs: connector, strategic broker, urban agent, knowledge mediator, and political resistor. These roles demonstrate how hubs facilitate interactions among the state, market, and civil society. The review also uncovers a predominant focus on the Global North and identifies gaps in the examination of economic, cultural, and social dimensions. By synthesizing dispersed evidence, this study introduces a new typology of creative hubs and clarifies their functions in collaborative governance, policy development, and cultural innovation. This research contributes to public and nonprofit management scholarship by theorizing how informal, hybrid, and participatory infrastructures enable public value creation and mediate urban transformations across varied institutional environments.

Rethinking Professionalization Models and Professional Associations' Role in Developing Countries

PNP
Priscilla Lambert (University of York)
Professionalization theories have been largely developed from Western contexts in which professions consolidate authority through state sponsorship or nationally autonomous professional associations. Yet in many developing countries, professionalization unfolds under different conditions, where domestic professional infrastructures and associations are weak and transnational actors play a vital role in domestic professional governance. In this study, I examine how newly emergent professions and their professional bodies develop under such conditions through a qualitative case study of actuarial science and coaching in Thailand, a non-colonized, non-statist, market-oriented developing economy. Drawing on reflexive thematic analysis of 21 interviews and extensive documentary materials, I identify four constitutive elements of professionalization: primary actors, sources of legitimacy, knowledge systems, and closure mechanisms. Across both cases, these elements diverge from assumptions embedded in classical models of professionalization from above and from below. Building on these findings, I advance professionalization from outside as a third ideal-type model that reconceptualizes professionalization as an externally anchored and globally embedded process shaped by transnational professional associations and governance arrangements. While grounded in Thailand, the model illuminates broader patterns across developing contexts and contributes to debates on globalization, transnational professional governance, epistemic authority, and power asymmetry. Keywords: Professionalization; professional associations; developing countries; transnational governance; power asymmetry

Managing Meaning During Organizational Entry: Sensegiving and Early-Career Newcomers in Public Orgs

PNP
Dylan Russell (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill)
Managers play a central role in shaping how newcomers interpret their organizational environments, yet public management research has paid limited attention to the interpretive mechanisms through which managers influence newcomer socialization. Drawing on a qualitative multiple-case study of early-career newcomers in public organizations, this paper examines managerial sensegiving as a key explanatory mechanism during organizational entry, focusing on early-career entrants navigating heightened ambiguity. Using longitudinal interview data from newcomers and their supervisors, we identify four recurring managerial sensegiving responses: clarifying and reframing ambiguity, coaching through resistance, validating and humanizing newcomer experience, and withholding or inconsistent sensegiving. These responses shape how newcomers interpret roles, authority, and belonging over time. We show that managerial sensegiving operates not merely through information transmission, but through authority-laden relational signals that either stabilize or destabilize newcomer sensemaking. By conceptualizing managerial sensegiving as an interpretive mechanism embedded in public sector authority relations, this study advances a process-oriented understanding of socialization in public organizations and offers insight into why traditional onboarding approaches may fail to support early-career entrants.
