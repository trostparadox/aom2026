Session overview

Organizational research has relied heavily on self-report methods to explain workplace behavior, capturing how individuals consciously perceive and evaluate their experiences. A growing body of evidence from psychology, neuroscience, and evolutionary biology indicates that many organizationally relevant processes, such as emotional reactions, stress responses, motivation, leadership dynamics, and social coordination, are shaped by automatic and physiological mechanisms that operate outside conscious awareness. This disconnect raises questions about what existing organizational theories capture and where they remain incomplete. This symposium examines when and how biological and physiological measures can extend organizational theory beyond the limits of self-report. Bringing together scholars with expertise in emotion, occupational stress, motivation, and leadership, the panel focuses on principled integration rather than methodological replacement. The symposium highlights organizational phenomena in which timing, automaticity, and interpersonal dynamics are central, and where biological measures can illuminate processes that surveys systematically miss. The goal is to articulate shared criteria for the responsible use of biological approaches and to outline a theoretically grounded agenda for integrating these methods into organizational science.
