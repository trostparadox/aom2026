Practices of Coherence: How Mission-Driven Ecosystems Maintain Responsiveness to the Social Issues

SAP
Erin Bass (University of Nebraska at Omaha); Ivana Milosevic (College of Charleston)
Unlike platform ecosystems, mission-driven ecosystems lack centralized control, instead relying on mission-driven processes to maintain the volunteer activities of diverse actors over time. Yet, few studies have explored how ecosystems achieve coherence, or consistency in relating, required for maintaining activities when the mission itself becomes ambiguous, and achieving it requires continual adjustments and the involvement of an ever-increasing number of new actors. To understand this question, we draw on a case study of the Community Catalyst Collective (CCC), a healthcare ecosystem formed with a mission to improve access to care for children in underserved communities. We discovered a recursive loop of practices through which actors interlink strategizing (setting new strategic imperatives) and organizing (accomplishing the coherence of the ecosystem) to maintain responsiveness to the complexities of the social issue. Specifically, our findings unpack practices through which they construct, fracture, and reconstruct coherence as they initiate and advance new strategic imperatives. Our findings, thus, extend the ecosystem literature by explicating how coherence as a mechanism of ecosystem maintenance is accomplished through a repertoire of strategic practices. In addition, we advance Strategy-as-Practice research by deepening the understanding of how knowledgeable actors do work of strategic importance in pluralistic, multi-actor contexts.

Looking in or Out? CEO Attention Meets Stakeholders in Green Innovation

SAP
Wei Chen (University of Mary Washington); Andy Yu (University of Wisconsin-Whitewater); shyh jer chen (National Sun Yat-Sen University); Yefeng Wang (University of Wisconsin-Whitewater); SHAN-KUEI TENG (National Sun Yat-sen University); Hui-Fang Yuan (National Sun Yat-Sen University)
This study examines how CEOs’ spatial attention orientations shape firms’ ambidextrous green innovation (GI) practices and how stakeholder orientations rebalance these effects. Drawing on the Attention-Based View and a strategy-as-practice perspective, we theorize that internally focused CEOs enact exploitative GI by refining existing processes, whereas externally focused CEOs enact exploratory GI by pursuing novel technologies and opportunities. We further argue that stakeholders’ environmental and social orientations condition how CEO attention is translated into GI practices. Using panel data from Taiwanese listed electronic manufacturing firms from 2018 to 2023, we combine computer-aided text analysis (CATA) of shareholder letters with patent-based measures of exploitative and exploratory GI. The findings show that externally focused CEOs are associated with exploratory GI, while internally focused CEOs favor exploitative GI. Stakeholder orientations significantly moderate these relationships. Strong environmental orientation redirects externally focused CEOs toward exploitative GI and encourages internally focused CEOs to pursue exploratory GI. In contrast, strong social orientation reinforces exploratory GI among externally focused CEOs but unexpectedly dampens exploratory GI among internally focused CEOs. This study advances GI research by showing how ambidextrous GI emerges from the ongoing interaction between managerial attention and stakeholder orientations in practice.

Practice Divergence Unveiled: Strategizing Faith-Based Organizations and Digital Technologies

SAP
Joakim Netz (Jönköping University and Chalmers); Karin Havemose (Jönköping University); Sotirios Paroutis (University of Warwick)
Strategizing solutions to societal challenges often involves deployment of digital technologies, yet we have limited understanding of how organizations facing similar challenges diverge in their implementation of these technologies. This study advances strategy-as-practice theory by introducing the concept of practice divergence. Drawing on a longitudinal study of seven Swedish congregations before, during, and after the Covid-19 crisis we explore how new online technologies are deployed to address challenges of equality, mobility, and integration in society. We empirically trace the sources of practice divergence in developing solutions, showing how the engagement of traditional and non-traditional strategists reinforces cycles of learning and framing. We find that engaging non-traditional strategists leads to novel grounding and inclusive framing, whereas draining of non-traditional strategists leads to passive learning and exclusive framing. This enriches our understanding of the importance of non-traditional strategists in strategy as practice, and the reinforcing nature of practice divergence while strategizing digital solutions.

Growing with Purpose: Designing a Social Value Diagnostic Tool for SMEs

SAP
Ahrabie Kirubasuthan (Brunel University London); Seyedeh Asieh Hosseini Tabaghdehi; Ainurul Rosli (Hult International Business School); Jane Y.C. Chang (Brunel University London)
Despite growing recognition of social value in small and medium-sized enterprises, there remains a lack of practical tools to help firms assess and integrate social value within their growth processes. This study addresses this gap by designing and evaluating a social value diagnostic tool tailored to SME realities. Using a Design Science Research approach, three iterative design and evaluation cycles were conducted with forty-two participants, including researchers, practitioners, and SME end users. The resulting tool enables SMEs to identify and reflect on the materiality, significance, and subjectivity of social value, supporting structured reflection and strategic alignment between social and economic objectives. Four design principles emerged from the study, offering guidance on creating proportionate and contextually grounded assessment instruments. The research contributes theoretically and methodologically to understanding how social value can be meaningfully embedded within SME growth trajectories.

Strategy and the Social Constriction of Equality: A Bourdieusian Perspective

SAP
Kerrie Howard (Royal Holloway - University of London)
Deploying a Bourdieusian perspective, this article examines how strategy field building among middle managers with a stake in being a strategist produces inequalities. Drawing on empirical materials from a single in-depth case study in a large U.S. corporation, the study analyses agentic positioning in the field of strategy game playing as individual middle managers construct themselves as: 1/. Strategy players; 2/. with relevant strategy habitus; and 3/. ability to develop resources valued as strategic i.e. economic, cultural and symbolic capitals. Findings show how inequalities are located in the agentive relations constructed in strategy practices and discourses that constitute those who play the strategy game. Offering an empirical account of how equalities are socially constricted within agent’s relational struggles to become stronger strategy agents, the paper contributes a theorisation of middle managers as strategy takers and makers. The paper also contributes to discussions on how societal phenomena such as inequalities are constructed in everyday action and maintained within organizational processes, rather than a mere inconvenient outcome. Insights and practical implications for the role of scholarship and business schools are offered.
