Learning from Failure and Success and the Role of Societal Collectivism (WITHDRAWN)

OB
Sophia Rehbein; Yannick Loeppke; Thorsten Semrau; Katrin Susanne Muehlfeld (University of Trier)
Learning from past experiences such as failure or success is crucial for both individual performance and organizational effectiveness, yet such learning does not occur automatically. Prior research has primarily focused on whether and to what extent individuals learn from their past failure and success, raising the question of which factors influence learning from past failure or success and whether these factors operate similarly for both. We address this gap by examining the role of societal culture, focusing on institutional and in-group collectivism. We combine two waves of multilevel data from 1,875 leaders in a multinational organization across 20 countries with country-level cultural practices from the GLOBE study. The results of hierarchical linear modelling show that institutional collectivism inhibits learning from failure but enhances learning from success. In contrast, in-group collectivism fosters learning from failure, but constrains learning from success. These findings emphasize the culturally contingent nature of learning from experience and advance research on learning in cross-cultural contexts.

Picturing a Realistic Future or a Pie in the Sky? A Persuasion Analysis of Leader Vision Expression

OB
Xianhui Ning (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Linkun Han (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
While leader expression of organizational vision endeavors to motivate employees toward vision realization, employees may consider it as the pie in the sky, hindering its motivational effectiveness. How organizational and managerial factors exert their influence on this process remains unclear. Drawing from a vision persuasion perspective, this research examines the detrimental influence of perceived organization’s poor vision management history and leader hypocrisy on employee vision pursuit and identifies vision plausibility as the mediating mechanism. Two experiments and a time-lagged survey were conducted. Study 1 manipulates perceived organization’s poor vision management history, and Study 2 manipulates perceived leader hypocrisy, addressing causal inferences. Study 3 collects data from employees with various job functions, enhancing generalizability of research findings. Perceived organization’s poor vision management history and leader hypocrisy are negatively related to employee vision pursuit through decreased vision plausibility. Besides, perceived organization’s poor vision management history and leader hypocrisy interact to influence employee vision plausibility and subsequent vision pursuit. This research advances leader vision research by identifying organizational and leadership factors, including vision management history and leader hypocrisy, that can undermine its motivational impact. It further introduces employees’ vision pursuit as a precise, context-specific measure of effectiveness and identifies vision plausibility as the psychological mechanism linking leader vision expression to engagement, thereby extending persuasion theory to organizational management.

Empty Talk or Useful Speech? Speaking Time as a Signal of Contribution in Leader Emergence

OB
Arran Caza (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Andrew Loignon
The babble hypothesis refers to the longstanding finding that individuals who talk more tend to emerge as leaders. While this association is robust, its interpretation remains uncertain. Does speaking time reflect self-assertion, such that individuals claim influence by speaking more? Or does it signal peer-validated contribution, so individuals who contribute more are given more time to speak? Drawing on expectation states theory, we propose that speaking time reflects peer validation and is thus a corollary of emergence rather than its cause. Instrumental variable regression using data from 446 senior managers in 75 teams participating in a high-fidelity, five-hour organizational simulation showed that speaking time predicted both peer-rated influence and peer-rated effectiveness. Moreover, speaking time did not significantly predict influence when controlling for effectiveness, suggesting that individuals who speak more are not merely claiming influence but may be exercising a privilege granted in recognition of their contributions. The results suggest that rather than causing emergence, speaking time may reflect one’s contributions to the group. These findings can clarify the interpretation of the babble effect, suggesting that speaking time signals contribution instead of substituting for it. Our study supports the behavioral view of leadership as an interactive process, underscoring the value of examining how influence is granted and sustained in context.
