Redefining Work-Life Balance for Generation Z: A Systematic Review of Evolving Expectations

CAR
Jihye Oh (Azusa Pacific University); Jennifer Jihae Park (University of North Carolina Wilmington)
This review examines the evolving work-life balance expectations of Generation Z, a cohort projected to constitute 30% of the global workforce by 2030. Raised in an unprecedented digital environment, Generation Z exhibits distinct attitudes and expectations from prior generations. With a multigenerational workforce, understanding these differences has become crucial, as misalignment with younger generations’ expectations may hinder organizational success. Using a systematic literature review combined with structural topic modeling, this review aims to examine Generation Z’s unique work-life balance expectations, a concept that has gained significant importance in contemporary human resource management (HRM). This study reveals that the concept of work-life balance for Generation Z is expanding beyond family support to encompass flexibility, autonomy, and personal growth. Moreover, this review highlights the transactional and relational dynamics between Generation Z and employers, where organizations are expected to proactively support Generation Z’s work-life balance needs. Finally, we identify key gaps in the existing literature and propose five avenues for future research. Addressing these gaps will enrich understanding and guide future scholarly and practical efforts.

Spouse as a Co-Experiencer of Career Change: Unravelling the Unheard Voices

CAR
Akhila Veldandi (Institute of Management Technology Hyderabad); Sushanta Kumar Mishra
Family support plays a pivotal role in career development, particularly during career transitions (Holmes & Cartwright, 1994; Guan et al., 2015), which involve significant changes in role, employer, industry, geography, and the potential forfeiture of accrued experience and effort (Carette, Anseel & Lievens, 2013; Dlouhy & Biemann, 2018). Despite its importance, existing research largely neglects the role of family dynamics, especially spousal experiences, during such transitions. Hence, through qualitative inquiry, this study explores the distinctive experiences of 30 spouses of individuals undergoing career changes, complemented by a second set of 30 interviews for triangulation. The lived experiences of spouse elicited that the change experience was a bitter-sweet symphony while navigating change through faith and adapting to changes. They experienced melancholy characterized by challenges including loneliness. Spouses reported experiencing melancholy but simultaneously served as steadfast sources of support and positivity for their partners. This duality highlights the complex interplay of emotional and practical support within familial contexts. Building on these insights, the study proposes a process model capturing the spousal experience during career transitions. This model underscores the importance of fostering resilience and a family-centric perspective, enriching the understanding of career changes at an integrative, systemic level. This study extends Schlossberg’s Transition Theory by introducing a dyadic lens that foregrounds spousal experiences, demonstrating how career change is co-experienced and jointly navigated within intimate relationships rather than solely managed at the individual level.

Working to Survive or Crafting to Flourish? Work-Family Negotiation of Exceptional Caretaker Parents

CAR
A.K. Ward (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Maura Mills (University of Alabama); Nina M. Junker (University of Oslo)
Parents of children with special medical needs – termed exceptional caretaker parents (ECPs) - are more likely than others to have their employment situations impacted - often to the point of leaving the workforce - due to unique, intensive caregiving demands that often become harder over time. Employment maintenance may be even more critical in this population due to high healthcare costs and insurance needs, compounded by the fact that these families are more likely than others to face financial hardships. The purpose of this paper is to gain knowledge of how ECPs come not only to survive at work but potentially flourish in both work and family dimensions. Following best practices for grounded theory research, we interviewed 27 ECPs and used their narratives to provide a foundation for a new model of flourishing in this unique context, in which childcare often becomes more demanding over time. Participants’ stories suggested that the path to flourishing was generally not aided by formal policies, as they may be more effective for short-term care needs. Rather, participants crafted psychological realities based on borders set by partners and more informal aspects of work management, then enacted behavioral approach versus avoidance strategies to craft a balance between work and family dimensions. Participants’ narratives suggested that ECPs were most likely to flourish (in terms of both work and family) when their cognitive and behavioral crafting strategies were aligned around an approach strategy that blended work and family together.

Stardom and Fertility: Gendered Career-Family Trade-Offs in Hollywood

CAR
Marcelo F. Cano Kollmann (Ohio University); Ahreum Lee (State University of New York College at Oswego)
This paper investigates how professional stardom shapes fertility, addressing the underexplored reverse causality between careers and family life. Using a unique dataset of 560 U.S. Oscar winners and nominees in acting categories born over a 94-year period, we examine whether and how career recognition influences reproductive outcomes. We theorize that stardom amplifies gendered opportunity costs and workplace structures: while men convert high status into reproductive advantage, women face intensified trade-offs between career continuity and motherhood. Consistent with this framework, we find that actresses have substantially lower fertility and higher childlessness than both actors and women in their birth cohorts, while male actors achieve fertility rates above replacement level. Early career success (i.e., winning an Oscar by age 30) increases women’s fertility, suggesting that delayed recognition exacerbates postponement pressures. By demonstrating that organizational recognition can widen gender gaps in family outcomes, this study contributes to research on gender and careers, work–family trade-offs, and the opportunity costs of achievement. Our findings also speak to broader debates in management and policy on how professional success interacts with biological constraints, shaping both organizational diversity and demographic trends.
