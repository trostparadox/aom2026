Code as Keystone: Algorithmic Orchestration, Rigidity, and Ecosystem Scaling

TIM
Chun Chun Hsieh (National Taiwan University)
Cross-border digital ecosystems face a trust bottleneck: onboarding unfamiliar complementors and coordinating them under rules that remain credible across fragmented regulatory regimes. We theorize protocol-centric (algorithmic) orchestration, where Digital Governance by Bits (DGB)—governance architectures providing programmable enforceability, shared visibility, and embedded auditability—functions as the governance keystone, partially substituting for a focal firm’s discretionary enforcement. DGB reduces (i) commitment frictions by making selected obligations self-executing, (ii) coordination and verification frictions by providing a shared reference for state changes, and (iii) regulatory-compliance frictions by generating reusable compliance evidence by design. These friction reductions enhance compliance portability and enable trust-minimized ecosystem internationalization, observed as faster partner onboarding and broader ecosystem scope with lighter asset commitments—an internalization shift from assets to rules. Yet protocolization has a dark side: under institutional and regulatory dynamism, deterministic code can generate algorithmic rigidity and maladaptation costs that erode scaling benefits. We introduce governance capability—the dynamic capability to modularize rule architectures, manage exceptions, and reconfigure decision rights between code and human discretion—and develop propositions specifying when DGB accelerates scaling versus when rigidity costs dominate.

Framing Dynamic Between Focal Firm and Nascent Ecosystem Members During Geopolitical Crisis

TIM
SHIYAN SHENG (University of Leeds); Josh Morton (University of Leeds)
This paper investigate how do platform leaders and ecosystem members interact through framing under conditions of geopolitical crisis. Prior research has conceptualized framing as a unidirectional managerial tool, offering limited insight into its dynamic and interactive nature in nascent ecosystems facing sustained geopolitical crisis. We address this gap through a qualitative single-case study of Huawei’s HarmonyOS ecosystem, employing a two-study research design. In Study 1, we analyze conference speeches and archival materials to examine how the focal firm’s framing strategies evolve over time. We identify three coexisting but dynamically rebalanced framing strategies—crisis framing, technology framing, and ecosystem relation framing—each playing distinct roles across stages of ecosystem development. In Study 2, we analyze large-scale social media discourse using BERTopic modeling to examine how ecosystem members respond to and reinterpret focal firm framing. Comparing the two studies reveals a bidirectional framing dynamic characterized by alignment, selective amplification, and reverse influence. Together, our findings advance framing theory by demonstrating how framing emerges through reciprocal interactions between firms and ecosystem members, and illuminate how legitimacy is constructed in nascent platform ecosystems under geopolitical crisis.

From Seed to Synergy: How a New Ecosystem Emerges from and Co-Evolves with Established Ones

TIM
Wanying Xing (Zhejiang University); Yibo Gao; Yongjiang Shi (University of Cambridge)
The emergence of new ecosystems is often deeply intertwined with established ecosystems, especially when resource complementarities and market demands align. This paper examines how the e-commerce ecosystem in Zhejiang Province emerged and evolved through interactions with the local garment industry, a long-established economic pillar of the region. By analyzing the distinct challenges faced, co-evolution mechanisms leveraged, and ecosystem-building milestones achieved during this transformative process, we identify three critical phases: 1) the discovery of a value proposition and alignment of ecosystem functions, 2) the expansion of complementors and collaboration mechanisms, and 3) the upgrading and maturation of the ecosystem. These phases illustrate how the e-commerce ecosystem addressed bottlenecks, developed complementarities, and co-evolved with the garment industry to achieve sustainable growth. By providing insights into ecosystem co-evolution dynamics, this study contributes to the understanding of how emerging ecosystems benefit from established industries and co-evolution.

Supporting Collective Action Through Contextualized Innovation Intermediation

TIM
Andrea Kerstens (Delft University of Technology); Angela Greco (Delft University of Technology); Hans Wamelink (Delft University of Technology)
Collective action is essential for addressing complex societal challenges. To this end, cross-sector partnerships (CSP) are regarded as a key vehicle for collective action, with innovation intermediaries playing a central role in sustaining commitment and coordinating resources among other roles. Yet, many CSPs struggle to move beyond superficial collaboration, remaining characterized by fragmented efforts, misaligned goals, and limited collective impact. Despite growing attention to the role of innovation intermediaries, we know surprisingly little about how intermediation practices become effective in specific organizational and societal contexts. We fill this gap by drawing on a retrospective, multi-case analysis of six CSPs. We identify eight components of collective action: mission framing, stakeholder structuring, governance design, knowledge translation, capacity building, resource coordination, trust building, and strategic scaling. We distinguish between intermediary practices and micro-practices. While intermediary practices comprise a set of recurring support components used to facilitate CSP, micro-practices refer to their situated enactment. We show that these micro-practices are highly contingent on where and when intermediation occurs, resulting in distinct configurations across contextual conditions. We contribute a framework of contextualized intermediation thereby providing micro foundations for collective action in CSPs. By doing so, we contribute to the understanding of intermediation by demonstrating that intermediary effectiveness depends on the fit between contextual condition bundles and configurations of practices, showing how intermediaries adaptively bridge structural and behavioural dimensions of collective action
