AI Usage in Co-Founder Identification: The Role of Trust and Expected Congruence

TIM
Michael Maximilian Moedl (KU Leuven); Karin Hoisl
AI-powered co-founder matchmaking platforms are emerging as a viable new way for entrepreneurs to identify potential co-founders. This paper, grounded in a cost-benefit framework, investigates how trust in predictive artificial intelligence (AI) and the expected congruence between human and AI judgements influence entrepreneurs’ intention to use such platforms. We draw on data from exploratory interviews with founders and three scenario-based experiments. Study 1 examines expectations about AI-generated co-founder matches. Studies 2 and 3 manipulate levels of trust and expected congruence experimentally. The results show that greater trust in AI increases both intended use and expected congruence. Furthermore, the effect of expected congruence on use intention is positive, though it exhibits diminishing marginal returns, meaning that additional increases in congruence yield progressively smaller gains in use intention. Mediation analysis indicates that expected congruence substantially accounts for the effect of trust on AI adoption, explaining a majority of the impact on use intention. Our findings deepen the understanding of trust dynamics in AI-assisted decision-making and have practical implications for designing effective AI tools for entrepreneurial matchmaking.

Strategic Decision-Making, Prediction Machines, and Knightian Uncertainty

TIM
Nicolai J. Foss (Copenhagen Business School); Paul Huenermund (Technische Universität München)
Strategic decision-making often occurs under conditions of Knightian uncertainty, while artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning, is typically understood as a tool for prediction. Yet, empirical research examining the role of AI in addressing prediction tasks under Knightian uncertainty remains limited. This gap may stem not only from the novelty of both themes within the strategy literature but also from a lack of construct clarity surrounding Knightian uncertainty itself, and therefore a lack of understanding of how exactly AI may assist in strategy-related prediction tasks under Knightian uncertainty. We demonstrate that the concept carries multiple meanings (that is, there are distinct forms of Knightian uncertainty) and argue that the suitability of different AI approaches depends on which meaning is invoked. Building on this insight, we sketch an empirical research agenda to investigate the interplay between Knightian uncertainties and AI.

The Evolving Landscape of AI in Management Research: A Systematic Literature Review

TIM
Natalie Aleksic (Stockholm School of Economics); Pär Åhlström (Stockholm School of Economics); Frida Pemer (Stockholm School of Economics); Erik Wilhelm Wetter (Stockholm School of Economics); Villim Prpic (Stockholm School of Economics)
Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly generative AI, is widely expected to transform organizations, yet effective adoption and use remain challenging. Although organizations recognize AI’s potential, capability gaps and unintended employee-level consequences persist. Despite rapid growth, management research on AI in organizations is fragmented across domains, levels of analysis, and assumptions. This article presents a systematic literature review of 458 peer-reviewed business and management studies published between 1991 and 2025. Adopting a longitudinal and integrative perspective, we expose implicit assumptions about AI, clarify core constructs by distinguishing analytically distinct domains (e.g. development, adoption, use, and impact), and establish field-level boundary conditions. The review reveals a heterogeneous and unevenly developed research landscape and offers a framework and methodological approach to guide cumulative and coherent future research.

When Judgment Erodes: Epistemic Drift and Resilience in Algorithmic Organizations

TIM
Latha Poonamallee (The New School)
As algorithmic systems increasingly mediate organizational decision-making, a puzzle emerges: why does reflective judgment progressively attenuate among competent professionals who retain expertise, authority, and commitment to independent evaluation? We introduce epistemic drift and epistemic resilience to explain this phenomenon. Epistemic drift refers to the cumulative, self-reinforcing reorganization of attention, interpretation, and epistemic responsibility as algorithmic mediation reshapes the conditions under which judgment is enacted. Drift unfolds through recursive interactions between attentional contraction (progressive narrowing of information search), interpretive narrowing (cognitive entrainment to algorithmic framings), and responsibility transfer (shift of epistemic authority from human to system). These mechanisms interact to produce trajectories of diminished judgment that stabilize into organizational patterns resistant to reversal. Epistemic resilience describes the hierarchical regulatory architecture required to interrupt these pathways. It coordinates attentional durability (sustaining vigilance against habituation), interpretive adaptability (maintaining flexibility against algorithmic framing), and metacognitive autonomy (preserving independence against authority transfer) through continuous monitoring and calibrated deployment. We theorize ER's developmental pathways, boundary conditions, and testable propositions, repositioning judgment as ongoing regulatory accomplishment rather than episodic evaluation. The theory reveals fundamental tension in contemporary organizing: systems enhancing efficiency through automation simultaneously erode regulatory capacities making human discretion meaningful, requiring deliberate cultivation of epistemic ecologies enabling reflective judgment.

Who Forecasts Best? A Meta-Analysis of Human, AI, and Human–AI Performance

TIM
Patricia Stella Hornstein (Technical University of Munich); Fabian K. Ahrens (Universität der Bundeswehr München)
Accurate forecasts are critical for decision-making across domains because they inform choices with financial, organizational, or life-threatening consequences. Prior research shows that algorithms outperform humans on average in forecast accuracy, although humans retain advantages in certain contexts. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), however, challenge this view by enabling richer contextual processing while also introducing new vulnerabilities. As a result, long-standing assumptions about the relative performance of humans, AI and human-AI collaboration (HAI) require reexamination. To address this gap, we conducted a meta-analysis of 23 studies comprising 157 effect sizes and 31,567 forecast tasks comparing forecast accuracy across humans, AI, and HAI. Guided by the Brunswik lens model, we examined how task characteristics shape relative forecast accuracy. Our results show that, on average, HAI significantly outperforms human forecasters. We find no significant average performance differences between humans and AI or between AI and HAI. Instead, forecast accuracy strongly depends on task characteristics, specifically, cue richness, cue abstraction, and environmental volatility. We provide the first quantitative synthesis of forecast accuracy across humans, AI, and HAI and offer decision-makers guidance on when to rely on humans, AI, or their combination to improve forecast accuracy and reduce costly errors.
