Quality of Life Through Residents’ Eyes: Satisfaction and Importance in Flemish Nursing Homes

HCM
Candice Gruyaert (Ghent University); Jeroen Trybou (Ghent University); Delphine De Smedt (Ghent University)
Context. The rapidly ageing population increases the demand for long term care, with shifted expectations toward person centred and relationship oriented care, emphasising autonomy and involvement in daily decisions. This study examines how Flemish nursing home residents rate and prioritise ten QoL domains. Methodology. A cross sectional survey was conducted in 377 nursing homes across two waves between 2024 and 2025. Trained volunteers interviewed 8,100 residents using the validated interRAI QoL LTCF survey (50 items; 10 domains), including satisfaction (5 point scale) and importance ratings. Analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, and multiple linear regressions examining QoL satisfaction, relative importance, their associations, and resident characteristics. Results. Residents were predominantly female (71%) and aged over 85 (66%). Satisfaction was highest for privacy, safety, and comfort (>90%), and lowest for staff resident bonding, activities, and personal relationships (22-33%). Most domains were rated as important (>90%), except social domains, especially personal relationships (49%). Correlations were strongest for socially oriented domains. Relative importance was the strongest predictor across all models (ß?=?0.28-1.74). Discussion. Findings highlight strong performance on structural care aspects but persistent challenges in autonomy and interpersonal domains. Priority gaps in bonding, activities, and relationships indicate areas where targeted improvements could meaningfully enursing homeance daily life.

A Multi-Level Framework for Patient Care Quality in a Hospital ED

HCM
Subhajit Chakraborty (Coastal Carolina University)
While crowding in hospital emergency departments (EDs) and subsequent deterioration in patient care quality (PCQ) delivered in the ED have been pressing problems for most U.S. hospitals for a while now, those that have successfully managed to tackle this problem to had direct and visible involvement of their top management team (TMT) that includes the hospital’s CEO, CMO, CNO, CFO, CIO, and COO. Using Donabedian’s Structure-Process-Outcomes (SPO) framework as the foundation, we draw from the tenets of Upper Echelons Theory (UET) to suggest that a SPO perspective on PCQ in the ED can benefit from UET because ED is a reflection of the hospital TMT. Specifically, using a holistic excogitation of PCQ comprising its four facets—administrative, environmental, interpersonal, and technical quality, we offer a conceptual framework linking a hospital TMT’s diversity characteristics—goal preference diversity, and functional experience diversity—to the PCQ (in the ED) and on to the patient satisfaction, the hospital reputation and its financial performance. We also suggest that the extent of adoption of the electronic medical record (EMR), an end-to-end digital version of a patient’s healthcare record at the hospital moderates the first relationship. Healthcare research and medical practice implications are discussed. Keywords: Hospital emergency department (ED); top management team (TMT); goal preference diversity; functional experience diversity; patient care quality (PCQ); electronic medical record (EMR).

Governance Luck in Early Cancer Detection: A Principal–Agent Framework for Delegated Detection

HCM
Joël Le Bon (Johns Hopkins University)
Early cancer detection is commonly attributed to screening programs, clinical vigilance, and diagnostic innovation. In practice, however, population-level evidence shows that many cancers are detected through unplanned and serendipitous pathways, such as incidental findings, unexpectedly early testing, or unrelated clinical encounters, and that these "lucky" detections are unevenly distributed across health systems characterized by enduring socioeconomic, ethnic, and geographic inequities. This paper reframes diagnostic luck as a governance and organizational design problem, rather than a clinical anomaly. Drawing on principal-agent theory, we conceptualize early cancer detection as delegated detection under uncertainty, characterized by non-contractible effort, asymmetric information, and imperfect monitoring across multiple system levels. We introduce Governance Luck as a system-level construct capturing morally relevant residual loss generated by governance design, observable as unmanaged variance and inequity in diagnostic opportunity. Using population-level empirical regularities from cancer registry data as stylized facts, we develop a multilevel conceptual framework linking macro-level stewardship, meso-level contracting, organizational design choices, and micro-level diagnostic judgment. The paper advances theory-driven research propositions and a discovery-oriented empirical agenda, and identifies governance levers, including incentive alignment, equity-sensitive monitoring, organizational coordination, and AI governance, through which health systems can reduce reliance on luck and engineer fairer opportunities for early cancer diagnosis.

Learning on the Verge of Failure: Clinician Insights on Learning from Near Misses Versus Errors

HCM
Eda Algur (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania)
Near misses—incidents in which an error or failure is detected and corrected before reaching the patient—are an underutilized source of individual and organizational learning in healthcare. Drawing on interviews with 45 clinicians from two specialties, this study examines how clinicians and their teams learn from near misses and how such learning differs from learning that follows errors causing patient harm. Although near misses and errors often share similar underlying causes, learning from them diverges across four domains: emotional responses, timing of learning, location of learning, and clinicians’ relational autonomy in choosing learning partners. These differences reveal how learning from near misses departs from established accounts of failure learning. The findings contribute to research on failure learning, safety science, and quality improvement in healthcare organizations.

Same System, Different Outcomes: How Behavioral Determinants Shape Healthcare Performance Management

HCM
Jenna M. Evans (McMaster University); Elana F. Commisso (McMaster University, Canada); Zeenat Ladak (University of Toronto); Sarah Wheeler (University of Toronto); Gregory Richards (Telfer School of Management, University of Ottawa); Jeremy Veillard
Despite widespread adoption of performance management systems in healthcare, evidence of their effectiveness remains mixed. Prior research often examines individual performance management interventions in isolation and rarely applies a behavior change lens to understand how performance management systems influence individual and collective action over time. This study examines how behavioral determinants shape responses to sector-level performance management and how these dynamics unfold over time across diverse healthcare networks. We conducted comparative case studies of eight healthcare networks operating under the same centrally governed public performance management system in Ontario, Canada. Data collection and analysis were guided by the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behavior (COM-B) model and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF). Data included 98 semi-structured interviews with administrators and providers, site-visit observations, document review, and a TDF ranking survey. Findings show that performance management interventions are activated through their interaction with behavioral determinants, enabling them to operate as a small set of distinct functions: Trigger, Mirror, Amplifier, Integrator, Gatekeeper, and Noise. Over time, different configurations of these functions produced different improvement trajectories, including system-led, context-led, hybrid, stalled, and system-independent. Together, these findings inform a mid-range theory of performance management that explains how behavioral determinants moderate the functioning of performance management interventions.
