Impacts of ESG Factors on Corporate Firm Performance and the Mediating Role of Employee Satisfaction

MC
Oluseyi Ajayi (Case Western Reserve University); Timothy J Fogarty (Case Western Reserve University); Philip A. Cola (Case Western Reserve University); Yunmei Wang (Case Western Reserve University)
As environmental, social, and governance (ESG) considerations rapidly migrate from peripheral reporting requirements to central elements of corporate strategy, management consultants are increasingly asked to advise clients on where, how, and why ESG investments create value. Yet, despite the proliferation of ESG metrics, empirical clarity on the differentiated performance pathways of ESG components remains limited. This study develops and empirically tests an integrative model that disentangles the distinct effects of environmental, social, and governance performance indicators on two outcomes of direct relevance to consulting practice: corporate financial performance and employee satisfaction. Using Structural Equation Modeling, we analyze longitudinal secondary data from S&P 500 firms spanning 2015–2019, drawing on COMPUSTAT financials, ISS/KLD ESG indicators, and employee satisfaction data from Glassdoor. We found that employee satisfaction has a significant positive impact on corporate financial performance and that the social aspects of ESG have a significant positive impact on employee satisfaction. The study, therefore, establishes the importance of employee satisfaction as the mechanism by which the social element of ESG affects corporate financial performance (CFP). This study is essential to decision-makers as they grapple with resource allocation decisions, especially as the talent competition amongst the younger generation who deeply care about these issues heats up. It will further enhance our understanding of relationships more holistically. For management consultants, these results offer evidence-based guidance for ESG prioritization, sequencing, and client-specific strategy design. The study suggests that ESG initiatives do not operate as a monolithic construct but instead deliver value through distinct stakeholder channels, with important implications for capital allocation, talent strategy, and transformation roadmaps —particularly in an increasingly competitive labor market where younger employees place heightened value on social commitments. By clarifying the performance logic underpinning ESG investments, this research supports more precise, outcome-driven advisory engagements and advances a more nuanced, practitioner-relevant understanding of ESG strategy execution.

Hidden Cost and Performance Concept: A Tool for Stimulating Environmental Performance

MC
Françoise Goter-Grivot
Many organizations attempt to measure the social and environmental impacts of their activities, especially in response to growing regulatory demands, but the best method for doing so remains widely contested. According to the theory of hidden costs and benefits, a true cost accounting approach may be relevant. As a contribution to a broader research effort to address stakeholders’ social and environmental expectations, the current article proposes a general model of hidden costs that integrates ecological issues. Tests conducted through multiple interventions reveal the theoretically predicted link of hidden social and economic costs with hidden environmental costs. Combining measures of social, economic, and environmental impacts can both improve the quality of sustainability reporting and facilitate environmental performance management. The proposed approach encourages companies and organizations focused on social and economic performance to address environmental performance too.

Comprehensive Research Proposal: Gender Identity & Organizational Silence: An IPA Study

MC
Amanda Jeppesen (Western Michigan University)
Organizational silence, a phenomenon by which employees withhold ideas, concerns, or feedback negatively impacts workplace culture, innovation, and employee well-being (Morrison & Milliken, 2000). While existing research explores organizational silence broadly, limited work addresses how gender identity shapes these experiences. This presents a gap in understanding how individuals of different gender identities interpret and navigate organizational silence and employee voice. This proposed phenomenological study would examine the lived experiences of employees of different gender identities and industries to understand how workplace silence manifests and how employees’ experiences differ. It addresses the research question “How do the experiences of organizational silence and employee voice in the workplace differ based on employee gender identity?” Using semi-structured interviews within the qualitative Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis methodology, this study investigates three core constructs: 1) how gender identity influences decisions to speak up or remain silent; 2) the psychological, social, and organizational factors shaping those decisions; and 3) how power structures mediate voice and silence. Findings will contribute to the fields of organizational silence, organizational leadership, inclusion, and workplace policy by identifying barriers to voice, mechanisms of silence, and informing strategies to foster more inclusive environments.

MC
AROUA ZNAZEN; Marie-Christine Chalus-sauvannet; Katia Lobre (Magellan Research Center); jean-fabrice lebraty (Magellan Research Center)
Social entrepreneurs are expected to address societal challenges while remaining financially viable, yet their hybrid objectives expose them to strong constraints such as limited funding access, misaligned funders’ criteria, and heightened uncertainty. Although entrepreneurial support organizations (e.g., incubators, accelerators) increasingly rely on consultants to guide social ventures, little is known about how this support shapes financial decision-making in practice. Drawing on Naturalistic Decision Making (NDM), this study examines how financing decisions unfold under strong constraints and uncertainty. We conducted 25 semi-structured interviews with 15 social entrepreneurs and 10 expert consultants and analyzed the data using the Gioia methodology. Our findings show that financing decisions are not linear or purely rational but emerge through situated sensemaking processes shaped by value tensions and evolving cues. We identify how consultants reconfigure financing trajectories by structuring strategic and financial reasoning, supporting interpretation of funding criteria and values alignment, and providing access to networks and legitimacy. This study extends NDM to social entrepreneurship and highlights entrepreneurial accompaniment as a key mechanism shaping financial decision-making in hybrid organizations.
