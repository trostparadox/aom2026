Generating AI-Deploying Organizations: Legitimation and Positioning Through AI Principles

CTO
Miikka Huhta (University of Jyväskylä); Anu Sivunen (University of Jyväskylä); Ward Van Zoonen
As artificial intelligence (AI) adoption accelerates, organizations must communicate its role, value, and governance to diverse stakeholders. A common response is to “get principled” and create AI principles that respond to stakeholder demands for transparent, fair, ethical, explainable and safe AI use, while fostering employees’ AI adoption. Based on 99 publicly available AI principles from FT Global 500 companies, this study examines how large corporations use these texts as institutional messages that declare organizational beliefs and rules for AI use. We examine how they legitimize and position organizations as AI deployers. The analysis shows that AI principles function as strategic business communication that blends ethical commitments, socio-technical safeguards, and stakeholder value propositions into three institutional positions. The study contributes to the discussions on institutional theory, organizational communication and AI governance by explaining how governance-related corporate texts construct legitimacy and organizational identity amid technological change in contemporary global corporate communication.

From Participation to Compliance: Rethinking Information Systems Adoption Under Asymmetric Power

CTO
Chuying Ding (Durham University); Spyros Angelopoulos (Durham University)
The literature largely presumes that Information System (IS) adoption rests on users’ discretionary engagement, shared sensemaking, and reciprocal adjustment. We challenge this assumption by examining how IS become normalized in organizations where authority is centralized and power is asymmetrically distributed across user groups. Through a qualitative case study in a Chinese state-owned enterprise (SOE) and drawing upon Normalization Process Theory (NPT) as a sensitizing framework, we show that normalization can instead unfold through an enforcement-based, role-differentiated logic. We find that managers—as normalization impellers—structure meaning, participation, and evaluation through hierarchical directives and formalized monitoring, while employees—as normalization implementers—enact normalization primarily through routinized compliance embedded in everyday work practices. Our work contributes to the IS adoption literature by i) challenging the engagement-centric assumption through theorizing enforcement-driven normalization; ii) extending NPT by conceptualizing normalization as a power-structured process unevenly enacted across impellers and implementers; iii) theorizing power as constitutive of IS adoption rather than as merely contextual condition. For practice, our findings suggest that effective IS adoption requires sensitivity to power distribution and a calibrated balance between enforcement-based governance and engagement-enabling arrangements.

From Fealty to Ontology to Fit-to-Problem: Reframing Explanatory Standards in Technology, Work And

CTO
Edvalter Holz (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute)
The technology–work–organization (TWO) literature is often evaluated through ontological adjudication – recasting prior studies as deterministic rather than assessing their explanatory payoffs. This article conducts an interpretive, periodized review (1950s–present) and synthesizes 320 contributions to develop a pragmatic evaluation heuristic. The heuristic operationalizes three criteria – internal coherence, contextual adequacy, and problem-solving value – to appraise explanations on their own warrants. Read non-teleologically, prior research aligns with the material regimes of its era and becomes more comparable when assessed against stated assumptions, boundary conditions, and mechanisms. The heuristic is illustrated through comparative assessments that relocate attention from metaphysical commitments to evidentiary alignment: whether mechanisms linking artefacts, routines, and outcomes are inspectable; whether socio-technical conditions are specified; and whether evidence can arbitrate between rival explanations. The article reframes ontological disputes as problem-solving variation anchored in concrete settings and offers a portable evaluation template for authors, reviewers, and editors in Communication, Digital Technology, and Organization research. Practically, it informs evidence-guided governance of digital controls (e.g., algorithmic management) by specifying minimum evidentiary requirements and transfer boundaries for claims about digitally mediated coordination, accountability, and control.

Community-Care Meets Technology

CTO
Swati Agarwal (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Vinayak Ram Tripathi; Pankaj Shah
This paper explores how frontline nurses engage with an evolving healthcare management information system and the technological mediation leading to recalibration of technological and hidden work, subsequently shaping organisational work. Using a workplace studies approach, we analyse thirty-one episodes collected as part of an ethnographic work conducted in a 250-bed forty-five-year-old hospital that provides quality patient care for free to the bottom of the pyramid tribal population. Our first level of analysis reveals that the introduction of HMIS leads to the emergence of three trajectories of technological work: a) nurses comply when the functionality is mandated by process, b) nurses exercise discretion and technological work is deprioritized, and c) reallocation of technological work happens. Our second level of analysis reveals that nurses engage in additional activities such as managing downtime, coordinating across departments, and adapting to backend incompleteness. Much of this work remains hidden unaccounted for in formal descriptions of technological use yet is essential for sustaining care delivery. We identify three distinct trajectories through which technological work unfolds. First, when organisational logic prevails, nurses comply with system requirements and absorb the additional work into routine practice. Second, when occupational logic prevails, nurses exercise discretion and are granted limited autonomy, particularly during peak loads or technological crises. Third, when moral order prevails, hidden work becomes visible and leads to the redistribution of responsibilities through task switching or task shifting across professional roles. Visibility of hidden work emerges as a critical factor shaping these trajectories.

AI as Facilitator or Expert? Role Orientation and Human Re-Engagement with an AI Teammate

CTO
Liangdongsheng Lyu (Northwestern University); Haiqi Henry Li (Northwestern University); Dingcheng Wang (Northwestern University); Evan Yicheng Le (Northwestern University); Leslie A. DeChurch (Northwestern University); Noshir Contractor (Northwestern University)
Most generative AI systems are currently used as individually invoked reactive tools, but emerging designs place AI at the core of team interactions. We study an AI “teammate” that is persistently embedded in small-group conversation and can initiate turns, and ask how the type of assistance it provides reshapes team interactions. In a randomized remote-collaboration experiment (63 ad hoc teams; 149 participants), teams completed survival-ranking and creativity tasks across a human-only round and two rounds with an AI teammate delivering either Taskwork (content guidance) or Teamwork (process facilitation) assistance. We model each utterance as a continuous-time, polyadic event and estimate hierarchical relational hyperevent models (RHEM) to capture how interaction history conditions subsequent addressing. Two behaviors index human re-engagement with the AI: reciprocation to the AI (direct reply to an AI-initiated message) and repetition to the AI (returning to the AI as the sole addressee). Participants generally reciprocated when addressed by the AI but avoided repeated solo addressing. Taskwork increased reciprocation to the AI, whereas Teamwork attenuated—and often reversed— reciprocation while strengthening avoidance of repeated solo addressing. We highlight assistance mode as a role-level design lever and provide event-level evidence on when AI is treated as a conversational partner versus background scaffolding.
