The Moral Curriculum: Ethics, Value Formation, and Character in Business Education

Session overview

Can management education cultivate genuine moral character, or does it merely perform ethics? This session confronts the knowing-doing gap at the heart of business ethics education: from the normative paradox that turns responsible management curricula against their own intentions, to structural blind spots that produce unintended ethical failures, to the challenge of teaching moral reasoning when AI mediates the very conditions of agency. Drawing on Stoic philosophy, experiential learning, and critical normative theory, these papers offer a rich, multi-perspectival toolkit for educators committed to genuine moral formation rather than surface compliance.

With Great Innovation Comes Great Responsibility…: Introducing Ethnovation as a Competency Framework

MED
David Nawrath (Technical University of Munich); Anne Tryba (Technical University of Munich)
Ethics and innovation are often taught in separate silos in management education, leaving undergraduates underprepared for the ethical tensions inherent in innovation work. This study addresses that divide by examining how universities can purposefully integrate ethical and innovative thinking in undergraduate management curricula. We conducted a qualitative case study of a business-focused undergraduate program at a leading U.S. university known for innovation and a strong ethics mission. Drawing on 703 syllabi, 19 faculty interviews, and 21 non-participatory classroom observations, we inductively developed the Ethnovation Framework. The framework specifies nine ethical-innovation competencies organized into three clusters (Perception and Recognition, Assessment and Evaluation, Motivation and Action) and maps them to three sequential learning-format clusters (Foundation, Experience, Reflection). By linking competencies to concrete pedagogical formats, the framework shows how ethics can be embedded within, rather than appended to, innovation education and offers a practice-oriented model for cultivating ethically engaged innovators.

Leader Reflexivity as Reflection on the Normative Paradox of Management Education

MED
Dirk C. Moosmayer (KEDGE Business School); Matthias Philip Huehn (Saint Vincent College); Anne Sebastian-Fischer (University of Navarra)
The Normative Paradox of Responsible Management Education established that business academics intend to imbue social values in their students but deliver programs, contents, and methods that actually promote selfishness and egoism. In this essay, we develop a values lens on dominant theories and education for management. We develop a better understanding of the reasons for this case of negative performativity. We propose that management academia engages with values too little. Staying within the rationalist paradigm of management enforces a logos that has one correct solution for every values-related problem. We therefore offer two propositions for business pedagogy. First, make students reflect on their values and discuss the values underlying the theories we teach. Second, engage in robust discussions about these values for students to discover their personal priorities. This approach will nourish students’ ability to apply their newly developed values ability by looking at low-level, everyday managerial situations. Our argument contributes first to the performativity debate by deepening our understanding of values’ role in the performative construction of managerial practice; and second suggestions of addressing the established normative paradox to the debate about management education’ desirable and undesirable outcomes.

Unintended Ethical Failures: An Experiential Exercise

MED
Yongha Kwon (Oberlin College)
Ethical failures in organizations often arise not from malicious intent but from structural and cognitive constraints that limit decision makers’ ethical awareness. In particular, specialized organizational structures and competition for attention across operational and ethical concerns can lead well intentioned actors to reach harmful outcomes without recognizing their ethical implications. Although these mechanisms are well documented in the literature, they are difficult to convey effectively in classroom settings using traditional teaching methods. This paper introduces an experiential negotiation exercise designed to help students recognize how organizational structures and processes shape ethical decision making in practice. By experiencing these constraints prospectively and in real time rather than through retrospective analysis, students can develop a more durable ability to recognize, evaluate, and respond to ethical issues in organizational decision making.

Balancing Worldview in Management Education: Wisdom-Based Ethical Leadership Lessons from Aurelius

MED
Jin Oh Bae (University of Hawaii at Mänoa); Dharm Prakash Sharma Bhawuk
Although calls to rehumanize leadership and improve character development in management education are rising, many instructors face practical challenges that hinder their ability to implement such methods. Instead of a complete curriculum overhaul, this paper highlights simple, repeatable practices that can be integrated into existing courses. Using Marcus Aurelius's philosophy, we introduce an approach to worldview cultivation that helps students develop a consistent framework for judgment and decision-making in complex managerial situations—a foundation for wise and ethical leadership. In practice, this approach offers instructors accessible entry points to build momentum and monitor student engagement. We also apply the SHIFT framework to identify strategic levers that educators and community leaders can use to foster lasting change. The framework also stresses that change occurs gradually, collectively, and from the inside out.

Ethics Education Under AI Mediation: A Threshold-Based Approach to MBA Pedagogy

MED
Bart Irwin Victor (Vanderbilt University); Benjamin Nathan Baki Alexander (California Polytechnic State University-San Luis Obispo)
The rapid integration of artificial intelligence into management alters the conditions under which ethical judgment is exercised, often rendering conventional ethics instruction temporally misaligned. This paper argues that the primary challenge AI poses is pedagogical rather than technological: when agency is mediated and responsibility diffused, ethical failure often occurs upstream of conscious deliberation. Drawing on behavioral ethics and professional formation, we reframe ethics education as a problem of capability formation—specifically identity stability, vigilance, and composure—that must precede ethical judgment. We develop a threshold-based pedagogical framework that specifies the sequencing required for these capacities to function under algorithmic opacity. The argument is illustrated through the design logic of an MBA elective, analyzing mechanisms that enforce sequencing by deliberately withholding judgment and voice. The paper contributes to Management Education and Development scholarship by articulating a formation-oriented approach to AI-mediated responsibility.
