Linking DEI and Sustainability: Implications for Environmental Performance

SIM
Sumit Kumar Maji (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Apalak Khatua (XLRI Xavier School of Management)
Firms operate in complex institutional environments characterised by multiple and sometimes competing pressures to demonstrate environmental responsibility. While prior research has largely emphasised the role of external regulation and market forces, less attention has been paid to how internal orientations, such as Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI), shape firms’ environmental behaviour. This study investigates whether and how firms’ DEI practices are linked to their environmental performance. Using panel data on firms across multiple countries, we find that a stronger DEI orientation is positively associated with superior environmental performance. We further show that this relationship varies with regulatory conditions. By examining DEI beyond its traditional role in social or human resource domains, this study contributes to broader debates on corporate sustainability by highlighting the relevance of internal organizational practices for environmental performance. The findings suggest that DEI matters, particularly in contexts where external regulatory enforcement is limited.

Toward Equitable and Inclusive Stakeholder Balancing: A Conceptual Framework

SIM
Žan Pirc (School of Economics and Business, University of Ljubljana); Metka Tekavcic (School of Economics and Business, University of Ljubljana)
Building on the tradition of balancing stakeholders and their often-conflicting claims, our conceptual research addresses the need for a stakeholder-centric, participatory, and network-aware approach to recognizing diverse stakeholder relationships. We acknowledge that stakeholders are not equally impacted by project implications, nor are they able to affect the project and its management in the same way. To foster deliberations on the equitable translation of these imbalances into project evaluation, giving a voice to those most affected, we conceptualize stakeholder balance – a framework for recognizing, analyzing, and communicating stakeholder relationships. We propose its nuanced participatory assessment by considering stakeholders’ mutual perspectives on these relationships, specifically their attributes, which are inherently unstable, subjective, and multilateral. To systematically visualize stakeholder relationships, we introduce the stakeholder balance network. Finally, we discuss the implications of stakeholder balancing for participatory project evaluations and project management, including when augmented by artificial intelligence, and outline further research opportunities. By understanding stakeholder relationships and their significance, (project) organizations can more effectively assess stakeholder influence on strategic decisions when responding to various stakeholder claims.

Why Are the Needy Still in Need? Social Concern, Family Values, and the Well-Being of Vulnerable Pop

SIM
Maria Jose Bosch (ESE Business School, Universidad de los Andes); Matias Braun
Despite similar levels of economic development, countries exhibit substantial variation in the relative well-being of vulnerable populations. We examine whether societal concern—aggregate attitudes toward vulnerable groups— affects their material poverty and psychological distress. Using data from the European Values Survey, we employ both country-level and individual-level analyses to establish robust evidence. Results reveal striking heterogeneity. Societal concern is associated with significantly lower poverty and unhappiness for the elderly and disabled, with effect sizes comparable to GDP per capita (0.4-0.7 standard deviations per standard deviation increase in concern). Effects are weaker for the unemployed and null for immigrants, suggesting the "cultural safety net" is bounded by deservingness perceptions and institutional access barriers. Government expenditure is primarily associated with reduction in material poverty while family importance primarily alleviates psychological distress, indicating distinct channels. Our findings show that cultural attitudes toward vulnerable populations matter as much as economic resources in determining their well-being, but solidarity remains selective rather than universal. Keywords: Subjective well-being; Poverty; Societal concern; Vulnerable populations; Elderly; Disabled; Unemployed; Immigrants; European Values Survey; Instrumental variables

Firm-Level ESG Rating Divergence Through a Measurement Theory Lens

SIM
Karolina Ludzinska (University of Warsaw)
This study reframes firm-level ESG rating divergence as a latent measurement problem. Drawing on measurement theory, it conceptualizes ESG rating divergence as a complex, multidimensional construct evaluated in terms of fitness for use, defined by validity, legitimacy, and functionality. Using a Rapid Literature Review (RLR), the study synthesizes recent empirical research on the measurement sources, implications, and responses to ESG rating divergence. The findings indicate that divergence arises primarily from measurement-related limitations, including heterogeneous construct definitions, indicator selection, weighting schemes, data sources, and corporate disclosure practices. ESG rating divergence has implications for capital markets, corporate governance, and investment decision-making by undermining comparability, reliability, and decision usefulness. The study contributes by positioning ESG rating divergence as an inherent measurement problem and by offering stakeholder-specific recommendations and directions for future research.

Dual Orchestration: Collective Action Between Global Value Chains & MSIs to Address Grand Challenges

SIM
Rakesh B. Sambharya (Rutgers University-Camden)
This paper develops an integrated theoretical framework explaining how multi-stakeholder initiatives (MSIs) interacting with global value chains (GVCs) can effectively address grand challenges through meaningful stakeholder engagement. We introduce dual orchestration—a novel concept involving the simultaneous coordination by GVC lead firms and MSI secretariats using GVC governance theory, orchestration theory, and institutional entrepreneurship. This paper develops a comprehensive theory of dual orchestration—a meta-governance process in which GVC lead firms and MSI secretariats jointly coordinate vertical and horizontal governance mechanisms to achieve synergistic and adaptive governance outcomes. Our framework highlights how these dual orchestrators shape GVC-MSI interactions that mitigate grand challenges, offering foundations for future research and practical interventions.
