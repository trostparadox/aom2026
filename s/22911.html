Cracks of Opportunity: Unveiling the Impact of Misconduct Disclosure on Workers’ Careers

OMT
Moksh Garg (Massachusetts Institute of Technology); Emilio J. Castilla (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)
Organizational misconduct and its disclosure can have lasting effects on the careers of key organizational members, yet little is known about its impact on lower-ranked employees. Prior research focuses primarily on elites, offering only a partial understanding of how disclosure shapes outcomes across the broader workforce. To extend this line of inquiry, we develop a dual-lens theoretical framework that conceptualizes misconduct disclosure as both discrediting and reformative. While disclosure can stigmatize employees through association with wrongdoing, it can also disrupt exploitative organizational arrangements, potentially creating opportunities for upward mobility among lower-ranked workers. Leveraging detailed firm-level microdata and Brazil’s major state-led anti-corruption crackdown as an exogenous shock, we find evidence consistent with our framework: upper white-collar workers experience significant wage declines and are more likely to exit the formal sector, whereas blue-collar employees who remain in or re-enter formal employment experience large and persistent wage gains. These effects hold across gender and racial groups and withstand multiple robustness checks, suggesting a wage-leveling mechanism triggered by the disruption of exploitative pay structures. Together, our findings reveal how misconduct disclosure, while punitive for some, can unintentionally advantage others by reshaping organizational hierarchies and reducing pay inequality.

Beyond Additive Intersectionality: How Marginalised Workers Navigate Organisational Evaluation

OMT
Ruqiya Arif (University of Cambridge); Lionel Paolella (University of Cambridge)
Despite increasing attention to diversity and inclusion, marginalised workers continue to experience marginalisation within organisations. Although intersectionality has become crucial for understanding these inequalities, organisational research has often treated it primarily as a descriptive tool rather than an explanatory theory. This paper advances intersectionality by conceptualising it as a dynamic evaluative system through which differences are judged, legitimised, or erased in organisational settings. We argue that intersecting identities do not yield uniform outcomes because marginalisation is enacted through organisational evaluation rather than solely through identity categories. To develop this perspective, we introduce two complementary concepts. Buffering intersectionality explains how specific configurations of intersecting identities can disrupt dominant evaluative prototypes and weaken marginalisation. Experiential intersectionality captures how marginalised workers exercise agency by navigating, reinterpreting, or working around organisational categories when their identities are not fully recognised. We further identify three evaluative mechanisms - evaluator cognition, the visibility and concealability of identity cues, and occupational role expectations - that shape when and how these outcomes emerge. Together, this framework repositions intersectionality as an explanatory theory of marginalisation, legitimacy, and agency within organisations.

Whose Cultural Conventions? Theorizing Moral Infringements in Cultural Entrepreneurship

OMT
Valeria Cavotta (Baruch College, CUNY)
The literature on cultural entrepreneurship has predominantly examined how organizational actors use culture to advance their goals, with scant attention to the negative implications such uses may impose on other constituencies. In contrast, this paper analyzes the use of community culture by non-community actors as an illustrative case of a problematic cultural deployment—one that produces harm across multiple registers. Drawing on semiotics and using examples of Native Hawaiian and African American cultural conventions, I theorize how cultural entrepreneurs flatten culture through resignifications that trivialize and/or disconnect community meanings and disregard the bond between the source community and its culture. These resignifications, in turn, illuminate the contestations and reclamation work by source communities seeking to address harms across multiple registers. The paper contributes to research on cultural entrepreneurship by advancing theory on moral infringements by cultural entrepreneurs.

Examining Conditions of Craft Organizing That Render Damage Salient & Craftspersons Experience of it

OMT
Shikha Dagar (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); K V Gopakumar (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); George M. Kandathil (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Pradyumana Khokle
In this paper, we examine craftspersons’ experience of damage that arise in the course of their work which we refer to as emergent damage. While damage has received scant attention within the craft literature, we study the conditions under which a craft is organized that lends prominence to the question of damage within it. To do so, we examine the work of repair which has been regarded as a craft. We draw on the setting of a mobile phone repair cluster in India and the work carried out by repair workers there. We employ a field ethnography to study the damage that arises in the craft of repair. Using systematic inductive data analysis, we arrive at the material and relational conditions of craft organizing in and through which repairer’s experience damage as an inherent part of their work.

Hit or Miss? The Individual Career Effects of Marginal Ranking Entry in the Music Industry

OMT
Yuan Shi (Cornell University); Jaewoo Lee (Cornell University)
This study examines how marginal entry into an authoritative ranking may shape mobility and exploration in an individual’s creative career. To address this question, we develop two competing theoretical accounts: the career broadening account, which posits that ranking entry expands resource access and opportunities and thus facilitates mobility and exploration in one’s career, and the performance preservation account, which argues that rankings reinforce existing organizational and categorical affiliations, thereby constraining these outcomes. Leveraging the inclusion threshold for Billboard’s Hot 100 chart, the benchmark for commercial performance in the U.S. music industry, we implement a regression discontinuity design that compares artists debuting just inside the Hot 100 with those narrowly missing the cutoff. Our analysis of a novel dataset covering three decades of Billboard chart entries reveals that the consequences of Hot 100 entry are contingent on individuals’ organizational affiliations: chart entry constrains mobility for artists affiliated with major labels but boosts mobility for indie-affiliated artists. It also suppresses genre exploration for those who remain with their incumbent labels. Overall, our work offers partial support for both proposed accounts under distinctive boundary conditions and highlights how third-party rankings influence micro-level labor market dynamics by reconfiguring individuals’ career trajectories.
