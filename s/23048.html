The Making Of 'Uncomfortably Numb' Practitioners: Identity Work In Human Rights And Business

CMS
Alix Blanchard; Rajiv Maher (Monterrey Institute of Technology)
This paper examines how managerialism shapes practitioner subjectivities in ethically charged organizational work. Drawing on a collaborative autoethnography of our experiences implementing Business and Human Rights (BHR) across two contexts, a European human rights institute and a French multinational corporation we develop the concept of uncomfortable numbness defined as an affective condition produced by structural constraint, in which subjects are rendered spectators to harms they cannot unsee and cannot meaningfully contest, transforming awareness into a site of suffering rather than action. We identify three mechanisms through which managerialism produces this condition: epistemic injustice, which privileges managerial expertise while marginalizing rightsholders' knowledge and critical perspectives; proceduralism, which elevates compliance processes over substantive outcomes; and reductionism-incrementalism, which simplifies complex harms into manageable metrics. Theoretically, we contribute to Critical Management Studies by specifying how managerialism operates affectively and ethically, not only cognitively. We extend Foucauldian organization studies by showing how askesis (ethical self-work) and parrhesia (truth-telling) remain active yet are systematically neutralized through mundane organizational techniques. Methodologically, we demonstrate collaborative autoethnography's power to trace subjection and subjectivation dynamics across temporal and institutional distance. The concept of uncomfortable numbness offers a framework for understanding why ethically committed practitioners become worn down without being fully converted, with implications for sustainability, internal activism, and value-driven work.

Silent Collapse: Moral Crisis and Precarious Subjectivity in China's White-Collar Workforce

CMS
Wanying Zheng (University of Essex); Rashedur Chowdhury (University of Essex); Young-Ah Kim (University of Essex)
In the contexts of white-collar workers in a leading Chinese electric vehicle (EV) manufacturer, we develop a critical perspective on how ideological surveillance and precarious working conditions jointly erode individual morality and subjectivity. We enrich the concept of surveillance with a pervasive mode of cultural, ideology, and moral governance that compels employees to internalize organizational values which challenge their own ethical framework. This moral dissonance gradually undermines the individual selfhood, pushing them into a condition of silent collapse, an ethical precarity marked by emotional exhaustion, meaninglessness, and withdrawal from social life. We argue that this process results in collective individual isolation, where suffering is privatized, and solidarity becomes impossible. Our study highlights the urgency for business ethics scholars to reconsider how organizations ethically engage with white-collar workers under highly controlled and surveillant environments and inform our understanding of marginalization as a structural consequence of capitalist governance.

Putting the 'Cult' in Organizational Culture: A New History of Organizational Culture Management

CMS
Todd Bridgman (Victoria University of Wellington); Ryan Haigh (Victoria University of Wellington)
Organizational culture is an important topic for those managing organizations as well as scholars of management and organization. It is often referred to as a critical part of what leaders do in organizations – they create and manage organizational culture. While it offers the promise of cohesive and high performing organizations where the hearts and minds of employees are aligned, critical scholars argue that organization cultures can become cults, making culture management a potentially sinister tool for enhanced organizational control. To better understand why that is, we present a new history of organizational culture management, focusing on the research of Edgar Schein and Margaret Thaler Singer, who investigated ‘brainwashing’ techniques used by Chinese communists on US prisoners of war in Korea in the 1950s. Both drew on that research later in the careers to achieve positions of prominence. Singer became a leading anti-cult campaigner and Schein arguably the most influential scholar-practitioner on organizational culture. Their theorization of the social influence processes used on American prisoners became the blueprint for understanding how people get trapped in dangerous cults, as well as for developing strong corporate cultures. Recovering these origins illuminates the ethics of highly engineered and controlling organizational cultures.

Walls and Organization: Border Crossings, Art and Interfaces in the Organization of Peace in Belfast

CMS
Garance Christine Marechal (University of Liverpool); Stephen Andrew Linstead (University of York)
Walls have a long history of dividing conflicted territories, countries, regions and even cities. The Berlin Wall is perhaps the most famous single fortified dividing wall, but the city of Belfast in Northern Ireland is an example of a fragmented city in which there are many walls and forms of “defensive architecture” constructed during the Troubles (armed civil conflict from 1969-1998) but continuing since. Indeed the number of walls has increased dramatically since the Good Friday Peace Agreement of 1998. In this paper we look at Belfast as an example of the multifaceted nature of such walls which on the one hand screen off one community (Catholic republicans) from the other (Protestant loyalists) but also act psychologically and sociologically as screens on which fantasies of the other and one’s own community identity are projected. Proximity to these walls – which can be dangerous – creates “interface communities” which form the anxious and fearful fringes of their host communities. These communities are often worried that their identity and even existence will be threatened or betrayed by too much contact with the other and symbolic communication occurs to protect against this through murals on the walls – what Derrida calls the establishment of “auto-co-immunity”. In this paper we use extensive visual archive materials and our own field photographs to explore the evolution of two particular walls and two artists as part of the long process of healing bitter local post-colonial conflicts and establishing global resonances to secure a peaceful future for the region without walls.

Forgiveness as Discipline: A Critical Interpretative Analysis of Academic Contrapower Harassment

CMS
Devi Akella (Albany State University)
This paper integrates Nietzschean and Foucauldian frameworks within a critical interpretive qualitative approach to examine how power and control can lie embedded within acts of forgiveness in cases of academic counter-power harassment. Theoretical arguments raised in the paper are empirically supported through eleven semi-structured interviews with faculty members who had experienced student-initiated harassment. Empirical findings suggest that forgiveness can be conceptualized as a political act- a moralized technique of power that stabilizes institutional order and reinstates the prevailing workplace status quo. Instead of revolving around concepts such as humility and patience, forgiveness operates as a disciplinary practice that protects hierarchical order and relieves wrongdoers of accountability. The objective is moral closure rather than repairing harm.
