Pollution Hell Hypothesis: Why Stringent Environmental Regulations Attract Producers of Complex Good

IM
Soni Jha (Purdue University Fort Wayne); Rimi Zakaria (University of Wisconsin-Whitewater)
This study examines how environmental policy capacity, the institutional ability to develop, implement, and enforce effective environmental policies, shapes multinationals’ location decisions. The ‘pollution haven hypothesis’ argues that lax environmental regulations attract foreign direct investment (FDI) by lowering compliance costs. We argue that this overlooks two critical factors: multinationals vary in their learning capabilities, and demanding regulatory environments can create strategic advantages. Drawing on organizational learning theory, we propose the ‘pollution hell hypothesis,’ arguing that leading manufacturers of complex products strategically seek locations with high environmental policy capacity. ‘Leading’ multinationals possess monopolistic learning advantages and high absorptive capacity, enabling them to learn faster from demanding regulations, widening the competitive gap with less capable rivals. However, geopolitical risk (interstate tensions that create policy uncertainty and operational disruptions) undermines learning returns and reverses the attractiveness of locations with high environmental policy capacity. We test our theory by analyzing 4,256 multinationals in the global automotive industry from 2003 to 2024, and find that leading firms strategically self-select into demanding regulatory environments that match and reward their learning capabilities. We contribute to institutional theory by distinguishing between the effects of within-country institutional quality (environmental policy capacity) and geopolitical risk (interstate conflicts and tensions threatening that country). Our study implies that even countries with strong institutions can fail to attract FDI when geopolitical risks rise because strong domestic institutions cannot insulate firms’ FDI from the pernicious effects of geopolitical risks.

Environmental Policy Uncertainty and Limits to Green Firm-Specific Advantage Transfer

IM
Anna Kehl (University of Bremen); Gunnar Leymann (University of Bremen); Peter M. Bican (IMC University of Applied Sciences of Krems)
Multinational enterprises are central to the global diffusion of green technologies, yet they remain selective in transferring environmental firm-specific advantages abroad. We argue that policy dynamism constitutes an undertheorized deterrent to green technology transfer, distinct from policy stringency or distance. Conceptualizing cross-border patent filings as real options, we theorize that dynamism can collapse option value by undermining the structured criteria firms need to evaluate investment decisions, producing a state of strategic inaction we term green paralysis. Analyzing 59 large MNEs' green patent filings across 47 countries over 14 years (2009–2023), we find that a one-standard-deviation increase in policy dynamism reduces patent transfers by 13 percent; comparable in magnitude to policy stringency. Contrary to expectations, financial slack, technological capabilities, and host-country experience offer no buffer; high-performing firms react more negatively to dynamism. These findings extend the FSA-CSA framework by identifying dynamism as an orthogonal dimension of country-specific disadvantage, establish boundary conditions for real options theory in non-market contexts, and suggest that policy credibility may matter as much as policy ambition for attracting green investment.
