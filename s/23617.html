Session overview

This session examines how neurodivergent employees experience inclusion, stigma, and organizational support across different work contexts. The papers explore workplace reactions to neurominority employees, autistic adults' lived experiences, self-narrative as a route to inclusion, and how stigma shapes attitudes and practices in Saudi Arabia. Together, they advance understanding of neurodiversity as a critical dimension of workplace inclusion and highlight both structural barriers and employee-driven pathways toward more inclusive organizations.

The Challenges Facing Female Talent Development: A Potential Roadmap

DEI
Irene Campos García; Eva Pelechano Barahona; Carmen De la Calle Durán
The purpose of this work is to identify the challenges firms will be facing over the coming years in matters of female talent development. Social Role Theory, Role Congruity Theory, Social Capital Theory, Organisational Justice Theory, Self-efficacy Theory and Stereotype Thread Theory provide the underpinnings for the research involving a sample of 306 Spanish management staff through the application of a collective AI method in a two-stage analysis. The challenges are grouped into the following two main areas: 1) equal opportunities and the removal of invisible barriers, including the challenges associated with meritocracy, biases, discrimination, the glass ceiling, and the gender distribution across sectors; and 2) more collaborative working environments, where the challenges involve the work-life balance, shared accountability, a culture of teamwork, self-confidence, and the maternal wall. The most widely accepted initiatives prompt a series of winning strategies and key proposals. This research constitutes an opportunity for academic research on a theoretical and methodological level. In practical terms, it may help to transform structural challenges into strategic opportunities and galvanise the removal of gender differences, driving progress in the Sustainable Development Goals SDG 5 —Gender equality— and SDG 8 —Decent work and economic growth—.

Shattering the Glass Ceiling or Fixing the Broken Rung: Where are the Benefits of Work-Life Programs

DEI
Yang Yang (Rowan University); Frank (Guoliang) Jiang (Carleton University); Alison M. Konrad (University of Western Ontario)
Although work-life programs (WLPs) have the potential to benefit women in management (WIM) by offering various forms of flexibility, research findings are often equivocal. Additionally, it is unclear whether the benefits of WLPs are more pronounced at higher or lower management levels. Building on the ideal worker norm and stigmatization theory, we hypothesize contingencies that influence the relationships between WLPs and WIM. The results, obtained by analyzing a panel of over 1,000 public firms, indicate that WLPs are beneficial for promoting women to executive positions, but not to senior or junior level management positions. Considering the normalization of WLPs during the COVID-19 pandemic, WLPs had a more positive impact on promoting women into executive and senior management positions during the pandemic than before. The effectiveness of WLPs is also practice contingent, with practices providing flexibility in location, time variation, and work continuity exhibiting greater effectiveness than practices involving reduced work volume. This study provides insights into the ongoing discussions regarding the WLPs-WIM relationships and the debate between the “glass ceiling” and the “broken rung” at the bottom as barriers to women’s careers, thereby opening up future research opportunities.

Invisible Boundaries: Within-Gender Faultlines in Women’s Executive Advancement

DEI
Ji Young Jung (Ewha Womans University); Seong Ji Park (Ewha Womans University)
This study examines gender inequality within organizations by focusing on the internal structural configuration of gender groups rather than on individual characteristics or overall gender composition. Drawing on the concept of faultlines, the study conceptualizes invisible boundaries as structural alignments of attributes that shape patterns of interaction and opportunity distribution. Using firm-level data from a nationally representative corporate panel, the study separately measures faultline strength within male and female groups and analyzes its association with women’s representation among executives. The results reveal asymmetric effects across gender groups. Higher faultline strength within the male group is associated with an increase in the number of women executives, suggesting a weakening of male-centered opportunity monopolization. In contrast, lower faultline strength within the female group is associated with higher female executive representation, indicating that internal cohesion among women facilitates access to upper-level positions. By demonstrating that faultline strength operates differently within male and female groups, this study offers a structural perspective on how invisible boundaries contribute to organizational inequality.

Gaining Status but Not Influence: A Gendered Paradox in Servant Leadership

DEI
Karryna Madison (The Australian National University); Zuzanna Staniszewska (Kozminski University in Warsaw)
Servant leadership is widely assumed to foster positive follower responses through reciprocal processes. Yet, do all servant leaders foster positive reciprocation equally? Across two studies, we investigate how servant leadership translates into follower felt obligation through perceived leader status and whether these processes differ for women and men leaders. In Study 1, a between-subjects experiment provides evidence for the proposed mediation pathway by showing that servant leadership increases follower felt obligation via leader status. Study 2, a time-lagged field survey, extends these findings by examining how leader gender moderates this relational mechanism. Results indicate that servant leadership increases perceived leader status more strongly for women leaders, whereas perceived status predicts follower felt obligation more strongly for men leaders. These findings challenge assumptions that gender effects on leadership outcomes are uniformly positive or negative and instead reveal that gender shapes different components of the influence process. Overall, the research advances theory by demonstrating that social exchange relationships are interpreted through gendered expectations and that the relational returns of servant leadership vary across leader identities.

The Gendered Effects of Leader Beliefs: How Leadership Schemas Influence Women Followers’ Fit

DEI
Sarah Kostanski (University of Massachusetts Lowell); Karoline Evans (University of Massachusetts Lowell)
Women remain underrepresented and less likely to persist in male-dominated STEM fields, in part because they experience lower perceptions of fit. Although prior research has shown that leader behaviors can shape women’s fit, less is known about the beliefs that give rise to those behaviors. Drawing on social cognition theory and the concept of relational schemas, we investigate how leaders’ beliefs about leadership and followership roles relate to women followers’ self-concept, social, and goal fit. Using a mixed-methods design in an entry-level engineering course, we collected multisource survey data at two points in time and conducted semi-structured interviews. Quantitative results show that leaders’ leadership structure schema (LSS) and passive role orientation (PRO)—but not co-production role orientation (CRO)—were positively related to women’s fit over time relative to men. Qualitative analyses reveal behavioral pathways through which these relational schemas manifested: more shared LSS was reflected in empowering, fair, and inclusive leadership behaviors that fostered women’s self-concept and social fit, whereas high PRO was reflected in directive, structured behaviors that supported women’s goal fit. We integrate these findings with Schmader’s gender-fit framework to propose a model explaining how leader beliefs shape leader behaviors and, in turn, women followers’ experiences of fit in male-dominated fields.
