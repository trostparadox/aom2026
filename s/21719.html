Taking the Leap: What Metaphors Reveal About Women’s Transitions Into Entrepreneurship

Authors: Stephanie Elizabeth Raible University of Delaware; Karen Williams-Middleton Chalmers University of Technology

Session overview

Metaphors are a way to model one’s thoughts (Lakoff, 1993), serving as a mechanism to elevate the unconscious impressions, intuitions, intentions, and feelings individuals hold (Kahneman, 2011). In entrepreneurship research, metaphors help bridge lived experience into familiar conversational frames and offer insight into entrepreneurial identity construction. Rather than homogenizing women’s entrepreneurial experiences relative to men, this study focuses exclusively on women entrepreneurs to explore how metaphors of transition into entrepreneurship shape understandings of nascent entrepreneurial careers. This study draws on 53 one-on-one, semi-structured interviews with women in the United States within the first 3.5 years of their full-time entrepreneurial careers. Participants described their careers before and after transitioning into entrepreneurship and constructed metaphors representing themselves across these phases. Metaphor-related transcript segments were analyzed using an inductive Gioia methodology. Preliminary findings suggest that women narrate their transitions using metaphors that reflect shifts in purview, agency, and identity. Pre-transition metaphors reflected both constraint and stability, while post-transition metaphors emphasized creativity, freedom, and expanded perspective alongside ongoing uncertainty. These findings highlight purview as a critical yet underexplored dimension of entrepreneurial transition and suggest implications for mentoring and preparing aspiring women entrepreneurs.
