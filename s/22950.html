Beyond Experience: Learning Systems in Mergers and Acquisitions

ODC
Jana Sophie Herbig (Toulouse School of Management)
Research on mergers and acquisitions (M&A) has emphasized experience accumulation, learning curves, and discrete learning practices to explain why some firms develop acquisition capabilities while others do not. Yet empirical findings remain mixed, and firms with comparable acquisition experience often display markedly different learning outcomes. We argue that these differences cannot be fully understood by focusing on experience volume or isolated practices alone. Instead, they reflect deeper variation in how firms organize learning from acquisitions as organizational systems. Drawing on organizational learning, capability theory, and configurational perspectives on organizational design, we reconceptualize M&A learning as a firm-level learning system, a patterned arrangement through which acquisition experience is retained, transferred, and reused over time. We develop this argument through an abductive, comparative qualitative study based on 29 semi-structured interviews with M&A, strategy, and integration actors across 20 large European firms, complemented by extensive secondary data. Using within-case analysis and systematic cross-case comparison, we identify recurring and internally coherent patterns in how firms organize acquisition learning. Our analysis reveals four distinct M&A learning system configurations: an institutionalized learning execution system, an expert-centered learning system, a practice-based learning system, and a decoupled formal learning system. These configurations differ along two core dimensions: the locus of learning (where learning primarily resides) and the degree of institutionalization (how durably learning is embedded and reproduced). Rather than representing performance rankings, the configurations constitute ideal types that capture alternative, internally coherent ways of organizing M&A learning, each associated with characteristic strengths, vulnerabilities, and learning dynamics. We show that learning outcomes such as the retention, scalability, adaptability, or fragility of acquisition-related knowledge are systemic properties of these configurations rather than direct consequences of experience or specific learning mechanisms. Viewed through a capability lens, learning systems shape whether acquisition capabilities accumulate, decay, or remain person-dependent over time. Highly institutionalized systems support durable capability development by embedding learning beyond individuals, whereas expert-centered and practice-based systems enable flexibility but face scalability and retention challenges. Decoupled formal systems illustrate how learning can be symbolically institutionalized yet weakly enacted. By shifting the unit of analysis from experience or practices to learning systems, this study advances M&A learning research, contributes to organizational learning theory by conceptualizing learning failure as systemic, and grounds acquisition capability development in organizational design choices. For managers, the findings highlight that effective learning from acquisitions requires diagnosing and deliberately designing learning systems rather than adopting isolated best practices.

Paradox in Serial Acquisitions Integration:How Haier Balances Tension in Building a Health Ecosystem

ODC
Yumeng Fu (Renmin University of China); KAI ZENG (Renmin University of China); Yuxi Cao (Renmin University of China)
This study explores how cross-border enterprises dynamically balance the paradox between autonomy and control during post-acquisition integration within a serial acquisition aimed at ecosystem construction. Through a longitudinal case study of Haier’s Great Health Ecosystem, we identify a three-stage evolution path of integration strategy: from “Regulatory Balancing” in the industry entry phase, to “Decentralized Balancing” during expansion, and finally to “Symbiotic Balancing” in the upgrade phase. We further reveal that this strategic evolution is fundamentally driven by the transformation of the core “autonomy-control” paradox itself, which morphs from an institutional logic conflict, to a resource-strategy conflict, and ultimately into a systemic governance conflict across stages. Employing a structuration theory lens, we uncover the underlying mechanism: the enterprise’s power structure is continuously reproduced through cycles of episodic actions and routinization, evolving from equity-based control, to scenario definition, to value-empowerment, and finally to ecosystem governance authority. This “path–driver–mechanism” framework contributes to M&A integration literature by shifting focus from static mode selection to dynamic evolution, advances paradox theory by highlighting the paradox’s role as a dynamic driver, and enriches strategic power perspectives by explicating the recursive process of power restructuring in complex acquisitions.

Integration in an Interdisciplinary Research Team: A Qualitative Case Study

ODC
Colleen Cuddy (Stanford University)
Integration, a process of blending ideas, perspectives, and methods, is fundamental to interdisciplinary scientific work; yet relatively little is known about how research teams actively enact integration in their day-to-day work and what drives integration. This qualitative case study examines an academic translational science team that operates across multiple STEMM (science, technology, engineering, mathematics, medicine) disciplines, exploring what contributes to and detracts from successful integration. Using the critical incident technique (CIT), 65 effective and ineffective integration incidents were collected from 13 team members, supplemented by a group reflection session and document analysis. Analysis revealed three aggregate drivers of interdisciplinary team integration: being together, being intentional, and knowing each other, supported by six themes. Findings show that social integration is a foundational process that enables cognitive, knowledge, and conceptual integration. The article discusses implications for small-group theory, interdisciplinary team effectiveness, and the intentional design of scientific team environments.

A Multilevel Model of Knowledge Transfer in Personnel Rotation: Linking Sharing and Receiving

ODC
Jin Oh Bae (University of Hawaii at Mänoa)
Knowledge transfer is regarded as important strategic asset for organizations, yet evidence shows that transfer outcomes are uneven across settings. While decades of work in knowledge management have documented how organizations create, share, store, and transfer knowledge, research has often developed along parallel paths. As a result, comparatively little few studies examine how individual sharing behavior translates into group-level transfer. This paper develops a multilevel model of knowledge transfer in personnel rotation contexts to address this gap. At the individual level, a 2×2 typology of knowledge sharers integrates the theory of planned behavior and self-determination theory to distinguish contributors based on intention strength and motivation quality. At the group level, the paper introduces group knowledge receptivity, defined by relational openness to outside input and task-related readiness to process and apply it, yielding four distinct group learning climates. Crossing the two typologies identifies configurations under which knowledge shared by rotating members is more or less likely to be received, integrated, and sustained in collective routines. The framework clarifies when sharing is sufficient for transfer, when it is neutralized, and when it is likely to be lost.
