Moving Money Around: Mobile Energy Storage and the Value of Geospatial Flexibility

OSCM
Vishrut Rana (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania); Christian Kaps (Harvard Business School); Serguei Netessine (Wharton School)
Battery storage has emerged as a key tool to store renewable electricity and address temporal and spatial mismatches between electricity supply and demand. However, storage developers face diminishing and uncertain returns. In this paper, we first demonstrate how local competition and transient transmission capacity congestion lead to low and uncertain returns for Stationary Energy Storage System (SESS) operators. Using data from the PJM Interconnection and a staggered diff-in-diff approach, we estimate that an additional storage installation reduces existing SESS's annual profit at that location by $7,820 per megawatt on average. We then estimate how Mobile Energy Storage Systems (MESS) -- an innovative business model that allows grid-scale batteries to be relocated -- can increase operators' returns and improve grid-level congestion by providing geospatial flexibility. We show that, even when relocation costs are high, MESS's profit consistently outperforms even the best-sited SESS by 11-47%. Our findings highlight how leveraging mobile storage inventory in the energy transition can help storage developers, grid planners, and policymakers to accelerate storage build-out, renewable integration, and grid flexibility.

Extending Power–Dependence Theory in Multi-Business Supply Chain Organizations

OSCM
Sparsh Sinha (University of Arkansas); Joe Roh (Texas Christian University); Travis Tokar (Texas Christian University); Matthew Alan Waller (University of Arkansas)
The consolidation of supply chain management (SCM) activities into centralized supply chain management organizations (SCMOs) promises efficiency gains but often creates tension with strategic business units (SBUs) that seek autonomy and responsiveness. We examine these intraorganizational tensions through the lens of power–dependence theory (PDT), integrating insights from differentiation–integration theory to explain how structural asymmetries influence conflict, power activation, and performance outcomes in SCMO–SBU relationships. Using an abductive multiple-case design across 7 multi-business firms, we elaborate PDT to account for contextual contingencies that influence whether formal structural power is realized. Our findings confirm the predicted relationships between organizational differentiation, conflict, and power activation, but also reveal that performance outcomes depend on the presence of integration mechanisms that channel power-balancing operations constructively. We further identify power-imbalance triggers—firm-specific conditions such as financial slack, executive orientation, and regulatory pressure—that determine the direction and intensity of power realization. Together, these insights extend PDT to intraorganizational contexts, linking power asymmetries directly to performance outcomes and demonstrating how integration mechanisms and contextual triggers govern the enactment of power within shared-service supply chain structures. Implications for theory, organizational design, and managerial governance are discussed.

Supply Chain Alignment as a Dialectical Process: Evidence from the European Car Industry

OSCM
Dawid Pasternak (Chalmers University of Technology); Ala Arvidsson (Chalmers University of Technology); Kostas Selviaridis (Lancaster University)
Supply chain alignment is essential for coordinating goals and operations across interconnected organisations, yet our understanding of how alignment dynamics unfold over time remains limited. In this paper we examine how alignment evolves across interconnected supply chain relationships over time by adopting a dialectical lens to explain the underlying dynamics. We conduct a longitudinal case study of a three-tier automotive electronics supply chain, comprising one European OEM, three Tier-1 suppliers, and one Tier-2 semiconductor manufacturer, during the 2020-2023 semiconductor shortage crisis and beyond. Our analysis reveals four distinct dialectical cycles through which alignment mechanisms emerged, interacted, and transformed via recurring patterns of conflict and synthesis. We find that alignment is a dynamic, recursive process: each synthesis simultaneously resolves immediate tensions while establishing conditions for subsequent conflicts. We also identify three distinct conflict patterns in supply chains: dyadic conflicts with triadic implications, genuine three-position conflicts, and alliance-based conflicts; that are not captured in traditional dyadic analyses. Our study contributes to supply chain alignment literature by demonstrating its processual nature, and extends how dialectical theory is applied to explain buyer-supplier relationship dynamics in supply chain contexts.

Distant Mines, Local Risks: The Impact of Deregulation on Fissured Workforce Risk Management Systems

OSCM
Jennifer Nowicki (Pennsylvania State U); Brian Lee (The Pennsylvania State University); Kevin Linderman (The Pennsylvania State University)
The mining of conflict minerals is associated with regional violence and forced labor, which poses operational risks to supply chains that involve fissured workforces. The Dodd-Frank Act includes a conflict mining provision that requires firms to report these risks, however this was partially deregulated in 2017. Through analyzing SEC documents over a 10-year span via natural language processing, we explore firms' risk management systems (RMS) and determine how closely they adhere to an international conflict mining framework, thereby creating a novel ‘fidelity’ measure based on system-framework alignment. Through a series of empirical analyses, we find that RMS fidelity increases post-deregulation among firms that continue to participate voluntarily. Furthermore, firms with voluntary, high-fidelity risk management systems realize several firm benefits post-deregulation including increased firm financial health and market valuation. We also find that high-fidelity systems mitigate the negative reputational impacts of prior sustainability and fissured labor-related risks on firm performance outcomes. We then conclude with a series of managerial insights for firms and directions for future research.
