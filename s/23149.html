Personifying the Faceless Boss: How Algorithmic Management Developers Design for Legitimacy

CTO
Omer Hacker (Columbia University); Lior Zalmanson (Tel Aviv University)
Algorithmic management systems are often presented as “faceless bosses” that govern through data-driven rules rather than human discretion. While such systems have been extensively examined in platform work, less is known about how they are designed and embedded in traditional employment contexts, where managerial authority is an essential aspect of the organization. Drawing on a 10-month ethnography of a retail technology startup, we examined how developers turned an initially autonomous, data-driven system into a hybrid, personified form of algorithmic management. We define personification as the systematic embedding of an algorithmic management system within the unique persona of a specific human manager. The findings showed that three interdependent mechanisms were implemented to generate personification: adopting a managerial playbook that aligned analytics with individual preferences, wearing a human “face” through shared avatars and communication styles, and inhabiting an organizational position within the existing hierarchy. Across these mechanisms, we identify a legitimacy–autonomy paradox: the system became more influential as its analytical independence decreased, and it gained compliance through institutional legitimacy rather than technical autonomy. These findings contribute to IS research by (1) reframing personification as an institutional embedding strategy that differs in many ways from anthropomorphism or personalization, (2) showing how developer practices are shaped by authority structures in employment contexts, and (3) theorizing the legitimacy–autonomy paradox as a boundary condition for algorithmic management effectiveness. This analysis advances sociotechnical accounts of algorithmic governance and offers design guidance for embedding algorithmic systems in organizations where managerial authority is culturally expected to be embodied.

Reconfiguring Infrastructures of Knowing for AI Integration: A Comparative Study

CTO
Korinzia Toniolo (Stockholm School of Economics); Bomi Kim; Stefan Haefliger (Stockholm School of Economics)
Organizations increasingly adopt artificial intelligence (AI) to automate routine and data-intensive forms of knowledge work. Yet, even in settings assumed to be analytically tractable and data-rich, AI systems frequently struggle to generate stable and actionable outcomes once deployed in practice. Drawing on a matched-pair, longitudinal qualitative study of AI implementations in diagnostic radiology and supply chain inventory management, this paper examines why such difficulties persist and how organizations respond to them. We conceptualize AI systems as algorithmic assemblages embedded within existing infrastructures of knowing—historically sedimented sociotechnical arrangements that organize how data are produced, validated, and transformed into actionable knowledge. Through comparative analysis, we show that AI integration repeatedly surfaces epistemic misalignments between algorithmic outputs and organizational ways of knowing. Empirically, we identify three recurring forms of data–knowing gaps: completion gaps, where data assumed to be sufficient lack critical contextual information; disambiguation gaps, where algorithmic outputs remain analytically plausible but ambiguous for action; and adaptation gaps, where environmental volatility undermines the continued relevance of historical data. Rather than resolving these misalignments through technical refinement alone, organizations stabilize AI use by selectively reconfiguring their infrastructures of knowing. In both cases, this process culminates in the institutionalization of automation thresholds—organizationally negotiated boundaries that delimit when algorithmic outputs can be acted upon autonomously and when human expertise must prevail. While these thresholds take different forms across contexts, they serve a common function: constraining algorithmic authority to preserve the workability of organizational knowing under uncertainty. By shifting attention from AI’s epistemic limits to infrastructural adaptation, this study advances research on AI and knowledge work and highlights infrastructures of knowing as critical sites through which AI becomes actionable in organizations.

Designing Algorithmic Management: A Process Perspective on Reconfiguration Cycles

CTO
Henning Schmid (Technical University of Darmstadt); Armin Alizadeh (Technical University of Darmstadt); Alexander Benlian (Technical University of Darmstadt); Martin Wiener (TU Dresden)
Algorithmic management (AM) transforms workers’ everyday lives, yet the designers whose choices shape these experiences remain largely out of view. Based on a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with ten AM designers and a triangulated analysis of 128 engineering, media, and organizational artifacts, as well as five SEC Form S-1 registration statements, we develop a grounded theory of AM system (re)configuration. Our theory explains how development imperatives, reconfiguration triggers, and decision heuristics jointly shape the continuous (re)configuration process of AM systems. Our findings show that (re)configuration is driven by often competing performance optimization and legitimacy assurance imperatives and initiated by risk- and opportunity-oriented triggers. In responding to these triggers, AM designers rely on decision heuristics that balance normative constraints with strategic considerations, enacted through recurring cycles of temporary equilibrium, misalignment, adjustment, and stabilization. By examining the (re)configuration process of AM systems, this study adds a designer perspective alongside the workers’ perspective to AM research and shifts attention from AM outcomes to the ongoing design process that ultimately shapes how workers perceive AM systems.

Energizing or Exhausting? The Dynamic Effects of Algorithmic Management on Employee Work Engagement

CTO
Ya Luo (Central South University); Jiyuan Mei (Central South University)
Algorithmic management, defined as the automated allocation, monitoring, and evaluation of work, is often portrayed as either an engagement enhancer or a source of exhaustion. We argue that it is both. Drawing on adaptation theory, we propose that employees’ engagement responses to algorithmic management are inherently dynamic, shaped by temporal adjustment processes, cognitive appraisals, and proactive behaviors. Across two studies, we test this dynamic framework. Study 1, a three-wave field survey (N = 303), shows that algorithmic management initially increases work engagement, but this effect diminishes over time as employees adapt. Study 2, a controlled experiment (N = 250), demonstrates that approach-oriented digital job crafting mitigates the declining effect of algorithmic management and amplifies its positive impact on engagement. Together, these findings reveal that algorithmic management is neither inherently energizing nor exhausting. Instead, its effects unfold over time and depend on how employees interpret and actively shape their digitally mediated work environment. Our research advances understanding of algorithmic management by highlighting adaptation and employee proactivity as central mechanisms underlying its contingent effects on work engagement
