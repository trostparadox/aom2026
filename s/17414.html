Session overview

This symposium will discuss how organisations justify and organise employee wellbeing in relation to performance in a rapidly changing world of work. In recent years, great effort has gone into establishing a scientific basis for an employee wellbeing business case and mutual gains agenda (e.g. De Neve & Ward, 2025; Kelly & Moen, 2020). Yet critics continually warn such optimism ignores tensions between organisational performance and individual wellbeing (Watson et al., 2023). Within organizational science and organizational life, wellbeing has been positioned as all of: a catalyst for performance (Bellet et al., 2024); a good that sits alongside performance in organizational science (Tay et al., 2024); the ultimate outcome of economic success (Coyle, 2025) and as a social and ethical value in opposition to and undermined by economic instrumentalization (Davies, 2011). The panel builds on a current research project, in which we develop the concept of “subordinated wellbeing”, which is the idea that wellbeing is embraced as an institutional logic only insofar as it can be aligned with dominant productivity goals. Wellbeing, as a social discourse, is then increasingly integrated into organisational strategy, but only so far as it aligns with performance imperatives. Our thinking is developed from a mixed-methods study of senior leaders in a multinational professional services firm, supplemented with a national survey of British employers and their HR practices. Institutional studies have regularly sought to examine how paradoxical, contradictory or hybrid logics are navigated by workers (e.g. Malhotra et al., 2021; Smith & Besharov, 2019), but analysis of wellbeing remains nascent despite public debate and media commentary. Our aim is to bring together scholars who span the spectrum of this debate from those who see wellbeing as a route to higher productivity (“happy worker, productive worker”) to those who view wellbeing discourse and practice as deeply problematic instruments of control. The topic is especially pertinent in the context of post-Covid hybrid and remote work, the mental health crisis, and the growth of AI and automation in workplaces.
