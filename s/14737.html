When Meaning Precedes Emotion: Spirituality and Occupational Grief as an Embodied Work Demand

Authors: Lara Bertola Rennes School of Business; Annalisa Grandi; Lara Colombo

Session overview

Some forms of work are widely understood as meaningful because they involve sustained engagement with care, responsibility, and fundamental human experiences. Funerary and cemetery work represents a particularly clear case. In these occupations, encounters with death are an integral part of everyday activities and are commonly associated with dignity and moral significance. Despite this, organizational research has tended to conceptualize grief primarily as a personal emotional response rather than as a condition generated within work itself. This paper develops a conceptual framework that positions spirituality at the beginning of the process through which grief becomes exhausting. Spirituality is understood as an interpretive orientation toward work— that is, a way of making sense of purpose and significance—rather than as a belief system or a coping mechanism. From this perspective, repeated exposure to death gives rise to occupational grief as a cumulative work demand. Because this form of grief is embedded in routine activity, it is not primarily resolved through emotional expression or reflection. Instead, it is increasingly lived through the body, contributing over time to emotional exhaustion. Gendered expectations of care further shape both exposure to grief and the embodiment of strain. By reframing grief as a work demand and locating embodiment at the centre of experience, the framework offers a distinct explanation for why exhaustion may develop even in work that is experienced as meaningful. The contribution lies in showing how spirituality shapes the conditions under which grief accumulates, rather than how individuals manage grief after it has occurred.
