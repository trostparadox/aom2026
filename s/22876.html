Serendipity Propensity: Why Some Gain and Others Strain from the Unexpected

OB
Yidan (Dani) Yin (The Pennsylvania State University); Christian Busch (University of Southern California); Cheryl Wakslak (University of Southern California)
Organizational life is filled with the unexpected. For some, these surprises lead to innovation; for others, they lead to exhaustion. What explains individuals’ differential capacities to turn surprise into value? We address this puzzle by introducing serendipity propensity, a multidimensional, individual-level construct that captures how people engage with surprise through encountering, connecting, and acting. Building on Conservation of Resources theory, we theorize three distinct tendencies with unique resource implications: encountering consumes attention and disrupts goals, predicting exhaustion; connecting conserves resources through sensemaking, predicting reduced exhaustion; and acting invests resources in new directions, predicting innovation. Because acting often entails pursuing ambiguous, unplanned paths that depend on others, we further theorize social support as a contextual resource that amplifies its innovative payoff. Across three studies, we develop and validate a new measure of serendipity propensity and test its predictive utility in a sample of postdoctoral scientists and in a global innovation accelerator with objective indicators of innovative performance. Our research advances the literature on serendipity by clarifying who turns surprise into value, why outcomes diverge, and how context shapes returns, thus shifting research from retrospective accounts of “happy accidents” toward actionable insights for designing organizations that cultivate innovation without amplifying strain.

Two Invisible Leashes: The Effects of After-Hours Norms on Job Satisfaction Among Korean Teachers

OB
Da Yeon Her (Michigan Technological University); Hye-Mi Lee; Byeolbee Um
This study examines how two distinct forms of after-hours norms—availability norms and off-duty (conduct) norms—are associated with teachers’ emotional exhaustion and job satisfaction. Drawing on the transactional stress model and role theory, we explore whether emotional exhaustion mediates the relationship between after-hours norms and job satisfaction and whether these indirect relationships vary across teachers’ job positions. The results indicate that emotional exhaustion mediates the relationship between off-duty norms and job satisfaction, whereas the indirect relationship between availability norms and job satisfaction via emotional exhaustion is contingent on teachers’ job positions. By differentiating between task-based and identity-based after-hours norms, this study demonstrates that the psychological mechanisms linking two types of after-hours norms to job satisfaction vary depending on teachers’ job positions. These findings extend human resource development (HRD) research by clarifying how teachers’ job positions condition the psychological impact of after-hours norms and underscore the importance of differentiated, HRD-informed approaches to managing organizationally embedded availability norms and community-based off-duty norms.

Restoring or Regulating? The Net-Benefit Trade-offs of Engineered Breaks in High-Pressure Work

OB
Peng Wang (Bayes Business School); Shuai Qin (Aston Business School); Xiaolan Chen (Aston Business School); Monder Ram
In high-pressure production work, reliability depends on sustained attention, coordination, and strict compliance, yet within-day recovery is often constrained by the recovery paradox. Increasingly, organizations engineer micro-breaks by standardizing not only when breaks occur but what employees do during them. We examine a common form of engineered breaks, designed play, defined as manager-organized, time-bounded playful activities embedded in work intervals and required as routine. Drawing on play-at-work and recovery theories, we theorize that break outcomes depend on a net-benefit trade-off shaped by two design features: work relevance (relevant vs. irrelevant) and interaction structure (solitary vs. interactive). We test these predictions using a field experiment in a labor-intensive steel manufacturing firm with more than 1,000 workers, including a baseline phase and a two-week intervention with random assignment to one of four daily break configurations. ANCOVA and fixed-effects difference-in-differences models indicate configuration-specific effects: relative to the work-relevant solitary baseline, the work-irrelevant solitary configuration yields the strongest gains in work well-being, whereas the two interactive configurations produce the largest increases in organizational belonging but also show weaker reductions in production deviance relative to the baseline configuration. These effects are stronger among employees higher in adult playfulness. The findings clarify when engineered breaks function as recovery tools versus governance risks in tightly coupled systems.

Why Do Defensive Routines Persist in Organizational Contexts? Disrupting Defensive Behaviors

OB
Mercedes-Victoria Auqui-Caceres (University of Padua); Andrea Furlan
Defensive routines are defined as habitual behaviors that people use in social interactions to protect themselves or others from embarrassing or threatening situations. Literature offers valuable insight into defensive routines which are acknowledged by academics as barriers to organizational learning and innovation. Nevertheless, we find that there is a lack of attention in examining why defensive routines are persistent in organizational life. To fill this gap, we conduct a two-year ethnographic action research in an Italian company. Based on an in-depth analysis of the fundamental concepts of defensive routines, and on data collected mainly by direct observations, interviews, and workshops, we propose a categorization with three types of defensive routines: blaming external forces, evading [authentic] dialogue, and avoiding confrontation. Drawing on an evolutionary perspective, we provide a framework that illustrates the mechanisms that explain the emergence and persistence of defensive routines and propose feasible ideas for interrupting the emotion-deliberation interaction dynamics that reinforce such routines. We intend to offer a pragmatic framework to better address defensive routines, as they perpetuate the status quo and block individuals’ ability to generate double-loop learning which is the type of learning that enables organizations to be innovative.

Martial Law–Triggered Rumination, Task Disruption, and Amplifying Role of Political Self-Efficacy

OB
MIN JU OH (Seoul National University); Sunghyun Chang (Arizona State University); Sunghyuck (Ryan) (Ryan) Mah (Seoul National University); Yejin Jeon (Seoul National University)
Societal disruptions, such as political upheavals, can intrude into employees’ work lives, yet little is known about the psychological processes through which macro-level events affect daily work completion. Drawing on the goal progress theory of rumination, this study examines how perceived uncertainty surrounding the declaration of martial law in South Korea influences employees’ daily off-task rumination and task completion. Using an experience sampling design, we collected data from 292 employees across five consecutive workdays, yielding 1,420 matched morning-afternoon observations. Results showed that on days when employees perceived greater uncertainty about the societal situation, they reported greater nonwork-related rumination, which, in turn, predicted more unfinished work tasks the next day. Moreover, political self-efficacy moderated this process such that perceived uncertainty was more strongly associated with rumination among employees with higher political self-efficacy. These findings highlight how externally generated societal uncertainty spills over into daily work through cognitive processes and underscore individual differences in susceptibility to these spillover effects.
