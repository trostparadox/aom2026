Early to the Game, Slow to the Sprint: The Impact of AI Adoption on Firms’ Dynamic Capabilities

TIM
Yicen Jiang (Renmin University of China)
We investigated the impact of Artificial Intelligence (AI) adoption timing on firms’ dynamic capabilities, with a specific focus on listed manufacturing firms in China. Drawing upon Dynamic Capability Theory and Innovation Diffusion Theory, we constructed a two-dimensional analytical framework encompassing AI adoption time and speed. Major findings include that an early and gradual adoption pattern yields significant advantages; the time effect of adoption is more pronounced than speed; and adaptive capability is less sensitive to adoption timing than innovative and absorptive capability. This research enriches the literature on strategic adoption of emerging technologies, offering robust theoretical underpinnings and practical implications for corporate strategic deployment and policy formulation.

From Where Do the Latecomer Firms Learn for Technological Catch-up: Universities vs. Overseas R&D

TIM
Jinhyuck (Joseph) Park (Sogang University); Keun Lee (Seoul National University)
Prior studies find that R&D specialization in technological domains featured by shorter cycle times has positive effects on technological catching up by latecomers. On top of this finding, we highlight the role of overseas R&D activities in the catching up process. We argue that latecomers are more likely to succeed in technological catching up with firms based in advanced countries if they perform overseas R&D activities. We also conjecture the presence of a positive synergy between short-cycleness of latecomers and their overseas R&D. In contrast, we argue that scientific collaboration with universities could not replace a role of overseas R&D for these catching-up firms. Our analysis of patent data of 539 firms based in Korea, Taiwan, and China in 1990-2010 broadly supports our arguments. Our study contributes to the catching up literature by showing that short-cycleness is not a sufficient condition for catching up but latecomers also need suitable channels where they can absorb frontier knowledge.

Hybrid Expertise in Technology Adoption: Coordinating Across Organizational Boundaries

TIM
Ignacio J Perez (University of Oxford)
How do organizations coordinate expertise to enable technology adoption across organizational boundaries? We address this question by introducing the concept of hybrid expertise: the integration of deep domain knowledge with cross-boundary spanning capabilities that enables actors to translate technical knowledge across institutional divides while maintaining epistemic authority. Drawing on a nine-month fieldwork-based qualitative study of big data adoption in a major Latin American public transport system, we trace how transport engineers strategically positioned themselves to shape technology implementation across organizational boundaries. Our findings reveal a four-stage process through which hybrid expertise develops and enables organizations to build capabilities to routinely generate, interpret, and act upon data in core operational and governance decisions: boundary establishment, strategic positioning, translation and capability building, and institutionalization. We also identify boundary conditions that constrain this process, including political vulnerability, governance interdependence requirements, and level specificity. By theorizing hybrid expertise as distinct from conventional boundary spanning, we contribute to research on knowledge boundaries and technology adoption by explaining how professional communities enable organizational capability development while preserving domain authority.
