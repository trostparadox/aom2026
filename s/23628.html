A Longitudinal Consulting-Based Intervention Showing How a Management System Supports Resilience

MC
Amandine Savall (ISEOR); Veronique Zardet
This paper examines a 40-year research-intervention conducted in a French family-owned industrial company that progressively evolved from a national firm into a multinational group. Drawing on a long-term collaboration between organizational actors and researchers, the study analyzes how a socio-economic management system was designed, implemented, and continuously adjusted as a central intervention artifact supporting growth, decentralization, and organizational resilience. From a management consulting perspective, the paper conceptualizes consulting not as a series of episodic problem-solving interventions, but as a sustained process of co-designing managerial frameworks, governance principles, and decision-making rhythms. The longitudinal case documents how consulting-based interventions contributed to maintaining organizational balance in the face of increasing environmental turbulence, including regulatory inflation, market globalization, socio-ecological transitions, and recurrent economic crises. The study combines a detailed analysis of strategic and organizational choices with quantitative indicators of economic performance over four decades, highlighting the links between consulting interventions, management practices, and long-term productivity and profitability. By focusing on the role of a coherent management system as a mediating mechanism between consulting work and organizational outcomes, the paper contributes to management consulting research by offering a rare longitudinal account of how consulting relationships can evolve into enduring strategic partnerships and support dynamic equilibrium in complex, decentralized organizations.

Qualimetric Intervention-Research: A Stimulating Approach to the Combined Involvement of Employees

MC
Mélanie Giraudet (ISEOR)
Qualimetric intervention-research: a stimulating approach to the combined involvement of employees and management. Case study of an accounting firm ABSTRACT Employee involvement within a company is a lever for its strategic development. This is particularly true in the case of accounting firms, which are unfamiliar with participatory management practices and yet are on the cusp of a major change: digitalization is taking on an important role in their activities, partly with the arrival of artificial intelligence, and customer demand is shifting towards tax, strategic, and organizational support. The lack of involvement observed in the case of the firm, the field of intervention-research, is at the heart of management's concerns in terms of getting employees and the firm's overall activity to evolve. This highlights the need for employees and management to work together to address and adapt to change. We seek to demonstrate how qualimetric intervention-research can stimulate the involvement of employees and management. Keywords: involvement, stimulation, qualimetric intervention-research, chartered accountancy

Hidden Costs of Human Interactions: Quantifying Performance Losses in Intercultural IT Collaboration

MC
Joy Sawaya (CORHIS -Université Paul Valery Montpellier France); Eric Scarazzini (CORHIS -Université Paul Valery Montpellier France)
Intercultural IT collaboration is often examined through the lens of cultural differences, communication styles, or leadership norms, yet the economic consequences of these differences remain insufficiently understood. While prior research documents how national and organisational cultures shape interactional patterns in global teams (Meyer, 2014; Schein, 2010), management studies rarely explain how culturally embedded interactions translate into measurable performance losses. This paper addresses this gap by examining the hidden economic costs generated by everyday human interactions in intercultural IT collaboration. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative and economic study of a French banking software publisher collaborating with an offshore IT partner in India, the analysis combines cultural frameworks with a socio-economic approach to performance. Building on Meyer’s cultural dimensions, Schein’s model of organisational culture, and interactional perspectives inspired by transactional analysis (Berne, 1961), the study identifies recurring interactional dysfunctions rooted in culturally differentiated assumptions regarding hierarchy, communication, responsibility, and time. These dysfunctions are shown to structure coordination failures, delayed issue escalation, and responsibility diffusion in daily work. Using a structured hidden cost methodology (Savall & Zardet, 2004, 2008), the paper traces how these culturally mediated interactional patterns generate cumulative performance losses, including wasted time, rework, non-production, and unrealised value creation. Rather than attributing performance erosion to cultural differences per se, the findings demonstrate that costs emerge when organisations fail to regulate interactions across cultural and hierarchical boundaries. This study makes three contributions. First, it connects intercultural management research to hidden cost theory by quantifying the economic effects of culturally embedded interactions. Second, it reframes cultural differences as interactional mechanisms rather than static attributes. Third, it proposes an interaction-based analytical framework that enables organisations to identify, measure, and mitigate the hidden economic impact of intercultural collaboration in IT settings. The findings further suggest that cultural differences related to ownership, hierarchy, communication, and time orientation generate hidden costs when left implicit, but can become a source of coordination strength when explicitly anticipated and regulated. Key words: Intercultural collaboration, Cultural differences, Organisational culture, IT offshoring.

Managing Productivity Through Proximity: Evidence from Longitudinal Intervention Research in Retail

MC
Amandine Savall (ISEOR)
This paper examines how proximity-based management systems can sustainably improve human productivity. Drawing on three longitudinal intervention research cases conducted in French mass retail over six to eight years, the study analyzes how transformations in management control systems contribute to productivity gains without relying on technological substitution or work intensification. The findings show that human productivity improvements emerge from the deployment of a decentralized and synchronized management system combining economic and social indicators, incentive mechanisms, and proximity-based managerial practices. Central to this system is the Hourly Contribution to Value Added on Variable Costs (HCVAVC), an indicator that links value creation, working time, and organizational dysfunctions. Used monthly, HCVAVC functions not only as a diagnostic control tool but also as an interactive device that supports dialogue, learning, and coordination between managers and teams. Across the three cases, human productivity increased significantly (+9%, +11%, and +25%), despite contrasting strategic contexts. The study contributes to the management control literature by showing how diagnostic and interactive control logic can be reconciled through proximity-based management practices. It also extends the control package perspective by highlighting the importance of synchronized deployment of control mechanisms at the local level. Finally, the paper contributes to the management consulting and intervention literature by conceptualizing management control systems as internal consulting infrastructures, in which indicators such as HCVAVC operate as boundary objects that support sustained organizational change and productivity improvement.
