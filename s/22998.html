Behind Enemy Lines: The Price Firms Pay for Straddling the Geopolitical Divide

IM
Constantin Lackinger (WU Vienna); Jakob Müllner; Jonas F. Puck (WU Vienna)
In this paper, we highlight how great power competition between the United States and China gives rise to a bloc-based logic of geopolitical risk that existing firm-level empirical research has yet to address. Drawing on the theory of political realism, we argue that as the international order bifurcates into competing spheres of influence, firms with subsidiaries across rival blocs face higher systematic risk. We test this argument on a quarterly panel of 31,235 stock-listed multinational enterprises (MNEs) across 124 countries from 2007 to 2023. Viewing the firm as a portfolio of subsidiaries, we find that subsidiary-weighted exposure to localized geopolitical risk increases systematic risk, revisiting the effect that country-specific conflict exposure has on the MNE. Beyond this, we find that rival embeddedness, that is, the share of subsidiaries located in countries aligned with the opposing geopolitical bloc, increases firm-level systematic risk. This effect emerges after 2016 and is stronger for firms in geopolitically strategic industries. Our findings contribute by introducing a bloc-based logic of geopolitical risk, showing how exposure to rival blocs translates into higher systematic risk and, through its pricing in capital markets, a higher cost of capital for firms.

Geopolitical Fault Lines: Alignment, Volatility, and Cross-Border Collaborations

IM
Gilbert Kofi Adarkwah (BI Norwegian Business School); Margaret Fong (HEC Montréal)
In the context of increasing geopolitical hostilities and ideological conflicts, we investigate the effect of geopolitical alignment -- similarity of national interests between countries in global affairs -- on firms’ strategic repositioning of innovation ties. We argue that geopolitical alignment affects how firms anticipate the reliability of partners and institutions in safeguarding shared value creation. As geopolitical tensions rise, governments and local stakeholders become less likely to credibly commit to honoring agreements and more likely to expropriate/misappropriate intellectual property. Antagonism between a firm’s home country and a foreign country fosters animosity between their citizens, undermining trust and leading to a reduction in innovation ties. Furthermore, we show that the interplay between the level of geopolitical alignment and the propensity for sudden and unpredictable shifts in that alignment -- i.e., volatility in geopolitical affinity -- shapes overall uncertainty and gives rise to nuanced pattern of cross-border activities among firms. Analyzing 1,685 US public firms and their innovation ties across 25 countries from 2000 to 2022, we find support for our hypotheses.

Host Countries’ Political Relations and Multinational Enterprises’ Foreign Location Decisions

IM
Julian Rehazek (University of Cologne); Tim Haarhaus (University of Cologne); Christian Schwens (University of Cologne)
Political relations are important for multinational enterprises’ (MNE) foreign location decisions. Yet, by primarily focusing on the effect of the political relations between a host country and an MNE’s home country, existing theory is inadequate to explain how political relations affect MNEs’ foreign production location decisions. Taking a network perspective on foreign production locations, we analyze the evolution of the production networks of 1023 Japanese MNEs from 2012 to 2018 with stochastic actor-oriented models. We find that more cooperative political relations with the existing host countries within an MNE’s production network increase the probability that an MNE decides to produce in a host country. Moreover, the level of global integration of value-added activities in an MNE’s industry serves as a boundary condition for this effect. Our study contributes to research on the role of political relations in MNEs’ foreign location decisions and, methodologically, to research on MNEs’ foreign location decisions more broadly.

Institutionalized Geopolitics: Firm Coalition Formation and the US-China Tech War in 3GPP Standards

IM
Mark P. Dallas (Union College); Jing-Ming Shiu (National Cheng Kung University); Masanori Yasumoto (Yokohama National University); Jing-Zhi Chen (National Cheng Kung University)
The post-crisis global value chain (GVC) literature has consolidated around firm strategies and state actions, yet largely sidelines the pre-existing institutional governance—private regimes, trade agreements, and standard-setting organizations—that continues to mediate geopolitical shocks. This paper addresses three interrelated problems: the neglect of institutional governance, the over-reliance on interests and strategic actions which neglects relational alignments like national identity, and the absence of an organizing mechanism. We examine these dynamics through quantitative analysis of interfirm cooperation in 3GPP, mobile telecommunication's global standard-setting organization, combined with qualitative case studies. Our quantitative findings reveal asymmetric bloc formation: Chinese firms exhibited high same-nationality collaboration before the technology war, which intensified significantly afterward, while US and European firms showed no corresponding nationalization – suggesting an emerging China bloc versus a globally-oriented coalition that excludes China. However, our case studies demonstrate that bloc formation operates within, rather than displacing, legacy institutional structures. Cross-bloc collaboration persists among the same firms in adjacent 5G domains—testing and applications, cloud services, fiber innovation, automotive standards—even as 5G equipment procurement becomes geopolitically segmented. We synthesize these findings through the concept of "institutionalized geopolitics": the process by which geopolitical tensions become embedded within, but also constrained by, pre-existing global governance architectures. This framework represents a fusion in which national re-embedding remains channeled through the legacy rules-based order.

Striking But Fleeting: How Economic Security Shapes Global Firms’ Nonmarket Strategy

IM
Yue Lin (University of California Berkeley)
Why do states that once championed economic openness increasingly restrict foreign firms in the name of national security, and how do such dynamics reshape multinational enterprises’ (MNEs) nonmarket strategies? I argue that economic securitization (ES) operates as a striking but fleeting electoral strategy. Politicians deploy national security framing against foreign firms to mobilize broad public support before competitive elections, yet legislative momentum often dissipates once electoral pressure subsides. In response, I argue that rhetorical ES and enacted ES laws generate distinct strategic responses. Public “naming and shaming” discourages elite-targeted lobbying while encouraging society-oriented ESG engagement, whereas formal ES legislation—by raising the transaction costs of elite access—induces both regulator-focused lobbying and expanded ESG activities. Empirically, I combine Difference-in-Differences analysis with elite interviews. I construct an original dataset of U.S. legislative ES attempts using machine learning and large language models, and draw on interviews with corporate executives and policy professionals. Preliminary findings show that electoral competitiveness predicts heightened legislative securitization and that foreign investors increasingly pivot from Washington lobbying toward community engagement. The study contributes to scholarly debates on economic de-globalization, political strategy, and corporate political behavior, offering implications for policymakers and global firms navigating securitized investment environments.
