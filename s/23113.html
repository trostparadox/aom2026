Hiding in Plain (Virtual) Sight: Overemployment as an Emerging Work Identity

HR
William J. Becker (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Samantha A. Conroy (Colorado State University); Liuba Belkin (Lehigh University); Jessica Misner George (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
The rise of remote work has enabled a new form of multiple job holding, overemployment, where individuals concurrently manage two or more jobs, often with overlapping work hours and without employer awareness. Despite its proliferation and growing industry attention, there has been limited organizational research on the phenomenon or how the concealment that is central to overemployment challenges traditional expectations of loyalty and identity. We reframe overemployment not simply as a subset of multiple job holding, but as an agentic worker response to fulfill psychological needs amid broad technological and social shifts in the employee-employer relationship. Across two studies, we develop and test a model of overemployment through the lens of self-determination theory and social identity theory. In Study 1, we utilize guided machine learning to analyze 739,826 Reddit posts from 97,447 unique users in online overemployment communities. In Study 2, we perform a confirmatory multi-wave survey of current and previously overemployed professionals. Empirical results from both studies show support for our theorizing that the secrecy inherent in overemployment reduces job-relatedness need satisfaction through diminished organizational identification. In addition, our research demonstrates that overemployed workers may subsequently satisfy their relational needs through identification with informal overemployment communities, compensating for weakened organizational ties through alternative affiliations. Our results contribute to the literature on remote work, multiple job holding, organizational identification and workplace loneliness by offering insight on how employees navigate secrecy, belonging and personal agency in an evolving remote employment landscape.

When AI Outperforms Humans: The Reversal Effect of Indifferent Interviewers on Applicant Reactions

HR
Hirofumi Tatsumoto (University of Tsukuba); Michika Rikihisa (Mizuho Financial Group, Inc.); Masataka Ban (University of Tsukuba); Maya Okamoto (Tsukuba Twin Spark, Inc.)
Prior research consistently reports that human interviewers are evaluated more favorably than AI interviews, but this conclusion implicitly assumes appropriate interviewer attitudes. This study examines whether indifferent human interviewers generate more negative applicant reactions than AI interviews —a phenomenon we term the reversal effect. Drawing on expectancy violations theory, signaling theory, and procedural justice theory, we hypothesize that applicants hold higher expectations for human interviewers than for AI, and that indifferent attitudes violate these expectations, producing evaluations worse than AI interviews. In a within-subjects experiment, 71 Japanese university students experienced three interview conditions: AI interview, friendly human interviewer, and indifferent human interviewer. Results supported the reversal effect: friendly interviewers generated higher procedural justice perceptions (SPJS) and interviewer impressions than AI interviews, whereas indifferent interviewers generated lower SPJS and interviewer impressions. Serial mediation analysis revealed that interview type affected emotional reactions to selection outcomes through SPJS, interviewer impressions, and organizational attractiveness. While the interviewer impression pathway was approximately 20% larger than the SPJS pathway, both pathways made substantial contributions to organizational attractiveness. These findings challenge the assumption that human interviews are inherently superior to AI interviews by identifying interviewer attitude as a critical boundary condition.

It is Not a Panacea:The Paradoxical Effects of Electronic Performance Monitoring on Workplace Safety

HR
Zhenhao Hui (Beijing Jiaotong University); PENGXIANG FAN (Beijing Jiaotong University); Wenyi Zhang (Beijing Jiaotong University)
Electronic performance monitoring (EPM) is increasingly integrated into workplaces and is widely believed to improve occupational safety. However, this study challenges the prevailing view that EPM always benefits workplace safety by proposing a dual-pathway model to explain its impact on unsafe behavior. Drawing on Regulatory Focus Theory, we propose that EPM activates a promotion-oriented cognition that enhances safety motivation and decreases unsafe behavior, while also triggering a prevention-oriented cognition, increasing unsafe behavior through negative work rumination. Importantly, perceived abusive supervision climate is a critical boundary condition: a high perceived abusive climate strengthens negative work rumination and intensifies safety motivation. Using three-wave time-lagged survey data from 402 frontline workers in China, the research reveals the double-edged sword effect of EPM on safety. The study provides theoretical insights into the literature of EPM and workplace safety and offers practical guidance for mitigating the dark side of digital monitoring.

The Janus Face of Job Autonomy in Self-Managing Organizations

HR
Valentina Voimavuo (Aalto University School of Business); Alexei Koveshnikov (Aalto University); Carl Fey (BI Norwegian Business School)
This study examines the dual effects of job autonomy, an opportunity-enhancing practice from the AMO framework (Ability-motivation-opportunity), on employee behaviors and organizational outcomes in self-managing organizations (SMOs). Building on the Job Demands and Resources framework, the research explores how job autonomy simultaneously acts as a job resource and a job demand, leading to both positive and negative consequences. The study surveyed 149 Finnish SMOs to investigate the relationships between job autonomy, its effect on employees (work intensification and promotive voice), and on organizations (innovative and political behaviors). Additionally, the research examines how process control and peer control moderate these relationships. The findings confirm the hypothesized dual nature of job autonomy, demonstrating that it can enhance opportunities for employee voice and innovation while also developing work intensification perceptions and increasing political behavior. The study contributes to the literature on AMO practices by providing a nuanced understanding of job autonomy, exploring its boundary conditions, and extending research to the context of SMOs. The results offer insights for managers implementing job autonomy practices and suggest the need to consider organizational control mechanisms in conjunction with AMO practices.
