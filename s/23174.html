How Social Interdependence Impacts Individual Complementor Engagement in Platform Ecosystems

CTO
Alexander Redlich (RWTH Aachen University); Frank T. Piller (RWTH Aachen University); Kathleen Diener (Niederrhein University of Applied Sciences)
A key driver the success of platform ecosystems is the engagement of complementors, independent third parties who contribute to the ecosystem’s shared value proposition. Complementors can be firms, but are often individuals like software programmers, data analysts, and other experts who contribute products, services or specific skills. Prior research has mostly overlooked the role of individuals in ecosystems. We address this gap by examining how perceived interdependencies influence individual complementors’ decisions to engage in a platform ecosystem. Building on prior research on technological and economic interdependence, we especially investigate the level of social interdependence preferred by individual complementors. Based on social exchange theory (SET), we hypothesize that when economic or technological interdependencies are high, complementors prefer a high level of social interdependence. As moderating effects, we examine individual-level characteristics social trust, self-efficacy, and risk proneness. We test our hypotheses using a pre-registered choice-based conjoint experiment with a sample of app developers (N=316). Our data confirms the important role of social interdependence in complementor engagement. Overall, our study contributes to a better understanding of the preferences of individual complementors and provides insights into how to engage this overlooked stakeholder group in platform ecosystems.

Informal Appropriation Mechanisms and Digital Product Performance in Mobile Apps: A Dual Perspective

CTO
yongdong LIU (Tongji University)
Digital innovation often faces substantial challenges in value capture, especially in digital environments where imitation is rapid and formal protection is limited. As a result, informal appropriation mechanisms have become an increasingly important means for digital product developers to secure returns from innovation. However, the internal relationships among different informal mechanisms and their roles in the digital value capture process remain insufficiently understood. Drawing on customer engagement and profit from innovation theory, this study examines how lead time, product complexity, and fast action influence digital product performance on digital platforms, and how fast action reshapes the effects of lead time and product complexity from a dynamic perspective. Using mobile application data from a major digital platform, we develop and test a moderated framework to explore these relationships. The findings indicate that lead time, product complexity, and fast action each contribute positively to digital product performance. More importantly, fast action strengthens the performance benefits associated with lead time while weakening those associated with product complexity, revealing a dynamic trade-off between speed-based and depth-based appropriation strategies. In addition, lead time and product complexity exhibit a substitutive relationship, and the effectiveness of informal mechanisms varies across product categories. Compared with utility-oriented applications, entertainment applications benefit more strongly from lead time and fast action. This study deepens understanding of how informal appropriation mechanisms jointly shape digital innovation outcomes and provides theoretical insights and practical implications for digital product developers operating on platform ecosystems.

Modularization and Integration of Firm Offerings: Implications for Firm Strategy

CTO
Hao Ma (Peking University); Mengyue Su (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
Modularization is often used to deal with complex systems as it breaks down a system into a set of self-contained modules that could be combined and recombined through mix-and-match according to certain grand architecture. A firm could gain a competitive advantage by providing uniquely valuable modularized offerings or excelling in proprietary architectural design and superior integration capability that could fully tap the potential of modularization. Both modularization and integration, as well as their interactions, affect the success of a firm’s value proposition. Moreover, the actual degree of modularization or integration is not merely driven by a firm’s intention and capabilities on the supply side, it is also dictated and shaped by the firm’s unique insights into customer preferences and choices, as well as other environmental factors such as government regulations, technological breakthroughs, and competitive rivalry. When both the firm’s and the customer’s preferences for modularization or integration are commensurate, the firm simply needs to do well what their customers want them to do as a dedicated modularized provider or total solution provider. When the firm’s preferences for modularization or integration differ from that of the customers, the firm needs to either exit the business or resort to other options such as intermediated virtual integrator and anchored virtual integrator. Firms focusing on modularization should strive to offer the best modular components that could function well as they are or be plugged into essentially any platform or ecosystem. Firms focusing on integration should design and manage an evolving architecture that best combines and integrates the modularized components available, proprietary or open, tangible or virtual.

Innovating At A Governable Tempo: Generational Product Innovation On Digital Platforms

CTO
Zhengyao Kang (University of Bristol); Haneul Choi (University of Bristol)
Firms increasingly rely on generational product innovations (GPIs) to renew offerings within established technological regimes, especially on digital platforms. While prior research emphasizes the technical magnitude or customer response to GPIs, we argue that the performance consequences hinge on how firms temporally govern recurrent releases. We conceptualize GPIs as temporal practices that create both generative benefits and temporal frictions, thereby requiring firms to coordinate and stabilize their innovation work. Using a panel of top mobile game developers on the Apple App Store, we find an inverted U-shaped relationship between GPI frequency and performance: moderate releases generate competitive gains, but excessive releases overwhelm temporal absorption capacity and reduce performance. We identify two moderators: Product iterations flatten the curve by providing corrective stability, whereas irregular pacing steepens it by amplifying temporal disruption. This study reframes generational innovation as a problem of organizing in time, advancing research on digital innovation and platform ecosystems by showing that value creation in the digital context depends not on innovating more, but on innovating at a governable tempo.

Deconstructing Shared Governance Platforms: Configurations for Platform Continuity in Data Spaces

CTO
Joana Piel (RWTH Aachen University); Frank T. Piller (RWTH Aachen University); Dirk Luettgens (RWTH Aachen University)
Digital platforms have become dominant economic institutions facing increasing criticism for their power asymmetries and centralized control. Consequently, shared governance platforms (SGPs) have emerged as an alternative. SGPs distribute decision and control rights among participants to ensure fair governance and value capture. A prominent form of SGP is data spaces: a federated platform that enables multiple organizations to share, access, and use data in a trusted and interoperable manner. Despite their appeal (participants retain sovereignty over their data assets) and ample support from the European Commission, many data spaces are facing a severe survival crisis: few have survived their initial stage. Hence, this study investigates whether specific SGP configurations support continuity. Using a qualitative-inductive mixed-method approach, we analyze a sample of 39 data spaces to develop a comprehensive taxonomy of design features of SGPs and apply fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) to identify configurations for continuity. Results show that data spaces decouple data from platform governance, operating along a monocentric-to-polycentric continuum. Data spaces that successfully reach platform continuity grant individual decision rights as an incentive to mitigate uncertainty and secure participant commitment during the initial stages. However, this strategy may conflict with requirements for collective ecosystem value as these platforms mature. By revealing these nuances, our study paves the way for new research on the evolutionary pathways and stabilization of shared governance structures.
