Women’s Pathways to Additional Board Appointments

STR
Daisuke Uchida (Keio University); Toru Yoshikawa (Waseda University)
As more women enter the market for corporate directors due to increasing practical, political, and scholarly focus on board gender diversity, their prominence has grown. However, there is limited understanding of how women secure appointments to additional boards following their initial appointment. This study leverages social role and institutional theories to develop a multilevel perspective on female appointments to additional boards. Using longitudinal data from 18,700 directors between 2002 and 2021, we found that women are more likely than men to obtain appointments to additional boards. This likelihood is moderated by micro-, macro-, and meso-level factors that mitigate the impact of social role perceptions and enhance the influence of institutional pressures. By illuminating the individual consequences for women in the market for corporate directors, this study contributes to the literature on board gender diversity in corporate governance.

Corporate Governance Responses to Labor Mobility Restrictions

STR
Pinghsun Huang (National Cheng Kung University); Hsin-Yi Huang (National Taichung University of Science and Technology); Yan Zhang (State University of New York at Binghamton)
This study examines how labor mobility restrictions imposed by noncompete agreements shape corporate governance, focusing on firms’ investment in employee-oriented workplace practices as reflected in KLD scores on employee relations and diversity. Exploiting 16 staggered state-level changes in noncompete enforceability as a quasi-experiment, our stacked difference-in-differences analyses show that stricter enforceability leads firms to increase, rather than decrease, such investment. This evidence is inconsistent with bargaining theory but aligns with human capital-centered theories. In further support of the human capital framework, this increase in investment is more pronounced for firms facing stronger product-market competition, where noncompetes tend to have a larger impact on labor mobility constraints and the risk of employee dissatisfaction. This investment effect is also greater for firms led by younger CEOs or with higher institutional ownership, governance characteristics typically associated with a long-term strategic orientation. Moreover, this response is stronger for firms in labor-intensive or high-skill industries, where investment in human capital and preemption of labor conflicts are particularly salient. These results illustrate a strategic governance mechanism through which firms invest internally to buffer against external mobility constraints. Collectively, our findings inform ongoing policy debates by providing new insights into how labor mobility frictions shape corporate governance and employee welfare.

The Struggle for Board Gender Diversity: Big Three Ownership and Social-Value Carriers

STR
Yujia Chen (Carlos III University of Madrid); Kurt Desender
This paper examines the factors that facilitate and hinder board gender diversity in a context, where gender equality lags behind much of the world. Leveraging the “Womenomics” reforms in Japan and a difference-in-differences design, we show that Big Three ownership is associated with greater board gender diversity after the reform. Our evidence further indicates that these improvements reflect substantive organizational changes rather than tokenistic compliance. In addition, we show that increased board gender diversity is positively associated with governance-related risk incidents filed by media and secondary stakeholders, who reveal prevailing social values. This association cannot be attributed to declines in governance quality, measured by firm performance and earnings reliability. Instead, it reflects resistance from social values carriers toward gender reform.

Your Bosses Matter: How Black Managerial Representation Reduces Racial Wage Gaps (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Pablo Leao (Copenhagen Business School); Michael J. Mol (Copenhagen Business School); Grazia D. Santangelo (Copenhagen Business School)
Employees’ remuneration can stem from how managers evaluate employees’ qualifications and assign them status in organizations. This study examines whether Black managerial representation (BMR) helps reduce the wage inequality gap between Black and White employees by reshaping these internal processes. We argue that greater BMR can alter biased interpretations of human capital and how status is attributed through tenure. Our findings show that the wage gap narrows in firms with more BMR, and more so for highly educated and mid-long-tenured Black employees. These results suggest that, beyond symbolism, BMR matters for how organizations assess value and allocate rewards. By focusing on race-specific mechanisms, we extend organizational inequality research and offer new insights into whether and when diversity in leadership contributes to more equitable outcomes.

Internal Governance and External Transparency: Product Safety in VC-Backed Life Science Ventures

STR
Ting Yao (University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee)
Product safety is a critical yet understudied challenge in venture capital–backed life science ventures, where innovation unfolds under intense pressure for speed, growth, and investor returns. This study examines how two governance mechanisms—board independence and public disclosure of venture-physician financial relationships mandated by the Sunshine Act—shape ventures’ ability to prevent and respond to product safety risks. Unlike public firms, ventures are not required to appoint independent directors, and many operate without them, leaving a critical gap in safety oversight. We find that independent directors play a preventive role by reducing adverse events and the severity of product harm. They also exhibit greater responsiveness through product recalls in ventures participating in government health programs, reflecting increased accountability in contexts with broader public visibility. However, financial transparency with physicians has limited direct influence on safety outcomes. Overall, these discoveries highlight the importance of independent directors’ active engagement in safeguarding product safety in VC-backed life science ventures.
