Pay Level, Internal Vertical Pay Gaps and Firm Performance: The Moderating Role of Family Control

OMT
Chien-Nan Chen (National Dong Hwa University); Jung-Ching Lin (National Dong Hwa University)
Compensation systems are a central component of human resource management and play a critical role in shaping employee motivation and firm performance. While wage differentials may function as incentives, they may also generate perceptions of unfairness that undermine cooperation and trust. Drawing on social comparison theory and tournament theory, this study examines how compensation structure affects firm performance from the perspective of employee perception. We distinguish between non-managerial employees’ pay level relative to industry peers and internal vertical pay gaps between top managers and non-managerial employees. In addition, we investigate the moderating role of family control through the lens of socioemotional wealth theory. Using data from publicly listed firms in Taiwan, our results show that non-managerial employees’ pay level relative to industry peers is positively associated with firm performance. We also find a significant inverted U-shaped relationship between internal vertical pay gaps and firm performance, indicating that moderate pay gaps enhance performance, whereas excessive gaps diminish it. Furthermore, family control weakens the positive effect of pay level on firm performance, but does not significantly moderate the curvilinear relationship between internal vertical pay gaps and performance. This study extends compensation research beyond its traditional focus on top management. Our findings also contribute to family business research by showing how family governance shapes the effectiveness of market-based compensation incentives.

Anchored Transparency: How Pay Visibility Can Reproduce the Gender Asking Gap

OMT
Tristan L. Botelho (Yale University); Lena Rudat (University of Mannheim)
Pay transparency is widely expected to reduce gender pay gaps by aligning wage demands to common benchmarks. We develop a theory of anchored transparency and its effect on the gender asking gap. When pay is visible, workers anchor wage claims to salient reference groups; in sparse platform environments, gender becomes a key cue, so women benchmark to women rather than to the market. We test this theory using data from a freelance platform where entrants view peers’ posted rates before setting their own. Using entrant sign up time as random, we reconstruct the varying set of other freelancers and thus posted rates that entrants see when they sign-up. Despite transparency and self-set wages, women post asking wages about 15.7% lower than men, on average. However, female visibility systematically narrows the gap: Seeing at least one woman in the top three profiles reduces it by 11.5%. Women also raise their asks when the (quasi-random) maximum visible female rate is higher; male rates and average benchmarks have no effect. These exposure effects concentrate among inexperienced entrants and attenuate with prior experience. Overall, pay transparency equalizes only when it broadens reference groups rather than reinforcing identity-bounded comparisons.

On the Basis of Leverage: What the Salary History Ban Reveals About Offer-Setting (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Shiya Wang (Columbia Business School)
Theories of statistical discrimination predict that removing information will increase, rather than decrease, disparities across demographic groups. However, using a large dataset of more than 700,000 initial salary offers made to job candidates in the United States between 2017 and 2020, I find that the removal of past salary information reduced, rather than widened, gender disparities in pay. To unpack this theoretical puzzle, I conduct two dozen in-depth interviews with hiring managers and compensation experts. The qualitative findings reveal an alternative framework for how offers are set: not as a direct function of perceived quality, but as a function of perceived leverage--that is, candidates’ outside options and bargaining power. I then test this leverage-based model by empirically demonstrating a positive relationship between leverage and offers, a relationship that is magnified when past salary information is removed. I conclude by discussing how understanding offer-setting advances research on gender inequality, labor markets, and organizations.

It Pays to Refuse: How Refusing to Disclose Salary History Benefits Job Candidates

OMT
Laura Adler (Yale University); Mai Dinh (Yale University)
Salary negotiations are pivotal in shaping workers’ earnings. Recent research considers how disclosing information about salary history affects pay outcomes, increasing both salaries on average and the pay gap between men and women. However, salary negotiations are shaped not only by the availability of information, but also by the interpersonal dynamics through which information is disclosed. We consider how job candidates interact with employers who ask about salary history to consider: can refusing to disclose salary benefit workers? A key limitation of prior research is the lack of access to individuals’ prior salary, making it difficult to disentangle the effect of disclosure from the effect of the information disclosed. Leveraging individual-level data from Payscale, including repeated observations of workers across multiple jobs from 2018 to 2023, we isolate the impact of refusal from that of past salary levels. Our analyses show that refusers receive salaries that are 1.5% (˜ $700 on $45,000) higher and achieve 2.4% (˜ $1,100 on $45,000) greater pay increases than disclosers, net of demographic, occupational, and organizational characteristics. Two survey experiments shed light on the mechanisms underlying these patterns. First, employers appear to treat refusal as a neutral, non-informative signal: they do not penalize candidates who refuse to disclose; however, in the absence of an anchor, they make more variable offers. Second, candidates take advantage of this variability by engaging in strategy updating: they calibrate disclosure decisions to the initial offer they receive. Together, these findings advance understanding of the dynamics of pay negotiations, clarify the consequences of refusal behavior, and illuminate when nondisclosure can be an advantageous strategy for job seekers.

Organizational Practices in Context: When Do Market Dynamics Fail to Worsen Precarious Employment?

OMT
ZHIPENG ZHOU (Cornell University)
Marketization in industrial economies has coincided with both the expansion of precarious employment and rising earnings inequality. Yet few studies explicitly link these two phenomena by investigating (a) how earnings returns to precarious employment vary across levels of marketization, or (b) how such variation contributes to overall inequality. Drawing on China’s institutional changes in marketization, this study argues that meso-institutional contexts affect how organizations structure dual employment relationships that differentially reward long-term and short-term workers. Private sector employers, under market capitalism, pursue employment flexibility and offset the higher costs of stabilizing long-term employment by paying short-term workers less. In contrast, constrained by socialist personnel systems and heightened staffing demands, state-sector employers preserve labor rents for permanent employees while subjecting short-term workers to market competition. Using nationally representative data from 2018 and leveraging provincial marketization variations, I find that the state sector imposes a larger earnings penalty on short-term employment. This penalty becomes smaller in more marketized state organizations, thereby mitigating overall earnings inequality, whereas the opposite pattern emerges in more marketized private organizations. Results are robust to alternative measures of marketization. I discuss theoretical implications for comparative study on institutional changes in marketization, organizational employment practices, and labor markets.
