Rising Tide Does Not Lift All Ships: AI Literacy Gaps and Daily Employee Responses

OB
Yurong Liu (Hunan University); March To (RMIT University); Zhu YAO (Hunan University)
The dominant narrative about artificial intelligence (AI) in the workplace centres on technological substitution—how machines replace human labour and threaten job security. Yet this focus obscures a more immediate and pervasive challenge: the social threat arising from disparities in AI literacy, as employees adapt to these technologies at different rates on a daily basis. Drawing on social comparison theory, we examine how AI literacy gaps (AILG) elicit daily work-related threat appraisals that influence both assimilation- and contrast-based behaviours, with implications for work and organisational psychology. Using a 10-workday experience-sampling design with 106 full-time employees (561 daily observations), we examined the within-person associations between perceived AI literacy gaps and daily threat appraisals, which were, in turn, positively related to both self-improvement (an assimilative response) and knowledge hiding (a contrastive response). Furthermore, learning goal orientation (LGO) strengthens the positive path from threat to self-improvement but does not buffer the path to knowledge hiding. Our research extends social comparison theory to the critical context of human-AI interaction, identifying a key source of threat that shapes employee adaptation to intelligent technologies. Practically, it highlights that organisations can steer employees toward constructive, rather than defensive, responses by fostering a learning-oriented culture.

When High-Quality AI Becomes a Threat: AI Risk Perception and Employee Voice and Turnover

OB
Jiaxin Li (Tianjin University); Meng Xi (Tianjin University); Xin Li (Tianjin University); Jiazhen Shi (Tianjin University)
Drawing from Conservation of Resources theory, this study develops a three-way interaction moderation model to investigate the mechanisms through which AI risk perception influences employee voice behavior and turnover intention. Using a three-wave survey of 402 employees, the results indicate that AI risk perception significantly reduces voice behavior and increases turnover intention. Job stress mediates the relationships between AI risk perception and both voice behavior and turnover intention. Furthermore, perceived quality of AI positively moderates the impact of AI risk perception on job stress, as well as the indirect effects on voice behavior and turnover intention through job stress. Most importantly, a significant three-way interaction exists between AI risk perception, perceived quality of AI, and perceived organizational support for AI use. Specifically, when perceived quality of AI is high and perceived organizational support for AI use is low, the positive impact of AI risk perception on job stress—and its subsequent indirect effects on voice behavior and turnover intention—are significantly strengthened. Theoretically, this study advances Conservation of Resources theory by conceptualizing AI risk perception as a salient resource threat and by elucidating how perceived quality of AI and perceived organizational support for AI use jointly shape employees’ stress-based behavioral responses to AI-driven change. Practically, the findings highlight the importance of simultaneously managing AI system quality and organizational support when addressing employee reactions to AI adoption.

How Does AI Assistant Intelligence Impact Employee AI Engagement?A Stress-as-Offense-to-Self Theory

OB
Jiaxi Chen (Nanjing University); Jin Qian (Nanjing University); Man Cao (Nanjing University of Science and Technology); Ru Li; Gang Chen (Nanjing University); Zheng zhiqi (Nanjing University)
With the higher intelligence of AI and its deeper embedding into organizations, employees’ positive psychological state in human-AI collaboration concerns the effective internalization of the AI capabilities and the full realization of AI productivity in organization. However, existing research still lacks exploring employees’ psychological state during human-AI collaboration. Based on the Stress as Offense to Self (SOS) Theory, this study explores the mechanism and boundary conditions of how AI assistant intelligence affect employees’ AI engagement. By a scenario-based experiment and a questionnaire survey, the results show that: (1) The AI assistant intelligence positively influences employees’ AI engagement; (2) Self-esteem threat plays a competitive mediating role in the relationship between the AI assistant intelligence and employees’ AI engagement. (3) The leader’s appreciation can buffer the positive effect of AI assistant intelligence on employees’ self-esteem threat and further weaken the competitive mediating effect of self-esteem threat in the above relationship. This study expands the research on the antecedents of AI engagement, deepens the application of the SOS theory in human-AI interaction scenarios, and provides targeted theoretical guidance and management inspirations for organizations to alleviate employees’ self-esteem threat and promote effective human-AI collaboration in the process of promoting AI implementation and upgrading.

It’s Not Me, It’s the AI: Low-Performance Attribution, Emotional Exhaustion, and Turnover Intention

OB
Minya Xu (Peking University)
As artificial intelligence (AI) systems become increasingly integrated into organizational workflows, understanding how employees psychologically process AI-related performance failures has become critical for both theory and practice. Drawing on attribution theory (Weiner, 1985) and conservation of resources (COR) theory (Hobfoll, 1989), we develop a moderated mediation model to examine when and why such attributions lead to emotional exhaustion and, ultimately, turnover intention. We propose that the positive relationship between employee attribution to AI and emotional exhaustion is moderated by the employee’s level of work AI dependence, and further jointly moderated by employee dependence and leader attribution of the low performance to AI. Specifically, the relationship is strongest under conditions of high AI dependence and high leader AI attribution. We then posit emotional exhaustion as a key mechanism, mediating this interactive effect on turnover intention. Across two complementary studies, including a multi-source field data from call center employees who vary in their AI dependence, we find consistent support for these predictions. These results reveal that in AI-augmented work contexts, classical attribution mechanisms reverse under conditions of technological dependence, with profound implications for employee retention and organizational AI implementation strategies.
