A Situated Perspective on Perceived Overqualification: How Gender and Parenthood Shape the Dynamics

Session overview

Perceived overqualification (POQ)—feeling that one's qualifications exceed job requirements—has been extensively studied as a predictor of outcomes. However, most research treats POQ as a static trait, overlooking its temporal dynamics. We examine how POQ and proactive behaviors (job crafting and career crafting) reciprocally influence each other over time, and how these relationships vary by gender and parenthood status in Japan's gendered work context. Using three-wave panel data (N=1,705; 9-month intervals) and random intercept cross-lagged panel models, we analyzed POQ's relationships with job crafting and career crafting across four groups: male non-parents (n=529), male parents (n=654), female non-parents (n=391), and female parents (n=131). Results revealed striking heterogeneity, challenging expectations that parents would show stronger POQ?career crafting relationships. Childless men's POQ prompted both job crafting (ß=.184) and career crafting (ß=.120-.127), with POQ showing carryover effects across time (ß=.163-.184). Fathers showed high job crafting carryover (ß=.135-.213) yet null POQ?crafting effects—proactivity disconnected from POQ. Women without children exhibited bidirectional relationships: POQ prompted career crafting, while career crafting reciprocally increased POQ (CC?POQ: ß=.228), suggesting career exploration clarifies possible selves, illuminating rather than resolving person-job misfit. These findings challenge static conceptualizations of POQ, revealing it can be dynamic developmental signal shaped by life context. We propose a situated perspective on perceived overqualification, demonstrating that POQ's meaning and consequences are constituted through gendered norms and structural contexts rather than existing as a stable person-job attribute.
