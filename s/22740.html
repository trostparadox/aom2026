Perceiving the Capable Machine: The Awe Experience of AI in Society

OB
Ziwei Shen (Peking University); Guiquan Li (Peking University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly perceived not merely as tools but as highly capable nonhuman agents, eliciting complex emotional responses from users. Little is known about how people emotionally respond when AI capability appears to exceed familiar human boundaries. Drawing on awe theory, this research investigates how perceived AI capability elicits awe and distinguishes between its positive and negative components. Across three experimental studies, we examine (a) whether AI capability evokes awe in a nonlinear manner, (b) how AI capability differentially shapes positive awe and negative awe, (c) the moderating role of AI human-likeness, and (d) whether AI and human agents are emotionally equivalent sources of awe. Results consistently show that higher AI capability elicits stronger awe, with effects becoming more pronounced at higher capability levels. Importantly, AI capability simultaneously amplifies positive awe and negative awe. AI human-likeness attenuates these effects, particularly by compensating for low AI capability in eliciting positive awe. Moreover, when capability is held constant, AI agents evoke stronger awe than human agents. These findings extend awe theory to human-AI interaction, reveal the ambivalent emotional consequences of highly capable AI. The study offers practical implications for the design and governance of advanced AI systems.

When Your Coworker Innovates with AI: A Tale of Two Envies and the Critical Role of Transparency

OB
Yunshuo Liu (North China Electric Power university); KENI SONG (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Fulei Chu
Employees' artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled innovative behavior, which includes proactive actions supported and empowered by AI to generate, promote, and implement novel and valuable ideas, products, or services, has gained prominence in the workplace. Most research on employees' innovation in the AI era has focused on how AI influences employees' innovative behavior. We advance theory on AI and innovation by examining how third-party observers react to coworkers' AI-enabled innovative behavior (CAIB). By integrating the social comparison theory, this study developed a moderated mediation model to explore how and when CAIB differentially affects observers' behaviors. Our research, comprising a multi-wave field survey (Study 1) and an online experiment (Study 2), demonstrated that CAIB indirectly predicts observers' informal workplace learning (IWL) and negative workplace gossip (NWG) by eliciting benign and malicious envy, respectively. Furthermore, we found that AI technology transparency moderates these relationships. When AI technology transparency is high, CAIB has a significantly positive indirect effect on observers' IWL through benign envy. Conversely, when AI technology transparency is low, CAIB exhibits a significantly positive indirect impact on NWG via malicious envy. Our study extends the literature on employees innovative behavior and offers meaningful implications for management practices.

AI Service Failure Severity, AI Support, and Employee Thriving: The Role of Emotional Contagion

OB
QIONG JIA (Hohai University); Yan Zhang; Yujie Cai (SKEMA Business School)
The growing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into organizational service systems has transformed employees from task executors to human-AI collaborators. Yet, how employees emotionally and behaviorally respond when AI fails remains underexplored in organizational behavior research. Drawing on Affective Events Theory and Emotional Contagion Theory, this study conceptualizes AI service failure as a distinctive affective event that influence employee thriving through affective transmission. Across one interview study and three scenario-based experiments, we find that higher AI service failure severity amplifies employees’ perceptions of customer anger, which in turn increases emotional exhaustion and reduces work thriving. Moreover, employees’ attention to others’ emotions strengthens, whereas organizational AI support weakens, the indirect effect of AI service failure severity on thriving. By integrating human-AI collaboration into the affective events framework, this research advances understanding of vicarious emotional contagion in technologically mediated work contexts and identifies key individual and contextual boundary conditions that shape employee affective experiences and well-being in AI-augmented organizations.

Cold Like a Machine: Erosion of Leaders’ Empathy Through AI Automation

OB
Moritz Remund (University of Zurich); Anand Prema Aschwin Van Zelderen (SKEMA Business School - Grand Paris Campus); Jochen I. Menges (University of Zurich)
The use of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has penetrated organizations at every level. Many leaders utilize this technology not only for cognitive tasks, such as creativity and problem-solving, but also for more emotional labor, including communication with coworkers and followers. Even though the effects on the receivers of such AI-generated leader communication have gotten some attention, little is known about the effects on the senders: the leaders. We propose a mechanism grounded in psychological ownership theory and the augmentation/automation discussion in management, by which using GenAI for leader communication leads to less empathy and closeness felt for the receiver of this communication. This disengagement, we propose, is due to a loss of psychological ownership over the written communication. In a randomized experiment (N = 99) wherein participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions of either no GenAI use, augmentative GenAI use, or GenAI automation, we show that using GenAI as automation, but not as augmentation, lowers psychological ownership. The loss of ownership led to participants feeling less empathetic towards the receiver, resulting in a behavioral outcome of deciding against supporting the receiver monetarily. Our paper contributes to our understanding of AI authorship in a leadership setting and, importantly, demonstrates that empathy erosion through GenAI automation could impact downstream leader decisions.
