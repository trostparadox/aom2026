Leaders’ Social Comparisons, Frontline Employees’ Stress and Organizational Citizenship Behavior

OB
Philipp Schreder (Seeburg Castle University); Clemens Hutzinger (Seeburg Castle University)
This work examines how leader-induced social comparisons influence frontline employees’ stress perceptions and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) toward their leaders. Based on social comparison theory and the challenge–hindrance stress approach, two experimental studies were conducted. The results of study 1 (n = 163) demonstrate that leader-induced upward comparisons (i.e., being compared with a better-performing colleague) result in significantly lower OCB toward the leader than downward comparisons (i.e., being compared with a worse-performing colleague). Study 2 (n = 308) reconfirms this main effect and extends it to the underlying mechanism. The results demonstrate that leader-induced upward comparisons increase hindrance stress and reduce challenge stress, whereas leader-induced downward comparisons have the opposite effect. Hindrance stress negatively affects OCB, whereas challenge stress positively affects OCB. Contrary to our expectations, perceived time availability for the task on which the social comparison was based had no significant direct effect (and interaction with social comparison) on challenge stress, hindrance stress, or OCB. Overall, the findings highlight the central role of leader-induced social comparisons in stress processes and frontline employees’ voluntary engagement within a work context.

Coping That Costs: How Leader Desensitization Perpetuates Workplace Trauma

OB
Nicole Alonso (Luiss university); Jennifer Mezzapelle (George Mason University); Matthew Crayne (University at Albany, State University of New York)
Scholars have demonstrated the positive impact of leader support on followers’ well-being and work quality. However, in the context of traumatic work, little research has investigated how leaders’ own trauma experiences and subsequent coping strategies influence followers’ experiences with traumatic work. Building on relational leadership theory and the concept of intergenerational trauma, this qualitative study explores how trauma is perpetuated or prevented in workplaces through the expectations and norms modeled by leaders. Analysis of 35 interviews with junior and senior researchers in terrorism and targeted violence research shows that leaders engage in either positive or maladaptive coping mechanisms to continue doing their work. The choice of coping mechanism in turn dictates whether leaders perpetuate or break cycles of trauma at work. Findings suggest that when leaders engage in maladaptive coping mechanisms in their work (e.g., desensitization), they make poor decisions regarding their research and mentoring which contributes to a cycle where traumatic exposures are insufficiently addressed, normalized, and even encouraged, ultimately placing subordinates’ well-being at risk. Conversely, leaders who engage in positive coping mechanisms (e.g., staying engaged in their work) make more sensitive decisions concerning research and mentoring which foster open acknowledgment and healthy processing of trauma, thereby breaking cycles of trauma in the workplace. This study demonstrates the need for greater awareness and action from leaders in traumatic work contexts to protect their followers and prevent cycles of trauma.

Managing Cognitive Load from W_ICTs: Information Processing Pathways to Daily Work Goal Progress

OB
Jiong Guo; Yuhui Jiang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Yan Pan (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Yufan Shang (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
Due to the widespread use of information and communication tools (ICTs), employees have to address work-related information outside of working hours. Drawing on cognitive load theory, this study examines how and when W_ICTs influences employees' daily work goal progress through information processing mechanisms. Using a 10-day diary study with 155 full-time employees in China (1,058 observations), we found that W_ICTs exerts a double-edged effect on work goal progress by simultaneously increasing information availability (which facilitates germane cognitive processing) and information overload (which induces extraneous cognitive load). Moreover, Lower emotional stability can amplify the negative effects of information overload. Our findings advance understanding of W_ICTs double-edged sword effects by revealing the dynamic information processing mechanisms through which after-hours connectivity shapes daily performance outcomes.

When Digital Demands Undermine Safety: ICT Demands, Negative Rumination, and Safety Performance

OB
Xinglan Yang (Harbin Institute of Technology); Jianing Guo (Dalian University of Technology); Chuanyi Luan (Harbin Institute of Technology); Li Zhang (Harbin Institute of Technology)
This study examines how information and communication technology (ICT) demands influence nurses’ safety behavior through negative rumination, and how ego strength and age jointly shape this process. Drawing on the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) framework, we explain how digital job demands undermine safety performance. A two-wave survey was conducted with 498 nurses from ten hospitals in China, separated by a three-week interval. We tested a three-way moderated mediation model linking ICT demands, negative rumination, ego strength, and age. ICT demands increased negative rumination, which in turn reduced safety compliance and participation. The indirect effects varied by ego strength and age: they were stronger among younger nurses with low ego strength and middle-aged nurses with high ego strength. The findings highlight that managing ICT demands and fostering personal resources are critical for maintaining safety in digitalized workplaces. Age-specific interventions that enhance psychological resilience may buffer the adverse effects of digital stress. This research integrates cognitive-affective mechanisms into the JD–R framework and identifies a three-way boundary condition, advancing understanding of leadership and resource management in technology-driven healthcare contexts.

Distinguishing Work Engagement from Burnout and Job Attitudes: A Meta-Analysis

OB
Xiaohong Xu (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Peng Zhao (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Hope Jeanes (The University of Texas at San Antonio); Christian Dormann (Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz); Jie Ma (Lanzhou University)
Empirical studies predominantly treat work engagement as a unidirectional antecedent of job performance, often overlooking alternative temporal orderings, the time element, and ongoing debates about its distinctiveness from burnout and job attitudes. To address these issues, we employed meta-analytic cross-lagged panel analyses to (1) test the temporal relationships between work engagement and job performance, (2) examine boundary conditions (i.e., time lag and performance dimensions), and (3) compare these dynamic relationships with those involving burnout, job satisfaction, and organizational commitment. We found an asymmetric, reciprocal relationship between engagement and overall job performance such that the effect of work engagement on subsequent overall performance was stronger than the reverse effect. Further, the dynamic relationships between engagement/burnout and performance diverged across task performance, citizenship behaviors, deviance behaviors, creative performance, and proactive performance. We also found that these relationships changed over time: the effect of engagement on subsequent performance decreased over time, whereas the effect of burnout on subsequent performance increased. Finally, the dynamics relationships between engagement and performance differed from those involving burnout, organizational commitment, and job satisfaction. Our findings provide important theoretical and practical implications, highlighting the critical role of time involving causal processes, and the distinctiveness of engagement from burnout and job attitudes.
