Stay or Go? Coordination and Shared Situational Awareness Under Temporary Interdependence

OMT
Kyosuke Tanaka (Aarhus University); Dorthe Doejbak Haakonsson (Aarhus University); Erik Reimer Larsen (Aarhus University); Santiago Arango (National University of Colombia)
This paper examines how coordination under temporary interdependencies can be facilitated by information structures that serve to foster shared situational awareness. Today’s central challenge of coordination is no longer just aligning tasks among agents but managing the temporary interdependencies that emerge as agents act, uncover new information, and respond to shifting environments. We hypothesize that in this organizational work setting, situational shared awareness (SSA)—a group-level understanding of the current state of the task, others’ actions, and resource availability—serves as the key mechanism explaining the relationship between information structures and coordination. To test this idea, we conduct a group experiment with 66 seven-person groups (N = 462 participants). In our experiment, participants engage in a group resource coordination task in randomly assigned information structures: independent, centralized, and decentralized. Results show that a decentralized structure facilitates a higher level of SSA and, in turn, leads to more successful coordination than centralized and independent structures. Contrary to our hypothesis, a centralized structure does not differ from an independent structure in fostering SSA and coordination due to awareness tension between central and peripheral members. Our findings contribute to the literature on coordination, information processing, and organization design by providing empirical evidence that SSA mediates the relationship between information structures and coordination.

Algorithmic and Relational Drivers of Declining Bargaining Power on Digital Platforms

OMT
Ayaj Rana (Cornell University)
Digital freelance platforms govern work by coupling algorithmic reputation systems with incentives to sustain recurring client relationships, embedding freelancers in evaluative and relational forms of dependence. How these intertwined mechanisms of platform governance shape freelancers’ bargaining power vis-à-vis clients remains theoretically underexplored. This study addresses this gap through a complementary qualitative and quantitative investigation of freelancers on Upwork and Fiverr. Qualitative evidence shows a patterned erosion of freelancers’ bargaining power as a platform-mediated exchange unfolds across contracting, execution, and delivery, driven by the dual imperatives of maintaining algorithmic visibility and preserving client ties. Each stage reveals distinct dependence dynamics: visibility pressures soften negotiation during contracting; hidden feedback and client expectations prompt scope stretching during execution; and clients’ outsized control over ratings and refunds makes dissent prohibitively costly at delivery. Quantitative analyses model bargaining power as a within-project trajectory and demonstrate that recurring-client dependence and reputational standing each predict steeper declines, with their interaction amplifying the pattern. The findings advance theories of platform governance by conceptualizing control as the engineered design of dependence, extend relational governance by identifying a boundary condition under algorithmic evaluation, and recast autonomy in digital work as a contingent capacity to manage evaluative and relational dependence.

The Buffering Trap :Resource Allocation and the Dynamics of Dependence Under Structural Uncertainty

OMT
Oliver Pan (National Taiwan University)
This paper theorizes why firms “panic hoard” inventory under geopolitical turmoil and structural uncertainty. Rather than treating inventory as operational waste, it reconceptualizes trade wars, sanctions, and regulatory volatility as dependence threats that destabilize firms’ access to critical inputs, transforming calculable parameter risk into existential dependence risk. Drawing on Resource Dependence Theory, the paper develops a framework in which firms manage this threat through two mechanisms: buffering (inventory accumulation) and bridging (spatial restructuring of supply). It argues that under structural uncertainty, buffering and bridging are temporally bracketed substitutes competing for limited financial slack: firms often buffer first to survive the response latency required to bridge, yet excessive buffering can deplete the resources needed for structural exit, creating a self-reinforcing “Buffering Trap.” The model further specifies a hierarchy of constraints—asset specificity as a feasibility filter and organizational complexity as an execution filter—that shapes firms’ ability to move from temporal autonomy to structural autonomy and explains persistent, suboptimal hoarding behavior across otherwise similar policy environments.

Government Mandates, Manager Anticipatory Compliance, and a Partisan Enforcement Expectation Filter

OMT
Ben A Rissing (Cornell University); Katie Apker (Cornell University)
Government mandates (e.g., laws, executive orders) are often initially change-prone and subject to legal contestation. Yet, some managers promptly comply despite uncertainty regarding mandate legality and scope. Less is known about the drivers of managers’ anticipatory compliance decisions. Studying this, we examine U.S. President Trump’s 2017 “Muslim ban” executive order, which sought to ban U.S. entry for immigrants from seven majority-Muslim countries. Leveraging government administrative records on employer-sponsored immigrant work authorization applications, we analyze managers’ response to the ban through anticipatory compliance (voluntary application withdrawal). Using a difference-in-differences analysis, we find withdrawal rates increased from 0.3 to 8.5 percent for immigrants from targeted majority Muslim countries in the year after the ban, relative to the year before, peaking at 29 percent. No withdrawal increase occurred for immigrants from non-targeted majority Muslim countries or the rest of the world. We further find that ideological considerations moderate the ban’s withdrawal effect: no withdrawal increase is observed for immigrants from targeted majority Muslim countries when applications are filed by managers at employers with Democratic-leaning executives or employers in Democratic-leaning counties. Findings emphasize a partisan filter in regulatory enforcement expectation, managers’ responsiveness to mandate specifics, and government leaders’ capacity to shape labor market dynamics.
