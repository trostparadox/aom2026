Social Entrepreneurs’ Ethical Dilemmas in the New Era of Mandatory Sustainability Reporting

SIM
Sophie Bacq (IMD - International Institute for Management Development); Florian Hoos (IMD - International Institute for Management Development); Georgios Polychronopoulos (IMD - International Institute for Management Development)
Mandatory sustainability reporting is reshaping how organizations measure and communicate their impact, and its influence is no longer limited to large corporations. Emerging regulatory frameworks and standardized metrics are expected to trickle down into the world of social entrepreneurship, a field historically grounded in mission-driven values, context-specific outcomes, and normative commitments to justice, inclusion, and systems change. We identify five interrelated ethical dilemmas on ideology, identity, mission drift, resourcing, and collaboration, that arise as social entrepreneurs navigate the ethical tensions between external reporting demands and their normative commitments. Drawing on ethical decision-making, we analyze how these dilemmas emerge across the stages of moral awareness, moral judgment, moral intent, and moral behavior. In doing so, we offer a “thick” conceptualization of mandatory sustainability reporting as a moral issue and show how it may catalyze a paradigmatic shift toward the formalization of social entrepreneurship as a field increasingly governed by standardized accountability logics.

From Organized Immaturity to Disorganizing Measures: Dissensus and Exit Rights

SIM
Marian Eabrasu (EM Normandie)
Technologization is intensifying knowledge asymmetries between market actors and deepening dependence on socio-technical systems, raising social-issue questions about autonomy, domination, and the possibilities of emancipation in contemporary business. Joining the organized immaturity conversations, this paper theorizes expertise delegation as a central mechanism through which knowledge advantages translate into control over less knowledgeable principals. It develops a conceptual argument that delegation does not inevitably produce dependence when principals retain the capacity to revise, reroute, or terminate delegation arrangements. By shifting attention from constraining expert agents through top-down regulation alone to enabling principal capabilities, the paper contributes an empowerment-oriented account of autonomy under widening knowledge gaps.

Second-Order Hybrid Organizations

SIM
Joao Miguel Peixoto Guimaraes Milanez (Wilfrid Laurier University); Anicet Fangwa Nantcho (McGill University); Luciano Barin Cruz (HEC Montréal); Bertrand V. Quelin (HEC Paris)
In the management literature, hybridity refers to the coexistence of competing institutional logics, such as social enterprises balancing social and market goals. By employing market practices to deliver social benefits to people in need, these hybrid organizations are essential to tackle complex societal challenges that neither governments nor market-driven companies can solve alone, such as climate change, social inequality, and access to essential services. Scholars have also explored and explained the challenges that hybridity entails: hybrid organizations are constantly dealing with tensions between the social-driven mission and the market-driven need of profitability to ensure its long-term survival, between their stakeholders’ social and private interests, and between social legitimacy and economic efficiency. However, this traditional definition conceives hybridity as an exclusively intra-organizational phenomenon, missing emergent organizational forms that operate beyond the boundaries of a single entity. In this paper, we propose the concept of Second Order Hybrid Organizations (SOHO) to describe arrangements that embody hybridity not within one organization, but between two organizational units or governance domains functioning as an interorganizational arrangement, such as dual-purpose dyads or cross-sector joint ventures. This study sharpens the definition of second-order hybrid organizations and distinguishes them from “first order” hybrids such as social enterprises and traditional PPPs. It examines how these multi-organizational arrangements are sustained across divergent governance modes and institutional logics, what are the mechanisms that enable coherence, and what are the distinct tensions and legitimacy challenges they face. Finally, the paper elaborates on the mechanisms of value creation and value appropriation that SOHO mobilize. The notion of SOHO extends the hybridity literature in two important ways: first, it reframes hybridity as a systemic property, rather than an organizational one; and second, it offers a way to theorize the adaptive advantages of structural separation between members of an organizational arrangement.

Navigating the Efficiency–Participation Paradox in Decision-Making: Adaptations of the Ringi System

SIM
Yuri Hirayama (EGADE Business School sede Monterrey); Rajiv Maher (Monterrey Institute of Technology)
This study explores how Japanese managers in multinational corporations (MNCs) navigate the efficiency–participation paradox in corporate sustainability (CS) decision-making. Drawing on Paradox Theory and the concept of explicitization, we examine how traditionally implicit, norm-based practices, specifically the Japanese ringi decision-making system, are reconfigured into structured processes that preserve participation while enhancing efficiency. Using qualitative data from 30 managers, mainly from Japan, we show that contemporary ringi practices strategically explicitize participation, align CSR and ESG goals early, and incorporate formal approval and performance monitoring, enabling managers to manage contradictory stakeholder expectations without sacrificing legitimacy or execution speed. Our findings suggest that explicitization produces a hybrid governance mechanism in which participation, hierarchy, and accountability coexist dynamically while preserving culturally embedded norms. We contribute to existing literature by showing how Japanese MNC managers navigate the efficiency–participation paradox through contemporary adaptations of the ringi system, suggesting that explicit CSR practices do not necessarily undermine implicit CSR and that organizations can simultaneously sustain broad participation and timely decision-making. Practically, we propose that managers in other organizational settings, including Western contexts, can adopt culturally relevant variations of the ringi system to sustain both participation and efficiency while managing the inherent paradox.

Empowerment For Democracy Through Multistakeholder Partnerships

SIM
Clara Emefa Asempapa (Ca' Foscari University of Venice); Andjela Pavlovic (Venice School of Management - Ca' Foscari University); Francesco Rullani (Venice School of Management - Ca' Foscari University)
This paper examines how multistakeholder partnerships generate empowerment among marginalised beneficiaries in global supply chains. Drawing on survey data from 129 cocoa farmers in a Fair-Trade project spanning Europe and West Africa, we analyse how different forms of participation relate to empowerment in the family and cooperative levels. Our findings challenge the common assumption that direct beneficiary involvement in partnerships necessarily enhances empowerment. Beneficiaries’ familiarity with the project partners (used as a proxy for direct engagement) is not associated with higher empowerment and in some cases, it is negatively related. In contrast, beneficiaries who perceive that their cooperative is influential in the partnership feel more empowered both in their families and cooperatives. These patterns suggest that empowerment is mediated through intermediary organisations rather than direct engagement with external partners. A closer examination of one cooperative show that cultural norms and centralised governance can further constrain empowerment, even when beneficiaries are highly exposed to all actors of the partnership. Overall, this study highlights the central role of intermediaries in fostering democratic participation, challenging models of empowerment based on beneficiary inclusion alone.
