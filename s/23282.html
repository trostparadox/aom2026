Coimprovisation and Coevolution: Customer Engagement, Entrepreneurial Capacities, and Value Creation

ENT
Jie Wu (University of Aberdeen); Patrick J. Murphy (University of Alabama at Birmingham); Tingting Zhou (University of Aberdeen)
This paper introduces coimprovisation as a distinct form of organizational coevolution that integrates customer engagements and firm innovation capabilities. We review research and theory in strategic management, entrepreneurship, and marketing to develop a coimprovisation framework rooted in original theories from Lewin and Volberda (1999) and Abatecola et al. (2020). The framework defines coimprovisation in terms of evolutions that adapt continuously and reciprocally based on interactions between firms and customers. Coimprovisation generates value for firms by extending the traditional duality of exploration and exploitation based on unique entrepreneurial capacities. The framework in this paper entails four value creation dimensions: (1) continuous customer participation in innovation processes, (2) prompt and explosive bidirectional signals, (3) customers as co-creators and innovation catalysts across firm boundaries, and (4) emergent relational dynamics that create shared meaning and emotional resonance between firms and customers. These activities go beyond transactional exchanges to encompass the symbolic and experiential dimensions of value creation. On these grounds, the coimprovisation framework complements conventional supply and demand-based approaches and customary firm boundaries with a richer conceptualization of interrelations across those traditional boundaries. This paper contributes to organizational coevolution theory by reframing the micro-level mechanisms of firm-customer adaptation and integrating them with entrepreneurship and innovation activities.

Community Engagement as Moral Governance: A Process Model of Value Co-Creation in Social Enterprise

ENT
Diwakar Singh; Richa Awasthy
Social enterprises increasingly rely on community engagement to sustain social impact and financial viability, particularly in resource-constrained and morally sensitive contexts. However, existing research offers limited processual explanations of how community engagement enables value co-creation under conditions of asymmetry, vulnerability, and ethical obligation. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative study of three Indian eye-care social enterprises, this study develops a four-stage process model explaining how community engagement becomes a morally grounded governance mechanism for sustained value creation. Based on 132 days of immersive fieldwork, we show how engagement unfolds sequentially through (1) moral framing of service, (2) relational trust-building through patient-centered practices, (3) reciprocal role stabilization between organizations and communities, and (4) delegated co-production that sustains social, economic, and moral value. We further demonstrate how outcomes of co-production recursively reinforce moral legitimacy and trust, enabling scale without mission drift. By reframing community engagement as a dynamic, morally grounded process rather than a static attribute or normative ideal, this study advances theories of value co-creation, hybrid organizing, and moral legitimacy. The findings offer a processual explanation of how social enterprises mobilize communities as contributors to ethical scale and multidimensional sustainability. Keywords: Social enterprises, Community engagement, Value co-creation, Moral legitimacy, Hybrid organizing, Process theory

Creating More with Less: Social Value Creation in Resource-Constrained Environments

ENT
Yang Liu (University of Southampton); Mokter Hossain (Qatar University); Ira Chatterjee (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Suman Phalswal
Existing studies rarely examine how resource access and dependence shape the process of social value creation. Furthermore, different forms of social value creation remain largely unexplored. To address these gaps, we investigate how social entrepreneurs create both base and supplementary social value in resource-constrained environments. Drawing on resource dependence theory and an inductive multiple case study of 12 social enterprises in India, we distinguish between base social value that arises from the pre-defined social mission, and supplementary social value, which emerges organically through activities beyond the mission’s scope. Our findings show how entrepreneurs’ marginalized backgrounds and resource constraints, when interacting with ecosystem support and venture development, shape the moral, relational, and material motivations for supplementary social value creation. We develop a process model illustrating how entrepreneurs evolve from recipients to providers of resources, thereby restructuring dependencies. This study contributes to resource dependence theory and social entrepreneurship research by showing how constraints, support, and value creation interact, highlighting the generative potential of scarcity for social value creation.

Exogenous Shocks in Social entrepreneurship: A Systematic Review and Agenda

ENT
John Aiyede (University of Louisville); Sergey Anokhin (University of Louisville)
This study advances a legitimacy-centered explanation of social value creation under macro-level change by theorizing how unpredictable–gradual shocks enable, rather than merely constrain, social entrepreneurship. Drawing on a structured synthesis of prior studies in conflict-affected regions, public-health crises, climate-stressed communities, and historically traumatized societies, the analysis integrates the external enabler framework with institutional theory to examine how normative and regulative legitimacy operate through distinct institutional carriers. The study shows that normative legitimacy primarily authorizes collective action by embedding moral obligations, shared values, and expectations within symbolic and relational systems, while regulative legitimacy stabilizes coordination by translating these moral claims into routinized practices and material artifacts that substitute for weak or fragmented state institutions. By tracing how legitimacy travels through symbolic systems, relational authority structures, organizational routines, and artifacts, the study reconceptualizes legitimacy as a carrier-specific enabling mechanism under slow-burn uncertainty. The findings contribute to entrepreneurship and organization theory by clarifying how legitimacy converts cumulative shocks into durable social value creation and by offering a process-oriented framework for understanding entrepreneurial action in fragile and transforming institutional environments.

How Do Intrapreneurial Units Achieve Value Co-Creation Based on Optimal Distinctiveness Strategies?

ENT
Huichen Shang (Nankai University); Wangbin Hu; Zhu Yu (Nankai University); Shuo Liu (Nankai University); boxiong li (Nankai University)
With corporate social entrepreneurship increasingly becoming an important approach to tackling social issues, a key challenge faced by intrapreneurial units is balancing social missions with economic sustainability. Focusing on the dual-mission tension, this paper presents a longitudinal single-case study of Juzhi Holistic Health Technology Service Group Co., Ltd in China. Guided by the perspective of unison of recognition and action, this study integrates sense-making theory and opportunity-resource integration theory to clarify the optimal distinctiveness strategies of intrapreneurial units, thereby constructing a dynamic evolution model of the value co-creation modes. The findings reveal that the process through which intrapreneurial units achieve value co-creation occurs across three stages: the single-business pilot stage, the multi-business parallel stage, and the ecosystem expansion stage. Each stage is marked by distinct tensions. To address these varying tensions, intrapreneurial units consistently adopted differentiated optimal distinctiveness strategies based on the unity of recognition and action. Ultimately. These efforts drove the dynamic evolution of the value co-creation mode from the output-oriented mode to the shared mode, and further to the enabled mode. This study reveals the internal cognitive and behavioral mechanisms through which intrapreneurial units overcome mission tension and foster value co-creation in the context of corporate social entrepreneurship. It provides theoretical insights and practical implications for understanding the growth of intrapreneurial units.
