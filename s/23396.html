Decoding the Impact of Artificial Intelligence on ESG Performance: Mechanisms and Pathways

ONE
Hubin Li (School of Management, Northwestern Polytechnical University); Xue yan Dong (School of Management, Northwestern Polytechnical University); Jun Zhang
This study examines how artificial intelligence (AI) adoption affects corporate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance and when these effects are stronger. Using panel data on Chinese A-share-listed firms from 2011 to 2023, we identify AI adopters and estimate their ESG impact with firm- and year-fixed effects regressions and propensity score matching. We find that AI adoption is associated with higher ESG scores, consistent with AI improving information processing, monitoring, and coordination in corporate governance. These gains are stronger in firms with more developed corporate digital responsibility (CDR), which provides processes and norms that channel AI toward responsible and stakeholder-oriented uses. Internal Party organizations further reinforce the AI–CDR interaction by enhancing oversight and aligning ESG initiatives with institutional priorities. Overall, the findings show how digital governance capabilities and institutional embedding jointly shape the sustainability benefits of AI.

Managing the Environmental Impacts of the Substitution of Human Intelligence with Generative AI

ONE
Nathan Sorin (Technical University of Munich); Margherita Pagani; Rene Bohnsack
Firms increasingly infuse artificial intelligence (AI) in their products and processes by establishing relations with possessors of “AI boundary resources”. Previous studies show that reliance on AI boundary resources increases sales, reduces costs, and improves energy efficiency, resulting in economic and environmental benefits across the economy. Yet, we introduce a new substitution mechanism generated by large generative AI models: immaterial human intelligence is replaced by electricity and water-greedy AI in generative reasoning tasks. Specifically, we argue that industries’ environmental footprints unevenly changed based on their exposure to large language models (LLMs). We further hypothesize that the effect is more pronounced for industries exposed to large multimodal models (LMMs). Using industry-level panel data (1997-2024) on the US economy, we take advantage of the sudden release of ChatGPT to implement a regression discontinuity in time design and hence provide a clean causal estimate. We find LLMs significantly increase indirect electricity and water consumption through information technology (IT) outsourcing. We also confirm that the effect is amplified for LMMs. This amplification weakens over time, suggesting that increasing anti-deepfake policies may be mitigating the negative environmental impact of LMMs. However, we find a positive yet not significant effect on IT outsourcing itself. This result suggests AI boundary resource owners are subsidizing the use of LLMs and LMMs at the expense of environmental sustainability, hoping to control economic value capture in the future. We discuss implications for theory, management, and policy.

When Speed Becomes a Liability: The Pace of Digital Transformation and Environmental Sustainability

ONE
Elitsa Rumenova Banalieva (Northeastern University); Christian Keen (Laval University); Denis Lescop (Montpellier Business School)
We extend new institutional economics by introducing a dynamic perspective on digital transformation and its environmental consequences. Rather than focusing on the static scope of digital transformation—its level in a given year—we theorize and operationalize the pace of digital transformation, defined as year-to-year change. This construct captures both the direction and magnitude of digitalization and reveals dynamics that static measures obscure. We argue that a faster national pace of digital transformation can negatively affect sustainable environmental performance. In contrast to the conventional view that improvements in formal institutions reduce transaction costs and institutional voids, we theorize that rapid digital transformation may constitute an institutional liability by outpacing complementary regulatory, infrastructural, and organizational adjustments, thereby intensifying environmental degradation. Integrating insights from dynamic capabilities theory, we develop a temporal alignment perspective that highlights the multilevel nature of digital transformation. We conceptualize the pace of firms’ digital transformation as a dynamic capability and argue that environmental outcomes depend on the temporal alignment between governmental and firm-level digital transformation. Specifically, we propose that countries achieve higher sustainable environmental performance when these paces are aligned—whether both are fast or both are slow—than when they are misaligned. We test our hypotheses using a panel dataset of 38 OECD countries from 2010 to 2022. The results provide broad support for our framework.

Carbon Accountability Beyond Visibility: Rethinking Emissions Governance in Cryptocurrency Economy

ONE
Md Noor Uddin Milon (University of Wollongong); Abdullah Al-Mamun (University of Wollongong); Walid El Hamad (University of Wollongong)
Cryptocurrency generates substantial carbon emissions, yet these emissions remain largely invisible within carbon accounting frameworks (CAFs). This study examines why crypto-generated emissions escape formal accountability in corporate disclosure and how existing governance regimes structure their exclusion. Drawing on qualitative, documentary-based textual analysis, we analyse sustainability disclosures from 20 publicly listed cryptocurrency-mining and digital-asset treasury companies, alongside five major CAFs, covering the period 2021–2025. The findings reveal a systematic accountability gap characterised by selective disclosure, weak integration of carbon information into financial reporting, and limited assurance practices. We demonstrate that this gap is not driven by technical measurement constraints. Rather, it arises from institutionalised logics embedded in CAFs—specifically entity-bounded, production-centric, and control-based assumptions that privilege traditional organisational forms while marginalising decentralised digital infrastructures. Drawing on institutional logics and legitimacy theory, the study explains how these frameworks render crypto-carbon emissions institutionally invisible while enabling symbolic compliance with sustainability expectations. To address this misalignment, we introduce the Crypto-Carbon Accountability (CCA) framework, which reconceptualises carbon responsibility along relational, computational, and institutional dimensions. While grounded in the cryptocurrency context, the study offers broader insights into the limits of firm-centric carbon governance and contributes to organisational research on accountability, sustainability, and emerging digital economies.

Beyond The Hype: A Literature Review of Blockchain and Environmental Sustainability (WITHDRAWN)

ONE
Alexandros Eleftheriadis (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Andreas Alexiou (University of Southampton Business School); Brian Vincent Tjemkes (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
The potential of blockchain technology for environmental sustainability remains a subject of debate. We provide a systematic review of 116 empirical studies to understand how this technology serves ecological ends in practice. Using sustainability-oriented innovation (SOI) as our analytical lens, we organise the literature around SOI stages – Operational Optimization, Organizational Transformation and Systems Building and detail on antecedents, mechanisms, and consequences. Our results also indicate different environmental sustainability frames employed across studies: Instrumental-Constraint, Instrumental-Opportunity, and Relational. By juxtaposing the SOI stages with environmental sustainability frames, our results indicate that these frames shape blockchain approaches and their implementation. Guided by these results, we discuss two thematic patterns permeating the literature – (1) the decentralization paradox and (2) the anthropocentric boundary – enabling us to develop a Blockchain-environmental sustainability typology: Efficiency Optimisation, Stakeholder Awareness, Coordination Improvement, and Relational Transformation. We contribute to the literature by stipulating that technology and sustainability framing are intertwined, thus challenging technological determinism in sustainability research. Furthermore, we conclude that Blockchain possesses latent coordination capabilities that remain dormant under instrumental framing but are realised under relational framing. Framing is not merely rhetorical; it is constitutive. We conclude with a research agenda for the future.
