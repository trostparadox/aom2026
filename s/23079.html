A Person-Centered Examination of Motivational Profiles and Employment Preferences Across Age Groups

HR
Daisy Wang (University of Maryland Global Campus); Zheni Wang (Southern Connecticut State University)
This cross-sectional study examines how age-group differences in work motivation and basic psychological needs relate to preferences between self- and wage-employment. Drawing on Self-Determination and Lifespan Development Theories, age groups are conceptualized as life-stage categories rather than birth cohorts, and motivational profiles are compared in the post-COVID US labor market. Online survey data were collected from 1,484 adult Americans across five age groups, from Gen Z to Traditionalist. Multi-group CFA for measurement invariance, ANOVA, latent mean comparisons, and latent profile analysis with distal outcome comparisons were performed. Results indicated significant, albeit small, age-group differences in employment preferences and motivational constructs. Gen Z-young adults reported relatively stronger preference for self-employment than older age-groups, whereas Gen Y (Millennials), early to mid-career, exhibited the highest autonomous motivation, the lowest amotivation, and greater satisfaction of autonomy and competence needs at work. Five distinct motivational profiles were identified from latent profile analyses; low-intensity and controlled–amotivated profiles were most strongly associated with a preference for self-employment, whereas a high-involvement yet conflicted profile showed the strongest preference for wage-employment. Younger participants, particularly Gen Z and Gen Y, were more likely to fall into globally elevated and psychologically complex profiles. Overall, the findings suggest that age-linked life stages are associated with nuanced motivational configurations that only modestly translate into employment preferences, highlighting the value of developmentally sensitive, person-centered approaches to understanding contemporary work, employment preferences, and entrepreneurial orientations.

Benefits or Burdens? The Mixed Blessings of Age-Inclusive HR Practices for Older Employees

HR
Xiao Jun Zhan; Yi Wan (Jiangxi University of Finance and Economics); Xue Han (Renmin University of China)
Age-inclusive human resource practices (AIHRP) have often been considered beneficial for older employees. This study goes beyond this perspective and examines whether AIHRP can also impose unintended demands and strain on older employees. Drawing on the job demands–resources model and cognitive appraisal theory of emotion, we examined the double-edged sword effect of AIHRP on older employees’ organizational citizenship behavior toward the organization (OCBO). Through an interview-based qualitative study (Study 1) and a time-lagged field study (Study 2), we found that AIHRP trigger two distinct pathways: (a) a resource–motivation pathway, in which AIHRP are positively associated with OCBO via perceived organizational career opportunities and state gratitude, and (b) a demand–strain pathway, in which AIHRP are negatively associated with OCBO via their perceived organizational expected contribution and workplace anxiety. We further identified positive framing as a boundary condition underlying the demands–resources paradox of AIHRP. The theoretical and practical implications of our findings are discussed.

How Collaborating With AI Helps Aging Employees Mitigate Career Plateau and Shape Workplace Behavior

HR
Xinyu Zhang (Beijing Technology and Business University)
As organizations increasingly adopt AI tools to enhance productivity, the implications for aging employees, those nearing the latter stages of their careers, remain largely unknown. Aging employees often face unique challenges, including hierarchical and job-content plateaus, which can stifle motivation and engagement. Simultaneously, AI collaboration presents opportunities for skill development and competence enhancement, potentially mitigating these plateaus by fostering a sense of mastery and relevance. Drawing on self-determination theory, we propose that aging employees’ collaboration with AI satisfies their competence need, which reduces hierarchical and job-content plateaus, and these relations are moderated by perceived organizational age-support. Furthermore, hierarchical and job-content plateau are expected to be associated with taking more charge at work and less undermining of younger coworkers. We test our theoretical model using a multi-source, multi-wave field study and an experiment. All predicted relationships are supported, except that the expected positive association between job-content plateau and undermining younger coworkers does not emerge. The results clarify how and when AI collaboration can revitalize late-career development and improve intergenerational dynamics at work.

Age Discrimination and Quiet Quitting Among Older Employees: A Blame Attribution Perspective

HR
Quan Li (Nankai University); Zhuolin She; Mengzhen Guo; Xiaoning Hu (Huazhong Agricultural University)
As organizations grapple with rapidly aging workforces, understanding how age discrimination may trigger subtle yet consequential behaviors like quiet quitting has become increasingly critical. Drawing on the approach-inhibition theory of power and attribution theory, we examine how age discrimination influences older employees’ quiet quitting behaviors. Across a three-wave field survey (Study 1) and a vignette-based experiment (Study 2), we consistently find that age discrimination reduces older employees’ sense of power, which in turn increases their likelihood of engaging in quiet quitting. Moreover, the blame attribution serves as a key boundary condition such that self-directed blame attribution strengthens the negative effect of age discrimination on sense of power, whereas other-directed blame attribution weakens it. The moderated mediation analyses further show that the indirect effect of age discrimination on quiet quitting via diminished sense of power is more pronounced among employees adopting self-directed blame attribution than those adopting other-directed blame attribution. Overall, our findings deepen understanding of the psychological and behavioral costs of age-based bias, underscore the critical role of attributional processes in shaping older employees’ responses to discriminatory experiences, and offer actionable implications for building age-inclusive and empowering workplaces.

Aging Employee Creativity: The Drive of AI Mindfulness and the Drag of Perceived Age Discrimination

HR
Beini Liu (Beijing Technology and Business University)
Grounded in the job demands-resources theory, we focus on AI mindfulness, which reflects the extent to which employees focus on the present, pay attention to detail, demonstrate a willingness to explore alternative uses, and show genuine interest in investigating AI features and failures. We theorize that AI mindfulness enables aging employees to better manage cognitive job demands and mobilize internal strength-based resources. Specifically, we propose that aging employees’ AI mindfulness leads to both cognitive effort reduction and increased personal strengths use, which in turn enhances work engagement and ultimately fosters creativity. Moreover, we argue that perceived age discrimination disrupts the translation of cognitive effort reduction and strengths use into work engagement, thereby weakening the overall indirect effect of AI mindfulness on creativity. We tested these hypotheses through a multi-wave field study in China (N = 324) and a scenario-based experiment in the United States (N = 236), both of which provided consistent support for our proposed theoretical model. These findings advance our understanding of how aging employees engage with AI in the workplace and highlight pathways through which they can sustain creativity during digital transformation.
