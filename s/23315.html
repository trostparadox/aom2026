Digital Technology and Firm Strategy

Tuesday, Aug 4 2026 · 11:30AM–1:00PM
Pennsylvania Convention Center — 110B
STR

People

Moderator: Feng Wan Zhejiang University

Session overview

Why do firms that make credible commitments to emerging technologies still fail to fully implement them? Existing research explains technology adoption through competitive pressures, resources, managerial cognition, and institutional dynamics, but provides less insight into why firms differ in implementation outcomes post- commitment, producing a technology assimilation gap. I argue this gap arises because governance structures that facilitate implementation commitment can later constrain execution. Drawing on Transaction Cost Economics and Information Processing Theory, I introduce coordination specificity: the extent to which a firm’s information structures, communication routines, and information flows are customized to governance-specific organizational boundaries. I argue that coordination specificity creates stage-contingent information adjustment costs across two implementation stages: initiation, where firms make credible implementation commitments, and deployment, where firms scale technologies across the organization. Coordination specificity, operationalized through vertical integration, reduces initiation costs by improving estimation of value-chain information restructuring requirements, but increases deployment costs by creating architectural misalignment with emerging technology standards. Using panel data on smart meter implementations by U.S. electric utilities from 2010–2024, I find a stage-contingent paradox: vertical integration increases implementation initiation but reduces deployment intensity. I further show that inter-firm coordination through external governance mechanisms attenuates deployment frictions by reducing firm-specific architectural misalignment. Together, these findings advance an information-based explanation of the technology assimilation gap by showing how legacy governance structures generate opposing effects across implementation stages.

Corporate Digital Responsibility and Diversification: Evidence from Text Analysis Using LLM

STR
Ming Lyu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Caishi Zhang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)
This study adopts a corporate digital responsibility (CDR) perspective to extend the understanding of the internal decision-making mechanisms underlying diversification strategies in the digital economy, revealing how CDR reshapes firms’ trade-offs between diversification and focused strategies. Compared with prior studies that rely on primary data such as surveys, this study constructs a novel measure of CDR using large language models applied to corporate social responsibility reports. Based on a large-scale publicly available sample of 7,006 firm-year observations from 1,269 Chinese listed firms, we systematically examine the impact of CDR on diversification strategies. The results indicate that: CDR significantly constrains diversification, with effects amplified by supply chain concentration, digital asset intensity, and corporate risk-taking. These findings not only extend the theoretical understanding and underlying decision-making logic of diversification in the digital era but also deepen our comprehension of the consequences of CDR, highlighting that CDR is not merely a regulatory requirement but also a strategic consideration for firms.

A Theory of Digital Diversification

STR
Gianvito Lanzolla (Bayes Business School); Constantinos Markides (London Business School)
Why do some digitally enabled firms sustain broad, seemingly unrelated diversification while others remain constrained by traditional scope limits? Existing diversification theories explain scope expansion through asset relatedness and demand-side complementarities, implicitly coupling opportunity visibility with asset redeployability. We challenge this assumption by developing a theory of digital diversification that disentangles the ontology of productive assets from the mode of opportunity discovery. We argue that AI-mediated generative assets and Behavioral Opportunity Discovery jointly reshape how diversification opportunities are generated, enacted, and bounded. We introduce an engine–enactment framework in which generative representations enable the inferential discovery of opportunities across heterogeneous domains, while organizational mechanisms—cross-context inference, ecosystem-mediated coordination, and algorithmic control—govern how scope scales. We further theorize the endogenous limits of digital diversification, showing how representational fragility, algorithmic interdependence, and cross-context boundary friction attenuate or reverse scope expansion. Together, the theory reframes firm scope as a dynamic, path-dependent outcome of epistemic systems rather than a static choice among markets, and identifies a distinct logic of digital diversification that complements—rather than replaces—existing diversification theories.
