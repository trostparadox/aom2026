Creative Destruction? Mass Layoffs and Innovation Spillovers

TIM
SOO YEON KIM (University of California Merced)
Mass layoffs are typically viewed as harmful to regional economies. However, they may also connect otherwise disconnected innovation hubs by reallocating skilled workers and facilitating the flow of ideas across firms. This paper examines the causal effect of displaced inventors on the innovative productivity of their new coworkers. I construct a novel dataset linking LinkedIn job histories to USPTO patent records, enabling inventor-level tracking of mobility and innovation outcomes over time. To address endogeneity in inventor mobility, I use an instrumental variable based on geographic proximity to mass layoff events. The results show that incumbent inventors experience sizable increases in patent output and citations after being exposed to displaced inventors. These gains emerge quickly, persist over several years, and are concentrated among incumbents with lower pre-exposure productivity. In contrast, high-productivity incumbents experience little benefit or modest declines. The paper discusses potential mechanisms, including access to new technological knowledge and expanded external collaborative networks.

Firms’ Skills Shortages and Search Strategies

TIM
Paolo Carioli (Copenhagen Business School); Dirk Czarnitzki (KU Leuven)
Skills shortages represent a major challenge in developed, innovation-oriented economies and significantly affect firms’ innovation activities. Although firms may rely on external knowledge search to compensate for skill deficits, the skills and knowledge embedded in their workforce remain essential prerequisites for effective open innovation strategies. This study examines how firms’ skill scarcity shapes their innovation openness, which is a key determinant of innovation performance. We hypothesize that skills shortages are associated with a broader external knowledge search for innovation, and that this relationship is weaker for firms operating closer to the technological frontier. Using data from the German Com-munity Innovation Survey (CIS), we find support for our hypotheses, highlighting the close interrelationship between skills shortages and innovation openness.

The AI Reshuffle: Technology Adoption and Workforce Reorganization Within Firms

TIM
Dongmiao Zhang (Carnegie Mellon University); Nikhil George; Christophe Combemale (Carnegie Mellon University); Ramayya Krishnan (Carnegie Mellon University); Miguel Godinho de Matos (Católica Lisbon School of Business & Economics); Rahul Telang (Carnegie Mellon University)
We provide longitudinal evidence on how artificial intelligence (AI) adoption reshapes workforce composition using comprehensive administrative data linking AI adoption to employment outcomes in Portuguese firms (2014--2023). Using a staggered difference-in-differences design with two-way fixed effects, we document that AI adoption increases the employment share of domain technical experts (workers combining technical and domain-specific expertise) by around 0.7 percentage points, representing a 79\% increase from the pre-adoption baseline of 0.89\%. This reallocation begins immediately upon adoption and strengthens over time, indicating a structural rather than transitory adjustment. We further document pronounced heterogeneity by adoption intensity: AI adopters with high integration complexity experience a statistically significant 0.7-0.9 percentage-point larger increase in the share of domain-technical workers compared to low-complexity firms. This reallocation pattern suggests that AI integration requires simultaneously assessing technical feasibility and business value. It is inefficient to perform this task by separate technical and domain specialists, in particular when quick iteration is needed. Hybrid workers solve this by internalizing both knowledge types, enabling them to identify valuable applications that specialist coordination cannot reach and to optimize solutions without costly iterative cycles. Our results challenge automation-centric narratives, demonstrating that AI adoption drives systematic capability building favoring workers who integrate technical and domain knowledge, with implications for skill demand and organizational design.

Unequal Complements: How Digital–R&D Complementarity Shapes Individual Career Outcomes

TIM
Wenjing Zhu (Copenhagen Business School)
This study examines how complementarity between digital and R&D capabilities translates into career outcomes for individuals. Using matched employer-employee data on 4,677 employees with digital human capital in Denmark (2008-2016), we analyze the effect of firms’ R&D intensity on their long-term wage and promotion trajectories. In contrast to prior research showing that digital-R&D complementarities enhance firm performance, digital workers employed in R&D-intensive firms earn lower wages and receive fewer promotions. We explain this paradox through two mechanisms: (1) a learning-based perspective where digital skills become embedded in firm innovation, shifting rewards from monetary wages to non-monetary human capital and reducing individual bargaining power; and (2) a power-capture perspective where promotion is dictated by the scientific logic and symbolic capital of dominant R&D leadership, which marginalizes digital expertise. Our findings reveal a decoupling between firm-level value creation and individual-level value appropriation, although firms benefit from digital-R&D complementarities, individual rewards are unevenly distributed.
