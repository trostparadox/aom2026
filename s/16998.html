The Relative Value of Elite Education in Low-Trust Labor Markets

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 2:30PM–3:30PM
Sheraton Philadelphia Downtown Hotel — Poster Wall 4
OMT

People

Author: Gabrielle Lamont-Dobbin Columbia Business School

Session overview

Educational credentials–especially those from elite universities–are commonly assumed to offer a clear pathway to desirable jobs. Degrees typically convey competence, broad preparation, and long-term potential (Arrow, 1973; Di Stasio & Van de Werfhorst, 2016; DiPrete, Eller, Bol, & Van de Werfhorst, 2017). I examine how elite credentials may not always convey the same information; employers may draw inferences that diverge substantially from what graduates intend to signal. I show that while graduates expect their degrees to secure employment and access to higher-quality jobs, employers draw different inferences from the same credentials. For many employers, elite education signals: (1) theoretical rather than practical knowledge, (2) resistance to learning from supervisors, and (3) low organizational commitment. Thus, while employers do not necessarily discount elite education, they associate it with characteristics they seek to avoid. The research contributes to research on hiring and selection in developing economies, job seeker signaling, and elite credentials.
