The Strategic Blind Spot: Female CEO Succession and Competitors’ Risk-Taking

STR
Chenxi Zhang (Renmin University of China); Chunling Zhu (Renmin University of China); Ruxi Wang (Renmin University of China)
CEO succession in a rival firm creates a perceived window of opportunity for competitors. We challenge this view and argue that the window shuts when the new CEO is female. Integrating social role theory with competitive dynamics, we posit that gender stereotypes lead competitors to perceive female CEOs as less formidable rivals, reducing their strategic risk-taking. A difference-in-differences analysis of Chinese listed firms from 2011 to 2022 supports this. Competitors significantly decrease strategic investments after a peer firm’s female CEO succession. This effect is mitigated when a local female political leader legitimizes female authority, or when the female CEO is an insider, providing individuating information that counters stereotypes. Our study reveals a strategic blind spot where gender biases cause firms to forgo opportunities.

Glass Ceiling Disruption: Female CEO Appointments and Competitive Attacks

STR
Katherine Maich (Texas A&M University); Eric Y. Lee (Texas A&M University)
Disruption following a CEO succession event is expected because human and social capital changes at the most powerful and important position will likely resonate throughout the entire firm. We know less, however, about how disruption to the firm’s culture, following CEO succession, can impact the firm’s competitive outcomes. We investigate this in the context of female CEO appointments, as recent research has shown that compared to their male counterparts, female leaders often establish an organizational culture that fosters more open communication. We theorize that this disruption to the firm’s culture can attract competitive attacks from competitors who seek to capitalize on this period of disruption before the benefits of this cultural change can manifest. We also suggest that competitive attacks are less likely when there are more female employees working at the firm and more female directors serving on its board. We find support for our hypotheses and in supplemental analyses we find that being attacked more mediates the relationship between female CEO appointment and increased R&D expenditure. This is perhaps one reason why female CEOs are associated with short-term performance losses (i.e., being attacked) but long-term gains (i.e., more R&D investment).

How Does Female Leadership Matter? Value Creation Versus Value Capture in Mergers and Acquisitions

STR
Yonghong Jia (Iowa State University); Xinghua Gao (Washington State University); Zixuan Wang (School of Economics and Management, Tongji University)
Whether female leadership enhances shareholder welfare remains a debated question in strategic management. We address this inquiry by examining mergers and acquisitions (M&A), a setting that allows us to observe two fundamental yet distinct outcomes: value creation (total synergy) and value capture (the distribution of surplus). Integrating upper echelons theory with the gender-based information processing perspective, we theorize that the comprehensive search and rigorous scrutiny characteristic of female executives map asymmetrically onto these two dimensions. Using a sample of U.S. acquisitions, we find that while female executives do not differ from their male counterparts in generating total synergies, they are significantly more effective at securing a larger share of the surplus for their shareholders. These findings suggest that female leadership contributes to shareholder welfare specifically through a bargaining advantage that prevents value leakage. We further demonstrate that this advantage is contingent on the information environment: it is amplified when targets exhibit high volatility or asset intangibility but is attenuated by the acquirer’s prior M&A experience. Our study advances research by highlighting how female information processing styles serve as a critical mechanism for protecting and maximizing acquirer wealth.

Gender Balance in Top Management Teams and Firm Resilience

STR
Furio Urso (University of Palermo); Cristina Leone (University of Palermo); Pasquale Massimo Picone (University of Palermo); Maria Francesca Cracolici (University of Palermo)
This study examines how gender balance within top management teams (TMTs) affects firm resilience across different contextual conditions. We argue that the managerial challenges underlying firm resilience vary along two dimensions: stock market phases (bull vs. bear) and the type of uncertainty (systematic vs. unsystematic). We test our hypotheses using panel data from 505 publicly listed European firms and a dynamic model generating counterfactual stock price trajectories from 2016 to 2024. Comparing counterfactual and observed performance allows us to assess firm-specific exposure to market phases. Our findings show that gender balance does not inherently enhance resilience. We contribute to the upper echelons theory by clarifying when and why gender balance in TMTs supports resilience. We also participate in the debate of the contextual nature of resilience.

The CEO Gender Compensation Gap: Do Bargaining Position and Willpower Matter?

STR
Andrew Valentine (UNSW Sydney); Massimiliano Tani; Keiran Sharpe
Existing evidence has shown that female CEOs earn similar or greater total compensation than comparable males. However, gendered differences in the composition of CEO compensation remains relatively unexplained. Our study sheds light on the CEO gender pay gap by analysing 1,174 outsider CEO successions over the past three decades across 18 countries. We integrate the literatures on CEO compensation and gender, wage bargaining and corporate governance, and argue that gendered differences in bargaining position affects negotiating willpower which can explain masked gender differences in CEO pay structure. That is, female CEOs receive a lower proportion of fixed to total compensation than comparable males. We interpret this outcome as the results of gendered risk preferences, which exacerbate the pay gap where there is bargaining over pay. We also find support for the hypothesis that a more balanced gender composition in companies’ boards can help females close the gap in pay structure.
