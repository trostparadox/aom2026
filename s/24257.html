Family Digital Labor Across Contexts: Building Theory from Experiences

CTO
Ariane Ollier-Malaterre (Université du Québec à Montréal (UQAM)); Camille Desjardins (American University of Beirut); Yang Hu (UCL - University of London); Ellen Ernst Kossek (Purdue University); Heejung Chung (King's College London); Ameeta Jaga (University of Cape Town); Dominique Kost (BI Norwegian Business School)
Management research has actively theorized the digitalization of work and organizations but has largely overlooked the digitalization of everyday family life documented by sociology and family studies. Considering that individuals increasingly “do” family online, this manuscript introduces family digital labor as the work performed through digital technologies for family-related communication, coordination and maintenance of socio-emotional relationships, as well as the management of devices for family members and the development of digital literacy and cultural capital in the family system. Drawing on the distinctive method of reflective accounts by a working group of scholars from various disciplines and countries spanning from Africa, the Middle East, Asia, Europe and the U.S., this paper critically engages with lived experiences of family digital labor at the intersection of several social and geopolitical contexts (i.e., social class, income, place, immigration, digital governance, critical events such as wars). In light of these contextualized accounts, we build theory. First, we position family digital labor as a distinctive form of digital labor that is intensely relational and intimate, embedded within inevitable care obligations, entangled with other forms of mental, emotional and physical family labor, and paradoxical as it both empowers and pressurizes individuals. Second, we propose a 2-by-3 matrix of family digital labor, composed of self-directed and family-directed digital labor, each with its own cognitive, affective and behavioral dimensions. Third, we foreground context as a source of family digital labor and individual control over family digital labor, situated at the intersection of digital affordances, individual factors, family dynamics and layers of macro-level context. Last, we derive a research agenda. Overall, this paper significantly advances the theorization of family digital labor and highlights the importance of richly contextualizing lived realities of digitalization across layers of context.
