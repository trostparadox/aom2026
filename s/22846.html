The Role of Covert Competition, Distrust, and Justice in Workplace Knowledge Hiding

OB
Tao Shen (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics)
Extensive research has examined the antecedents of knowledge hiding, identifying various factors that contribute to this behavior. However, the role of covert competition—a subtle and concealed form of workplace rivalry—remains underexplored. Integrating Social Exchange Theory and the literature on workplace distrust and knowledge hiding, this study develops a moderated mediation model to investigate how covert competition fosters knowledge hiding through distrust, while also examining the moderating role of perceived organizational justice. Based on a multi-wave field survey and an experimental study, the findings suggest that covert competition increases distrust, which in turn promotes knowledge hiding behaviors. Moreover, perceived organizational justice weakens both the direct effect of covert competition on distrust and its indirect effect on knowledge hiding. These findings contribute to the literature on workplace competition and knowledge management by uncovering the hidden relational costs of covert competition and highlighting the role of justice perceptions in mitigating its negative impact.

Development of the Inconsistent Fairness Perceptions Scale

OB
Taylor A Carroll (University of Waterloo); Ramona Bobocel (University of Waterloo)
Employees develop overall impressions of their supervisor’s fairness. Importantly, supervisors can be inconsistent in how fairly they treat employees, and thus employees may form impressions of both their supervisor’s overall fairness and the consistency of that fairness. Given that uncertainty is psychologically aversive, perceptions of inconsistent fairness may have consequences for employee well-being that are distinct from the effects of overall fairness impressions. We developed and validated the Inconsistent Fairness Perceptions Scale (IFPS), which measures employees’ perceptions of inconsistent supervisor fairness. Across three studies, the IFPS demonstrated content validity, reliability, convergent and discriminant validity, and incremental validity. Notably, inconsistent fairness perceptions predicted lower trust in supervisors and greater emotional exhaustion, above and beyond overall fairness. These findings suggest that the IFPS should be used alongside overall fairness measures to provide a more complete assessment of supervisor fairness in organizational research.

Supervisor Equity Violations: Identity Motives, Team Trust, and Subordinate Fairness Perceptions

OB
Harry Wang (Siena University)
This study draws on the justice enactment model to examine whether supervisors with identity-maintaining motives adhere to equity norms when allocating rewards and how it affects subordinates' fairness perceptions. Integrating allocation preference theory, I propose that supervisors demonstrating stronger identity-maintaining motives deviate from equity and shift toward equality or need principles. Team trust in the supervisor is hypothesized to strengthen this relationship by granting supervisors greater discretionary latitude. Fairness heuristic theory further suggests that relative equity adherence mediates the relationship between supervisor identity-maintaining motive and subordinates' fairness perceptions. A multi-source field study from information technology organizations provided data for hypothesis testing. The multi-level analysis results supported the hypothesized moderation effect: team trust strengthens the negative relationship between identity-maintaining motive and relative equity adherence. Mediation and moderated mediation hypotheses also received partial support. Theoretical and practical implications for justice enactment and reward allocation are discussed.

Navigating Organizational Death: Stakeholder Experiences During College Closure

OB
Warren Cook (University of Wisconsin-Oshkosh); Haidy H. Brown (Russell Sage College)
Organizational death, the complete and permanent termination of an organization, remains understudied despite its profound consequences for stakeholders. Drawing on Sutton's (1987) process model of organizational death, psychological contract theory, and interactional justice, this exploratory qualitative study examines how faculty and students at a closing college navigate the transition period, how administrative actions facilitate or inhibit their adjustment, and what outcomes emerge from their experiences. Through interviews with 18 participants conducted two-to-five months after the closure announcement, we identify emergent themes organized around stakeholder responses, moderating factors, and interim outcomes. Our findings reveal how closure triggers perceptions of psychological contract violation for both employees (faculty) and non-employee stakeholders (students), and how the manner and timing of administrative communication shapes justice perceptions. We contribute to organizational change literature by extending psychological contract and interactional justice frameworks to organizational closure contexts and by demonstrating that students form psychological contracts with institutions that, when violated, produce responses paralleling those of employees.

Trust By Design: Comparing Uncertainty and Impression Management Approaches to Building Cooperation

OB
Scott E. Allen; Tony Simons (Cornell University); Rene Kizilcec (Cornell University)
Research on trust and cooperation increasingly uses experiments, yet it remains unclear which design logic most effectively produces practical, trust-building strategies. We report mixed-methods, multi-phase, controlled field experiments (285 participants across two universities) comparing two approaches to fostering trust. The first, uncertainty management, clarifies expectations and reduces ambiguity about how to participate meaningfully. The second, impression management, emphasizes leader benevolence or integrity. The setting is an introductory physics pre-assessment—an ungraded diagnostic at the start of term. Because instructors use aggregated results to improve future courses while students receive no immediate benefit, effort on the task serves as a behavioral measure of cooperative, organizational-citizenship effort. Participants who received the uncertainty-management intervention spent about 25– 30% more time per item than controls, whereas impression-management framings produced no reliable gains. The effect did not replicate after instruction, when task expectations were already clear, confirming a planned low-uncertainty boundary condition. Planned mediation tests showed that theory-based mechanisms (benevolence, behavioral integrity, expectancy, instrumentality) did not explain the behavioral effect, suggesting that these constructs were not psychologically active in this context. Instead, interventions derived from a user-centered logic, which surfaced ambiguity reduction directly from participants’ own accounts of cooperation barriers, yielded more accurate behavioral predictions than those derived from a theory-grounded logic. Findings provide causal, field-based evidence that clarifying expectations is a practical and context-dependent lever for cooperation.
