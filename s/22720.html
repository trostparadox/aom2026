Asymmetric Effects of Climate Risk on Corporate Supply Chain Resilience

OSCM
Han WANG (Harbin Institute of Technology); Fusheng Wang (Harbin Institute of Technology); Chang Liu (Harbin Institute of Technology); Zhan Wentao (Harbin Institute of Technology)
This study examines the impact of climate risk on supply chain resilience, focusing on firms' adaptive responses to environmental shocks. Using a panel dataset of Chinese manufacturers (2012–2023), we find that climate risk exposure enhances overall supply chain resilience, with asymmetric effects: it strengthens upstream (supplier-side) resilience but weakens downstream (customer-side) resilience. Mechanism analysis shows that operational efficiency drives this adaptation, while a short-term crowding-out effect limits innovation. The adaptive response is also heterogeneous, proving more pronounced in mature and smaller firms, yet negative for state-owned enterprises. Furthermore, we find that financial constraints act as an amplifier, strengthening this pressure-induced effect. These findings contribute to the supply chain risk literature by revealing the dual and asymmetric impacts of climate risk, offering a network-level perspective on resilience. Our results provide practical insights for logistics and supply chain managers to prioritize upstream resilience and mitigate downstream vulnerabilities in the face of climate-related disruptions.

Social Supply Chain Mindfulness as a Driver of Resilience: A High Reliability Theory Perspective

OSCM
Chi Zhang (KEDGE Business School); Mengyang Wang (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); Anne Ratsimandresy (KEDGE Business School); Marc Ohana (KEDGE Business School)
As social sustainability risks increasingly threaten supply chain continuity, firms face growing pressure to manage social issues while maintaining operational resilience. Drawing on high reliability theory, this study introduces the concept of socially sustainable supply chain mindfulness (social SCMIN) and examines how it enhances supply chain resilience through socially sustainable sourcing (SSS), under varying levels of digital technology advancement (DTA). We test a moderated mediation model using survey data from 297 UK manufacturing firms. The results show that social SCMIN positively influences SSS, which in turn strengthens supply chain resilience. Moreover, digital technology advancement amplifies the positive effect of SSS on resilience. This study advances resilience and mindfulness research by showing that social sustainability is not only a matter of ethics or legitimacy, but a core reliability mechanism: through socially sustainable supply chain mindfulness, firms translate attention to social risks into proactive sourcing practices that strengthen supply chain resilience.

Operational Efficiency Under Disruption: How Firms Manage the Operational Fallout Chain C

OSCM
Xinyue Wang (Manchester University); Tahir Abbas Syed (The University of Manchester); K. Nadia Papamichail
Supply chain crises, driven by geopolitical disruptions, natural disasters, and systemic shocks, pose growing threats to firm operations. While prior research has focused primarily on financial outcomes, this study shifts the lens to operational efficiency, a core but underexamined dimension that reflects a firm’s capacity to transform inputs into outputs during crises. Drawing on information processing theory, we conceptualize Supply chain crises as disruptions that amplify firms’ information-processing demands, thereby impairing operational efficiency when these demands outpace capacity. Using panel data on 89 United States-listed firms experienced a crisis event between 2011 and 2021, and matched controls, we show significant operational efficiency deterioration post-crisis. However, firms with strong data analytics capabilities, sufficients numbers of suppliers, and inventory slack demonstrate operational resilience. In contrast, capacity slack and supplier geographic dispersion offer little protection. This study contributes by extending information processing theory to the context of supply chain crises, identifying operational efficiency as a critical performance dimension, and clarifying the contingent effectiveness of mitigation strategies. For practice, the findings emphasize targeted investments in analytics and strategic buffers over indiscriminate redundancy. This research offers a mechanism-based foundation for rethinking how firms preserve efficiency under disruption.

Addressing the Blind Spot: The Contribution of Network Intermediaries to Supply Network Resilience

OSCM
Matthias Winter (University of Applied Sciences Upper Austria); Robert Wiedmer (Arizona State University); Mikaella Polyviou (Arizona State University)
Global supply networks are increasingly disrupted by natural disasters and man-made events, with network intermediaries, such as maritime ports and ocean carriers, serving as critical yet vulnerable nodes. Despite their importance, ports and ocean carriers, and their role in supply network resilience, remain conspicuously overlooked. Our study investigates ports disrupted by low-probability, high-impact events and how horizontal logistics complexity – proxied by the number and proximity of alternative ports to a disrupted port – and vertical logistics complexity – proxied by the number of ocean carriers calling at a port and their importance for that port – moderate the impact of these disruptive events on port operations. Using a unique dataset of 61 hurricane-induced disruptions at U.S. ports, combined with import and carrier data, we estimate mixed-effects models to test the moderating effect of horizontal and vertical logistics complexity on a disrupted port’s import volume. Our findings, counterintuitively, reveal that having many alternative ports around the disrupted port does not influence the port’s import volume. Also counterintuitively, the geographic proximity of the closest alternative port exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with the disrupted port’s import volume: the volume declines the least at moderate distances (~100 km), and the most at very close or very distant alternatives. Finally, a higher number of ocean carriers calling at a port mitigates disruption impact, while carrier concentration mitigates impact at low and high levels and worsens it at moderate levels. Our findings have important implications for researchers, ports, importers, and policymakers.
