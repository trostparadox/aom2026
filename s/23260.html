Dirty Opposition, Clean Support in Global Corporate Climate Lobbying

ONE
Benjamin Leffel; Thomas Peyton Lyon (University of Michigan)
Anecdotal evidence suggests that corporate lobbying often blocks climate policy in countries around the globe, but the content of such lobbying has remained speculative due to data limitations. We examine the actual corporate lobbying engagements and positions taken by 2,969 firms across 82 countries between 2010 and 2023. We document a “dirty opposition, clean support” pattern by which high-GHG-emitting (dirty) firms are more likely to oppose climate policy and low-emitting (clean) firms are more likely to support climate policy via lobbying, a gulf that is widest across lobbying on carbon taxes. Clean supporters implement more internal decarbonization initiatives, suggesting alignment, on average, of firms’ public and private climate behaviors. We also find that the dirtiest opposers deploy a wider array of lobbying tools, combining direct and indirect lobbying strategies. Opposers tend to be in the mining, oil, aviation and electric utility industries while supporters tend to be in finance, insurance, real estate and electronics. Our findings dispel any notion that business opposition to climate policy is monolithic, providing new and detailed insight into the struggle within the business community to influence climate policy.

Resilience Across Scales in the Face of Climate-Related Risks

ONE
Janis R. Hilaricus (University of the French West Indies)
Over the past two decades, research in management and organization studies has increasingly focused on how firms develop resilience in the face of crises and disruptions. Yet, most of this literature conceptualizes resilience as an organizational property, often examined at a single level of analysis and through discrete events. This article adopts a multi-level and socio-ecological perspective to examine resilience as a co-constructed process between organizations and their environments. Drawing on complexity theory, we argue that organizational resilience is not only shaped by internal adaptive capacities but also by the resilience of the socio-ecological systems in which organizations are embedded. Our empirical study investigates small tourism enterprises located on the eastern coast of Martinique, a region recurrently affected by massive Sargassum seaweed invasions since 2011. Through a series of semi-structured interviews conducted with tourism professionals, local authorities, and community organizations before, during, and after the 2024 Sargassum season, we explore how resilience emerges through dynamic interactions between firms and their socio-ecological environment. Findings reveal that resilience in this context is mutually dependent and cumulative: the adaptive strategies of organizations contribute to, and simultaneously draw from, the broader resilience of the ecosystem and local community. This study advances the literature by extending organizational resilience beyond firm boundaries, illustrating how cross-scale interactions sustain the long-term resilience of both organizations and their territories.

Field Relationality in Climate Change: A Macroscopic Perspective

ONE
Lars Feuerlein (University of Hamburg); Achim Oberg (University of Hamburg); Olaf Kellermeier (University of Hamburg)
Climate change is a defining global challenge of the Anthropocene, requiring coordinated action across social, political, and economic domains. While research has examined climate action within individual organizational fields—such as states, corporations, civil society, and scientific bodies—less is known about how multiple fields interact to enable decarbonization. In this paper, we adopt a macro-perspective to investigate relationality among organizational fields on a global scale. This approach draws on neo-institutional theories of organizational, issue-based, and strategic action fields to conceptualize a cumulative account of field relationality on the topic of climate action. Based on this account, we investigate literature on climate action through a semantic network analysis to map field relationality and relational formats. Our findings reveal a complex global structure of interdependent fields, showing that decarbonization emerges through diverse yet relational social, political, and economic practices. By combining multiple field perspectives, we uncover a broader and more nuanced understanding of field relationality, highlighting how coordination, diffusion, and translation across fields facilitate innovative climate action. This approach provides a novel framework to study relationality among fields in times of global challenges such as climate change.

Prestige as a Diffusion Mechanism: How Business Schools Diffuse Climate Change Knowledge

ONE
Maria Jose (Majo) Murcia (IAE BUSINESS SCHOOL - AUSTRAL UNIVERSITY); Marcelo Felipe Valdospinos (University of Otavalo)
Climate change is a defining grand challenge for contemporary organizations, yet business schools’ capacity to diffuse climate-related knowledge into the management field remains poorly understood. While prestigious schools are often assumed to act as effective diffusion channels, empirical evidence on how institutional characteristics condition this role is scarce. Drawing on Sperber’s epidemiology of representations, this study conceptualizes knowledge diffusion as a selective and uneven process shaped by institutional ecologies of visibility and recognition. Using Web of Science data on climate-related research in Business and Management (2020–2025), we construct an institution–year panel and estimate Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood models with fixed effects. Institutional prestige is operationalized through lagged global rankings, and publication output is attributed under a winner-takes-all recognition regime. Results reveal a sharp threshold effect: membership in the Top100 business schools more than doubles attributed climate-related output, while marginal rank differences within the elite are inconsequential. These findings suggest that prestige structures diffusion through categorical visibility filters rather than continuous amplification, helping to explain persistent gaps between research production and societal impact.

Turning Climate Concern into Climate Courage: Female Parliamentarians Environmental Taxation and SDG

ONE
Asif Saeed (EMLV Business School, France)
Environmental issues, including climate change, biodiversity loss, and air pollution, have received heightened international attention following the adoption of the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which aim to promote global sustainable development. The presence of women in parliament has proven to be highly effective in advancing environmental tax policies that help reduce vulnerability to climate change. Using panel data from G20 and N11 economies between 2002 to 2022, this study provides evidence that women's presence in the parliament is a critical determinant of achieving environmental sustainability. The models’ results show that female parliamentarians with significant representation are more effective, and they reach the ‘Climate Action’ goal by reducing CO2 and GHG emissions, among other environmental targets. Notably, the country's ecological achievements can be intensified by implementing more efficient environmental taxation policies. We mitigate the endogeneity by using Lewbel-IV and GMM estimation. Moreover, this study also highlights the significance of this relationship when the government invests more in renewable energy, emits less CO2, has a significant forest reserve, and offers better employment opportunities.
