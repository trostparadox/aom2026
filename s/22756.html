Gossip or Silence? How Abusive Supervision and Its Differentiation Shape Employees’ Coping Strategie

OB
Wen Zhang (Beijing Normal University); Yuanyuan Wang (Beijing Normal University)
While abusive supervision is known to harm well-being, it remains unclear why similarly abused employees react divergently—some fighting back, others withdrawing. We address this ambiguity by theorizing abusive supervision differentiation (ASD) as a critical boundary condition. Drawing on social identity theory and approach–avoidance frameworks, we articulate a dual-pathway model linking the distribution of abuse to distinct coping orientations. We propose that when abuse is shared (low ASD), the context facilitates negative gossip as a mechanism to validate shared interpretations, which is associated with approach-oriented resistance (supervisor-directed counterproductive work behavior). In contrast, when abuse is targeted (high ASD), the isolating nature of mistreatment may heighten the need for self-protection; here, defensive silence is theorized to serve a protective function, relating to avoidance-based withdrawal. We find robust support for these pathways across three studies (including a field study, a multi-wave longitudinal design, and a multi-level study). By highlighting how differential mistreatment shapes the functional nature of coping—orienting it toward either confrontation or retreat—this research clarifies the relational dynamics of supervision and offers a diagnostic framework for managing toxic leadership.

The Green Shield: How Pro-Environmental Advocacy Protects Employees from Supervisor Ostracism

OB
Gerui Zhao (Tsinghua University); Yan Tang (Beijing Normal University); Ruobing Lu (Beijing Normal University); WeiFeng Wang (Beijing Normal University)
Supervisor ostracism represents a pervasive and detrimental workplace stressor, yet existing research has predominantly focused on reactive coping mechanisms, leaving a critical gap regarding how employees can proactively prevent such mistreatment. To address this problem, this study draws on signaling theory as an overarching framework—integrated with social exchange theory as a downstream mechanism—to propose that employees can actively construct a “moral shield” by engaging in green advocacy, a high-cost, self-transcendent behavior that signals intrinsic moral character. We tested our theoretical model using a multi-method design. Study 1, a scenario-based experiment with 146 supervisors, provided causal evidence that green advocacy leads supervisors to objectively grant interpersonal moral credits, which subsequently reduces their behavioral intentions to ostracize. Study 2, a three-wave time-lagged survey of 434 employees, complemented these findings by confirming that green advocacy is associated with employees' perceived moral credits and reduced perceived ostracism in a field setting. Furthermore, we found that this signaling process is contingent upon the receiver’s interpretation: the protective effect of green advocacy is amplified when Supervisory Support for the Environment (SSE) is high. This research contributes to the literature by identifying a novel, behavior-based signaling strategy for averting social exclusion and validating the dual nature (granted vs. perceived) of moral credits in hierarchical interactions.

Twisted Reality: Unpacking the Effect of Supervisory Gaslighting on Work and Nonwork Engagement

OB
Saba Rahimian (University of North Texas); Hoda Vaziri (University of North Texas)
Supervisory gaslighting, a form of supervisory mistreatment involving manipulation that undermines employees’ perceptions of reality, is increasingly recognized in organizational settings but remains underexplored. Drawing on conservation of resources (COR) theory, we examined how supervisory gaslighting influences employees’ work and nonwork engagement through core self-evaluations (CSE), and whether coworker support and cognitive reappraisal buffer these effects. Using a three-wave survey of full-time employees in the United States (N = 505), we found that supervisory gaslighting was negatively associated with CSE, which in turn predicted higher work and nonwork engagement. CSE mediated the relationship between gaslighting and both work and nonwork engagement. Contrary to expectations, the moderating effects of coworker support and cognitive reappraisal were significant but in the opposite direction: rather than buffering, both intensified the negative impact of supervisory gaslighting on CSE. These findings establish the distinctiveness of gaslighting from related mistreatments, highlight CSE as a key explanatory mechanism, and reveal that commonly assumed resources may sometimes worsen, rather than mitigate, the effects of supervisory mistreatment.

When Unreasonable Tasks Are Unequally Assigned: Relative Deprivation and Coworker-Directed Deviance

OB
Yang Sui (University of Science and Technology Beijing); hongyan chen (University of Science and Technology Beijing); Haozhe Zhang (University of Science and Technology Beijing)
This research draws from relative deprivation and displaced aggression theory and adopt a comparative lens to examine the impact of unreasonable tasks within team. It moves beyond asking whether absolute unreasonable tasks are harmful to explaining how supervisor-assigned unreasonable tasks compared to colleagues shapes an employee's feeling of deprivation, where the resulting damage is directed, and when these effects are weakened. Based on a multi-source, time-lagged field study and a sample of 207 healthcare employees in China, we found being assigned more unreasonable tasks than coworkers fosters feelings of relative deprivation beyond absolute unreasonable tasks assignment, which in turn drives deviant behavior targeted at coworkers, rather than at the organization or supervisors. Furthermore, perceived decision legitimacy and task interdependence functioned as key boundary conditions that attenuate both the effect of comparative unreasonable tasks on relative deprivation, and its indirect effects on coworker-direct deviance via relative deprivation.

When Mentoring Turns Threatening: How Negative Mentoring Experiences Trigger Mentors’ Workplace Envy

OB
Miaomiao Li; Yang Song (Shanghai Customs College)
Although mentoring is often viewed as a positive developmental relationship, mentors may also experience negative interactions. Drawing on social comparison and social cognitive theories, this study examines how negative mentoring experiences affect mentors’ workplace emotions. We argue that negative mentoring experiences—manifested in protégé performance issues, interpersonal difficulties, and destructive relational patterns—heighten mentors’ perceived insecurity toward protégés, which in turn elicits envy toward colleagues. We further propose that general self-efficacy moderates this process by shaping mentors’ threat appraisals and attributional tendencies. Using multi-wave survey data from mentors in a service organization, we find support for a moderated mediation model: perceived insecurity mediates the link between negative mentoring experiences and envy, and general self-efficacy amplifies both the direct and indirect effects. By adopting a mentor-centered perspective, this study highlights the emotional costs of negative mentoring experiences, identifies mentors as a previously overlooked source of workplace envy, and extends social cognitive theory by revealing the contingent role of self-efficacy in relational emotional processes.
