Beyond the Black Box: The Sociotechnical Governance role of DAO in the Charity Sector

TIM
Mamadou Dosso (University of Southampton); PK Senyo (University of Southampton Business School); Ishmael Tingbani (University of Southampton)
Institutional trust theory posits that trust is increasingly placed in abstract organizational systems (e.g., charitable organizations) as such institutions derive legitimacy from legal recognition and facilitate trust between strangers who cannot develop personal relationships. Despite the legitimacy and social acceptance of charities, there has been a significant decline in donor trust due to several cases of fraud and misappropriation. Building on theoretical insights from institutional trust and information systems (IS) research, we explore donors' perception of DAOs-enabled NTFs that help restore trust in the charity sector. We theorized sociotechnical governance as an extension of institutional trust theory, capturing the decentralized governance dynamics of charitable endeavors. We specifically identify four main dimensions: DAO-based value co-creation, innovative collaboration, transparency by design, and trust building, which may help resolve the trust issue between charities and donors in providing humanitarian relief. Our research offers critical theoretical and practical insights into the importance of enhanced traceability, trackability, and collective decision-making in fostering trust in abstract organizational systems, which have significant long-term societal implications.

Trust Without Trustees: Theorizing Digital Trust Under Conditions of Algorithmic Disintermediation

TIM
Maksym Koghut (Manchester Metropolitan University)
Trust theory assumes human discretion remains available when complexity manifests problematically. Contemporary algorithmic systems structurally violate this assumption. We introduce Algorithmic Disintermediation Emergence (ADE) as the boundary condition defining when traditional trust mechanisms become unavailable: architectural disintermediation eliminates human intervention at execution while behavioral emergence produces outcomes not fully determined by design. Under ADE, neither trustee assessment nor delegation to expert intermediaries can reduce complexity. We theorize digital trust as the distinct trust form addressing this condition, operating through three mechanisms: reification, whereby algorithmic systems are cognitively stabilized as quasi-agents toward which trust attitudes can be directed; tolerance capacity assessment, whereby trustors evaluate their own structural ability to absorb adverse emergence; and tolerance architecture, whereby complexity is reduced by expanding tolerable outcomes rather than narrowing expected ones. The framework generates testable propositions, reinterprets empirical puzzles including algorithm aversion and declining trust trajectories, and identifies implications for design and governance.

Trust, Over-Trust and Co-Innovation

TIM
siyu wei (University of Glasgow); Dong-hyu Kim (University of Glasgow); Trevor Buck
Inter-organizational trust is widely viewed as a key enabler of co-innovation, yet prior research also warns of its potential downside, suggesting that excessive trust may also undermine performance. This study examined how trust operated in co-innovation and why its negative effects did not always materialize. Drawing on an 18-month longitudinal qualitative study of seventeen buyer–supplier relationships in Chinese electric vehicle (EV) industry, we analyzed trust dynamics from a supplier perspective across diverse co-innovation contexts. The findings suggest that trust was neither uniformly indispensable nor inherently dangerous. Its impact depended on the nature of the co-innovation task and the degree of interdependence between partners. Trust played a critical enabling role in complex and uncertain co-innovation, while in standardized, routine and supplier-led settings, collaboration often proceeded effectively with limited relational engagement. Although episodes of over-trust occasionally emerged under extreme pressure, their effects were typically temporary and self-correcting. Industry-level regulations, technical validation routines, and market accountability acted as structural constraints that prevented over-trust from stabilizing into persistent performance decline. This study contributed to research on trust and co-innovation by conceptualizing trust as a contextually-bounded governance resource and by refining existing theories of trust development under structural constraints.

When Rivals Become Partners: Influence of Geopolitical Risk on Corporate Coopetition

TIM
Xiaozhou Jing (Renmin University of China); Pengcheng Ma (Renmin University of China); Yan Yan (Renmin University of China)
Prior research on coopetition has largely focused on firms in politically neutral and stable contexts, paying limited attention to how geopolitical risks influence inter-firm coopetition. This study addresses this gap by examining how external geopolitical shocks reshape firms’ coopetitive behaviors. Specifically, we argue that exposure to geopolitical risks increases firms’ incentives to collaborate with competitors. We further examine heterogeneity in these mechanisms, showing that firms with strong external reliance have greater motivation, while firms with high industrial positions possess stronger capacity to form coopetitive ties. Using data from 37 treated firms and 131 matched control firms over 2015-2024, we find empirical supports for our hypotheses.. Our findings contribute to the literature on coopetition and geopolitical risks by demonstrating the critical role of geopolitical risks in shaping coopetition and revealing the mechanisms underlying firms’ heterogeneous strategic responses.
