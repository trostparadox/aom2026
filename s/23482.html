AI–Based Measurement of Innovation: Mapping Expert Insight into Large Language Model Applications

TIM
Robin Nowak (University of Passau); Patrick Figge (University of the Bundeswehr Munich); Carolin Haeussler (University of Passau)
Measuring innovation often relies on context-specific proxies and on expert evaluation. Hence, empirical innovation research is often limited to settings where such data is available. We investigate how large language models (LLMs) can be leveraged to overcome the constraints of manual expert evaluations and assist researchers in measuring innovation. We design an LLM framework that reliably approximates domain experts’ assessment of innovation from unstructured text data. We demonstrate the performance and broad applicability of this framework through two studies in different contexts: (1) the innovativeness of software application updates and (2) the originality of user-generated feedback and improvement ideas in product reviews. We compared the performance (F1-score) and reliability (consistency rate) of our LLM framework against alternative measures used in prior innovation studies, and to state-of-the-art machine learning- and deep learning-based models. The LLM framework achieved higher F1-scores than the other approaches, and its results are highly consistent (i.e., results do not change across runs). This article equips R&D personnel in firms, as well as researchers, reviewers, and editors, with the knowledge and tools to effectively use LLMs for measuring innovation and evaluating the performance of LLM-based innovation measures. In doing so, we discuss, the impact of important design decisions—including model selection, prompt engineering, training data size, training data distribution, and parameter settings—on performance and reliability. Given the challenges inherent in using human expert evaluation and existing text-based measures, our framework has important implications for harnessing LLMs as reliable, increasingly accessible, and broadly applicable research tools for measuring innovation.

Human–AI Collaboration in Science at Scale: A Large-Scale Randomized Field Experiment

TIM
Binglu Wang (Kellogg School of Management, Northwestern University); Weixin Liang (Stanford University); Jiahui Xue (Northwestern University); Yuhui Zhang (Stanford University); Hancheng Cao (Emory University); Dashun Wang (Northwestern University); Yian Yin
Collaboration is the defining mode of modern science, yet its core mechanism—feedback—remains hard to observe, difficult to scale, and unequally distributed. Here we test whether large language models (LLMs) can contribute to this hidden but vital practice. In a large-scale randomized field experiment, we delivered customized LLM-generated feedback for over 34,000 arXiv preprints across 8 STEM domains and 131 geographic regions. Relative to controls, authors who received feedback had a significantly higher likelihood of revising their manuscripts, corresponding to a 12.5% relative increase over the baseline revision rate. These effects nearly doubled among authors based outside English-dominant research regions, consistent with the idea that AI feedback provides the greatest benefit where access to timely critique is otherwise limited. These revisions extended beyond language polishing: treated authors in these settings were significantly more likely to strengthen theoretical soundness and improve novelty. Further heterogeneity analyses reveal a non-linear pattern by intellectual proximity to AI: effects peak at moderate distance, indicating impact is maximized where relevance is high but redundancy is low. Importantly, exposure also increased subsequent adoption of LLM tools in authors’ later papers, suggesting longer-run shifts in scientific practice. Together, these results provide causal evidence that AI can participate in a central collaborative practice of science, expand access to critique for scholars working in feedback-scarce contexts, and accelerate the diffusion of new tools, carrying broad implications for scientists, their collaborators, and the institutions and policymakers that shape the increasingly AI-integrated global research ecosystem.

The Disruptive Power of AI: How AlphaFold Reshapes Scientists Collaboration and Knowledge Production

TIM
Zhixin XU (HKU Business School, University of Hong Kong)
This paper examines how domain-specific artificial intelligence (AI) reshapes collaboration and expertise within scientific organizations. Using the 2021 open-source release of AlphaFold2 in structural biology as a natural experiment, I construct a large-scale author-coauthor panel spanning 2015-2025 to estimate AI’s effects on collaborative behavior. The results show that AlphaFold2 increases the formation of new partnerships by 12-15%, particularly between structural biologists and computational scientists. The analysis further reveals heterogeneous and disproportionate effects across groups and collaboration structures: scientists’ responses to the AI shock vary systematically with their disciplinary background, academic status, and prior collaboration intensity. In contrast to earlier automation tools such as Phaser, AlphaFold2’s generative capability stimulates complementary collaboration rather than task substitution. These cross-disciplinary ties also elevate dry-lab researchers to more prominent authorship roles, redistributing cognitive authority and team hierarchy. Overall, the findings demonstrate that AI’s impact extends beyond productivity gains, reshaping cooperation structures, expertise allocation, and interdisciplinary boundaries in knowledge creation.

Unpacking Technological Indicators for Biodiversity: A Patent-Based Framework

TIM
Fabrizio Tuzi; Viviana D'Angelo (Catholic University of the Sacred Heart); Antonio Zinilli; Andrea Filippetti
This paper introduces a novel methodological framework to measure technological capabilities in biodiversity protection, using USPTO patent data analyzed through machine learning and thematic analysis. The study identifies the most relevant IPC classes and thematic areas, revealing that patent activity in the field of biodiversity is predominantly focused on agricultural biotechnology, with particular emphasis on plant breeding, genetic engineering, and resistance to diseases and stress. By overcoming the limitations of traditional patent classifications, the proposed approach provides more accurate indicators for evaluating the effectiveness of policies and initiatives supporting green technology development. The methodology, validated by experts and replicable in other sectors, offers valuable tools for policy action, while acknowledging certain limitations related to geographic coverage and patent classification.

What Are the Work Implications of AI in Scientific Research? A Scoping Review

TIM
Priscila Ferri (The University of Manchester); Barbara Ribeiro (SKEMA Business School); Philip Shapira (The University of Manchester)
The rapid uptake of artificial intelligence (AI) across the sciences has attracted renewed scholarly and policy interest. Yet, little effort has been made to consolidate key trends related to AI in scientific research, especially the work implications emerging from its adoption and use, including implications for scientific training and skillset, collaboration and workforce. Using a scoping approach guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews, this study explores the current scholarly literature to identify the work implications associated with the use of AI in scientific research. Forty-four publications were identified through searches of the Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus databases, as well as backward and forward tracking. Drawing on a thematic analysis, this review identifies a number of work implications associated with AI in scientific research pertaining to six main work dimensions: Scientific Skills & Training; Scientific Collaboration; Task Content, Structure & Performance; Roles & Role Relationships; and Algorithmic Management of Scientific Work. The analysis demonstrates that, while studies on the implications of AI for scientific work are on the rise, scholarly contributions remain fragmented, conceptual and visionary, paying little attention to AI use in everyday scientific work practices. The paper contributes by providing a thematic map of existing literature, revealing open issues, and outlining a research agenda to address existing research shortcomings.
