When Leaders Admit Errors: How Psychological Safety Explains Followers’ Willingness to Share Errors

OB
Aï Ito (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Naruporn Chaiamarit (XJTLU); Gabriela (Gaby) Cuconato (Indiana University Kokomo)
This study examines how leader error sharing influences follower error sharing through the mediating role of psychological safety at the relational level. Drawing on social learning theory, we propose that when leaders openly acknowledge their own work-related mistakes, particularly those high in severity but low in visibility, it fosters follower perception of psychological safety and, in turn, encourage follower error sharing. To test these assumptions, a vignette-based experimental study was conducted with 175 full-time employees recruited via Prolific Academic. Participants were randomly assigned to a leader error-sharing or control condition. Results supported the proposed model, showing that psychological safety partially mediated the relationship between leader error sharing and follower error sharing. These findings extend prior research by shifting the analysis from the team to the relational level and by demonstrating that professional, rather than personal, disclosures can elicit positive follower responses. The study contributes to the literature on error management, leadership, and organizational learning, and highlights leader error sharing as a practical behavior that promotes a culture of psychological safety and open communication.

Learn Under Technology-Induced Job Insecurity: Dual-Motivation Pathway Moderated by Digital Mindset

OB
Jinghan Liang (Peking University); Chung Hong Wong (Peking University)
Digital technologies, by partially or entirely replacing human work, are rapidly reshaping capability requirements in the contemporary workplace, prompting both organizations and employees to increasingly recognize the importance of continuous learning. However, the accompanying technology-induced job insecurity also undermines employees’ basic psychological needs, creating internal tension about whether to learn or not to learn. Under such circumstances, how to sustain employees’ learning engagement has become a critical managerial concern. Drawing on self-determination theory, the present research adopts an individual-centered perspective to examine how technology-induced job insecurity influences employees’ learning behavior by affecting dual motivation pathways. We conduct two studies (Study 1, three-wave survey, N = 351; Study 2, quasi-field intervention, N = 242) and find that technology-induced job insecurity reduces learning behavior by undermining autonomous learning motivation on the one hand and promoting learning behavior by activating controlled learning motivation on the other hand. Further integrating mindset theory, we find that a digital growth mindset buffers declines in autonomous learning motivation and amplifies increases in controlled learning motivation, thereby fostering a high-autonomous and high-controlled motivational state and sustaining overall employee learning behavior under high levels of technology-induced job insecurity. Taken together, this research reveals the complex motivational mechanisms linking technology-induced job insecurity to learning behavior and extends our understanding of how a digital growth mindset protects employees’ learning behavior from technological disruption.

Psychological Capital and Learning Behaviour: A Meta-Analysis

OB
Mustapha Aliyu (University of South Africa); Betty Portia Maphala (University of South Africa)
Psychological Capital (PsyCap) has been shown internationally to enhance learning-related behaviours; its role in Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) is underexplored and theoretically disintegrated. This study synthesises global empirical evidence to generate a contextualised framework that informs capacity development in South African HEIs. Five databases (PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, and PubMed) were searched systematically with the help of the meta-analysis method to find peer-reviewed empirical studies (2020-2025). Inclusion criteria were operationalised using the PICOS framework. Using Covidence data extraction, 30 effect sizes revealed that PsyCap demonstrated a moderate positive association with workplace learning (r = 0.36), with significant effects for informal learning behaviours (r = 0.35) and academic/learning outcomes (r = 0.39). Moderator analyses showed that PsyCap effects were amplified in global empirical studies, adaptive behavioural, and supportive organisational climates within educational sectors. Thematic analysis acknowledged four recurring mechanisms: (i) PsyCap consolidates reflective learning behaviours, (ii) HERO dimensions moderate persistence during institutional turbulence, (iii) organisational climate moderates the activation of PsyCap, and (iv) adaptive behaviours moderate the transformation of psychological resources into learning participation. In the case of South African HEIs, the results suggest that enhancing PsyCap can be used to combat the elements of chronic change fatigue, resource imbalances, inconsistent developmental trails, and digital transformations. Integrating PsyCap-building micro-interventions and psychologically supportive climates into HRD, academic development, and leadership programmes to enhance staff and student learning cultures. The study makes a significant contribution by offering the first integrated synthesis of PsyCap and workplace learning, advances theory by demonstrating how individual psychological resources interact with contextual affordances to shape learning behaviour and proposes a new conceptual framework to guide PsyCap-informed capacity development in South African HEIs.

Cognitive Pathways from Task Autonomy to Job Performance: Ambidextrous Learning and Cognitive Styles

OB
Soo Bin Park (Seoul National University); Jin Nam Choi (Seoul National University)
This study advances the literature by elucidating the cognitive mechanisms through which job autonomy enhances job performance. Drawing on self-determination theory (SDT), we propose that job autonomy promotes two distinct forms of employee learning (exploitative and exploratory learning), which in turn affect job performance. To further elaborate these cognitive pathways underlying the autonomy–performance relationship, we theorize the moderating roles of task ambiguity and individual cognitive styles (adaptor and innovator), as well as their joint moderating effects. Our analysis draws on a field study (Study 1: 219 leader–member dyads) and an online scenario-based study (Study 2: 400 participants). The results provide empirical support for both the direct and indirect effects of job autonomy on job performance via exploitative and exploratory learning. The results indicate that task ambiguity strengthens the relationship between task autonomy and both forms of learning. Moreover, innovator cognitive style amplifies the positive relationship between exploratory learning and job performance, whereas adaptor cognitive style weakens this relationship. This study contributes to the job design literature by revealing the cognitive underpinnings of the autonomy–performance relationship and by identifying key boundary conditions that shape these effects.
