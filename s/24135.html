Build to Bend, Wired to Withstand: Building Resilience Through Organizational Cultural Paradox

OMT
Tal Katz-Navon (Reichman University); Marianna Delegach (Sapir Academic College)
Organizational resilience refers to an organization’s capacity to respond to and recover from stress and disruption. We argue, however, that organizations encounter qualitatively distinct patterns of adversity, namely chronic and acute, which differ in their controllability, severity, undesirability, and intentionality, and therefore require different value-driven response patterns to become resilient. Drawing on the Competing Values Framework, we propose that adhocracy culture, characterized by innovation and adaptability and frequently identified as conducive to resilience, is particularly effective under conditions of chronic adversity. By contrast, acute crises are more likely to benefit from cultural values that emphasize coordination, control, and clarity. To examine these propositions, this study employs a unique longitudinal, multi-source dataset that integrates Glassdoor employee reviews capturing organizational culture with financial data from 468 Fortune 500 firms during and following the COVID-19 crisis. The findings indicate that adhocracy culture supports resilience during chronic adversity, whereas hierarchy culture plays a central role during acute disruption. Moreover, under acute adversity, adhocracy contributes to resilience only when combined with strong hierarchical elements, suggesting that integrating flexibility and structure enhances organizational adaptive capacity. We further contribute a theoretical explanation of the mechanisms through which paradoxical cultural configurations can be integrated to enable organizational resilience.
