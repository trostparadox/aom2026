Session overview

This session explores how identity, politics, stigma, and social norms shape workplace voice and silence. The papers examine concealable stigma, gendered shareholder evaluations, political incongruence, harassment across cultural similarity, and collectivism in equality-related policy judgments. Together, they show that voice is never simply an individual choice, but is deeply structured by social risk, identity threat, and unequal power relations.

Voice Climate and Concealable Stigma: A Multilevel Examination of Identity Management Behavior

DEI
Mehreen Aslam (Bahauddin Zakariya University); Nosheen Sarwat (Carnegie Mellon University); Javaria Abbas (Bahauddin Zakariya University)
Employees with concealable stigmatized identities (CSIs) experience persistent challenges in navigating workplace inclusion, as their identities expose them to invisible yet consequential forms of inequality. While diversity, equity, and inclusion research has increasingly emphasized hidden forms of disadvantage, limited empirical work has examined how organizational context shapes concealable stigma management and how these strategies, in turn, affect behavioral outcomes in non-Western settings. Drawing on stigma management theory, this study adopts a multilevel perspective to examine how group-level voice climate relates to employees’ anticipated acceptance of stigma and how identity management via signaling is associated with in-role performance. Using a three-wave, multisource, multilevel design, data were collected from 488 employees nested within 94 workgroups in Pakistan. Employee self-reports of signaling were paired with supervisor-rated in-role performance, enabling separation of individual- and group-level variance and reducing concerns about common method bias. Multilevel structural equation modeling in Mplus was used to test the hypothesized relationships. Results indicate that workgroups characterized by stronger voice climates are associated with higher employee expectations that their concealable stigmatized identities will be accepted. In addition, signaling was associated with lower in-role performance, suggesting that stigma-driven self-protective strategies can impose cognitive and emotional demands that undermine task functioning. By foregrounding concealable stigma management as a multilevel process embedded in group context, this study extends DEI scholarship on invisible inequalities and highlights a critical tradeoff between self-protection and performance. Practically, the findings underscore the value of cultivating inclusive voice climates that reduce stigma-related concerns and enable more authentic participation at work.

The Influence of Target—Perpetrator Cultural Similarity on Workplace Outcomes in Gender Harassment

DEI
Marina Astakhova; Timothy James Morgan (The University of Texas at Tyler); Lynn Bowes-Sperry (California State University-East Bay)
Despite extensive research and organizational intervention, gender harassment (GH) remains pervasive in workplaces worldwide. Drawing on Cultural Betrayal Trauma Theory and Intergroup Contact Theory, this research examines how perceived target–perpetrator cultural (dis)similarity shapes the consequences of GH. Across two studies conducted in Nepal and the United States, we test a moderated mediation model linking GH to job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and turnover intentions through distinct identity-based mechanisms. Results show that when harassment originates from culturally similar perpetrators, it undermines targets’ sense of cultural belonging, which in turn explains adverse work outcomes. In contrast, when harassment originates from culturally dissimilar perpetrators, it erodes targets’ attitudes toward cultural diversity, producing similar downstream effects. By demonstrating that GH produces qualitatively distinct forms of identity-based harm depending on relational cultural context, this research advances theory on workplace mistreatment and offers practical insights for managing harassment in culturally diverse organizations.

Skill Submissiveness of Female Director Candidates and Gendered Voting Outcomes by Shareholders

DEI
Ya Jiao (Senshu University); Hitoshi Mitsuhashi (Waseda University)
Previous research consistently shows that women leaders are evaluated less favorably than men because leadership roles are perceived as incongruent with gendered expectations of communality. Research also finds some communication tactics and role positioning strategies to mitigate such incongruity. However, this literature has largely overlooked the role of female top managers’ managerial skills, leading us to examine how audiences evaluate female top managers by focusing on their skill portfolios and skill-based relationships with the CEO. Drawing on role congruity theory, we analyze shareholder voting outcomes for director candidates in Japanese TOPIX 100, leveraging newly disclosed skill matrix data. Contrary to conventional predictions, we find that female director candidates receive higher approval rates than male candidates, indicating a shift in the evaluative baseline for female leadership. We also find that female top managers receive lower approval rates when exhibiting extremely high skill overlap with the CEO. We propose this pattern as over-congruity, a condition in which excessive skill-based submissiveness creates a perceived misfit with situational demands for autonomy and judgment. These findings extend role congruity theory by highlighting the limits of excessive communal signaling and revealing how changing gender norms coexist with heightened expectations for balanced agency.

When Beliefs Divide The Ladder: Political Incongruence, Follower Resistance, And Humble Leadership

DEI
Daniel Aldave (Auburn University)
This paper delves into the repercussions of incongruent political identities between leaders and followers, specifically examining their effects on follower identity threat and the subsequent emergence of follower dysfunctional resistance. In this study, I make the case that disparities in political ideologies within the workplace may strain the rapport between leaders and their followers, potentially triggering adverse reactions from the latter. Grounded in social identity theory, this study posits that followers grapple with identity threats when their leaders exhibit conflicting political identities, thus hindering their capacity to enact or value their own. I argue that this sense of identity threat gives rise to dysfunctional resistant behaviors among followers, manifesting as deliberate covert disruptions to the organizational workflow. Additionally, this research explores the moderating role played by leader-expressed humility. I anticipate leaders who exhibit humility to mitigate the impact of political identity incongruence on identity threat and its downstream effects on follower dysfunctional resistance. By expanding the research on political ideology, identity threats, and leadership dynamics, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of how political differences influence subordinate social interactions.

Misperceiving Equality Unequally: The Role of Collectivism in Equality-Enhancing Policy Perception

DEI
Sylvia Chin (UC Berkeley, Haas School of Business); Drew Jacoby-Senghor
People and countries vary in how much they welcome policies that promote equality between advantaged and disadvantaged social groups. Past research finds that advantaged group members are often held back from endorsing such policies because they perceive even win-win equality-enhancing policies as being harmful to themselves and the ingroup. Our project identifies collectivism as a cultural variable that may alleviate this misperception and improve attitudes towards equality-enhancing policy. Across 1 archival study and 3 pre-registered studies, we find that men’s collectivism predicts more accurate and less zero-sum perception of equality-enhancing policies. Collectivistic individuals report a stronger desire to achieve equality, which seems to buffer against perceptions of equality-enhancing policies as harmful.
