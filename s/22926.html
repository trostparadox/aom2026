Aligning the Unlike: Ownership Configurations and Firm Financial Performance

OMT
Patricia Gabaldon (IE University); Natalia Strelkova; Yuliya Ponomareva (Autonomous University of Barcelona); Sven-olof Collin
As ownership structure in listed corporations is becoming increasingly complex, there is a growing need to theorize how multiple owners jointly influence organizational outcomes. Drawing on behavioral theory and property rights approach, this study examines how five distinct types of owners, families, individuals, corporations, domestic and foreign institutional investors, pursuing two different ownership strategies (firm-oriented vs. financially-oriented) and embedded in distinct governance contexts (power asymmetry vs. power balance) are linked to firm financial performance. Applying configurational analysis on data from 245 firms listed on Stockholm Stock Exchange between 2010 and 2012, we identify several equifinal combinations of ownership types and governance contexts associated with high and low firm financial performance. Our findings reveal that high financial performance is associated with combinations of owners pursuing firm–oriented strategy (families, individuals, and corporations) and those pursuing financial strategy (institutional investors), but only when a coordination mechanism is present. In contrast, configurations with either a disengaged majority owner (foreign institutional investor), two highly similar owners (families and individual) or highly fragmented ownership with misaligned governance context are linked to low financial performance. This study contributes to the ownership and governance literature by offering a configurational perspective on how diverse owners and governance mechanisms interact to shape firm-level outcomes.

Stakeholder Enfranchisement Without Hold-Up:The Challenge of Governing Non-Contributing Stakeholders

OMT
William S Schulze (The University of Utah); Tianqi Yang (The University of Manchester)
Recent stakeholder theory focuses on actors whose relationship specific investments enhance firm productivity and innovation. Yet many stakeholders who make no such investments risk harm from the firm’s activities. Although these stakeholders have little incentive to cooperate, they often control resources that firms need, such as goodwill, land, and statutory permits. How should these stakeholders be enfranchised? We draw from property rights economics and explore this question using evidence from permitting regimes—domains where firms’ activities create broad societal benefits while exposing some to public and private bads. We conceptualize stakeholder enfranchisement as the allocation of control and claim rights among stakeholders in ways that enable exchange under two fairness imperatives: (1) prevent stakeholders from holding up projects that advance social welfare, and (2) ensure that firms internalize the negative externalities they create. Our framework explains how fair allocations of these rights vary with the potential for goal alignment between firms and stakeholders and the clarity of stakeholders’ property rights. By theorizing fairness-based enfranchisement as a property-rights design problem, as opposed to a moral imperative, we extend theory from a focus on who should be enfranchised to consideration of how the allocation of those rights shapes firm and stakeholder conduct.

CEO Pay, Blockholder Earnings and Firm Acquisitions: Distributive Justice Approach

OMT
Jeongil Seo (Sogang University); Gyeonghwan Lee (Dong-A University); Junghoon Kim (Sogang University); Bongsoon Cho (Sogang University)
In this study, we examine whether and how CEO relative pay to blockholder earnings affects CEO pay justice perception and subsequent CEO behaviors. Drawing on distributive justice theory and resource-based view, we propose that blockholders can be important comparison others for CEOs in evaluating their pay justice. Specifically, we argue that when CEO pay is relatively low compared to blockholder earnings, the CEO will perceive distributive injustice in his/her pay and engage in acquisitions to restore pay justice. Using a longitudinal sample of S&P 1500 firms for the years 2012 to 2019, we found that CEO underpayment relative to blockholder earnings is positively associated with acquisition activities and this association is stronger when the underpaid CEO is powerful.

Churn in the Corner Office: Evidence from India

OMT
Suyash Garg
Prior research in developed institutional contexts has predominantly examined board dynamics using directors’ demographic identities. We know little about board ideological dynamics, especially how ideological tension may represent a tussle between change and continuity during institutional transitions. By developing an institution-based faultline perspective, we study how board-level divergence between achieved and ascribed statuses of directors on Indian firms’ boards may split them into subgroups exhibiting opposing ideological pushes. This precipitates conflicts, behavioral disintegration, constrains CEOs’ functioning, and may even lead to their departure. Our analyses on 7,548 firm-year observations between 2010 and 2018 show that board divergence does lead to CEO dismissal and that this effect is subject to both internal (family ownership) and external (environmental complexity) contingencies. Our findings have important implications for corporate governance in emerging economies with social complexities.

Imagined Music As Self-Regulation In Cross-Race Interactions Between Top Managers And Directors

OMT
Michael McDonald (The University of Texas at San Antonio); James D. Westphal (University of Michigan)
This paper develops novel theory regarding the role that music can play in white executives’ efforts to regulate their emotions during interactions with racial minority executives about important career opportunities, available seats on corporate boards. We introduce the concept of “imagined music”, which is the mental replaying of pieces of music, as a means of self-regulation. We focus on imagined music with affiliative properties, which involves white executives mentally replaying musical pieces that are representative of stereotypically Black musical forms (e.g., jazz or hip hop) or that are performed by a mixed-race musical group. We theorize that white executives who can be considered “active music listeners” because they report regularly employing music as a means of self-regulation will often imagine music with affiliative properties during meetings with minority executives regarding corporate board seats. We argue that they will do so because imagining music with affiliative properties helps to mitigate aversive emotions (e.g., anxiety) often experienced in cross-race interactions. We further theorize that imagining music with affiliative properties will have two benefits: 1) a minority interlocuter will be more willing to recommend a white executive for a board seat and 2) minority and white executives will share more information regarding board seat opportunities. We explain that these benefits arise because the mitigation of aversive emotions through imagined music helps the white executive establish rapport with the minority interlocuter. We test our theory with a representative sample of corporate executives using a quasi-experimental approach with random assignment, finding strong empirical support.
