CEO Paranoia, Organizational Ambidexterity, and Executive Mental Health

STR
Daniel Zyung (Not Associated); M. K. Chin (Indiana University Bloomington); Stephen Xu Zhang (Baylor University); Marilyn A. Uy (Nanyang Technological University); Song Lin (Central University of Finance and Economics)
Executives construct “construed realities,” but research has rarely examined when those realities are systematically misaligned with objective conditions. Integrating psychiatric research with upper echelons theory, we model CEO paranoia as a threat-skewed information-processing tendency—vigilance, rumination, distrust, and hostile attributions—that can distort how CEOs read their environment. We argue that paranoid CEOs reallocate attention and tighten control in ways that reduce organizational ambidexterity and harm top executives’ mental health. We further propose that under high environmental hostility, paranoia may better match real threats and be less harmful (or even beneficial), whereas in benign environments it is more damaging. A three-wave survey of CEOs and their top executives provides overall support. Our findings extend strategic leadership research from differences in interpretation to the calibration of executive perceptions and identify executive cognition as a micro-level antecedent of ambidexterity and executive well-being.

It's All about... Us? CEO Narcissism and Business Ecosystems

STR
Anastasiya Shylina (University of Georgia); Moritz Maier (University of Passau); Andreas Sebastian Konig (University of Passau); Don Lange (Arizona State University)
We theorize how CEO narcissism shapes a defining, meta-organizational phenomenon of the digital economy: business ecosystems. The fulcrum of our model is the novel concept of ecosystem oligarchization— the ecosystem’s inherent tendency to be controlled by an elite stratum of privileged affiliates, with the ecosystem’s orchestrator acting as the “patron”. We propose that oligarchization is greater the more narcissistic the orchestrating firm’s CEO because the orchestrating CEO can obtain grandiose self-enhancement in exchange for oligarchic privilege, and because wealth defense is normal from the perspective of narcissistic dominance and antagonism. Furthermore, we argue that these mechanisms are reinforced as the orchestrating CEO’s narcissism triggers greater narcissistic participation—i.e., highly narcissistic affiliate CEOs and their firms engaging in the business ecosystem. Arguing that ecosystem oligarchization affects ecosystem value creation in a curvilinear fashion, we conclude that the orchestrating CEO’s narcissism can ultimately enhance ecosystem value creation; however, this effect diminishes beyond a certain threshold, when the toxic interorganizational effects of CEO narcissism begin to undermine value creation. Our theorizing blends the psychology of narcissism, the sociology of oligarchy, and business ecosystem research into a “meta-echelons” perspective, conceptualizing how business ecosystems become reflections of the top executives involved.

The Hidden Cost of Optimal Distinctiveness: Strategic Choice and CEOs’ Mental Health

STR
Yoonkyung "Yoon" Jin (Indiana University Bloomington); Steffen Burkert (LMU Munich School of Management); Aino Tenhiälä (IE Business School); M. K. Chin (Indiana University Bloomington); Jukka Pekka Partanen (University of Vaasa)
Firms often benefit from pursuing moderately distinctive strategies that balance conformity with differentiation. Yet, little is known about how pursuing such strategies affects the executives responsible for implementing them. We argue that managing moderate distinctiveness exposes CEOs to heightened psychological strain because it requires sustained reconciliation of competing and interdependent strategic demands. We further argue that this strain depends on the need for organizational coordination, as it makes strategic trade-offs harder to sustain and intensifies the burden placed on CEOs. We test these arguments using a population-level panel of Finnish firms and CEOs from 2009 to 2020, linking registry-based measures of firms’ strategic distinctiveness to administrative health records capturing CEOs’ mental health diagnoses and prescription medication use. Our analyses reveal an inverted U-shaped relationship between strategic distinctiveness and CEO’s mental health problems: CEOs leading moderately distinctive firms face the highest risk of mental health challenges compared to those leading either conforming or highly distinctive firms. This pattern is amplified in competitive industries and among publicly listed firms but attenuated in larger organizations. The findings of this study highlight the human costs of managing strategic balance and advance research on strategic leadership and optimal distinctiveness.

From Words to Syntax: A New Unobtrusive Measurement of CEO Hubris

STR
Yalin Wang; Guilhem Bascle (ESCP Business School)
Traditional measures of CEO hubris suffer from limited construct validity and are often confounded with adjacent traits. To address these challenges, we introduce to the upper echelons literature a novel, linguistic measure of CEO hubris, which uses dependency parsing to capture linguistic patterns derived from earnings conference call transcripts. We measure the CEO hubris of S&P 1500 firms from 2010 to 2019 and conduct validity tests. Our validated, unobtrusive measure offers a strong foundation for future research on CEO hubris.

Paranoid Enough to Reinvent? Influence of CEO Paranoia on a Firm’s Relative Exploration Orientation

STR
Tobias Heinrich (RWTH Aachen University); Carolin Krieweth (RWTH Aachen University); Malte Brettel (RWTH Aachen University)
Firms differ systematically in how strongly they prioritize exploration over exploitation, yet we know little about how CEOs, as firms’ most important decision makers, influence this trade-off. We address this gap by examining CEO paranoia, a prevalent but understudied personality trait characterized by heightened vigilance and threat sensitivity, as a determinant of firms’ relative exploration orientation (REO). Drawing on Upper Echelons and Trait Activation Theory, we argue that paranoia can have competing effects: concern about disruption may motivate exploration, whereas hypervigilance and control orientation may constrain experimentation. We further propose that these effects depend on contextual cues, specifically market turbulence and unabsorbed slack. We test our predictions using a longitudinal sample of 204 S&P 500 firms with patenting activity from 2007 to 2020. Results show that CEO paranoia positively affects REO, with the effect strengthened under high market turbulence and low slack, reversed under low turbulence, and dampened under high slack. Our findings contribute to innovation and strategic leadership research by showing how CEO paranoia shapes REO. We demonstrate how its strategic expression is activated by paranoia-relevant cues, revealing conditions under which paranoia benefits incumbents and echoes its recognition in managerial practice as vital for survival.
