Shifting Commitments: Technology-Enabled Entry and Supplier Engagement Models

TIM
Ankur Chavda (HEC Paris)
Research on technology-led industry change has largely focused on its effects on firms’ resources. Less attention has been paid into how technological change reshapes the way in which industry participants engage to create and capture value. We examine how the entry of a firm with access to a new technology can alter the supplier engagement models that incumbents utilize in new product development projects. Incumbents and most entrants typically favor milestone-based engagement that enables early termination and shifts risk to suppliers. This contrasts with commitment, where suppliers don’t bear such risks. However, when an entrant’s new technology makes commitment more effective, the entrant may alternatively offer suppliers commitment. Incumbents may be compelled to follow and selectively offer commitment despite its disadvantages relative to milestone-based development. We argue that such shifts are most pronounced for suppliers with strong track records, prior relationships with incumbents, and for more formulaic projects. Using data on television programing around Netflix’s entry, we find that incumbent networks increased their use of commitment relative to milestone engagement in these cases. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism through which technological change impacts incumbent firms, even when firms’ resources remain unaffected.

Asymmetric Structural Folds in Supply Chain Networks and Disruptive Technologies

TIM
Yuan Zhou; Weijia Liu (Tsinghua University); Fang Dong (Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications)
Driven by global specialization, supply chain networks are critical for accessing the heterogeneous knowledge required for Disruptive Technologies. Yet, maintaining deep embeddedness across multiple knowledge communities—the “multiple insider” ideal—is rarely feasible, as it imposes substantial cognitive and coordination burdens. Building on structural fold theory, we introduce asymmetric structural folds, a network configuration in which firms occupy insider positions across multiple communities with uneven depths of embeddedness. Using a dataset of Chinese listed firms (2010–2022) combined with 4.5 million patents, we identify community structures in supply chain networks via the SLPA algorithm and estimate causal effects using a Heckman two-stage model. Our findings show that asymmetric structural folds significantly promote disruptive technologies by broadening firms’ technological search scope and enabling novel knowledge recombination. Moreover, we reveal an asymmetric cognitive mechanism: strong technological proximity to a core community facilitates disruptive outcomes, whereas proximity to peripheral communities does not yield comparable benefits. This study advances network theory by revealing the generative power of asymmetric structural folds and offers practical insights for firms seeking to pursue disruptive technologies within complex supply chain networks.

How Different Digital Bundles Shape GVC Configuration and Eco-Efficiency? Evidence from G7 Manufactu

TIM
Sina Mirzaye Shirkoohi (Laval University); Muhammad Mohiuddin (Laval University)
Purpose: This paper examines how different digital technology bundles shape global value chain (GVC) configuration and eco-efficiency, and why effects are heterogeneous and time-sequenced. Design/methodology/approach: We develop a metabolism–cognition framework that distinguishes organizational (cognition-oriented) digitalization—technologies that improve cross-firm codifiability, verification, and synchronization—from operational (metabolism-oriented) digitalization—technologies that improve within-plant process capability and resource efficiency. We test the framework in a country–sector–year panel of G7 manufacturing from 2000–2022 using high-dimensional fixed effects, distributed-lag specifications, and interaction models with interoperability/standards intensity, electricity carbon intensity, and environmental policy stringency. Findings: Organizational digitalization is associated with higher GVC participation at short horizons, and the association is stronger where interoperability standards are more prevalent. Operational digitalization exhibits limited unconditional association with sectoral CO2 intensity, but becomes emissions-reducing under cleaner electricity and stronger policy incentives, with effects emerging at longer lags. Evidence further suggests complementarities between organizational and operational digitalization for participation and, conditional on cleaner grids, for eco-efficiency. Originality/value: The paper provides a mechanism-based sequencing logic linking differentiated digital bundles to GVC configuration and conditional decarbonization, with implications for allocating digital investment under carbon constraints.

Optimization of Supply Chain in Restaurants Using Artificial Intelligence

TIM
Martin Espitia (Tecnologico de Monterrey)
Purpose: This article presents a case study on the implementation of an artificial intelligence (AI) model to optimize the supply chain and reduce waste in a Colombian restaurant. The focus is on demand prediction and efficient planning of inputs, improving operational sustainability. Design/methodology/approach: The case study utilizes historical sales data and simulated consumption patterns to develop a predictive model based on machine learning. The model was validated through simulations that assessed waste reduction and operational savings. Findings: The model reduced food waste by 25% and operational costs by 15%, highlighting the effectiveness of artificial intelligence in inventory management in the food industry. Originality/value: This study provides a practical and replicable approach that integrates technology and sustainability, addressing a critical problem in the food industry in a Colombian context. Practical implications: The model can be adapted to restaurants with similar characteristics to improve operational efficiency and promote sustainable practices.

When IoT Adoption Produces Partial Outcomes: A Mechanism-Based Study of Food Supply Chains

TIM
Menntullah Abdelnasser Abdou (The German University in Cairo); Yasmine Medhat Elhenawy (The American University in Cairo); Noha Amer; Mona Ali (The German University in Cairo)
Internet of Things (IoT) technologies are widely portrayed as capability-enhancing tools for supply chains, particularly in food systems where perishability and safety intensify coordination demands. Yet in many emerging-economy contexts, IoT adoption yields uneven outcomes that remain poorly understood. This study examines how IoT technologies are enacted in Egyptian food production supply chains. Drawing on a qualitative multiple-case study across agriculture, aquaculture, and poultry, and based on 18 interviews with adopters and technology providers, we analyze how digital systems are embedded in day-to-day operations. Using an inductive Gioia approach combined with a Context–Intervention–Mechanism–Outcome (CIMO) lens, we shift attention from adoption to enactment. The findings show that IoT is primarily used to stabilize operations rather than to reconfigure coordination or build new capabilities, with outcomes shaped by organizational conditions and implementation arrangements. The study offers a mechanism-oriented account of why digitalization often produces partial results in fragile supply chain contexts and highlights the theoretical value of African settings for refining assumptions about digital transformation. Keywords: Internet of Things (IoT); Food supply chains; Digitalization; Mechanism-based analysis; Emerging economies; Organizational fragility
