Leading Domestic Partners out of Common Good Tragedy

ONE
Longwei Tian; Wan Cheng
Pollutant emissions from firms in emerging countries have spilled over borders and caused damage in other countries. Due to their global visibility, multiple national enterprises in emerging economies (EMNEs) are facing mounting pressures from international stakeholders around the world to provide novel solutions to address the environmental grand challenge in these emerging countries. Viewing the environmental challenge due to firms emitting pollutants as a common good tragedy, we propose that EMNEs can employ digital initiatives not only directly curbing its own pollutant emissions to the natural ecosystem, but also indirectly help its domestic partners curb their pollutant emissions. In contrast, EMNEs whose actions damage the environment will serve as negative role models for their domestic partners. Our findings have theoretical implications for the literature on EMNEs’ role in protecting and providing environmental common goods to society.

Building a Better Future Beyond Academia: Aligning Higher Education Institutions with SDGs

ONE
Jeferson Silva Henrique; Sourabh Aggarwal (IAE BUSINESS SCHOOL - AUSTRAL UNIVERSITY)
Global sustainability rankings have become powerful instruments for evaluating and governing organizational performance worldwide. Although often portrayed as neutral measurement devices, these rankings operate as institutionalized evaluative regimes that shape what counts as “world-class” sustainable excellence. This paper asks: How do global sustainability rankings systematically misattribute sustainability performance across heterogeneous institutional contexts? Drawing on institutional theory and a systems perspective, we argue that sustainability rankings embed implicit assumptions about governance capacity, regulatory stability, and data infrastructures that reflect conditions prevalent in a narrow set of high-capacity environments. When these assumptions are universalized, rankings risk conflating organizational performance with contextual endowment. We conceptualize this dynamic as an institutional fallacy—the systematic attribution of sustainability outcomes to organizations while neglecting the institutional infrastructures that enable their realization. We develop a conceptual framework that explicates how evaluative criteria, data regimes, and governance signaling interact to reproduce cross-national asymmetries in sustainability recognition. By theorizing rankings as active components of sustainability governance rather than as passive assessment tools, the paper advances research on performance evaluation, institutional theory, and global sustainability governance, and calls for more context-sensitive evaluative architectures.

Diverging Futures, Shared Frames: How Organizations Widen or Narrow Futures in Collective Future-Ma

ONE
Larissa Marchiori Pacheco (Northeastern University)
This study examines how shared preferred futures influence the unfolding trajectories of organizations engaged in collective future-making. Prior research emphasizes convergence, suggesting that collective imaginaries coordinate action by reducing uncertainty and aligning actors. Yet this perspective underplays how organizations differently engage with shared visions over time—and how such engagement shapes the scope of their imagined futures. Drawing on a longitudinal, configurational analysis of 17 Brazilian energy firms over a decade-long UN-led sustainability initiative, we identify four distinct future pathways—Champion, Autonomous, Rigid, and Bound—and show how they result from differentiated engagements with centripetal and centrifugal motivational forces embedded in master frames. Our findings contribute to research on collective action, temporality, and field-level change by showing that subscribing to a collective preferred future does not uniformly equip firms to navigate uncertainty. Instead, future-making unfolds through differentiated engagements that shape how open—or closed—organizational futures become.
