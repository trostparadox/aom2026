The Algorithmic Leviathan or The Digital Agora?

SIM
Aybike Mergen (Ozyegin University)
The Algorithmic Leviathan or The Digital Agora? Mechanisms Of Enclosure and Pathways to Rights-Based Governance of Digital Commons Platform capitalism mobilizes the language of commons while deepening control over digital infrastructures that mediate work, identity, and participation. In this paper, we theorize how ‘commons’ rhetoric is so often used to legitimate enclosure by distinguishing charity-based from rights-based governance. More specifically, integrating moral economy, commons theory, and corporate responsibility critique, we develop a charity–rights framework that contrasts discretionary benefit distribution with enforceable entitlements to co-govern, share in value, and demand accountability. Conceptually elaborating the framework and illustrating with cases (e.g., Worldcoin, MIDATA cooperative, Indigenous data sovereignty frameworks), we specify mechanisms through which charity logics produce commons-washing: selective transparency, algorithmic opacity, emergency framing, and dependence on proprietary rails. We then adapt and extend Ostrom’s design principles to digital conditions (non-rival data, global scale, infrastructural concentration), by proposing institutional rules for boundary definition, federated and nested governance, independent monitoring, proportional sanctioning, and sustainable financing via taxation of extraction, membership dues, and arm’s-length public investment. We offer diagnostic indicators to distinguish genuine digital commons from commercial ‘commons’ that retain unilateral corporate discretion, and we outline transition pathways such as interoperability mandates, cooperative procurement, portability defaults, and structural antitrust, shifting bargaining power from corporate sovereignty toward democratic, rights-grounded digital governance. Critically, we emphasize how the choice between Algorithmic Leviathan (corporate sovereignty legitimated through charity logic) and Digital Agora (democratic governance grounded in rights) will determine whether digital society realizes democratic potential or consolidates new forms of domination under progressive rhetoric.

Digital Inclusion and Accessibility in AI Companies' ESG Communication Strategies

SIM
Jung Hyun Kim (Business School Lausanne); Hyunjee Hannah Kim (Korea Aerospace University); Sang Joon Kim (Business School Lausanne)
This study examines the ethical responsibility for digital inclusion and accessibility within the ESG disclosure systems of global AI companies. LDA topic modeling and TF-IDF analysis of reports from eight leading companies covering 2019-2024 revealed an overwhelming predominance of environmental (E) topics. The strengthening of accessibility discourse coincided with the timing of global regulations, suggesting coercive isomorphism rather than voluntary internalization. Furthermore, cross-analysis with the World Digital Inclusion Benchmark (WBA) revealed strategic selectivity, a ‘Say-Do Gap,’ and ethical decoupling by business model. This study advocates for the institutionalization of digital human rights through ‘Responsible-by-Design’ and measurable accountability indicators, providing a foundation for practical ESG assessments and industry-level ethical governance.

The Trust–Standardization Loop: A Recursive Paradox of Digital Governance in Data Ecosystems

SIM
Giulia Renzi (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Paula Ungureanu (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Selini Hadjidimitriou (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia)
Data ecosystems promise to unlock innovation by making data more accessible and reusable yet governing them proves deeply challenging. Standardization offers consistency and scalability, while trust enables collaboration under uncertainty. Prior research often treats these mechanisms as complements or substitutes, but in digital ecosystems—where infrastructures are immature, actors heterogeneous, and conditions volatile—their interplay is fragile and unstable. We investigate this puzzle through a qualitative case study of MobiDataLab, a European mobility data ecosystem. Using interviews, documents, and field observations, we analyse how stakeholders sought to replace trust-based collaboration with standards, only to find that each effort reignited the very trust dependencies it aimed to overcome. Governance unfolded through recursive trial-and-error practices of standardization, alignment, and collaboration, mediated by shifting forms of trust—interpersonal, procedural, and system-based—that redistributed but never resolved tensions. Our findings theorize a trust–standardization loop in which attempts at codification paradoxically increase the need for trust, leading to governance stagnation. We contribute to data ecosystem research by showing how governance unfolds through a recursive trust–standardization loop, where trust is continually redistributed under persistent ecosystem conditions, and to paradox theory by conceptualizing digital governance paradoxes and distinguishing recursive from generative forms.
