How Institutional Support Mechanisms Drive Opportunity Engagement in Necessity Entrepreneurship

ENT
Mmekidmfon Umanah (King's College London); Elisa Alt (King's College London); Michael Andreas Etter
Research increasingly recognizes that opportunity engagement can occur within necessity entrepreneurship, yet we still know little about how this occurs. Building on literature on the role of supportive intermediary institutions in shaping entrepreneurial activity, we explore how institutional support enables opportunity engagement under structural constraints. We examine motorcycle riders in Nigeria’s transportation gig economy who receive support from a work integration social enterprise (WISE). Drawing on an inductive qualitative study of 41 informants, we identify four institutional support mechanisms— working tool equipping, venture embedding, platform-enabled consistency, and capability deepening—that enable necessity entrepreneurs to navigate institutional barriers and participate meaningfully in the market. These mechanisms operate together to produce a 4As pathway of opportunity engagement —Activating, Absorbing, Anchoring, and Actualizing—that describes how entrepreneurs move from market entry to incremental livelihood improvements within constraint. Our study extends necessity entrepreneurship research by specifying how institutional support shapes entrepreneurial action in resource-constrained contexts, shifting attention from the existence of opportunity to the processes through which opportunities are engaged. We further contribute an empirically grounded account of opportunity engagement in necessity settings, conceptualizing it as the pursuit of actionable, incremental pathways through which entrepreneurs enhance their livelihoods and exercise greater economic agency within prevailing institutional constraints.

Institutions and Startup Survival: Necessity vs Opportunity

ENT
Tatiana Lopez; Claudia Alvarez (Tec de Monterrey)
Although scholars and policymakers have traditionally privileged opportunity entrepreneurship because of its association with innovation and economic growth, recent evidence suggests that necessity entrepreneurship can take multiple forms and may evolve into viable, enduring ventures. For this reason, our study moves beyond the debate over which type of entrepreneurship should be promoted. We examine how institutions—regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive—shape both opportunity and necessity entrepreneurship, as well as subsequent venture survival. Our findings can inform the design of more targeted policies and support mechanisms for each type of entrepreneurship. Using longitudinal data from the Panel Study of Entrepreneurial Dynamics (PSED II), we estimate a binary logistic model and conduct survival analysis. The results indicate that government support for entrepreneurship (the regulative dimension) does not account for opportunity entrepreneurship, yet it positively affects firm survival. We conclude by discussing the research and policy implications of these findings.

The Impact of Bankruptcy Laws on Entrepreneurial Business Nonregistration: A Cross-Country Analysis

ENT
Qidi Zhang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Zhongfeng Su (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Zhan Wu (The University of Sydney); Shihao Wei (Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics)
How do stringent bankruptcy laws affect entrepreneurial post-founding behavior in terms of business nonregistration? This study addresses this question using data of 7,042 observations across 35 countries. The findings reveal that stringent bankruptcy laws discourage entrepreneurs from formally registering their businesses. Moreover, this impact is stronger for female (versus male) entrepreneurs while weaker for opportunity (versus necessity) entrepreneurs. This study extends beyond prior studies that primarily focus on the impact of bankruptcy laws on entrepreneurial entry. It contributes to drawing a more comprehensive picture of the implications of bankruptcy laws for entrepreneurship, enriches our knowledge of the antecedents of business nonregistration, and adds to the literature on female entrepreneurship and opportunity entrepreneurship. Furthermore, this study contributes to the comparative international entrepreneurship literature by revealing the multilevel drivers of cross-country variations in business nonregistration.

The Necessity Mosaic: A Decision-Structure Reconceptualization of Necessity Entrepreneurship

ENT
Muhammad A. Muhammad (Texas Tech University)
Necessity entrepreneurship research lacks definitional precision, mechanistic explanation, and an account that moves beyond deficit framing. We address these problems by drawing on philosophy of need and risk sensitivity theory. From philosophy, we derive a rigorous definition: necessity is a structural condition characterized by a functioning gap that is involuntary and whose persistence constitutes harm. From risk sensitivity theory, we derive the mechanism: when low-variance alternatives cannot close the functioning gap, threshold-dependent risk sensitivity generates preference for high-variance options, including entrepreneurship. This reconceptualization shifts the foundation from demographics to decision structure, accommodating necessity across domains—financial, capability, moral, social—while maintaining principled boundaries. We develop eight propositions specifying the threshold mechanism, domain generality, satisficing behavior, and boundary conditions. The framework reframes necessity entrepreneurs from deficit cases to rational actors responding to structural constraints, with implications for theory, policy, and practice.

The Survival Ethic as a Foundational Root of Entrepreneurship in Brazil

ENT
Humberto Reis dos Santos-Souza (Federal Institute of Education, Science and Technology of Rio de Janeiro); Marcelo J. P. Paixão (The University of Texas at Austin); Pedro Jaime
This paper challenges the Weberian narrative that associates entrepreneurship with the Protestant Ethic and the rational pursuit of profit. We revisit the history of the field through the lens of the African diaspora in Brazil to argue that, within the context of colonial slavery, economic agency was driven by a Survival Ethic rather than accumulation. However, we emphasize that this is not a mere historiographical or sociological account. Rather, it is a theoretical effort to resignify management studies by repositioning Latin America as a locus of epistemic production. Through a theoretical analysis of historical phenomena, specifically the peasant breach, urban earning slaves, and quilombos, we construct a framework detailing three dimensions of this ethic: tactical self-preservation, communal protection, and political resistance. Distinct from the concept of necessity entrepreneurship born of scarcity, the Survival Ethic reveals that economic activities in contexts of systemic erasure are fundamentally projects aimed at reclaiming humanity. This study contributes to Critical Management Studies and Entrepreneurship by offering a decolonial genealogy that reframes Black entrepreneurial history not as a peripheral failure of capitalism but as a deliberate ancestral strategy of freedom that disrupts the opportunity versus necessity dichotomy and persists in the Global South today.
