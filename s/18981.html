Emotional Labor in the Age of AI: Disclosure Targets and Interdependence at Work

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 8:30AM–9:30AM
Philadelphia Marriott Downtown — Poster Wall 10

People

Authors: Ejun Jang Seoul National University; Da Gyeong Lee Seoul National University; Jin Nam Choi Seoul National University

Session overview

As employees increasingly interact with artificial intelligence (AI) for emotional expression and self-disclosure, understanding how these interactions shape emotional labor at work has become critical. This study examines how disclosing emotions or personal information to AI versus humans differentially influences employees’ emotion regulation strategies in interdependent work contexts. Drawing on emotional labor theory and human-AI interaction research, we distinguish between task interdependence and relational interdependence and investigate how AI emotional disclosure, AI self-disclosure, and human disclosure moderate their effects on surface acting and deep acting. Using a two-wave time-lagged survey of full-time employees who had recently used AI at work (N = 194), we find that relational interdependence is positively associated with deep acting, whereas task interdependence alone does not predict surface acting. Disclosure targets play a critical moderating role. AI emotional disclosure strengthens the relationship between task interdependence and surface acting while weakening the positive association between relational interdependence and deep acting, suggesting that emotionally expressive AI amplifies performative regulation and undermines relational authenticity. In contrast, AI self-disclosure reduces surface acting under high task interdependence, indicating that non-emotional disclosure to AI may function as a psychologically safe outlet. Human disclosure enhances deep acting in relationally interdependent contexts but shows no buffering effect in task-based work. Further analyses reveal that surface acting is positively associated with burnout, whereas deep acting is not. The findings highlight that not all forms of intimacy are equally beneficial, extending emotional labor theory by positioning AI as a novel social actor in workplace emotion regulation.
