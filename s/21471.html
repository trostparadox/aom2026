When Workload Becomes Leverage: Political Skill, Relationships, and Promotability in Healthcare

Authors: Tera Webb University of Alabama at Birmingham; JaKara Brown University of Alabama at Birmingham; Destini Kierra Elston University of Alabama at Birmingham; Anthony C. Hood University of Alabama at Birmingham

Session overview

Healthcare professionals work in chronically high-workload environments, yet workload is often treated as a uniformly detrimental job demand. Grounded in conservation of resources theory, this study conceptualizes workload as a socially embedded resource and distinguishes between imposed workload (externally driven role overload) and volunteered workload (discretionary extra-role effort). The study examines how these two forms of workload relate to job satisfaction, perceived promotability, and turnover intention, and how political skill, supervisor relationship quality, and coworker dynamics shape these relationships. Using a cross-sectional survey of clinical healthcare professionals, imposed workload is measured via the Quantitative Workload Inventory, while volunteered workload draws on organizational citizenship and initiative items. Political skill, leader–member exchange, coworker support and conflict, job satisfaction, perceived promotability, and turnover intention are also assessed with validated scales. Analyses will include descriptive statistics, correlations, and path analysis to test direct, moderating, and mediating effects. It is hypothesized that imposed workload will be negatively related to job satisfaction and promotability, whereas volunteered workload will be positively related to promotability. Political skill, as a key personal resource, is expected to buffer the negative effects of imposed workload and increase the benefits of volunteered workload. Favorable supervisor and coworker relationships provide additional social resources that mitigate strain and enhance gains. Job satisfaction and promotability are expected to mediate relationships between workload conditions and turnover intention. This study contributes to healthcare management research by challenging the assumption that workload is uniformly harmful, highlighting political skill and relationships as boundary conditions, and foregrounding promotability as a critical, underexplored outcome for workforce sustainability and talent development in healthcare settings.
