Women in Management: A Cross-Cultural Comparison of Brazil and Peru

IM
Cody Logan Chullen (East Carolina University); Dennis Barber (East Carolina University); Tope Adeyemi-Bello (East Carolina University); Flavia Cavazotte; Stella Tippin (Spring Arbor University)
This study examines how culture and gender shape attitudes toward women in managerial roles, comparing perceptions across Brazil and Peru. Integrating Social Role Theory and Implicit Leadership Theory, we argue that cross-cultural differences in gender role structures create distinct implicit leadership prototypes, which subsequently influence evaluations of women's leadership legitimacy and capability. In societies with more traditional gender role divisions, leadership prototypes emphasize stereotypically masculine attributes, creating greater perceived incongruity when evaluating women as managers. Conversely, in more egalitarian contexts, flexible leadership prototypes accommodate diverse leader characteristics, reducing gender-based evaluation biases. Survey data were collected from 352 Brazilian participants and 572 Peruvian participants drawn from university populations representing both undergraduate and graduate students with substantial workforce experience. Participants completed the Women as Managers Scale (WAMS), which assesses attitudes toward women in leadership positions. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses established a robust two-factor structure comprising Perceived Leadership Legitimacy and Perceived Leadership Capability of women managers. Measurement invariance testing confirmed equivalence of this structure across both cultural contexts. Results revealed significant main effects for both culture and gender, as well as a meaningful interaction between these factors. Brazilian respondents expressed substantially more favorable attitudes toward women managers than Peruvian respondents, and female respondents evaluated women leaders more positively than male respondents. Critically, the gender gap in perceptions was considerably larger in Peru than in Brazil, with Brazilian males rating women's leadership more favorably than even Peruvian females. These findings underscore that national cultural context exerts a stronger influence on leadership perceptions than gender alone, suggesting that efforts to advance women in management must address deeply embedded cultural role structures and associated cognitive schemas.

An Emergent Model Of Global South Leadership: Evidence of Achievement-Compassion

IM
Nigel Phang (Nanyang Technological University); Kok-Yee Ng (Nanyang Technological University); Thomas Rockstuhl (Nanyang Technological University); Sharon C. Ward (Nanyang Technological University); Soon Ang (Nanyang Technological University)
Cross-cultural leadership research has largely emphasized the East–West divide through cultural value frameworks such as individualism–collectivism. Yet the North–South divide, shaped by economic disparities between developed and developing nations, offers a critical and underexamined perspective. Despite growing interest in emic approaches, leadership in economically constrained Global South contexts—home to over 80% of the world’s population— remains insufficiently theorized. We address this gap through a mixed-method investigation of indigenous leadership behaviors in low-munificence settings. The first study employs scientometric analysis to map the intellectual landscape of leadership and culture research, revealing the dominance of Western theories and emphasizing the need for expanding subjective cultural values to objective values. The second study utilizes qualitative interviews with 74 nonprofit leaders across 26 developing countries to explore leadership in resource-scarce environments, culminating in identifying the paradoxical "achievement-compassion" leadership construct. The third study operationalizes this construct, applying structural equation modeling with data from 140 leaders across 45 countries to examine how achievement-compassion leadership influences perceived effectiveness through the mediating mechanisms of liking and leader insights. The paper concludes with a discussion of these findings' theoretical and practical implications.

Unraveling Internationalization’s Push for Women Leaders in Global South Family Firm

IM
Rui Jiang (The University of Texas at Dallas); Zhiang “John” Lin (The University of Texas at Dallas)
Family firms in the Global South are deeply embedded in patriarchal governance traditions that prioritize lineage continuity and male succession. Yet, as these firms expand globally, they confront international norms that valorize meritocracy and gender inclusivity. Drawing on a competing institutional logics perspective, this study conceptualizes internationalization as an institutional confrontation between family logic and global logic. Using a longitudinal sample of 3,314 family firms originating from China—a distinctive and influential Global South economy—we find that internationalization significantly increases the likelihood of female leadership appointments in family firms. This effect is more pronounced when internationalization targets other Global South countries, where cultural proximity enables a more authentic hybridization of global gender norms. However, this positive relationship is attenuated by concentrated family ownership and dense kinship ties, which reveal how entrenched patriarchal structures mediate external institutional pressures. By reframing internationalization as a catalyst for non-financial governance transformation, this study contributes to the understanding of institutional duality and the diffusion of diversity norms in emerging economies. It highlights how global engagement can both challenge and reinforce traditional power hierarchies.

The Silver Market Goes Global: Population Aging, International Demand Spillovers, and Multinational

IM
Marcelo F. Cano Kollmann (Ohio University)
Population aging is typically conceptualized in international business research as a labor-side constraint associated with workforce decline and skill shortages. This paper advances a complementary demand-side perspective, arguing that population aging fundamentally reshapes international markets, consumer behavior, and multinational enterprise (MNE) strategy. We introduce the concept of international silver markets: transnational demand spaces created by uneven aging trajectories across countries. Drawing on research in international marketing, demography, and international business, we develop a conceptual framework explaining how population aging generates cross-border demand spillovers, alters consumer preferences, and creates new internationalization opportunities for firms. We identify three mechanisms linking aging to international market strategy: demand amplification in aging lead markets, institutional mediation of age-related consumption, and strategic responses by firms in terms of market selection, adaptation, and timing of international expansion. Using illustrative evidence from healthcare, financial services, housing, mobility, and digital consumer services, we show how firms internationalize aging-related innovations across demographically heterogeneous markets. The paper contributes to international marketing and consumer research by repositioning demographic structure as a core driver of international demand formation.
