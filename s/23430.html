Censorship of Public Libraries: Strategic Responses to Organizational Identity Threats

PNP
Deneen Hatmaker (The University of Connecticut); Amy E. Smith (University of Massachusetts Boston); Shahidul Hassan (The Ohio State University)
Public libraries in the United States have long served as community anchors, providing safe and inclusive spaces for community members to obtain information and pursue learning. In recent years, however, rising calls for censorship and book bans have placed public libraries in the midst of a political battle that threatens their core identity—intellectual freedom, equitable access to information, and privacy and confidentiality. This study examines how public libraries respond to these pressures to protect their legitimacy and values that define their organizational identity. While similar dynamics are emerging in other public-sector domains in the United States (higher education), the public library context provides a particularly revealing case due to the intensity and visibility of current censorship pressures and the relative paucity of research on public libraries. We employ an inductive approach and examine the content of 188 newspaper articles published in the United States between 2017 and 2023 to understand the nature and scope of censorship pressures and how public libraries respond to safeguard their identity. We find that censorship efforts—often organized and grounded in ideological and moral frameworks—frequently involve tactics aimed at delegitimizing libraries, restricting patron access to materials, and undermining professional authority. Libraries respond with a range of strategies, including acquiescence, strategic accommodation, and resistance, while attempting to preserve their core identity and foundational values. These findings broadly advance research on how public organizations manage identity threats originating in their external environment.

Organizing Emotions in Humanitarian Nonprofits: An Ethnography of the Turkish Red Crescent

PNP
Ridvan Kocaman (Anadolu University)
Emotions are often treated as fleeting reactions or individual burdens in organizations; yet, this study shows that they operate as patterned, durable orientations that organize humanitarian work. Drawing on a year-long ethnography of the Turkish Red Crescent, the analysis demonstrates how emotions are systematically triggered, ritualized, controlled, and institutionalized in humanitarian settings. Four themes capture these dynamics: crises, campaigns, and media trigger emotions; ceremonies, embodied practices, and narratives sustain emotional rituals; display rules and neutrality norms enforce emotional control; and compassion, hope, and anger function as recurring affective orientations that shape identity, legitimacy, and mobilization. Synthesizing these insights, the study develops a framework of organizing emotions, illustrating how mechanisms of triggering, ritualization, and control generate enduring emotional orientations that underpin key organizational outcomes. Theoretically, the study bridges organizational emotions, institutional theory, and nonprofit management by positioning affect not as ephemeral institutionally patterned forces that guide practices, symbols, and meaning-making across micro, meso, and macro levels. In practice, it underscores that humanitarian organizations must treat emotions as strategic assets—mobilizing them to sustain solidarity and trust while carefully regulating their expression to preserve neutrality. By conceptualizing emotions as organized rather than incidental, the study demonstrates that affect is not a by-product but a foundational dimension of nonprofit survival and legitimacy.

Creativity and Adaptive Capacity the UK Third Sector: Lessons from COVID19

PNP
Jo Crotty (University of Sunderland); Victoria Foster (Edge Hill University); Jacob Obodai (Liverpool John Moores University)
This paper examines how creativity contributes to adaptive capacity within UK third sector organisations (TSOs) during the COVID-19 pandemic. Drawing on qualitative interviews with TSO leaders in Greater Manchester, we analyse how structural, cognitive, relational, and emotional resources were mobilised to generate creative responses to crisis conditions. Our findings show that while creativity was widely deployed, adaptive capacity was strengthened most where emotional and relational resources were effectively leveraged. Organisations that combined innovation with trust-building and stakeholder collaboration were more likely to embed long-term changes. Those lacking these resources focused primarily on survival and struggled to sustain adaptations.

Institutional Work in Crisis. Path Dependence in NGOs’ Reaction to the Ukrainian Refugees

PNP
Maria Aluchna (Warsaw School of Economics); Agata Chutnik (SGH Warsaw School of Economics); Benson Honig (McMaster University); Marta Pachocka
This article explores the role of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) in institutional work during a disruptive event. Specifically, it examines NGOs’ responses to the outbreak of full-scale war in Ukraine in 2022 and the subsequent influx of Ukrainian refugees into Poland. This crisis reshaped local perceptions of forced migration and highlighted the dynamics of polycentric governance and the diffusion of power, enabling the engagement and cooperation of diverse actors. Using a longitudinal qualitative research approach, we apply data triangulation based on: 1) semi-structured, in-depth interviews with representatives of Warsaw-based NGOs supporting Ukrainian migrants, 2) participatory observation conducted by two researchers on-site with NGOs active during the refugee crisis and 3) field materials, including contextual information about the institutional environment. We investigate the agency capacity of NGOs which undertake specific, purposive actions. In this study, we categorize these actions into three theoretical dimensions of institutional work including structural reconfiguring, relational embedding and discursive legitimacy across six dimensions of crisis-induced responses involving cooperation among multiple, interdependent actors. These forms represent a distinct mode of NGO agency aimed at transforming the institutional landscape for refugee support in Poland and illustrate how institutional work unfolds through organizational reactions to exogenous shocks, shaped by pre-crisis histories and embedded in context-specific dependencies. We also examine how NGOs’ path dependency influences their resource base and institutional repertoire, demonstrating their contribution to the emergence of institutional practices distinguishing three orientations: older NGO’s with stable routines and relationships, intermediate entrants formed in response to previous waves of civic mobilization, and de novo newly created NGO’s directly targeting the immigrant community. By analyzing the engagement and partnerships between NGOs and various stakeholders—including public institutions (central and local governments), businesses, local communities, experts, volunteers, beneficiaries, and the general public—we extend the concept of institutional work in addressing complex societal challenges.

Examining the Role of Values, Perceived Knowledge, and Risk Information on Covid19 Policy Support

PNP
Creed Tumlison (California State University-Bakersfield)
There has been much research conducted indicating that cultural values shape policy preferences across a wide range of issues, with policy preferences being greatly influenced by the values held by an individual. Similarly, the knowledge that an individual holds relating to a given policy issue has been found to shape policy preferences, though inconsistently. Utilizing a survey experiment with school employees in Arkansas, this paper examines the roles that cultural value predispositions and perceived knowledge play in shaping in-school Covid mitigation policy preferences under different information conditions. Respondents were randomly assigned to one of four information conditions (no risk information, higher risk information, lower risk information, and both higher and lower risk information) related to the Covid-19 Omicron variant in order to examine how the role of cultural value predispositions in shaping policy preferences is contingent on an individual’s perceived knowledge levels and the content of the information the individual receives. Broadly, results indicate that the relationship between cultural value predispositions and policy preferences are contingent upon the combination of perceived knowledge levels related to Covid-19 that an individual holds and the risk content of the information provided. Implications for both theory and practice are discussed.
