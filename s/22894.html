Shackles in the Name of Love: Understand the Dark Side of Pressure from Parents to Get Married

OB
Kun Xie (Tongji University); Zhu YAO (Hunan University)
Pressure from parents to get married permeates employees’ work and family lives, which may cause significant costs. However, scholarly investigations into this phenomenon remain limited. Drawing on role identity theory, this study examines the mechanisms through which pressure from parents to get married influences employees’ unethical pro-family behaviors (UPFB) and family incivility. To validate research hypotheses, we conducted a multisource (employee and their parents) and three-wave field study. Results demonstrate that pressure from parents to get married decreases employees’ family role identification, thereby reducing UPFB in the work domain while increasing family incivility in the family domain. Furthermore, work-family balanced human resource management practices (WFB-HRM) weaken the facilitating effect of family role identification on UPFB. Moreover, WFB-HRM weakens the inhibitory effect of family role identification on family incivility. Overall, this research elucidates potential psychological and behavioral responses among employees who are under pressure from parents to get married, and discusses its theoretical and practical implications, limitations, and future research directions.

The Effects of Infant Sleep on Parent Sleep, Work Engagement, and Task Performance

OB
Cristiano Guarana (Indiana University Bloomington); Christopher Barnes (University of Washington); Michael Jared Emanuel Nugent (Indiana University); Asiya Bari (University of Washington)
Family to work conflict research has largely emphasized waking parental demands like caregiving, supervision, and household responsibilities, overlooking the role of infant sleep. Drawing from family to work conflict theory and the sleep literature, we argue that infant sleep represents an overnight parental demand with critical implications for parent sleep and work outcomes. We propose and test a multilevel model in which infant sleep duration and quality predict parents’ sleep duration and quality, which in turn influence daily work engagement and task performance. We further examine parental cognitions— specifically, beliefs about responding to infant night wakings—as a boundary condition of these relationships. We conducted a five-week within-person field study of 125 full-time employed parents of infants, combining objective infant sleep data from a baby-monitoring device with repeated daily surveys of parent sleep, work engagement, and task performance. Results showed that infant sleep duration, but not quality, significantly predicted parent sleep and subsequent work outcomes, with indirect effects on work engagement and task performance mediated by parent sleep duration. Moreover, parents who strongly endorsed beliefs about responding to infant wakings experienced amplified effects of infant sleep on their sleep and work outcomes. Implications for theory and practice are then discussed.

Family Embeddedness, Organization Citizenship Behavior, and Burnout: The Moderating Role of Gender

OB
Debjani Ghosh; Jorge A. Gonzalez (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley)
Integrating Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and gender role theory, we developed and tested a novel theoretical model linking Family embeddedness (FE) to employee’s organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) and burnout, including the moderating role of gender on these relationships. With this study, we address the long-neglected role of family influence on organizational outcomes in the context of job embeddedness. We used time-lagged data from 339 employees and their immediate supervisors (n = 111) to test our hypotheses. The results show that FE promotes OCB more strongly for male than for female workers, as well as that FE buffers the resource loss of burnout more strongly for women than for men. Crucially, our findings contribute to scant yet necessary research on the intersection of work and family in the context of job embeddedness. The study was conducted in India, a country that is less gender egalitarian than Western societies.

CEO Private Life Events and the Mixed-Gamble Nature of Stock Option Incentives

OB
Jan Kretschmer (University of Münster); Colin Schulz (University of Münster)
This study draws on the behavioral agency model to examine how stock option–based incentives translate into CEOs’ strategic risk-taking and how this relationship is shaped by CEOs’ private life experiences. Using longitudinal data from U.S. S&P 500 manufacturing firms over a ten-year period, we analyze how internal and external private life events condition CEOs’ responses to current and prospective option wealth. The results indicate that CEO parenthood weakens both the risk-reducing influence of current option wealth and the risk-enhancing influence of prospective option wealth. In contrast, exposure to terrorist attacks strengthens both relationships. These findings advance understanding of option-based incentives as mixed gambles by showing that CEOs’ private life experiences recalibrate their sensitivity to potential losses and gains. By integrating developmental and stress perspectives into the behavioral agency model, this study identifies CEO private life events as an important boundary condition for incentive-driven strategic risk-taking.
