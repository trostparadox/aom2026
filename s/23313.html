Contested Signals: Electoral Uncertainty and the Interpretive Volatility of ESG Engagement

STR
Raymond Adu (University of Huddersfield); Sondos Gamal Abdelgawad (IE Business School); Daniel Gyimah (Ajman University); SAMUEL ADOMAKO; Mujtaba Ahsan (San Diego State University)
Strategic signals are effective only insofar as stakeholders converge on what those signals mean. We argue that political elections disrupt this convergence by heightening uncertainty not only about future policy trajectories but also about how firms’ actions are interpreted. Building on signalling theory, we conceptualise electoral uncertainty as a trigger of interpretive volatility, a condition in which stakeholder consensus over the meaning of responsibility oriented signals fractures amid ideological contestation. We focus on ESG initiatives as a prominent class of such signals and propose that, in contested interpretive environments, firms often temper or retrench ESG engagement not because their underlying commitment diminishes, but because retrenchment can reduce reputational exposure when signals are most likely to be politicised and reinterpreted. Using longitudinal evidence, we show that election periods are associated with declines in ESG performance. This relationship is contingent in that firms with global operations, those that engage in lobbying, and those operating in higher quality institutional environments are more likely to sustain ESG engagement despite electoral uncertainty.

Signaling in Flux: The Non-Linear Impact of CSR Sectoral Breadth Instability on Job Satisfaction

STR
Kaiqi Mao (University of Macau); Lin Yuan (University of Macau); Naveen Kumar Jain (Augustana College)
While existing Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) literature predominantly evaluates static portfolios, this study investigates the temporal dynamics of CSR strategies, specifically focusing on the instability of CSR sectoral breadth. Drawing on signaling theory within a dynamic context, we theorize that the volatility of a firm’s resource allocation across social sectors acts as a critical signal of organizational intent decoded by employees. We propose an inverted U-shaped relationship between the temporal instability of CSR sectoral breadth and employee job satisfaction. Specifically, we argue that low instability signals organizational rigidity and stagnation, while high instability suggests strategic incoherence and opportunism, both the situations would lead to negative employee satisfaction; conversely, moderate instability signals an optimal balance of principled consistency and adaptive resilience. Empirically testing this hypothesis using a unique longitudinal dataset combining firm-level disclosures from the Indian Ministry of Corporate Affairs and employee reviews from AmbitionBox, we find support for this non-linear relationship. This study contributes to strategic management by shifting the analytical lens from static positioning to dynamic trajectories, highlighting how signal consistency over time shapes internal stakeholder perceptions and organizational outcomes.

Acquirer Charitable Donations and Long-Term Acquisition Performance: The Role of Target CEO Turnover

STR
Miaohan Zhang (Hubei University of Economics); Si Shen
Why some acquisitions generate sustained value while others fail remains a central question in strategy research. Prior studies largely focus on financial and structural explanations, overlooking how acquirers’ strategic use of prosocial actions shapes post-acquisition integration and executive retention. Drawing on social identity theory, we argue that acquirers’ charitable donations serve as a strategic signal that reduces identity threats faced by target firm CEOs, thereby lowering the likelihood of voluntary CEO turnover and enhancing long-term acquisition performance. Using a sample of acquisitions conducted by Chinese A-share listed firms from 2007 to 2019, we measure long-term acquisition performance using three-year buy-and-hold abnormal returns (BHAR) and distinguish voluntary from forced CEO turnover based on public announcements and news reports. Employing Heckman two-stage models and propensity score matching, we find that acquirer charitable donations are positively associated with long-term acquisition performance, partially through reduced target CEO voluntary turnover. This mediating effect is stronger when target CEOs are female or hold greater equity ownership. Our findings highlight corporate philanthropy as a strategic instrument for post-acquisition integration and long-term value creation.

When Reputation is at Risk: The Signal Architecture Behind a Credible Commitment

STR
Lorenzo Lesana (IESE Business School); Romain Boulongne
Firms often attempt to mitigate reputational risk by issuing costly signals, yet even such actions may fail to convince stakeholders. We argue that the credibility of a signal sent by a firm facing reputational risk depends on its architecture. We theorize two core dimensions of signal architecture: intensity, which captures the prominence and visibility of the signal, and purity, which reflects its narrowness of scope and interpretive clarity. Using a panel analysis on a matched sample of firms issuing ESG-labeled bonds, we examine how capital allocation decisions shape investors’ evaluations of firms confronting reputational risks. We complement this analysis with an online experiment to identify the mechanisms underlying investors’ evaluations. We find that signals with high intensity and purity lead to positive firm evaluations through three mechanisms: visibility, interpretability, and costliness. Taken together, these dimensions explain the extent to which key stakeholders perceive a firm’s commitment to change as credible. These findings advance research on signaling, reputation, and commitment in strategy research by showing that stakeholders rely on architectural features of signals to assess a firm’s willingness to enact substantive change.

Making Sense of Mixed Signals: CSR Signal Incongruence, Investor Search, and Firm Valuation

STR
Daisy Bei He (Singapore Management University); Ilya Cuypers (Singapore Management University)
Prior research in signaling theory has largely emphasized the negative consequences of signal incongruence, arguing that inconsistencies across signals create information ambiguity and lead to adverse outcomes. We challenge this prevailing view by arguing that it underestimates the active role of audiences as information seekers. Specifically, we propose that ambiguity arising from signal incongruence should not be seen as an end state but as the starting point of an information search process. We propose that at low to moderate levels of incongruence, this search helps audiences resolve ambiguity by seeking and integrating additional information, leading to more favorable firm evaluations. At high levels of incongruence, however, bounded rationality limits audiences’ ability to reconcile conflicting signals, so uncertainty rises and evaluations deteriorate. As a result, we predict that the relationship between signal incongruence and firm valuation follows an inverted U-shape. We further extend our theory by introducing investor sophistication as a key contingency. We test these predictions in the context of corporate social responsibility signals using a panel of U.S. publicly listed firms from 2002 to 2023 and find support for our predictions. We further demonstrate the robustness of our findings using analyst recommendations as alternative signals.
