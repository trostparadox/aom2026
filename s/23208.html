From Patient Innovator to Patient Entrepreneur

ENT
Shibo Zhou (Copenhagen Business School); Jose Mata (Copenhagen Business School); Pedro Oliveira
We study patients and caregivers who developed innovations to address needs arising from their own or relatives’ diseases and examine how they disseminate these innovations. Based on a survey and in-depth interviews, we report two main findings. First, patients are more likely than caregivers to create profit-oriented organizations. Second, motivation matters, whereas process factors show no consistent association with outcomes. The motives that trigger innovation—such as seeking cost-effective alternatives or responding to unavailable solutions—are associated with later self-reported performance (perceived competitiveness, confidence in sales growth, and altruistic fulfillment). By contrast, how innovators proceed—participation in training, collaboration, or seeking professional advice—does not consistently relate to performance. Overall, personal identity and initial motivation appear central to who becomes an entrepreneur and how successful they feel, while execution choices play a smaller role.

It Takes Two to Tango: A Configurational Study on Accelerator – Startup Relationship

ENT
Hoan Van Nguyen (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Max Mulhuijzen (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Wouter Stam (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Since the establishment of Y Combinator – the very first accelerator – in 2005, the number of accelerators have been mushrooming around the globe. Running parallel to their expansion is the growing amount of research regarding the impact of accelerator programs on startup performance. Most studies assume accelerators affect startup performance by providing them with various kinds of resources; yet, their findings have been mixed and inconsistent. We argue that the heterogeneous accelerator effects occur due to the fact that previous studies in their investigation disregard the interaction between accelerator and startup resources, especially how they might complement and/or substitute one another in order to boost startup performance. Based on the framework of resource orchestration, we posit that in accelerator programs, both accelerator and startup resources are deployed to create bundles that help improve startup performance. Using data of accelerators and startups worldwide, we adopt a configurational approach and find several resource bundles that lead to high performance levels of accelerator startups. Our results – whilst reinforcing and extending extant literature on resource orchestration – offer novel insights into the relationship between accelerators and startups, especially in their selection and cooperation processes

Mentors Don’t Pick Winners: Revealed Preferences in Early-Stage Venture Matchmaking

ENT
ANDRES GUERRERO (Universidad de los Andes); LUIS JIMENEZ (Universidad de los Andes)
Entrepreneurial mentorship is widely recognized as a channel through which early-stage founders acquire human and social capital, yet we know little about how mentors select mentees inside structured programs. We study mentor–venture matching in PEMP, a university-based pre-accelerator in Colombia. Using baseline venture surveys (113 ventures) and mentor rankings from a pitch-based speed-dating event in which mentors used a forced-choice scale (1 = first choice, 2 = second choice, 3 = discarded), we estimate ordered response models with mentor-clustered standard errors to identify the attributes that drive mentor choice. Financial performance signals (revenues, investment, employment) do not predict selection. Instead, mentors favor ventures with smaller teams, secondary education as the highest team attainment, and founders who display adaptability and networking capacity. Proxies for structural social capital are not associated with selection. We interpret the pattern as a “developmental potential” logic: mentors prioritize coachability and relational engagement over traction. The paper contributes by opening the black box of matching mechanisms in entrepreneurship support programs and by outlining a feasible design to estimate downstream effects of mentor selection on venture outcomes.

Venture Capitalists‘ Trait Aggressiveness and Venture Performance: Selection and Treatment Effects

ENT
Jonas Bickert (RWTH Aachen University); Sebastian Kruse (RWTH Aachen University)
This paper examines how venture capital (VC) investors’ trait aggressiveness – the disposition to engage in aggressive behavior – shapes investment decision-making and subsequent venture performance. Despite personality being a central factor in decision-making, it has received limited attention in entrepreneurial finance research. Focusing on aggressiveness, a personality dimension particularly relevant for VC investors, we analyze a global sample of investments made between 2000 and 2019, identifying performance effects at both selection and treatment level. Our findings suggest that VC investor aggressiveness predicts venture performance through founder aggressiveness – aggressive investors are more likely to select aggressive founders, while aggressive founders attract more funding but tend to exhibit lower survival rates. However, when both investor and founders display similar levels of aggressiveness, survival and IPO likelihood are substantially increased, suggesting that trait alignment matters for venture success. Our results advance a personality perspective in entrepreneurial finance research by uncovering both mediating and moderating mechanisms of VC investor aggressiveness as a driver of venture performance.

Why Costly Signals Fail: The Limits of Signaling in Early-Stage Selection

ENT
May Huiso (University of Jyväskylä)
As demand for accelerator programs has grown, admission has become increasingly competitive, with acceptance rates in some cases below 1%. Accelerator programs rely on multi-stage selection processes in which early screening serves as a critical gatekeeping stage. Drawing on signaling theory, founders signal opportunity, team, traction, and growth potential through applications, but producing high-cost signals often requires resources many early ventures lack. This creates a paradox: signals treated as credible during screening may reflect resource endowments rather than underlying venture potential. Using 1,324 applications from a Finnish mentorship-oriented accelerator (2020–2022), this study examines how signal cost shapes initial screening decisions across venture stages within the Real–Win–Worth framework. The findings show that signal cost alone does not drive early-stage selection; rather, costly signals matter only when they are easily observable, interpretable, and relevant to execution capacity. By showing that signal credibility depends on evaluative feasibility, this study extends signaling theory to high-volume screening contexts and explains why accelerators reward signals that can be processed quickly and consistently during early selection.
