Beyond Direct Contact: Work Design Strategies to Unlock Relational Value from Indirect Beneficiaries

OB
Laijie Yang (Tsinghua University); Wenchen Lin (Capital University of Economics and Business); Chen Zhang (Tsinghua University)
Relational work design research has established direct contact with beneficiaries as a pathway to relational value for workers, yet it has largely focused on direct beneficiaries (those who personally receive the service) while neglecting indirect beneficiaries (parties who do not receive service directly but entrust direct beneficiaries to workers’ job). Unlike direct beneficiaries, indirect beneficiaries cannot observe work in real-time and often fall outside workers’ established relational repertoires, rendering their investment in work quality a source of suspicion rather than affirmation. Drawing on sociometer theory, we theorize two work design strategies that restore the bases for relational value absent from direct contact with indirect beneficiaries: Evidential grounding, which anchors evaluations in systematically documented evidence, and Interaction buffering, which introduces a protective third party during encounters. Using a three-wave field study of 688 daycare interns, we found that both strategies generated cognitive rewards of relational value (i.e., perceived task significance), yet only interaction buffering generated affective rewards (i.e., pride). Moreover, interaction buffering exhibited significantly stronger potency in generating pride, an effect that further intensified under hostile contact conditions (i.e., higher levels of indirect beneficiary incivility). These cognitive and affective rewards, in turn, predicted job satisfaction and career commitment.

How Can I Proactively Play at Work?: Towards a Processual Model of Playful Work Design from the Self

OB
Fatih Avcilar (University of Bath School of Management); Qiying Du (University of Bath)
Playful work design (PWD), offering a self-initiated angle for play at work, provides a psychological approach to make work tasks more engaging and enjoyable by reframing them without changing their formal structure of work. Although scholars have shown motivational, cognitive and performance benefits of this orientation, current literature on how to engage playful reframing remains fragmented. Playful reframing is an internal, experiential way of making work engaging, existing work design approaches often fall short, and studies can conflate individuals' playful inclinations with actually performing it, making it complex to embrace. Therefore, we propose a processual model that conceptualises PWD as a self-regulatory strategy unfolding in two phases. Integrating the process with job demands–resources perspective, the model conceptually designs the process within a broader context of perceived demands and resources, and portrays cognitive drivers, personal focuses which activate (or deactivate) and sustain playful reframing. This research extends the current literature through introducing goal-perspective explanation to playful proactive reframing, providing a ground for how playful thoughts are realised in playful actions. With an experiential approach to a self-initiated behaviour, we further offer a focus that pays attention to contextual and personal factors for playful engagement beyond individual differences, and finally outline potential questions for future research.

Performance at a Distance: A Meta-Analysis of Remote Work Usage and Intensity

OB
Amelie Marie Fischer (University of Konstanz); Maria Gaudiino (Hanken School of Economics); Florian Kunze (University of Konstanz)
Despite rapid growth in research on remote work, evidence on its performance implications remains mixed. One reason is that different forms of remote work and performance measurement approaches have not been systematically disentangled. Drawing on 89 quantitative studies, this meta-analysis examines how remote work usage and remote work intensity relate to performance, and whether these relationships vary by performance rating source, voluntariness, and temporal context (pre-, during-, and post-COVID-19 pandemic). Results show small but consistent positive associations between remote work usage and remote work intensity and individual performance, primarily for self-rated performance. In contrast, associations based on supervisor-rated and objective performance measures are weak and statistically non-significant. Organizational-level effects are imprecisely estimated and not statistically reliable. Contrary to expectations, voluntariness and COVID phase do not systematically moderate the remote work-performance relationship. Overall, the findings suggest that performance benefits of remote work are modest, level-specific, and measurement-dependent, with implications for work design theory and the evaluation of performance in hybrid work arrangements.

Investigating Paradoxical Effects Between Job Design and Coping Strategies Among European Workers

OB
Giacomo Carli (The Open University); Filippo Ferrarini (University of Modena and Reggio Emilia); Davide Giacomini (University of Brescia)
This paper investigates the paradoxical relationship between job design—specifically job autonomy—and coping strategies among European public sector workers facing emotionally demanding environments. Drawing on an integrated framework of the Conservation of Resource (COR) theory and the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model, we examine how job autonomy can function as a critical job resource, enabling active coping behaviors such as participation in decision making, which may ultimately enhance individual wellbeing. Using data from over 6,000 public servants across Europe, collected through the European Working Condition Telephone Survey 2021, the study provides empirical insights into how emotional exhaustion operates not merely as an antecedent or outcome of coping, but as a contextual factor moderating the activation of job resources. Our findings contribute to the literature by highlighting the underexplored role of job design in shaping coping strategies and offer practical implications for organizational interventions in high-stress public sector settings.

Reduce or Compress? Household Panel Evidence on Working Time Arrangements and Wellbeing

OB
William Joseph Fleming (University of Oxford)
The 4-day work week (4DW) has garnered much attention following recent high-profile trials across several countries. At the same time, the compressed work week (CWW) has made a comeback, enshrined in new law and receiving renewed academic attention. The rise of two comparable working time arrangements (WTA) raises the question: which is best for workers? To compare, this article uses nationally representative panel data from the United Kingdom which observes both WTAs. Fixed-effects panel regression and within-person effects are used to evaluate each WTA on a range of subjective wellbeing outcomes. Results unsurprisingly corroborate that the 4DW is good for employee wellbeing, especially mental health and leisure satisfaction. In contrast, the CWW has no corresponding positive effect, and even negative effects for women on average. However, the harms of the CWW are alleviated among women when it corresponds with schedule control increases to ‘a lot’, indicating the importance of placing WTAs alongside other working conditions. Overall, the gender differences are notable: women get less benefit from a 4DW and suffer the CWW more. One possible explanation for the initial discrepancy is women’s hours of housework significantly increase, whereas for men they do not. Offering more promise for gender equality, men do take up more hours of childcare when moving to both WTAs. This study demonstrates that, theoretically, researchers should not be so ambivalent about the relationship between wellbeing and working time: working less is better. In terms of organisational and public policy, confusing or equivalating the 4DW and CWW is unhelpful for guiding working time interventions for worker wellbeing.
