Fossil Fuel Hegemony Within Universities: The Cycle of Normalising Corporate Influence

CMS
Maria Rogieri Pelissari (The University of Queensland); Csilla Demeter (The University of Queensland); Daniel Nyberg (The University of Queensland)
Corporate influence within civic institutions has become increasingly normalised, even as concerns about its social and political consequences intensify. Drawing on Gramsci’s concept of hegemony, this study examines how corporate influence is experienced, negotiated, and normalised within universities through the engagements of fossil fuel firms with Australian academia. Based on 32 semi-structured interviews and document analysis, we show how influence is enacted through relational and resource allocation mechanisms, that shape agendas and decision-making processes. We also identify four interpretative strategies through which this influence becomes normalised: pragmatism, reframing, avoidance, and denial. From that, we develop a cycle of normalised influence: a self-reinforcing configuration of processes that frames corporate involvement as legitimate and credible. This model shows how civic institutions function as hegemonic apparatuses, reproducing corporate dominance through consent and everyday practices. The study advances critical perspectives on non-market strategies by explaining how corporate influence is exerted within civic contexts. It also extends conceptualisations of hegemony by explaining how influence is consensually normalised within civic spheres. It then highlights how normalised influence undermines society’s capacity to respond to grand challenges, such as the climate crisis.

The Politics of Delay: How Ideology Conceals Climate Change Contradictions

CMS
Csilla Demeter (The University of Queensland); Daniel Nyberg (The University of Queensland); Christopher Wright (The University of Sydney)
Climate change is producing catastrophic consequences that destabilize ecosystems, displace communities, threaten human survival, and deepen inequalities. Despite decades of organizational initiatives and policy interventions, dominant discourses that deflect responsibility, sustain the status quo, and limit transformative action continue to hinder meaningful emission reductions and address environmental justice. This article advances ideology critique in management and organization theory by showing how dominant discourses mobilized to address climate change sustain existing inequalities. We theorize four ideologically formed contradictions: Global North/South, labor/capital, nature/material, and private/public goods, and conceptualize how they are discursively managed through the dominant discourses of Green Economy, Energy Transition, Technological Solutions, and Energy Justice. These discourses work by reframing antagonisms as manageable tensions, deploying temporal and spatial framings that contain opposing demands such as urgency and deferral or expansion and containment. This spatiotemporal displacement explains climate inaction not as governance failure, but as hegemonic accomplishment. In response we suggest counter-hegemonic practices that expose these contradictions and prefigure alternative socio-ecological arrangements, positioning organization theory as a site for critical analysis and political imagination.

Strategic Agnoses and the Epistemic Infrastructure of Fossil Hegemony

CMS
Charles Barthold; Richard Longman (The Open University); Caroline Clarke (The Open University); Matthew Melsa (The Open University)
Despite decades of scientific consensus and growing activist mobilisation, decisive climate action remains elusive. Public awareness of fossil-fuel-driven climate change has increased, yet support for measures that would disrupt fossil-dependent economies remains weak, while far-right leaders openly deny or trivialise the crisis. We argue that this persistent inaction is not only a matter of interests or ideology but is underpinned by an epistemic infrastructure that sustains fossil hegemony. Drawing on ignorance studies, elite theory, and research on organisational violence, we theorise a politics of agnoses: the strategic production and management of ignorance through which elite actors stabilise fossil capitalism. We develop a multidimensional conceptualisation of three interlocking mechanisms of strategic agnoses – silencing, deceiving, and deflecting – specifying their temporal horizons, objectives, organisational practices, and leading elite actors across business, state, and civil society. Silencing deploys symbolic and material violence to exclude disruptive voices; deceiving manipulates public understandings of the fossil-climate nexus; deflecting accepts surface-level consensus on climate science while diverting attention towards market fixes, adaptation, and technosolutionism. We contribute to climate crisis debates in Management and Organisation Studies by foregrounding epistemic struggles as central to the endurance of fossil hegemony, and by showing how authoritarian governance and violence increasingly function as regulatory tools in the management of climate knowledge.

Gaia’s Intrusion in Organizational Studies: Proposing Planetary Sensemaking in Urgent Times

CMS
Vitor Carvalho Gomes (UNIVERSIDADE FEDERAL DE MINAS GERAIS - UFMG); Valderí de Castro Alcântara
This essay proposes an ontological inflection in Organizational Studies grounded in Isabelle Stengers’ formulation of the “intrusion of Gaia” and in the implications of the Anthropocene and the Planetary (Dipesh Chakrabarty) for theories of sensemaking. We argue that classical sensemaking (Karl Weick), even in its later developments (critical sensemaking, ecological sensemaking), remains anchored in a modern metaphysics that separates subject and world and preserves an epistemic anthropocentrism, treating the planet as an interpretable context rather than as a force capable of producing material reorganizations prior to any cognitive elaboration. To confront this limitation, we articulate two strands: (a) the sociotechnical networks approach, which highlights distributed agency among humans, non-humans, and more-than-humans in organized action; and (b) a pragmatic epistemology of alerts, which shifts the analytical focus from interpretation to the trajectories through which material signatures become inescapable and gain public recognition. On this basis, we introduce the concept of planetary sensemaking, defined as the sociotechnical process through which organizational meaning emerges in hybrid networks activated by planetary intrusions and by chains of evidentiary visibility, culminating in material, political, and institutional reconfigurations. We present analytical protocols and methodological implications aimed at tracing signatures, translations, disputes, and infrastructures of resistance as the production of proof. We conclude that rethinking sensemaking in the Anthropocene requires describing with a planet that acts, intrudes, and redistributes vulnerabilities, thereby expanding the explanatory power of the field for urgent times.

Opening the Door to Eco-Anxiety: An Arts-Based Instructional Innovation for Business Schools

CMS
Anaïs Del Bono (HEC Montréal)
Learning about sustainability today inevitably means confronting the emotional toll of the climate crisis — including the rising phenomenon of eco-anxiety, defined as the unease or distress individuals feel in response to environmental disruption and uncertainty. In this article, I present a pedagogical innovation that brings eco-anxiety into the classroom, not as a problem to solve, but as a phenomenon to name, feel, and reflect upon. Through an arts-based approach, students engage with contemporary ecological artworks as a way to connect emotionally and critically with the climate crisis. The activity invites them to move from individual emotional resonance to shared dialogue, and finally to collective reflection on real-world events, such as activist interventions in museums. It offers students with no prior background in either art or sustainable development a way to apprehend the emotional dimensions of sustainability challenges — and to situate them within larger debates on social movements, degrowth, and the role of organizations in shaping the future. While eco-anxiety remains a nascent topic in management education, this contribution argues for its place in sustainability teaching. Arts-based methods can serve as powerful pedagogical tools to surface the emotional complexity of environmental crises and to build bridges between individual experience and critical and systemic thinking. Keywords: arts-based teaching, experiential learning, eco-anxiety, sustainability education, business schools
