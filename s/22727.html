Face-Off: Paradoxes Impacting Facial Recognition in Retail Asset Protection Environments

OSCM
Derek Dubois (University of Rhode Island); Muhammad Hasan Ashraf (California State University-Long Beach); Mehmet G. Yalcin (University of Rhode Island); Doug Hales (University of Rhode Island)
Facial recognition technology (FRT) is being increasingly marketed to retailers as a means to combat theft. However, despite its potential to reduce retail shrinkage, existing research overlooks the contradictions experienced by retailers who must balance improved safety and cost-saving measures with ethical concerns about perpetuating bias. This study examines how paradoxical tensions manifest in decisions to deploy FRT in retail asset protection systems through the lens of the Paradox Theory (PT). Leveraging a systematic literature review, we identify three core categories of tensions: ethical concerns, governance failures, and issues related to the human-technology interface. These categories of paradoxical tensions are tied to societal values and concerns over privacy, stem from regulatory and transparency deficiencies, and are rooted in technical limitations. To navigate these tensions, this study advocates for a targeted FRT deployment strategy that focuses on employee management and training, striking a balance between operational efficiency and ethical safeguards. The findings advance FRT adoption research in retail operations settings by uncovering underexplored organizational and ethical factors, identifying paradoxical tensions that shape deployment decisions, and explaining how retail leaders navigate these tensions more effectively.

Seasonality, Attribution, and Customer Complaints in Airline Operations Across Business Models

OSCM
Songze Wu (Rutgers Business School); Arash Azadegan (Rutgers University-New Brunswick); Mahour Parast (Arizona State University); Xin Ding (Rutgers University)
Why do similar flight delays generate different customer complaints? Customer complaints represent a critical indicator of service quality in airline operations, yet similar operational conditions may often generate different customer responses. Drawing on attribution theory, this study examines how seasonal context and airline business models condition when operational disruptions translate into formal complaints. Using a longitudinal panel of U.S. domestic airlines (2004–2019) and monthly DOT complaint data, we estimate fixed-effects models relating complaint behavior to operational disruptions, seasonal context, and airline strategy. We find systematic heterogeneity in how disruptions affect complaints across seasons and across low-cost and legacy carriers, reflecting differences in service design and recovery capacity. Consistent with attribution-based expectations, we find that complaint sensitivity to disruptions intensifies during Q3 but attenuates in Q4, with these seasonal effects differing systematically across low-cost and legacy carriers. We also find that higher passenger volume increases complaints only for low cost carriers, suggesting that crowding is blamed on airline choices rather than system constraints.These findings integrate attribution theory into service operations research and offer managerial guidance for aligning capacity planning, operational slack, and communication strategies with seasonal demand conditions.

Transparency Paradoxes and Responses in Fashion Supply Chains: A Supplier Perspective

OSCM
Melek Akin Ates (Sabanci University); Davide Luzzini (EADA Business School); Veronica Haydee Villena (Arizona State University)
Supply chain transparency (SCT) is widely promoted as a cornerstone of ethical and sustainable fashion, yet its implications for suppliers remain insufficiently understood. Shifting the lens from brands to suppliers, this study examines how suppliers experience and respond to SCT paradoxes. Drawing on a longitudinal, multiple case study of 14 suppliers across fashion supply chain tiers in Türkiye (2020–2025), we identify four interrelated SCT paradoxes, i.e., learning, belonging, organizing, and performing, that vary in intensity across tiers and accumulate unevenly over time. Overall, first-tier suppliers experience stronger and more cumulative SCT tensions, particularly organizing and performing paradoxes. We further show that suppliers adopt a broad repertoire of responses that evolve as SCT tensions intensify. We identify four active responses, i.e., instrumental acceptance, critical acceptance, temporal separation, and synthesis; and four defensive responses, i.e., masking, low-capability exit, high-capability exit, and strategic reconfiguration. Early responses are dominated by acceptance and temporal separation across tiers, yet with lower-tier suppliers raising more voice. Over time, responses diverge based on tier position, resources, and buyer–supplier power asymmetries. While tier-1 suppliers with high resources engage in synthesis, strategic reconfiguration, or high-capability exit depending on power asymmetry, lower-tier suppliers resort to low-capability exit or high-capability exit depending on resources. Notably, masking emerges as a system-level unintended consequence of SCT. This study extends paradox theory to interorganizational supply chains characterized by unequal power and advances supply chain management research by focusing on the supplier perspective.

The Role of Online Grocery Delivery in Facilitating Healthy Eating in the US

OSCM
Na Hyun Kim (Wharton School); Serguei Netessine (Wharton School); Dmitry Mitrofanov (Boston College)
The US federal government spends over $140 billion annually on food and nutrition assistance programs, yet nutrition–income gaps persist. One popular explanation is limited physical access: low-income ZIP codes host fewer grocery stores and more convenience or dollar stores, whose assortments are narrower and less healthy. Online grocery delivery could bridge that gap and recent work, exploiting the rollout of SNAP Online, has indeed found a significant and material increase in spending on healthy food items. In contrast to the new work, which studies the overall effect of digital access as a whole, we ask a more granular question: What is the marginal impact of adding one digital storefront on the nutritional density of a consumer's grocery purchase? And how does it vary by various subpopulations of interest, such as consumers in low access neighborhoods and characteristics of the entering stores? We base our estimation on transaction-level data from a leading US online grocery delivery platform, which at the time of observation was undergoing a rapid expansion in its digital coverage of stores. We leverage the fact that new storefront activations occurred in an ad-hoc sequence, unanticipated by both consumers and participating stores. As a result, we find that consumers in low income zip codes increase spending on fresh produce by 3.3%.
