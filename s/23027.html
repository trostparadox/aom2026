Managing Vocation and Profession: Navigating HR Practices in Faith-Based Leadership

MSR
Megan Bolin (John Carroll University)
Faith-based organizations operate at the intersection of spiritual mission and organizational management, placing unique demands on their leaders who are simultaneously called to serve and employed to perform. This dual identity creates persistent management challenges as faith-based organizations attempt to implement professional human resource practices without undermining leaders’ sense of vocation, moral authority, and spiritual identity. This conceptual paper examines the tension between vocation and profession in faith-based leadership, focusing on four interrelated challenges: pay equity, performance evaluation of clergy, accountability structures, and the misalignment between spiritual calling and secular HR norms. Drawing on interdisciplinary literature from organizational behavior, human resource management, leadership studies, and management and spirituality scholarship, the paper argues that conventional HR frameworks are often ill-suited for vocation-driven contexts. Practices such as standardized compensation, performance metrics, and formal evaluation—designed to promote fairness, transparency, and accountability—may conflict with theological understandings of calling, service, and self-sacrifice. In response, many faith-based organizations rely on informal or ambiguous management practices that can exacerbate role conflict, inequity, burnout, and organizational strain. To address this gap, the paper proposes a vocation-sensitive management framework that integrates professional accountability with spiritual meaning. This framework emphasizes stewardship-based compensation, relational and narrative approaches to performance evaluation, and contextualized accountability structures that preserve vocational integrity while supporting organizational sustainability. By reframing vocation as an organizational resource rather than a managerial obstacle, this paper contributes to scholarship on values-based organizing and offers insights applicable to faith-based organizations and other mission-driven contexts where meaning and moral purpose shape work.

Mindfulness and Systems Thinking

MSR
Marissa Kimsey (Vienna University of Economics and Business)
Systems thinking is easier to invoke in words than to enact in organizational practice. This paper explores the potential of mindfulness as an epistemic capacity for systems thinking in organizations, drawing on early Buddhist philosophy. I offer a framework that links the practice of mindfulness to the enactment of systems thinking in organizations. Mindfulness can be the basis for experiential attunement to embodied interdependence, impermanence, and decentered self, whereby systems thinking becomes more alive and ethical in organizational life. I suggest not only the individual cultivation of mindfulness but also organizational and institutional conditions for mindfulness to support systems thinking in organizations. My framework rethinks systems thinking and mindfulness with implications for leadership development.

Beyond Individual Coping: How Organizations Institutionalize Spiritual Coping

MSR
Gahana Rao (School of Business Management, Narsee Monjee Institute of Management Studies, Mumbai, Maharashtra); Veena Vohra
Organizations increasingly introduce meditation, yoga, and related spiritual coping practices in response to chronic workplace strain, yet the translation of these initiatives into sustainable routines remains poorly understood. We use a two-study sequential design. Study 1 systematically reviews 50 workplace spiritual coping studies and shows that institutionalization hinges on three recurring requirements: legitimacy under secular norms, adoption through reduced participation friction in everyday work design, and sustainability by managing tensions between meaning-based experience and metric based accountability. Study 2 draws on 12 semi structured interviews with executives, HR and learning leaders, and program sponsors in Indian organizations to develop a temporal process model of initiation, diffusion, and institutionalization. Initiation relies on risk problematization and secular translation. Diffusion occurs via temporal entrainment and structural signaling that align practices with organizational rhythms and authority. Institutionalization is sustained through material anchoring and selective coupling that embeds programs in routines and budgets while protecting core experiences from reductive metrics. The study theorizes spiritual coping as organizational work unfolding over time and extends institutional theory by specifying phase mechanisms and transition conditions through which contested practices become routine.

Faith as a Governing Logic: How Religious Values Shape Ethical Decision Making in Family Firms

MSR
Megan Harper (Florida Atlantic University); Roland E. Kidwell (Florida Atlantic University)
This qualitative, theory-building case study examines how religious values operate as a governing logic that reorients inward determinants of ethical decision making in family firms. Building on institutional logics theory (Thornton et al., 2012) and Hadjielias et al.’s (2025) framework (family influence, family values, family conflict, family legacy), we analyze the Snyder family’s leadership of In-N-Out Burger across multiple generations of dysfunction, including addiction, unplanned succession, and grief. Using archival materials, public interviews, and autobiographical sources, we examine how faith-based commitments were institutionalized in routines, symbols, and leadership practices. We find that religious values reframed (a) concentrated influence as stewardship, (b) operational values as durable moral commitments, (c) conflict as opportunities for forgiveness and repair, and (d) legacy as a spiritual calling, together transforming potential liabilities into mechanisms of ethical resilience. The study contributes to family business ethics and institutional logics by theorizing religion not as a background belief system but as a governing logic, an internalized meaning system that stabilizes ethical interpretation and guides moral action under pressure. We show how moral meaning systems can convert dysfunction into ethical resilience in family firms.

How Does Culture Influence Supply Chain Risk? Evidence from Confucianism in China

MSR
Jinglu Zhao (Renmin University of China)
Culture serves as a key informal institution essential to effective business management. China, with its vast territory and profound cultural heritage, is particularly characterized by Confucianism, which represents the most influential traditional philosophy. Confucianism profoundly shapes individuals' cognitive patterns and value systems, subsequently influencing their decision-making processes and behavioral choices, thereby playing a pivotal role in corporate risk management. Using data from Chinese A-share listed firms from 2007 to 2022, this paper empirically investigates the impact of Confucianism on firm-level supply chain risks. The results show that firms more deeply embedded in Confucian culture exhibit significantly lower supply chain risk, with this effect being more pronounced among non-state-owned enterprises and firms operating in regions with lower levels of marketization. Mechanism analyses demonstrate that under the influence of Confucianism, firms exhibit higher quality internal control, lower "bullwhip effects", and increased supply chain diversification. Through these three channels, the supply chain risks faced by firms are effectively reduced. This study extends the cultural lens to supply chain management, illuminating the substantive role of culture as an informal institution in constraining and guiding corporate behavior while strengthening risk management capabilities.
