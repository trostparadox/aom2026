Minority Report: Examining the Impact of Digital Platform Generativity on Entrepreneurial Equity

CTO
Sumin Song (Concordia University); Emmanuelle Vaast (McGill University)
Digital platforms are widely celebrated for their generativity, yet growing evidence suggests that the same mechanisms enabling innovation may also reproduce inequality among minority entrepreneurs. Drawing on generativity theory, this study examines how demand-side dynamics shape entrepreneurial outcomes by focusing on user feedback and audience discourse. Using data from 5,818 YouTube videos and more than 760,000 comments in the technology content domain, we analyze how evaluative and biased feedback structures discourse and influences engagement. The findings show that positive evaluative feedback promotes discourse stabilization, which constrains subsequent engagement across both visibility-oriented and participatory outcomes. Biased language fragments discourse but exhibits limited direct effects on engagement. These processes are minority contingent. Focusing on gender as a salient dimension of minority status, evaluative feedback translates more strongly into discourse stabilization for women creators, producing larger indirect constraints on engagement despite modest baseline differences. Overall, the study shows how entrepreneurial equity is shaped through audience discourse and algorithmic amplification rather than direct feedback alone.

Towards a Cheap Personalization of Political Messaging with LLMs?

CTO
Philine Widmer; Tiffany Kreutschy (University of Lausanne)
The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has intensified concerns about their use in political microtargeting, where messages are tailored to individuals’ demographic or ideological characteristics. Although prior research shows that LLMs can match human-level persuasion, little is known about when personalization boosts their persuasive power and when it does not. We report results from a preregistered online experiment (N = 5,198) in which participants received a single AI-generated Twitter-like message about either polarized issues (minimum wage, universal healthcare) or a bipartisan issue (veterans’ education funding). Messages varied in the extent of demographic matching (0–6 attributes: gender, age, race, party, ideology, income). We measured attitudinal change and a behavioral outcome: participants’ willingness to exert real effort to donate money for or against the issue. We find that personalization yields gains in attitudinal persuasion when matching on political party and primarily among Democrats. We provide evidence for a potential mechanism: LLM bias. The LLMs used in our study exhibit a Democratic bias, reducing their effectiveness for Republican participants. We do not find any evidence for personalization’s effectiveness on behaviors. Our contribution is threefold: (1) we demonstrate that LLMs can generate personalized political messages that produce measurable persuasive effects; (2) we suggest that the success in personalization depends on how well the LLM “complies” with the requested partisan leaning; and (3) we assess the model’s own explanations for why its messages should persuade specific demographic groups and find that these self-attributions are rather accurate.

Funding Inclusion: Organizational Ideology and the Production of New Knowledge

CTO
Swagatam Sinha (Tilburg University); Rene Bakker (Tilburg University)
Organizational innovation depends not only on the generation of new ideas, but also on how organizations select most promising ideas for subsequent funding and development. ‘Idea selection’ determines how organizational resources are allocated to a subset of promising projects; hence errors at this stage can foreclose promising directions or lock organizations into unproductive paths. Yet, organizations struggle with selecting ideas that align with their current strategy while also maximizing long term organizational performance, because the selection process is influenced by factors external to the organization itself (Pahnke, Katila and Eisenhardt, 2015). In our paper, we address a fundamental question: when external shocks transform strategic imperatives of an organization, how does idea selection shift as a consequence? We build on the literature on institutional logics to provide a theoretically grounded answer to this question. We study texts from 260,000 funded project proposals (abstracts and titles) from the National Institute of Health (NIH) database, which were funded during January 2022 and October 2025. We use a widely reported transition in government strategy introduced by the new US federal administration inaugurated in January 2025, to show how the process of idea selection changed in 2025 compared to the previous years. These findings support our theory linking changes in institutional logics with systematic changes in the idea selection and innovation process. These findings contribute both to the literature on organizational innovation and institutional logics.
