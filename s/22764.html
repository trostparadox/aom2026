Effects of Family-Supportive Supervisor Behavior: The Role of Job Level and Gender

OB
Genping Ye (University of Southampton Business School); Michail Veliziotis (University of Southampton); Guy Vernon (University of Southampton)
Supervisors play a crucial role as gatekeepers of organizational work-family policies. Using four-wave panel data (n = 2,739) and, moreover, fixed-effects modeling to control for the often neglected matter of time-invariant individual heterogeneity (e.g., personality), this study confirms that family-supportive supervisor behavior (FSSB) reduces turnover intention (TI), consistent with Social Exchange theory. The findings also reveal that work-to-family conflict (WtFC) and job burnout (JB) both individually and serially mediate the relationship between FSSB and TI, aligning with the Conservation of Resources theory. Regarding moderation, gender does not generally significantly shape the indirect effect of FSSB on TI via WtFC and JB. However, gender interacts with job level to do so; specifically, managerial roles strengthen the effects of FSSB in alleviating WtFC and JB, and ultimately TI, for women only. Resources-of-higher-status, particularly resource reinforcement rather than substitution, and stress-of-higher-status, are thus influential in shaping how FSSB affects women specifically. This seems likely because women both bear heavier work-family burdens and possess greater capability in leveraging organizational work-family resources. Although FSSB matters for all, it matters much more for women managers than others.

When Microaggressions Backfire: Destructive Voice, Workplace Embitterment, and Inclusive Leadership

OB
Ghulam Murtaza (KEDGE Business School); Rahman Khan (Paris School of Business); Athar Hameed (Westminster International University in Tashkent); Kashif Ullah Khan (Wenzhou-Kean University); Qurat-ul-ain Talpur (ESSCA School of Management)
This research explores the role of microaggressions towards women in promoting destructive voice behavior at work. Building on the social exchange perspective and the norms of reciprocity, we propose that workplace embitterment mediates the relationship between experienced microaggressions and destructive voice behavior among female employees. We also provide a boundary condition, i.e., inclusive leadership, that can buffer the impact of workplace embitterment on destructive voice behavior. Our findings from a vignette study (Study 1) conducted with 146 female employees confirmed that microaggressions towards women lead to destructive voice behavior. Furthermore, a video vignette-based study (Study 2) involving 102 female employees revalidated the role of microaggressions towards women in predicting destructive voice. The findings of the content analysis showed that participants in the high microaggressions towards women condition had 67.2% responses with elements of destructive voice behavior, compared with participants in the low microaggressions towards women condition, who had 8.5% responses with such indications of destructive voice. Additionally, the results of a two-wave time-lagged survey study (Study 3) conducted with 278 female employees across different industries provided empirical support for our hypothesized model. Specifically, microaggressions towards women significantly predicted destructive voice behavior directly as well as through the mediating mechanism of workplace embitterment. Furthermore, inclusive leadership significantly weakened the relationship between workplace embitterment and destructive voice behavior, thus confirming its buffering role in our research model.

Making Sense of Inclusion: A Process Theory of Perceived Workplace Inclusion

OB
Sampath Chandana Patabandige (The University of Waikato); Peter Yih-Tong Sun; Brad Jackson (The University of Waikato)
In this paper, we problematise the concept of perceived workplace inclusion by showing that existing models remain conceptually fragmented and theoretically under-specified. Most inclusion research privileges organisational practices and group-level outcomes, while providing limited theorisation of how individuals actively construct and perceive inclusion in everyday interactions. To address this gap, we propose the Inclusion Sensemaking Framework (ISF), a process-based theorisation underpinned by sensemaking theory. We reconceptualise perceived inclusion as a dynamic, recursive, and co-created phenomenon. This approach explains how inclusion is perceived and why employees arrive at different subjective inclusion states and clarifies the role of individual agency and identity complexity in shaping these inclusion interpretations. We contribute to the both sensemaking and inclusion literature by shifting attention away from content-based, structural approaches towards more processual, psychological and socio-cognitive mechanisms of inclusion. Our framework opens new research avenues for understanding inclusion as an ongoing interpretive accomplishment rather than a stable organizational condition.

Diversity Branding in Job Vacancies and Employee Turnover (WITHDRAWN)

OB
Laura De Boom (University of Antwerp); Kim De Meulenaere (University of Antwerp)
Prior research suggests that organizations’ external diversity branding shape applicants’ expectations and signal how organizations value diversity and inclusion (D&I), yet little is known about its effects after entry. Because such signals inform newcomers’ beliefs about how they will be treated, they become part of their ideological psychological contract, leading newcomers to interpret them as implicit promises about how diversity will be supported after hire. Diversity branding therefore creates newcomer’s expectations of inclusion that may be fulfilled or breached depending on the organization’s inclusive climate. This study examines whether diversity branding relates to early turnover among new hires and how its effects vary with the level of inclusive climate. We argue that diversity branding is associated with higher early turnover among new hires when the inclusive climate is low, but that this relationship reverses when the inclusive climate is high. Through an organizational-level study of 272 Belgian firms, we show that diversity branding has no direct effect on early turnover among new hires. However, its effects are conditional on the organizations’ inclusive climate: diversity branding predicts higher early turnover when inclusive climate is weak, whereas the relationship attenuates and reverses at high levels of inclusive climate. Our findings highlight that diversity branding is not uniformly beneficial. Instead, its consequences hinge on the alignment with the internal reality, revealing potential backfire risks when branding outpaces reality and underscoring the importance of substantiating diversity commitments to support retention.

“What’s in It for Me?”: The Personal Case for Diversity and Broad-Based Support

OB
Kendall Yamamoto (University of Virginia); Insiya Hussain (Not Associated)
Given the divisive nature of race-based workplace diversity initiatives, organizations typically seek broad support from members of both advantaged and disadvantaged racial groups to achieve their diversity goals. However, the two rationales used most often to promote diversity—the business case and the moral case—each have limitations, as they can fail to resonate with either one group or the other. Specifically, the business case can make disadvantaged group employees feel instrumentalized, while the moral case can provoke reactance among advantaged group employees. In this research, through an inductive, qualitative study, we uncover a third rationale for the value of diversity—the personal case—which frames diversity as valuable to individual employees’ personal growth, relationships, and enjoyment at work. Drawing on identity threat and self-expansion theories, we predict that the personal case is uniquely effective, as it lowers status threat among advantaged group members while enhancing belonging among disadvantaged group members. We validate our measure for the personal case for diversity, and find support for our theory in a lab study and an online labor market field experiment. Our work meaningfully extends theory on organizational diversity cases, while providing leaders with a more broadly resonant approach to their diversity-related communication.
