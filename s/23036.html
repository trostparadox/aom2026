Family Owenership, Tax Haven Internationalzation and Intra-Firm Trade: SEW Perspective

IM
Yoonjeoung Heo (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Jae C. Jung (University of Missouri-Kansas City); Unjung Whang (Chonbuk National University)
This study examines how family ownership levels influence MNEs’ foreign entry decisions and post-entry governance mechanisms. Particularly, our two-stage research framework focuses on tax haven internationalization as a foreign entry decision and intra-firm trade as a post-entry governance mechanism. Drawing on the multidimensional and evolving nature of socioemotional wealth (SEW), we theorize that there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between family ownership levels and the two international strategies. At low to moderate levels of family ownership, the desire for control by owner families may drive tax haven internationalization and intra-firm trade. However, at moderate to high levels of ownership, owner families’ heightened reputational concerns and pursuit of long-term competitiveness likely discourage these strategies. Furthermore, we propose that the affiliation of MNEs with Chaebols reshapes the evolution of these SEW priorities. Specifically, affiliation may flatten the inverted U-shaped relationship for tax haven entry but sharpen the relationship for post-entry intra-firm trade. Our analysis of Korean MNE data, utilizing a two-step control-function estimator, supports these predictions. Overall, this study offers a deeper understanding of the dynamic and context-sensitive effects of SEW on sequential entry and governance decisions in the international context.

Triangulating Family Firm Internationalization: A Sequence Analysis of FDI Patterns in Family Firms

IM
Danyang Chen (Shanghai University of Finance and Economics); Chi-Nien Chung (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Yoonjeoung Heo (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
How emerging-market family firms internationalize over time remains insufficiently understood, particularly beyond the incremental logic emphasized by the traditional Uppsala model. This study adopts a process-oriented perspective to examine whether and how family governance and social embeddedness shape firms’ foreign direct investment (FDI) sequences. Using sequence analysis on a longitudinal dataset of listed Taiwanese family firms’ FDI activities from 1996 to 2006, we inductively identify distinct patterns in the order, scope, speed, and transition probabilities of international expansion. Our analysis reveals four recurring FDI sequence patterns: Uppsala followers, Technology Anchors, Hub Users, and Hometown-focused firms. We further demonstrate that these patterns are systematically associated with family ownership concentration, family involvement in management and boards, business group affiliation, and state involvement. High family ownership is linked to conservative, path-dependent internationalization, particularly when stability and socioemotional wealth considerations dominate, whereas family managerial involvement is associated with more exploratory and non-linear expansion sequences. Business group ties facilitate hub-mediated and resource-supported transitions across markets, while state involvement reinforces localized and legitimacy-oriented strategies. By integrating sequence analysis with family governance perspectives, this study advances research on internationalization processes, family firm heterogeneity, and temporal approaches in strategy, offering a systematic framework for understanding how family priorities and institutional contexts jointly shape internationalization paths over time.

Fictive Kinship and Family Firm Professionalization: Implications for Cross-Border Expansion

IM
Christos Antoniou; Hanna Gajewska-De Mattos (University of Leeds); Luis Alfonso Dau (Northeastern University); Malcolm Chapman (University of Leeds)
We examine how the informal institution of “fictive kinship” impacts the international scope of family firms. Building on internalization theory, institutional theory, and social anthropological literature, we offer a new perspective on family firms’ internationalization, exploring their contextual heterogeneity. We unpack family structures, which are idiosyncratic but with tangible consequences for internationalization decisions. We propose that internalization across borders will be constrained by the liability of kinship, which is especially pronounced in markets where fictive kinship informal institutional structures are dominant. Where fictive kinship is more prevalent, family firms remain longer in their early governance structures. This is due to the greater availability of family resources and increasing concentration on the family’s socioeconomic wealth, leading to reduced interest in internationalization. We examine these arguments using a qualitative study of a Greek medium-sized family firm in the hospitality industry. We find that actors’ definition of family depends on the context of family firms and impacts their motivation to internationalize.
