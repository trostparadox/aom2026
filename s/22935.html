Beyond Corporate Solutionism: The Coordination Bind in Healthcare AI Research Labs

OMT
Shira Zilberstein (Harvard University)
This study demonstrates why artificial intelligence (AI) projects, even those developed in collaborative, non-corporate settings, often fall short of their social goals. Drawing on a comparative ethnography of three machine learning labs based in a university, a research institute, and a hospital developing healthcare solutions, I introduce the coordination bind: a framework that captures the tension between the work required to build collaborative and cross-sectoral AI projects and the institutional and economic arrangements of technology development and deployment that constrain applied goals. While prior scholarship attributes AI’s shortcomings to the reductive and profit-driven logics of corporate engineering, I show how cross-sectoral research labs confront pressures that redirect projects toward narrow, incremental, or stalled outcomes compared to the healthcare impacts that inspired them. Through analysis of how labs negotiate relationships and resources, I reveal how distinct coordination mechanisms—markets, networks, and hierarchies—shape AI efforts across sectors. The coordination bind captures how the very collaborative and non-corporate structures proposed to make AI more responsive to social goals also reproduce constraints and institutional frictions that limit technological trajectories. This framework advances scholarship on AI impacts and the marketization of science and medicine, showing how organizational processes shape the potentials of applied science.

Governing Medical AI Amid Regulatory Misalignment: A Perspective on Localized Self-Regulation

OMT
Angela Aristidou (UCL and Stanford DEL/HAI); Giulia Cappellaro (Bocconi University)
New technologies are increasingly deployed in real-world organizational settings long before regulation meaningfully adapts. A central regulatory problem of technological change is that such technologies enable new forms of conduct that existing legal and regulatory frameworks were not designed to govern, creating conditions of regulatory misalignment. This problem is especially acute for artificial intelligence (AI) and persists even in highly regulated environments such as health care, where dense sector-specific regulation coexists with limited technology-specific guidance. How, then, do organizations govern the concerns triggered by new technologies when regulation is simultaneously present and insufficiently applicable? We address this question through a longitudinal ethnography of the clinical deployment of a medical AI system in a UK Health Trust. Tracing the integration of AI into routine clinical work, we identify the data governance concerns that emerge under regulatory misalignment and examine how clinicians, managers, and technologists respond to them. We show how actors engage in voluntary, coordinated, and localized efforts to govern AI use—what we conceptualize as localized self-regulation. We theorize localized self-regulation as an emergent, in situ mode of governance through which organizations bridge regulatory misalignment.

Look up! Toward an Organizational Theory of AI as a Grand Challenge

OMT
Gildas Agbon (Laval University); Luc Pierre Bres (Laval University); Yves Gendron (Laval University)
Organization and management theory (OMT) has so far accounted for artificial intelligence (AI) primarily at the focal level of organizational practices, with limited theorization of how these practices relate to the broader societal challenges associated with AI. For OMT to partake more explicitly in the ongoing society-level discussions on AI, this paper theorizes AI as a societal “grand challenge” and develops an agency-based framework for addressing it. We argue that, unlike well-known grand challenges such as climate change or global health, which typically confront society with externally defined problems, the AI grand challenge is immanent in nature, that is inextricably embedded in mundane organizational and institutional practices. As such, it manifests as a threefold “drift” – instrumental, moral, and cognitive – in the current societal equilibrium, giving rise to, or exacerbating, social problems that are systemic in nature and resistant to straightforward solutions. These three drifting processes function as interconnected yet theoretically independent archetypes, each internally constituted by distinct mechanisms, some ontological (what makes AI a critical societal problem) and others praxeological (why the problem is difficult for actors to tackle). Building on this analysis, we introduce the concept of “organizational substantive agency” to propose a multi-level, value-infused process through which actors can collectively tackle AI as an immanent grand challenge. To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first in OMT to theorize AI as a grand challenge, and to provide a detailed conceptual program tailored to its distinctive features.

Nonhuman Attentional Control: Organizational Import of External Institutional Logics

OMT
Purushothaman Padmanabhan (Independent Researcher)
Organizations are increasingly integrating artificial intelligence (AI) developed within social systems that differ fundamentally from their own. When organizations adopt AI from external institutional contexts, they may unknowingly import the institutional logics embedded within these systems. Building on the institutional logics perspective and attentional control theory, we theorize AI as a nonhuman carrier of institutional logics. We propose that AI from external social systems constitutes external attentional control: shaping what organizations attend to, how problems are framed, and which alternatives surface as legitimate. Through exploratory experiments testing base models from five AI companies across four institutional origins, we find systematic divergence on value-laden decisions even under controlled conditions with neutral prompts. Models from different origins produce different outputs on identical tasks; reasoning patterns diverge even when decisions converge; and institutional distance operates on ideological dimensions within and across national boundaries. Divergence is more pronounced in peripheral functions than core functions. We contribute by extending institutional logics theory to nonhuman carriers and applying attentional control theory to cross-system AI adoption.

When Digital Technologies Help and Hurt: A Technology–Stress–Performance Framework of Knowledge Work

OMT
Amlan Haque (Central Queensland University)
Digital technologies are central to knowledge-intensive work, yet organization and management theory offers limited explanations of how heterogeneous technologies used concurrently shape stress and work outcomes. Prior research often treats digitalization as a homogeneous condition or examines single technologies in isolation, obscuring how technologies may simultaneously function as resources and demands. Addressing this gap, this conceptual paper introduces the Technology–Stress–Performance (TSP) Framework, which we argue provides a novel, integrative explanation of how distinct digital technologies influence performance and satisfaction through stress appraisal processes. Integrating stress appraisal theory, cognitive load theory, and technology acceptance research, the framework develops a dual-pathway logic: AI-enabled task-support technologies reduce stress appraisal by alleviating extraneous cognitive load, whereas social-interaction technologies tend to intensify stress appraisal by fragmenting attention and increasing cognitive demands. Stress appraisal is theorized as a central cognitive–affective mechanism linking digital technology use to work outcomes, particularly in cognitively demanding contexts, with task complexity specified as a key boundary condition. The TSP framework advances theory on digital work and, more modestly, provides a structured foundation for cumulative empirical research.
