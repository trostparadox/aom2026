A Configuration Theory of CEO Career Variety and Cognitive Orientation

STR
Shuang Liu (Georgia State University)
CEO career variety has traditionally been viewed as a source of cognitive diversity and strategic adaptability. However, prior research has often treated career variety as an additive construct, overlooking how different types of experiences interact and unfold over time. This paper develops a configurational theory of CEO career variety and strategic orientation. We conceptualize career variety as a multidimensional formative construct that includes industry, functional, and temporal dimensions, each representing distinct sources of experiential input. Temporal variety, which encompasses both pacing (the speed at which experiences are accumulated) and sequencing (the order in which experiences occur), introduces a critical dynamic component that shapes how executives integrate and apply their career histories. Drawing on configuration theory and cognitive perspectives, we argue that the relationship between CEO career variety and strategic orientation is contingent on specific combinations of experiential dimensions. We identify four archetypal forms of strategic orientation (i.e., exploration dominant, exploitation dominant, balanced, and fragmented) and theorize how distinct career configurations are more or less likely to give rise to each form. In doing so, the paper addresses inconsistencies in prior findings, enriches the understanding of executive cognition as shaped by career trajectories, and offers implications for executive selection, development, and succession planning.

The Double-Edged Nature of CEO General Human Capital

STR
Anthony Kim (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Tim Heubeck (TU Dortmund University)
Research is increasingly highlighting how CEOs shape organizational outcomes, with growing attention to general human capital as a key driver of strategic choices and performance. Although generalist CEOs possess broad, transferable experience that can stimulate exploration and innovation, this same versatility can expose firms to execution risks, particularly in technologically complex and safety-critical industries. We argue that a CEO’s general human capital, while expanding a firm’s opportunity set, also elevates the likelihood of operational failures such as product recalls. We further propose that this relationship is contingent on two firm-level capabilities: value-creation capability, which amplifies exploratory tendencies, and value-appropriation capability, which disciplines strategic risk-taking. Drawing on a multi-source panel dataset from the U.S. medical device industry, which links CEO career histories to FDA recall records, we test these predictions using panel-data econometric techniques and find support for our hypotheses.

CEO Generalist Experience and Organizational Resilience: The Value of Experience Breadth in Crises

STR
Tobias Lang (RWTH Aachen University); Malte Brettel
Prior research has established a consensus that chief executive officer (CEO) generalist experience is detrimental to firm performance. The literature further suggests that this relationship is moderated by CEO role tenure. We revisit these assumptions by examining CEO generalist experience in contexts where organizational resilience, rather than average firm performance, becomes the focal outcome. Using data on S&P 500 firms during the Global Financial Crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, we analyze the two dimensions of organizational resilience, stability and flexibility, based on stock price measures. We find that CEO generalist experience is positively associated with organizational stability and exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with organizational flexibility. Contrary to established research, we find no evidence that CEO role tenure moderates these relationships. By shifting the focus from average firm performance to organizational resilience, this study challenges the prevailing consensus and shows that CEO generalist experience can enhance firms’ ability to withstand and recover from crises, independent of CEO role tenure.

Towards A Contingency Model of Generalist CEO Leadership

STR
Anmol Pandey (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); Michael A. Abebe (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); Mark Kroll
Generalist CEOs—those with extensive experience across executive positions, firms, and industries—lead a significant number of large, publicly traded firms. Research has shown that generalist CEOs command higher compensation compared to their specialist counterparts. Despite these facts, recent studies have demonstrated that generalist CEO-led firms perform poorly compared to those led by specialist CEOs. In this study, we address this theoretical puzzle by arguing that the skills and experiences of generalist CEOs are likely to improve firm performance when they are aligned with the demands of their organizational and industry contexts. In particular, we propose that generalist CEOs, compared to their specialist counterparts, improve firm performance among firms with greater administrative complexity. Furthermore, we identify the environmental contexts (i.e., munificence, dynamism, and competitive intensity) in which generalist CEOs are more or less likely to enhance firm performance. Finally, we also examine the propensity of generalist CEOs to engage in domain-expanding corporate strategies—such as geographic diversification and acquisitions—as mediating mechanisms that help explain how their strategic choices improve firm performance. Our analysis of firms listed in the S&P 1500 between 2010 and 2021 provides support for most of our predictions. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

Crossing Domains, Shifting Perspectives? CEO Generalism and its Influence on Firm Strategy (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Marc Kowalzick (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Kai Frömsdorf (University of Mannheim)
CEOs’ career trajectories are becoming increasingly heterogeneous, as a rising number of executives build their careers through moves across different firms and industries. Prior research emphasizes the cognitive and motivational characteristics of more generalist (as opposed to more specialist) CEOs in terms of their dispositional desire for change and awareness of a broad range of perspectives, and demonstrates their broad impact on organizations. We highlight that cognitive–motivational perspectives on career variety provide an incomplete portrayal of CEO generalism and lay out the paradigmatic characteristics associated with CEOs’ repeated exposure to different institutional domains. To demonstrate the broad relevance of this “paradigmatic dimension” of CEO generalism, we hypothesize on its effect along various consequential organizational outcomes—firms’ competitive repertoire complexity, corporate investment horizons, and organizational growth activities—and propose that the valence of CEOs’ paradigms, shaped by their dispositional focus on the past, serves as the underlying driver of these effects. Using hand-collected information on the career trajectories of S&P 1500 CEOs, we find considerable support for our theorizing. Thereby, we contribute to a broader understanding of executives’ career trajectories and inform career choices of aspiring leaders.
