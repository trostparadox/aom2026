Development and Validation of a Systemic-Psychodynamic Organizational Diagnostic Questionnaire

OB
Anne-Maria Müller (University of Freiburg); Sophia Sachs; Yannik Rieder; Claas Lahmann
Background: Systems psychodynamics provide valuable insights into organizational development. To date, instruments that can reliably assess organizations based on systems psychodynamic theories are lacking, though. The Systematic Multidimensional Organisational Assessment (SyMOA) is a qualitative instrument that provides an in-depth, systems psychodynamic analysis of organizational dynamics by using a semi-structured interview guide. To complement the method, a standardized, quantitative self-assessment questionnaire capturing interrelated psychodynamic aspects of organizational functioning was developed and validated. Objective: The aim of this study was to develop and psychometrically validate an instrument for assessing organizational health based on the SyMOA diagnostic system. The questionnaire is intended to provide a scientifically grounded yet practical diagnostic tool applicable in both research and corporate practice. The findings aim to contribute to the advancement of systems psychodynamic theory and serve as a foundation for evidence-based interventions in organizational change processes. Methods: The study follows a multi-stage development and validation process. Throughout the process, item selection was guided by both psychometric criteria and theoretical considerations regarding the functional coherence of the proposed dimensions. First, the SyMOA construct was transformed into a questionnaire battery, and the items were evaluated by experts (expert validity). The items were tested through three rounds of factor and item analyses in an online panel (each n=150) and iteratively refined. Subsequently, factorial validity, discriminant validity, and test-retest reliability were examined before standardizing the instrument with a larger sample (n=825). Results: Confirmatory factor analysis of the Systematic Multidimensional Organizational Assessment Questionnaire (SyMOA-Q) supported a seven-factor model with excellent global fit, ?²(1869) = 2879.38, p < .001, CFI = .967, TLI = .966, RMSEA = .04, SRMR = .042. Standardized factor loadings ranged from .61 to .84 (M = .74), and all constructs demonstrated strong internal consistency (a = .83–.94; ? = .89–.96) and adequate convergent validity (AVE = .53–.63; CR = .87–.95), supporting their interpretation as stable, interrelated functional facets. Conclusion: Although discriminant validity was partially violated (e.g., HTMT > .90 for BI–KI, EWKA–STO, SEW–STS), the model aligns with theoretical expectations derived from systems-psychodynamic theory. The observed inter-factor correlations reflect functional interdependence among systemic capacities such as communication, reflection, and regulation. Retaining all seven subdimensions was therefore theoretically justified and practically valuable, as it allows for differentiated diagnostic interpretation in applied organizational contexts. The SyMOA-Q thus provides a psychometrically sound yet conceptually nuanced framework for assessing organizational functioning. Parallel field application of the SyMOA framework in organizational settings complements the quantitative development by offering insights into its real-world relevance and usability. This integration underscores the instrument’s translational value, while also illustrating the practical challenges of applying systems psychodynamic diagnostics in organizational contexts without reducing organizational functioning to a single global score.

Virtual Organizational Climate in Technology-Mediated Work: Conceptualization and Measurement

OB
Gil Luria (University of Haifa); Adi Luria (Ruppin Academic Center); Ofer Arazy (University of Haifa); Ian R. Gellatly (University of Alberta)
Traditionally, organizational climate encompasses employee perceptions of management practices and interpersonal experiences in face-to-face settings. As remote and hybrid work have become commonplace and digitally mediated interactions increasingly structure daily work, it is important to consider how virtual experiences reshape organizational climate, particularly given its historical grounding in co-located interaction. This study conceptually extends organizational climate to encompass online workspaces (e-mail, forums, video conferencing, etc.), and examines the utility of a virtual organizational climate construct. Specifically, we focus on three climate dimensions especially salient for technology-mediated collaboration—involvement, integration, and reflexivity—and demonstrate their relevance in technology mediated workplaces. Study 1 surveyed 565 full-time employees who interacted online daily, establishing the reliability and structural validity of the virtual climate measures. Study 2 used a time-lagged analytic strategy involving 538 respondents to assess psychometric properties and predictive validity for affective commitment and psychological safety. Results confirmed the distinctiveness of traditional and virtual climate measures, with the latter explaining unique variance in work-related outcomes. We discuss implications for climate theory and technology-mediated organizational practices.

Competitive Organizational Climates Increase Material and Symbolic Zero-Sum Beliefs

OB
Roman Angel Gallardo (University of Chicago); Kevin Wei (University of Chicago); Jane Risen
Across five studies (n = 4456), we examine the impact of competitive organizational climates on organization members' perception that one employee’s gain equates to another’s loss (i.e., zero sum beliefs; ZSBs) for organizational resources. In Study 1, a competitive (versus collaborative or neutral) organizational climate increased ZSBs for both material resources that are inherently limited (e.g., money) and symbolic resources that are not necessarily limited (e.g., respect). Study 2 replicated the effect of a competitive climate across both competitive and collaborative organizational task incentives. Study 3 extended these findings to a real-world setting: MBA students who perceived a more competitive climate at their business school reported higher ZSBs for material and symbolic resources and engaged in less positive behavior toward the organization. Finally, Studies 4 and 5 provide support for two theoretically motivated interventions that reduced ZSBs in competitive organizational climates: signaling an abundance of material resources and signaling an abundance of symbolic resources, respectively. Taken together, the current work establishes that competitive organizational climates increase both material and symbolic ZSBs, identifies their downstream behavioral consequences, and offers practical, theory-driven strategies for organizations to mitigate ZSBs in such climates.

Digital Leadership Climate: A Dual-Pathway Model of Performance and Well-Being

OB
Anna Franziska Stolle (University of St. Gallen); Heike Bruch (University of St. Gallen)
The competitive pressure to rapidly respond to digital disruption is a core challenge for contemporary organizations. Although prior research highlights that digital leadership across hierarchical levels is pivotal in enabling such responsiveness, research on digital leadership climate remains scarce. We address this gap by developing a multilevel, dual-pathway model through which a digital transformation-oriented leadership climate affects performance and well-being. At the organizational level, we draw on the theory of dynamic managerial capabilities and propose that a digital transformation-oriented leadership climate strengthens digital maturity and, subsequently, performance. At the individual level, we build on the job demands-resources model and predict that the same climate, particularly when coupled with a relation-oriented leadership climate, mitigates technostress and, thereby, emotional exhaustion. The dual-pathway perspective is essential because intensified technology-related demands can undermine well-being and erode performance if left unaddressed. We test our model using a multi-source dataset with 21,914 employees from 126 companies. Results support our hypotheses across both pathways. We extent research on leadership in digital transformation by introducing digital transformation-oriented leadership climate as an integrative lever to enhance performance while protecting well-being. We further contribute to emerging research which suggests that effective leadership in digital transformation integrates digital- and relation-oriented behaviors.

The Double-Edged Effects of Competitive Psychological Climate on Knowledge Hiding

OB
Arpana Rai; Chandra Pathki (Texas Tech University)
While competitive psychological climate (CPC) is often regarded as a driver of knowledge hiding (KH), integrating the approach-avoidance framework and social cognitive theory, this research proposes a dual-pathway model that uncovers the double-edged effects of CPC on KH. We posit that given the opportunity and threat cues embedded in CPC, it simultaneously activates approach and avoidance motivational orientations, which operate through two distinct psychological mechanisms —learning demands (approach mechanism) and self-serving cognitions (avoidance mechanism)—to influence KH. Specifically, CPC exerts a negative indirect effect on KH via learning demands (approach pathway) and a positive indirect effect via self-serving cognitions (avoidance pathway). Guided by social cognitive theory, we further position job-based self-efficacy (JBSE) as a confidence filter that shapes the strength of these pathways: high JBSE amplifies the approach pathway, whereas low JBSE amplifies the avoidance pathway, thereby influencing the overall impact of CPC on KH. Evidence from two multi-wave field studies and one experiment supports our model, offering important implications for CPC and broader competition literature.
