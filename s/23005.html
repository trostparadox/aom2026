Clearing the Relational Fog: How Platform Organizations Advocate Transparency in Supplier Industries

SAP
Alexander Engelmann (Vienna University of Economics and Business); Patrick Gregori (University of Klagenfurt); Georg Reischauer (Leuphana University Lueneburg & Johannes Kepler University Linz)
In supplier industries, economic transactions are underpinned by long-term relationships between established organizations with shared histories of repeated exchanges. Platform organizations – digital infrastructures that mediate transactions among affiliated users – seek to enhance transparency by strategically managing information visibility to drive user engagement. Yet such transparency can threaten the relational ties through which many established organizations conduct their exchanges. This study examines how platform organizations advocate for platform-mediated transparency in relationship-driven supplier industries. Adopting a practice-theoretical lens to capture the social complexity of interorganizational relationships, we conduct a comparative study of seven platform organizations in the German industrial equipment industry. Our analysis reveals that early attempts to rationalize information disclosures by emphasizing the economic benefits of transparency provoked resistance from established organizations. In response, platform organizations engaged in calibrating and auditing information disclosures and disembedding entrenched relationships to sustain transparency advocacy. Together, these practices enabled them to establish greater industry transparency despite enduring relational constraints. Based on these insights, we advance a practice-theoretical framework of platform transparency. By bridging research on platform strategy and practice theory, our study suggests that mitigating the opacity of strong interorganizational relationships requires a situated approach to transparency advocacy.

Open Innovation Tensions in Acquisitions: Learnings from Big Tech

SAP
Nevena Isic (Hanken School of Economics)
Acquisitions are a critical growth strategy for firms, but when the targets involve open-source communities (OSCs) engaged in product development, traditional integration approaches often fall short. This study examines how acquirers navigate the unique tensions that arise when combining corporate structures with decentralized and community-driven principals. Drawing on case studies from Google, IBM, and Microsoft, the findings show that tensions between acquirers and OSCs, while challenging, can also spark unexpected innovation. However, to sustain long-term innovation, acquirers need to prioritize collaborating with OSCs over integrating them. The findings contribute to the literature on balancing control and autonomy in acquisitions.

Introducing Open Strategy in Hierarchical Organizations: The Evolving Roles of Upper Management

SAP
Catherine Archambault (Excelia Business School); Ghassan Paul Yacoub (EDHEC Business School)
While Open Strategy (OS) scholars have investigated OS’ potential for higher innovation, better strategy implementation and effective strategic renewal, the question of how OS is introduced, especially in large, hierarchical firms, remains fairly unexplored. To understand the process of how OS comes to life in contexts with limited pre-existing levels of agility and flexibility, we explore the crucial roles of Upper Management (UM). Our longitudinal case study of the multinational firm Kiabi allows to offer a Five-Step Process Model for the effective introduction of OS in organizations. Such model specifies the roles UM take and how they modulate three criteria of openness (and closure), access to sensitive information, modes of participation, and modes of decision making, to discover, execute and sustain openness over time. Additionally, we contribute to OS literature and by extent TMT research by showing that while UM’s deliberate roles shape openness, the very process of openness also shapes UM’s roles: OS implementation effectiveness depends on UM’s capacity to embrace emergent roles and to recalibrate levels of openness and between the different roles and steps.

Opening Strategy After the Fact: A Genre Perspective on Reflective Strategic Communication

SAP
Josh Morton (University of Leeds); Christopher Golding (University of Reading); Eero Vaara (University of Oxford); Patrick Stacey (University of East Anglia)
This paper introduces reflective genres of strategic communication, characterized by dialogic, retrospective, and shared narration of strategy after the fact. While prior research has focused on forward-facing genres (e.g., speeches, plans) that privilege executive voice and projection, we examine how strategy narratives are reconstructed through retrospective reflection by those who enacted it. Drawing on an analysis of Iwata Asks - a series of interviews conducted by the late Nintendo CEO Satoru Iwata - we analyze how strategy is reconstructed through inclusive, curated dialogue. We identify four discursive practices - staging, enabling, constraining, and anchoring - that structure participation and meaning-making in these reflective conversations. These practices do not unfold linearly, but recur dynamically to open, shape, and close shared narratives. Our findings contribute to research on strategic communication, participation, and narrative by (1) theorizing reflective genres as a communicative form for post-hoc strategizing, (2) showing how retrospective narration constitutes a site of participatory strategy work, and (3) highlighting the tension between inclusion and coherence in these multi-voiced settings. By foregrounding the voices of developers and the dialogic role of leadership, we extend the strategy-as-practice agenda to examine how strategy is not only made but narrated and made concrete through collective reflection.
