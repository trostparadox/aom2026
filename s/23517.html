Do Political Ties Facilitate or Hinder Firms’ Exploratory Innovations? Theory and Empirical Evidence

TIM
Ru Cheng (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Qiang Wang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Yongchuan (Kevin) Bao (University of Alabama in Huntsville)
Although political ties have received considerable attention in prior research, their influences on firms’ exploratory innovation remains inconsistent. To contribute to this ongoing debate, we build on political embeddedness perspective to distinguishes between two types of political ties, primary ties and secondary ties, and investigates their distinct impacts on exploratory innovations. We theorize that primary ties exert a negative effect whereas secondary ties have a positive effect on exploratory innovation. To better understand the conditions under which political ties may influence exploratory innovation, we further investigate the contingency of these effects on firm cognition and capability as the prime organizational search factors, arguing that TMT attention to innovation and a firm’s technological capability mitigate the adverse effects of primary ties and enhance the beneficial effects of secondary ties on exploratory innovation. We test hypotheses based on panel data collected from 3,501 publicly listed firms in China during 2009 and 2022. The results largely support our predictions. By disaggregating political ties into primary ties and secondary ties, our study offers a new approach to advance the ongoing debate surrounding the innovation consequences of political ties and contributes to the political embeddedness perspective by addressing an ambiguity about the consequences of political embeddedness in the context of exploratory innovation. This research also connects the nonmarket strategy literature with the research stream of organizational search by examining the interplay of political ties with the factors of firm cognition and capability. The research findings offer practitioners useful suggestions on how to effectively leverage political ties to promote exploratory innovation.

National Governance and the Sustainability Power of Innovation: A Multidimensional View

TIM
Hsin-Lun Chiang (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University); Hsin-Ning Su (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University)
Prevailing literature affirms that strong national governance fosters innovation, yet this discourse often relies on patent counts, a metric that overlooks the vast heterogeneity in inventive value. This study advances the frontier by asking a more nuanced question: how does national governance shape the character and long-term impact of innovation? To address this, we first develop a novel theoretical framework, the Sustainability Power of Innovation (SPI), which conceptualizes and measures an innovation's influential footprint through three distinct dimensions: Spatial Impact (geographic reach), Temporal Impact (longevity), and Knowledge Impact (influence on subsequent knowledge). We then test this framework using a large-scale dataset of 200,000 U.S. patents, examining how the six disaggregated dimensions of the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) in an inventor's home country relate to the three dimensions of SPI. Our findings reveal a complex and non-uniform relationship. While most governance dimensions, such as Rule of Law (RL) and Political Stability and Absence of Violence (PV), are positively associated with an innovation's Spatial and Temporal impacts, others, like Regulatory Quality, exhibit significant negative effects. Furthermore, Political Stability is negatively correlated with Knowledge Impact, suggesting a trade-off between stability and the creation of disruptive, high-impact knowledge. This study contributes by offering a new, multidimensional framework for evaluating innovation outcomes and by demonstrating that the facets of governance have distinct, and sometimes contradictory, effects on the long-term sustainability power of innovation.

Government Guidance Funds and Startups’ Technological Innovation in Geopolitically Sensitive Areas

TIM
Wanru Deng (Shanghai University); Gang Zheng (Zhejiang University); Guangpei Chen (University of Electronic Science and Technology of China)
Rising U.S.-China tensions have increasingly exposed technological innovation in strategic sectors to geopolitical risk, constraining firms—particularly startups with limited resources and weaker political influence—through sanctions, supply chain disruptions, and other institutional contingencies. This paper examines the role of government guidance funds (GGFs), a hybrid instrument that combines state objectives with market-based mechanisms, in shaping startups’ technological innovation in geopolitically sensitive areas. Drawing on a panel dataset of 1,048 Chinese semiconductor startups from 2002 to 2020, we find that GGFs significantly enhance startups’ technological innovation in geopolitically sensitive domains, measured by patenting in technology classes associated with the U.S. Entity List. Moreover, this effect is particularly salient in industry segments that highly rely on imports, but is attenuated when founders hold political ties. These results remain robust through several additional checks, including a two-stage residual inclusion model that addresses endogeneity concerns. Our study advances understanding of innovation under geopolitical risk, and extends research on entrepreneurship by demonstrating GGFs as a hybrid strategic instrument for startups to navigate contested environments by balancing innovation with risk-hedging imperatives.

Reallocating Rights: A Property Rights Framework for Stakeholder Governance Under Permitting Reform

TIM
Nuno Gil; Tianqi Yang (The University of Manchester)
Democratic societies are undertaking sweeping reforms of permitting regimes governing the development of large-scale socio-technical systems—including transport and energy infrastructure, real estate, mining, logistics facilities, and AI data centres—to accelerate delivery while improving cost and schedule certainty for investors. These reforms foreground a persistent governance challenge: how to manage relationships with stakeholders who do not make development-specific investments in production or innovation, yet control resources essential to project realization—such as land, regulatory approvals, and political consent—and who may bear concentrated harms from development activity. Drawing on property rights economics and evidence from permitting reforms in the UK and Australia, we develop a 2×2 framework that distinguishes stakeholder governance arrangements based on how permitting laws allocate control rights and claim rights between development sponsors and affected stakeholders. The framework contrasts long-standing institutional regimes with emerging configurations and clarifies how different allocations of rights shape incentives for cooperation, bargaining, and strategic hold-up. We show how these arrangements condition the ability of societies to mitigate concentrated harms while realizing the diffuse collective benefits associated with large-scale system development. We conclude by discussing implications for managers and policymakers seeking to balance stakeholder enfranchisement, investor incentives, and public welfare amid accelerating institutional change.

Data Privacy Regulation and Innovation

TIM
Sukhun Kang (University of California Santa Barbara)
We investigate how data privacy regulations shape firms’ research and development decisions. While such regulations aim to increase stakeholder privacy and data secu- rity, they can also introduce compliance costs in settings where collecting, analyzing, and sharing sensitive data is central to innovation. We examine this tension in the context of the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the pharmaceutical industry. Using detailed clinical trial data and firm-level variation in exposure to the GDPR, we find that GDPR reduces clinical trials by 18 percent, and declines persist even after accounting for shifts in disease composition. GDPR also shifts research processes: firms conduct trials across fewer sites and countries. Research collaborations decline overall, particularly among young firms, and shift away from new partners towards established relationships. Finally, the GDPR shifts innovation out- comes: trials take longer to complete, are less likely to complete successfully, and results are reported with a greater delay. These findings reveal how data privacy regulations shape not only the rate of innovation, but also the process by which it is pursued and its outcomes.
