The Saturation Paradox: How AMR Keeps Producing 'New Theory' as Conceptual Space Crowds

OMT
Carlos Denner (University of Sherbrooke)
Theory fields valorize novelty, yet mature literatures may struggle to expand conceptual territory. We distinguish local differentiation (distinctiveness relative to contemporaries), from global saturation (proximity to the cumulative archive). Using AMR records (1995-2024, N=859) and a validated extraction pipeline, we find that size-adjusted local differentiation is stable (+0.002/year, p=0.66), while global saturation rises sharply (+0.29 z-score/year, p<.001). Authors maintain distinctiveness from peers even as the archive becomes increasingly crowded. This resolves an apparent paradox: a field can continuously produce "new theory" through positional differentiation (new labels, boundary claims) while the underlying claim-space fills and consolidates. The pattern is consistent with novelty norms operating as relational standards (standing out from peers) rather than cumulative standards (expanding the conceptual frontier), with implications for how evaluation regimes shape theorizing.

Towards Indigenous Theory Building

OMT
Ajnesh Prasad (Audencia Business School)
Theorizing in management studies continues to be dominated by knowledge bases from the Global North—mainly the US and western Europe. In recognition of the problems associated with the intellectual hegemony of western perspectives in the field, scholars have increasingly called for theorizing to become more geographically and culturally inclusive. As a response, this article contends that to achieve greater inclusion in theorizing, the field ought to move towards Indigenous theory building. Indigenous theory building is an approach to theorizing that is particularly adept to making sense of organizational phenomena from and about the Global South, and is predicated on two overarching but equally important principles. First, in terms of ontology, it requires researchers to adopt emic positioning when developing theory—to build theory about organizational phenomena in Global South geographical contexts from the inside. Second, in terms of epistemology, it demands of researchers to rebuke ethnocentric judgements when evaluating emic informed theory—to resist the temptation to evaluate the legitimacy of the theory by measuring it against established knowledge developed in the Global North. Applying these two principles to theory building in management studies offers a constructive path to achieve greater intellectual plurality and inclusion in the field by accounting for and respecting the uniqueness of hitherto understudied geographical contexts.

What Is Theory Performativity, Really? An AI-augmented Qualitative Meta-Synthesis

OMT
Zuyi Cao (Huazhong Agricultural University); Longcheng Zhang (Huazhong Agricultural University); Fenfen He (Huazhong Agricultural University)
Performativity has become a central concept in organization and management studies, widely used to explain how theories intervene in, shape, and transform organizational practices. Despite extensive qualitative research, existing studies on theory performativity remain fragmented and often rely on static typologies or linear sequences of effects. As a result, the field still lacks a coherent, process-oriented understanding of how theory performativity unfolds dynamically and recursively over time. To address this gap, this article develops an AI-augmented qualitative meta-synthesis of theory performativity research based on influential management journals. Methodologically, we combine large-scale, AI-assisted extraction and clustering of performative claims with interpretive qualitative synthesis, enabling the inductive reconstruction of performativity as a process architecture rather than a predefined set of types or outcomes. Our analysis reveals that theory performativity operates through a recursive, multi-stage process comprising four interrelated phases: performative activation, translational reconfiguration, institutional alignment and legitimation, and drift, contestation, and recursive re-engagement. Rather than progressing linearly, performativity is dynamic, unstable, and prone to breakdown, resistance, and re-entry into earlier stages. Thus, the study advances theory performativity research beyond static classifications, demonstrates how AI can support large-scale inductive theory building while preserving qualitative rigor, and provides a generative framework for future research on performative dynamics.

Motivation Without Mind: An Evolutionary Theory of Unconscious Behavioral Propensity

OMT
Yundong Huang (Saint Edward's University)
Motivation theories in management research typically assume that effort allocation originates from conscious or psychologically represented states such as needs, goals, and values. This paper challenges that assumption by proposing the Unconscious Motivational Evolution Model (UMEM), a minimal evolutionary framework that locates motivation at a pre-psychological level. UMEM conceptualizes motivation not as a mental state, but as a heritable energy-allocation propensity that governs how agents distribute limited resources across competing tasks. Using a stylized evolutionary simulation, we show that stable, directional, and interpretable patterns of effort allocation can emerge in agents that lack cognition, representation, and internal reward systems. These patterns resemble key motivational phenomena studied in organizations, including persistence, adaptive effort reallocation, and the endurance of non-functional behavior. Introducing a minimal cognitive module further reveals that cognition does not generate motivation, but modulates its expression—accelerating adjustment while also producing cognitive misallocation, the amplification of misaligned effort through imperfect experience-based inference. Overall, UMEM provides a generative foundation for motivation that clarifies the evolutionary origins of effort, the limits of cognitive intervention, and the interpretive nature of motivational constructs in organizational contexts.

A Dialectical Approach to Problem Formulation

OMT
Raj Krishnan Shankar (Great Lakes Institute of Management); Dean Shepherd (University of Notre Dame)
Equating the rigor-relevance divide to the theory-practice divide is confounding. Instead, we posit tension arises from the differences in how scholars and practitioners view relevance: theoretical relevance (as visualized by scholars) vs practical relevance (as visualized by practitioners). Despite proposals that problem-oriented research can help close the scholar-practitioner divide and increase scholarly impact, it remains underutilized. This underutilization of problem-oriented research likely arises from scholars’ and practitioners’ limited insight into identifying and defining a mutually beneficial problem for exploration. While scholars have used dialogue-based approaches to explore problems, their effectiveness has been limited because of the need to conform to the dualism of theory and practice. Instead, we propose using a dialectical approach to problem formulation, which retains and builds on the contradictions between scholars and practitioners to define a problem. Contradictions arising from divergent institutional logics, temporality, and the nature of knowledge lead to dynamic tensions between how scholars and practitioners view relevance. Retaining these dynamic tensions leads to defining mutually relevant and temporally useful problems that can be the sources of highly impactful research.
