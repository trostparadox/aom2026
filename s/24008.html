Learning to Disagree Civility and the Practice of Inclusion in Higher Education

MED
Tim Ewest
Efforts to advance diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) in higher education have endeavored to expanded representation and broadened access, yet inclusion alone does not determine how students engage one another when in shared learning spaces. Without pedagogical structures for navigating disagreement, DEI efforts may unintentionally produce avoidance, resentment, or ideological conformity. This paper argues that civility, understood as a relational ethic and a learning practice of engaging across difference, is necessary to sustain inclusion in educational environments. Drawing on a content analysis of over 200 syllabi across public universities in California, Texas, and Florida, this study applies a seven-dimension civility rubric to examine how discourse norms, reflection, and collaborative learning are embedded at the curricular level. Findings show strong emphasis on respectful behavior and reflective engagement, but limited scaffolding for conflict resolution and structured dialogue. These patterns suggest that institutions recognize the value of civility, yet underemphasize the practical skill-building needed to support democratic and inclusive learning. This paper argues that embedding civility into curriculum design (DEI–C) strengthens belonging, psychological safety, and students’ capacity to engage constructively with differing perspectives.
