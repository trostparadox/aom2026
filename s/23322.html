How Independent Board Chair Power and Compensation Influence the Likelihood of CEO Dismissal

STR
Gian-Luca Angelo Asquini (University of Zurich); Ryan Adam Krause (University of Iowa); Thomas Keil (University of Zurich)
A core assumption in corporate governance research rooted in agency theory is that independent board chairs direct the board’s monitoring efforts in alignment with board and shareholder interests. This neglects a second-order principal-agent problem: an independent board chair is an agent in their own right with their own incentives and motives. Our theory highlights this second-order principal-agent problem within the boardroom in the context of CEO dismissal. Specifically, we argue that powerful independent board chairs, ceteris paribus, will prefer not to engage in CEO dismissal, and as such will use what power they have to discourage CEO dismissal in the context of poor firm performance. However, when adequately compensated, independent board chairs’ preferences can shift into alignment with those of the principals because chair compensation changes how board chairs use their power. A quantitative analysis of S&P 500 firms from 2010 to 2019 supports our predictions and provides evidence for a critical, previously neglected agency problem within the board of directors.

Political Orientation Homophily: The Impact of Political Liberalism in Firms on CEO Succession

STR
Kalin D. Kolev (Marquette University)
In this study, I focus on an unexplored driver of CEO succession – the values and beliefs inside organizations. Drawing on Upper Echelon Theory and social identity theorizing, I argue that political orientation – associated with core values and beliefs on the liberal-conservative spectrum – affects the selection of a successor CEO. Developing a model of political orientation homophily, I theorize that liberal organizational decision makers – driven by preference to preserve their political identity and interact with similar others – select a liberal successor CEO. Consistent with these arguments, the results provide evidence that a liberal predecessor CEO, a liberal board of directors, and a liberal employee base are associated with hiring a liberal successor CEO. These relationships are strengthened when the successor CEO is an insider. This study contributes to research on CEO succession and political orientation by offering novel insights on the role of liberalism in achieving greater political homophily at the upper echelons of organizations.

When Political Risk Lingers: Firm-Level Political Risk and CEO Dismissal.

STR
Anamika Datta (The University of Memphis); Jihae You (The University of Memphis)
In contemporary political economies, corporate boards increasingly rely on CEOs who can navigate pressures arising outside traditional market competition. We examine whether persistent firm-level political risk (FLPR) influences the likelihood of CEO dismissal. Using data from S&P 1500 US firms between 2009 and 2020, we find that persistent FLPR significantly increases CEO dismissal likelihood, even after accounting for firm performance and standard governance factors. This effect is weaker when firms exhibit strong performance and operate in highly regulated industries. The study extends CEO-dismissal literature by identifying persistent FLPR as a nonmarket signal that shapes how boards evaluate CEO leadership capability. Sustained political exposure provides boards with a salient cue about a CEO’s ability to anticipate, manage, and adapt to political uncertainty, an increasingly consequential aspect of firm performance and survival. By examining board-, firm-, and industry-level moderators—board political ideology, firm performance, and industry regulatory burden—we further clarify when boards are more likely to interpret persistent FLPR as a leadership failure warranting CEO replacement. In doing so, this study offers a more nuanced understanding of how political environments can shape critical corporate governance outcomes.

Old Hands on the Wheel: Why Former Leaders Outperform in Distressed Successions

STR
Shuning Liu (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Jingfeng Yin (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Steve Sauerwald (University of Houston); Yi Huang (Tsinghua University); Weibo Ma (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Daniel Zyung (Not Associated)
While a board of directors can instantly transfer legal decision-making rights to a new CEO, it cannot instantly transfer the firm-specific knowledge required to exercise that power effectively. We leverage the distinction between formal authority (the right to decide) and real authority (the effective control over decisions) to theorize why re-hiring a former executive offers a distinct advantage during periods of organizational decline. We argue that unlike unparalleled successors, returning leaders possess latent human and social capital that minimizes the information asymmetry typically hampering early-tenure turnarounds. Using a panel of S&P 1500 firms facing negative performance feedback, our analysis reveals that re-hired former CEOs significantly outperform both internal and external successors in restoring firm viability. We further demonstrate that this performance differential is strictly conditional: the advantage erodes when the returning executive’s firm-specific knowledge has been diluted by interim external employment, yet it is catalyzed by the retention of legacy board ties and high requirements for strategic reorientation. These findings suggest that the value of "old" leadership lies in the immediate capacity to convert formal title into operative control when time is scarce.

Ambient Priming: Media Speculation and Market Reactions to CEO Dismissal

STR
Diego Villalpando (The University of Texas at Arlington); Angie L Cabrera Uribe (The University of Texas at Arlington)
CEO dismissals are among the most consequential board actions but investor reactions to them are inconsistent. We argue that this inconsistency stems from how dismissal announcements violate investors’ baseline expectations that the CEO will remain in office, particularly since impression management is infeasible prior to a dismissal. Drawing on expectancy violation theory and impression formation research, we develop the concept of “ambient priming”— when information outside the firm shapes stakeholder responses to an event by eroding their expectancies. We focus on one especially powerful source of ambient priming—media speculation about a possible CEO dismissal—and predict that it mitigates the expectancy violation of a dismissal which improves investor responses. We further theorize that this effect is amplified when ambient uncertainty is high, as this heightens investors’ sensitivity to expectancy-relevant cues. Our findings from an analysis of 141 CEO dismissals across 116 S&P 500 firms from 2013 to 2022 robustly support these predictions. We extend theory on investor reactions to CEO dismissals; complement work on media coverage volume, tone, and accuracy by emphasizing that mere incidence of coverage shapes stakeholder responses; and highlight ambient priming as a novel mechanism of impression formation.
