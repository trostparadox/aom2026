The Earnings Premium Puzzle of Entrepreneurial Experience: Who Benefits?

Friday, Jul 31 2026 · 1:00PM–2:00PM
Philadelphia Marriott Downtown — Poster Wall 3
ENT

People

Authors: Daniel Auguste Babson College; Todd Nobles University of Washington

Session overview

Entrepreneurship is widely promoted as a pathway to upward economic mobility, yet many entrepreneurs fail and return to paid employment. Prior research remains inconclusive on whether entrepreneurial experience yields an earnings premium after business failure, and little is known about how such earnings benefits vary by race. Using data from the Survey of Income and Program Participation (2014–2023), we analyze earnings among 69,309 wage and salaried workers, comparing former entrepreneurs to workers who have never been entrepreneurs. Random-effects regressions with robust standard errors reveal pronounced racial heterogeneity in the returns to entrepreneurial experience. Former White entrepreneurs earn approximately 11 percent more than White workers with no entrepreneurial experience, whereas Nonwhite former entrepreneurs earn approximately 16 percent less than their Nonwhite counterparts who have never been entrepreneurs. Substantial racial earnings gaps persist across employment histories: among former entrepreneurs, Nonwhite workers earn approximately 21 percent less than White workers, and among workers who have never been entrepreneurs, Nonwhite workers earn approximately 40 percent less. These findings suggest that entrepreneurship operates as a racially stratified mobility mechanism and that entrepreneurship-based policy initiatives may inadvertently reinforce racial inequality if post-failure labor market barriers are not addressed.
