Session overview

This session examines how motherhood shapes workplace experiences, career trajectories, and employee well-being. Across studies of discrimination, maternal guilt, return-to-work transitions, impression management, and policy context, the papers show how motherhood remains a powerful axis of inequality in organizations. Together, they illuminate the emotional, structural, and evaluative dynamics that influence mothers' attachment to work, health, identity, and advancement.

Papers (4)

Differential Moderating Roles of On- and Off-the-Job Embeddedness Under Motherhood Discrimination (WITHDRAWN)

DEI
Vesa Peltokorpi (Hiroshima University)
Working mothers often experience motherhood discrimination in the labor market and within organizations. This paper draws on conservation of resources theory to examine the moderated mediating effects of turnover intention on the relationships between motherhood discrimination and two outcomes: voluntary turnover and physical health. Specifically, we examine how on-the-job and off-the-job embeddedness moderate the first-stage relationships between motherhood discrimination and turnover intention, thereby shaping the indirect effects of discrimination on voluntary turnover and poor physical health. Findings from a time-lagged study of 719 working mothers suggest that turnover intention mediates the positive relationships between motherhood discrimination and both voluntary turnover and poor physical health, and that working mothers experiencing high on-the-job embeddedness and discrimination are first less likely to intend to quit and subsequently actually quit (voluntary turnover) their organizations. However, those who stayed in their organizations had poorer physical health symptoms. The findings suggest that off-the-job embeddedness has opposite moderating effects, strengthening the relationships between discrimination and turnover. This paper contributes to the literature by providing a conservation of resources-based rationale and empirical evidence demonstrating that on-the-job and off-the-job embeddedness represent distinct resource pools that influence how employees respond to adverse workplace conditions.

Bound by Duty, Pulled by Love: A Three-Wave Study of Maternal Guilt and Job Embeddedness

DEI
Rajula Abdul Rasheed (MES Mampad College); Nimitha Aboobaker; SHAHANAS BEEGAM PP
Drawing on identity control theory, this study examines the relationship between maternal guilt, work meaningfulness, and job embeddedness among working mothers in academia. It also investigates the moderating role of work-home segmentation in weakening the negative effects of maternal guilt on workplace outcomes. Through a three-wave time-lagged design, data were collected from 275 female faculty members at Indian higher education institutions. The model’s fit was assessed through confirmatory factor analysis using AMOS, and hypothesis testing was performed using hierarchical regression analysis and PROCESS Macro Models 4 and 7. The results found that maternal guilt had a negative indirect effect on job embeddedness through work meaningfulness, suggesting that when mothers experience guilt, they may question the value and purpose of their professional work, leading to diminished job embeddedness. Furthermore, the interaction term (work-home segmentation) positively moderated the effect of maternal guilt on work meaningfulness and the mediating effect of work meaningfulness on job embeddedness. This research contributes to the understanding of identity regulation and maternal subjectivity in the organizational context by exploring how maternal guilt influences job-related experiences and the role of boundary initiatives in reducing the detrimental effects.

Can She Have It All? Professional Image Maintenance as a Shield Against Supervisors' Maternal Bias

DEI
Yujie Yin; Erica Xu (Hong Kong Baptist University); Xu Huang (Hong Kong Baptist University)
This study examines how working mothers manage motherhood bias in workplace evaluations through social identity–based impression management (SIM) strategies—specifically, image maintenance and decategorization—to enhance perceived fit with supervisors’ implicit followership theories (IFTs). Results show image maintenance is more effective than decategorization in improving perceived IFT fit and promotability, with political skill as a critical interpersonal competency that enhances the credibility and influence of working mothers’ impression management efforts.

Navigating Motherhood and Career Under China’s Three-Child Policy

DEI
Meng Huang (University of York)
This study examines a contradiction: as China expands pronatalist support through the three-child policy, gender inequality deepens in professional workplaces. Drawing on photo-elicitation interviews with 24 banking professionals, the analysis shows how policy implementation is filtered through culturally embedded gender norms and translated into organisational expectations about women’s proper conduct. In practice, statutory entitlements are reframed as discretionary favours, while an availability-based risk calculus casts motherhood as a managerial liability. These dynamics crystallise into three normative “ideal female” templates: a devoted mother, a constantly available worker, and an accommodating wife, which guide recruitment, allocation, evaluation, and promotion decisions while remaining largely unspoken. Theoretically, the study develops the concept of cultural-institutional embeddedness to explain how cultural frameworks legitimise inequality regimes under shifting policy conditions. By linking institutional pressures, organisational practices, and gender performativity, the paper extends Acker’s inequality regimes, Scott’s institutional pillars, and Butler’s performativity to account for policy-driven, culturally normalised discrimination.
