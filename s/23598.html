Session overview

This session explores entrepreneurship in contexts marked by marginalization, crisis, migration, and structural disadvantage. The papers examine rural women in Lebanon, migrant women entrepreneurs, self-made women in France, Afghan immigrant entrepreneurs in Pakistan, and entrepreneurship training for underserved groups. Collectively, they show how entrepreneurs build agency and legitimacy under constrained conditions while revealing the institutional supports still needed for inclusive entrepreneurial ecosystems.

Growing Entrepreneurial Agency in Contested, Crisis Contexts: Lebanese Rural Women

DEI
Hayfaa A. Tlaiss; Dmitry Mikhail Khanin (The University of New Mexico)
Prior research has identified the structure and elements of entrepreneurial agency. Scholars also investigated entrepreneurial agency in constrained and crisis-ridden environments. However, little is known about the process of growth, development, and maturation of entrepreneurial agency. Our study seeks to fill this theoretical gap. We capitalize on Socio-cognitive theory’s key properties of agency to demonstrate how women in rural Lebanon transform these properties in to stages or phases of agency’s construction. Subsequently, we reveal how entrepreneurial agency develops from the intentionality stage to the self-reactiveness stage (at the foresight vs. correction phases) and to the self-reflectiveness stage. Our study contributes to research on entrepreneurial agency in constrained environments and in crisis contexts.

A Sponsorship-Driven Intersectional Perspective for Migrant Women Entrepreneurs

DEI
Dr. CASCANT Eunice (Jean Moulin Lyon 3 University); Aziza Al Ghafri (University of Gävle); William Degbey (University of Vaasa)
This study advances a Sponsorship-Driven Intersectional Perspective framework for Migrant Women Entrepreneurs (SDIP-MWE) to aid policymakers and entrepreneurship scholars comprehend how sponsorship-driven support, viewed through an intersectional lens, shapes inclusive and sustainable ecosystem outcomes for MWE. Drawing on a qualitative research design, we analyze 43 semi-structured interviews with (MWE) in France and Sweden, triangulated with secondary data. Our results identify interrelated dimensions of intersectional constraining elements, sponsorship-driven enabling activities mitigating these constraints such as inclusive approaches to justice and sustainable ecosystem outcomes. The SDIP-MWE framework offers novel insights and meaningful theorization into intersectionality entrepreneurship research encouraging dialogue between academics, policymakers, and sponsorships.

The Exception That Proves the Rule': How Self-Made Women Entrepreneurs Account for Their Success

DEI
Caroline Julie Demeyere (Université catholique de Louvain); Elina Meliou (University of Southampton); Florence Villeseche (Copenhagen Business School); Foteini Papadopoulou (University of Southampton Business School)
The article examines how self-made women entrepreneurs from underprivileged backgrounds account for their entrepreneurial success despite lacking conventional entrepreneurial resources. Prior research on ‘underdog entrepreneurs’ largely attributes success to exceptional skills and abilities. Using a Bourdieusian perspective on capital and the concept of the exception, we analyzed 31 biographical interviews with women entrepreneurs in France. We identify three mechanisms through which self-made women transpose their embodied capital into objectified, recognized entrepreneurial capital, which support gaining legitimacy as credible entrepreneurs: (i) learning from life experiences to claim cultural capital; (ii) differentiating from competitors to claim economic capital (iii) investing in social relationships to claim social capital. We contribute to the literature on self-made women and entrepreneurial success by complementing existing explanations based on adaptive skills and capacities, showing how they deploy agency to reframe their gendered trajectories into structurally valued forms of capital. We further show how, through this process, specific sources of gender inequality remain unchallenged.

Entrepreneurship at the Margins: Liabilities of Foreignness Among Afghan Immigrant in Pakistan

DEI
Bibi Raheela (National University of Science and Technology); Owais Anwar Golra (National University of Sciences & Technology (NUST) Islamabad); Zeeshan Rafiq (Information Technology University of the Punjab)
Purpose: This study explores the liabilities of foreignness experienced by Afghan immigrant entrepreneurs and how they navigate through legally and economically constrained entrepreneurial ecosystem in Pakistan. The liabilities are conceptualized via legal exclusion, social marginalization, structural precarity and economic informality. Design/methodology/approach: The study uses 31 in-depth, qualitative interviews and field observation in urban informal markets to build on. It incorporates Liabilities of Foreignness (LOF), Mixed Embeddedness Theory (MET) and Transnational Mixed Embeddedness Theory (TMET) to explore how these entrepreneurs respond to local and transnational exclusion by using both local and transnational strategies. Findings: The research findings reveal that Afghan entrepreneurs workaround structural and institutional exclusion through adaptive, innovative and bricolage practices. Entrepreneurs are resilient, have strategic collaborations, and always feel an urge to keep businesses afloat even in the absence of formal support. TMET emphasizes their bi-locational embeddedness relative to both host and home country ecosystems whereas MET expounds their strong reliance on co-ethnic relationships and informal opportunity structures. Research limitations/implications: The study is limited in exploring the ethnic diversity of the Afghan immigrant population since various ethnic groups might possess different cultural and entrepreneurial habits. However, the insights advance understanding of entrepreneurial agency in fragile and exclusionary ecosystems. Secondly, the difficulty of reaching out to some groups especially those who are undocumented migrants as well as women entrepreneurs is highly possible, the study has not reached the full extent of the pool of experiences within the immigration entrepreneurship ecosystem in Afghan case. Originality/value: This study makes a distinctive contribution to research since it studies the case of Afghan immigrant entrepreneurship in Pakistan as one of the most under-researched and politically subjective environments characterized by legal exclusion, displacement, and informality. It further expands on current scholarly perspectives of entrepreneurship among immigrants to include and substantively challenge Liabilities of Foreignness, Mixed Embeddedness Theory, and Transnational Mixed Embeddedness Theory in a Global South and refugee construct. It reveals how refugee entrepreneurs actively reshape their economic environments despite systemic exclusion, and calls for broader recognition of informal and transnational entrepreneurial ecosystems.

Systematic Review of Entrepreneurship Training for the Underserved and Underrepresented Communities

DEI
Angelea Lillian Dukes (Morgan State University)
Entrepreneurship training programs are crucial for overcoming systemic barriers that minority and disadvantaged groups encounter, as well as encouraging economic empowerment and social inclusion. This systematic review explores the design, implementation, and impact of such programs to identify best practices and shortcomings. The review examined 42 peer-reviewed papers, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework. Inclusion criteria were based on empirical studies, including qualitative research, as well as conceptual papers, addressing minority entrepreneurial training. Key findings show that effective programs combine culturally relevant pedagogies, mentoring, and practical learning to improve entrepreneurial self-efficacy and economic outcomes of minority entrepreneurs. However, ongoing shortcomings include insufficient long-term evaluation of business sustainability, restricted access to financial resources, and underutilization of community-driven approaches. Programs adapted to specific cultural contexts, such as those embracing Indigenous customs or tackling hurdles for women in STEM, were important for developing resilience and economic success. The review shows that entrepreneurship training can disrupt poverty cycles and cultivate generational wealth by equipping participants with skills and addressing systematic barriers through inclusive, culturally responsive program designs. Policymakers, educators, and practitioners are urged to promote inclusive, culturally responsive program designs that provide participants with technical skills while simultaneously addressing systemic disparities.
