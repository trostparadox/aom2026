Strategic CSR After Downsizing: Managing Financial Distress and Firm Recovery

SIM
Martin Osei (Stevens Institute of Technology); Josep A. Tribo (Stevens Institute of Technology); Mike L. Barnett (Rutgers University)
This study examines the impact on the short-term and long-term financial performance of combining labor cost-reduction strategies (such as downsizing) with post-downsizing changes in corporate social responsibility (CSR) in situations of financial distress. Downsizing, commonly employed to address economic strain, provides immediate financial relief but can yield mixed long-term results. We find that while combining downsizing and post-downsizing CSR reductions improve short-term financial performance by increasing firm’s risk, this is not the case for long-term performance. On the contrary, a balanced approach combining downsizing and post-downsizing increases CSR, although it has no significant short-term effect on performance, it enhances long-term financial performance through reduced risk. The latter result does not hold if firms merely maintain post-downsizing CSR at the level it was before downsizing. These findings contribute to CSR and turnaround management literature by offering valuable insights into the strategic use of CSR in response to downsizing during financial distress and how to handle CSR policy to ensure a firm’s financial recovery.

Shattered Shields or Silver Linings? Using Event System Theory for ESG–Financial Performance

SIM
Che-Yuan Chang (National Taipei University of Education); Po-Hsuan Chen (National Taipei University of Technology); Yu-Feng Cheng (National Taiwan University of Science and Technology)
Drawing on Event System Theory (EST), we theorize how event characteristics and industry context jointly condition the financial value of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG). Leveraging a Difference-in-Differences (DID) design with 9,591 firm-year observations from the Taiwan Economic Journal (2015–2024), we examine the manufacturing and financial sectors to uncover the boundary conditions of the ESG–performance link. While generally positive, ESG benefits are severely attenuated in the financial sector and during the COVID-19 pandemic. Crucially, we identify a "joint constraint" effect: The ESG– performance relationship flips to significantly negative for financial firms during the pandemic. We contribute to EST by revealing how high-strength external events and rigid institutional environments interact to suppress the strategic value of sustainability.

When ESG Exceeds Aspirations: Institutional Contingencies in Firms' Search Resonses

SIM
Tae Young Park (Yonsei University); Yoonjeong Kang (Yonsei University)
This study examines how ESG performance feedback shapes firms’ search behaviors and how this relationship is contingent on formal and informal institutional conditions. We conceptualize formal institutions using national voice and accountability scores and informal institutions using cultural tightness. Using a panel data on publicly listed firms incorporated in 45 countries and headquartered in 26 countries from 2012 to 2023, we test whether ESG under- and overperformance relative to aspiration levels trigger changes in search, and whether these effects vary across institutional contexts. Our results indicate that ESG overperformance is associated with greater slack search, whereas ESG underperformance has no significant effect on problemistic search. Further, voice and accountability significantly moderate the relationships between ESG feedback and search for above and below aspiration conditions, while cultural tightness exhibits only partial moderation for the above aspiration relationship. Overall, the findings suggest that ESG performance feedback does not uniformly operate in the manner implied by traditional BTOF predictions; instead, firms’ responses to ESG feedback are heterogeneous and systematically conditioned by institutional environments that shape accountability and stakeholder scrutiny.

Competing Through Doing Good: How Product Market Competition Shapes Firms’ ESG Performance

SIM
Jialin Huang (South China Normal University); Shule Yu (East China Normal University); LEYANG WANG (South China Normal University)
Contrary to the view that competition deters non-market investments, we argue it can be a primary driver of firms’ ESG performance. Using 2009–2023 Chinese listed firms, we present strong evidence that product market competition (PMC) significantly enhances ESG performance. By leveraging the Hurun brand list and the “Catalogue for the Guidance of Industries for Foreign Investment” as instrument variables, we demonstrate that competitive pressures significantly induce firms’ ESG behavior. ESG serves as a strategic purpose. 1) it acts as a reputation buffer, mitigating the negative impact of litigation risks induced by heightened competition; and 2) it functions as a differentiation tool, particularly through product-related initiatives, enabling firms to capture market share. Our results are more pronounced for firms in higher levels of intellectual property protection region, with greater geographical international diversification and less financial constraints. Overall, we highlight that ESG behavior, as non-market investments, is a key strategic tool for firms to navigate and gain an advantage in intensely competitive environments.

Overcoming the Tragedy of the Anticommons? How Diverse Actors Co-Construct Knowledge Commons

SIM
Evelyn Rita Micelotta (University of Vermont); Maggie Cascadden (University of Alberta); Frank Wijen (KU Leuven)
With the advent of digitization and the internet, scholarly knowledge can theoretically become a knowledge commons. The Open Access Movement was partially successful at catalyzing this reconstruction: some, but not all, scholarly work is available without a paywall. In this paper, we take a narrative approach to understanding how community (academics and librarians), state, and business (publishers) actors co-constructed scholarly knowledge as a new commons and why this movement was only partially successful. We find that the initial moral-based argument for commoning academic knowledge was ultimately unsuccessful and replaced with a narrative that governments should become architects of a reconstructed, open-access commons of scholarly knowledge. Instead of accepting this role, governments in the USA and UK chose to become arbiters of knowledge commons reconstruction and left it largely to market forces. Unexpectedly, publishers became important actors in the partial success of the OA movement: they realized a way to align commons values with their business interests by developing new business models that incorporated Open Access. We contribute to our understanding of new commons governance by challenging extant narratives of business and state interests as antagonistic to commons organizing and elucidating how governments and businesses not only play key roles in commons governance but also support commons construction in unexpected ways.
