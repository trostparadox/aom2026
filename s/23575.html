Heterogeneous Expertise and Innovation Resilience: Inventors’ Green Innovation After Katrina

TIM
Yuan Hu (The University of Utah)
This paper studies how exogenous disruptions to knowledge production affect the resilience of innovation when production relies on heterogeneous expertise. I focus on green innovation, an important technology for combating climate change, which draws on diverse knowledge and thus requires collaboration among inventors with heterogeneous expertise. Exploiting physical exposure to Hurricane Katrina, the costliest storm in U.S. history, as a quasi-natural experiment, I combine hazard and patent data for U.S. inventors in a difference-in-differences design. The results show that Katrina reduced the production of green patents by physically exposed inventors, while non-green patents were unaffected. The negative effects are more pronounced for patents produced by teams with greater technological distance and for inventors with more technologically distant collaborators before Katrina. These findings suggest that the heterogeneity of expertise, while beneficial for knowledge creation, can reduce the substitutability of directly disrupted inventors’ expertise and thereby undermine the resilience of technological development in the face of disruption.

Inventor Mobility and Exploratory Innovation: Evidence from Inventor Inflows and Knowledge Networks

TIM
Xiaodong Geng (Northwestern Polytechnical University)
Exploratory innovation is highly sensitive to human capital mobility, yet this relationship, particularly in the context of inventor inflow, remains underexplored in the existing literature. This study examines the effect of inventor inflow on firms’ exploratory innovation and the moderating role of knowledge network structures. Using panel data from 311 publicly listed biopharmaceutical firms in China over the period 2003–2023, we identify a robust inverted U-shaped relationship between inventor inflow and exploratory innovation. Furthermore, the number of direct ties among a firm’s knowledge elements intensifies the curvature of this relationship, magnifying both the benefits of moderate inflow and the adverse consequences of excessive inflow. In contrast, indirect knowledge ties flatten the inverted U-shaped relationship by attenuating the marginal effects of inventor inflow at both low-to-moderate and high levels. These findings highlight the contingent nature of inventor inflow effects and demonstrate how internal knowledge structures shape firms’ ability to leverage incoming human capital, while offering actionable implications for human capital management and knowledge integration in the biopharmaceutical industry.

Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A), Inventor Turnover, and Network Properties

TIM
Deepak Jena (Indian School of Business); Rajat Khanna; Siddhartha Vivekanand Komati (Cornell University); Kannan Srikanth (The Ohio State University)
The acquisition of high-technology acquisitions often triggers significant inventor turnover, jeopardizing the knowledge assets acquirers seek to obtain. Yet, our knowledge of the conditions that accelerate or ameliorate inventor turnover is limited. In this study, we examine how the target inventors’ pre-acquisition productivity and network position shape their post-acquisition mobility decisions. Using data from 139 U.S. technology acquisitions, we find that highly productive inventors and those occupying brokerage positions are less likely to leave. Furthermore, community density, the cohesiveness of a broker’s collaborative community, strengthens this retention effect. We also argue and show that inventor productivity mediates the relationship between brokerage and departure. Our findings advance understanding of post-acquisition human capital retention by specifying individual-level predictors and the mechanisms linking social structure to inventor mobility.

Triadic Firm–University–Rival Linkages: Asymmetric Knowledge Creation for Firms and Universities

TIM
Xiaozhou Jing (Renmin University of China); Jingjing Zhang (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences); Yan Yan (Renmin University of China)
While prior studies adopting a dyadic perspective regard firm–university collaborations as engines of knowledge creation, a triadic perspective reveals their dual potential to both foster knowledge flows and enable leakage. This study examines triadic co–authorship structures where a firm collaborates with a university that simultaneously co–authors with one of the firm’s rivals. We argue that these configurations expose firms to heightened risks of knowledge leakage, which diminishes their knowledge creation. At the same time, they enable universities to leverage cross–partner knowledge flows and enhance their own knowledge creation. This results in asymmetric knowledge creation outcomes for firms and universities. Furthermore, a firm’s centrality in the collaboration network amplifies the negative effects of such triadic structures on its knowledge creation, whereas a university’s centrality weakens the positive effects on its knowledge creation. Using data from 137 firms and 134 universities in the U.S. pharmaceutical industry (1997–2017), our empirical analysis provides support for most of the proposed hypotheses. This study advances understanding of the double–edged nature of open innovation and highlights the role of network positions in shaping knowledge flows.

University Associated Transfer and the Lemons Problem in University Technology Transfer

TIM
Haw Feng Pang (Zhejiang University); Can Huang (Zhejiang University); Huijun Shen (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
University Associated Transfer (UAT) refers to the commercialization process in which university patents are licensed or sold to companies that are founded, co-founded, or managed by inventors or members of the inventors’ teams. UAT has emerged as a critical mechanism in university technology transfer by mitigating the information asymmetry discount that arises when firms undervalue patents because they cannot fully assess patent value without access to inventors’ tacit knowledge. Despite its growing importance, the determinants of UAT remain underexplored. Using a unique dataset of 13,809 patents licensed or sold from National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC)–funded projects completed between 2017 and 2021, we find that technological novelty increases the likelihood of UAT. This effect is strengthened by technological complexity but attenuated by both inventor prestige and university prestige. Our findings underscore the need to value inventors’ knowledge alongside patents, thereby mitigating the information asymmetry discount that depresses valuations for novel patents and offering actionable guidance for innovation policymakers, inventors, and technology-transfer professionals.
