Essential or Extra? When Firm-Hosted Communities Become a Business Necessity

TIM
Ana Orelj (University of Iceland)
While user communities are generally welcomed as an important source of knowledge and market information, it remains unclear when community involvement shifts from being merely beneficial to becoming operationally necessary. I argue that this distinction is shaped by product features and by how product affordances are intended by producers and enacted by users. The analysis of co-creation, interaction, and knowledge flows between a company and its firm-hosted community reveals a gap between intended and enacted affordances, referred to here as affordance slack. Using the gaming industry as a setting, I analyze three scenarios: theme-park games, where designers control all interactions; open-world games, where users operate within predefined parameters; and sandbox games, where users can fundamentally reshape the product. Across these cases, games can be positioned along an affordance slack spectrum that captures the generative potential embedded in the design. The findings show that higher affordance slack increases the need for community involvement and that openness is rooted in the design core rather than added later as a technological feature. The paper is based on a longitudinal qualitative case study of the international game producer CCP Games and the community surrounding their most recognizable video game, EVE Online, complemented by supplementary data from other games, companies and user communities. It contributes to the user and open innovation literature by shifting attention away from the question of who is in charge of innovation toward how product design enables openness and invites community involvement. It further shows that interactions between firms and user communities differ depending on the product and proposes a framework to capture these differences. For practitioners, the paper offers guidance on how to balance their efforts according to the nature of the product to leverage community involvement as products and user behaviors evolve.

Product Novelty and the Effects of Digital Platforms

TIM
Zhao Zheng (INSEAD); Henrich Greve (INSEAD)
We examine how novelty in creative products affects consumer reception and market performance. We argue that the novelty penalty is not inherent to novel content itself but rather a function of audience-content mismatch: in traditional mass markets, unconventional content reaches heterogeneous audiences whose preferences do not align with its distinctive qualities, resulting in lower evaluations, greater polarization, and weaker commercial outcomes. We then explore how digital streaming moderates these relationships through algorithmic niche sorting— matching unconventional content with self-selecting audiences who specifically seek distinctive offerings. Analyzing data on 268,263 movies and over 15 million audience reviews from Rotten Tomatoes, combined with a difference-in-differences design and coarsened exact matching, we find that narrative novelty significantly depresses audience ratings, increases disagreement, and reduces box office performance in theatrical markets. Yet streaming reshapes this penalty: novel movies are partially shielded from rating declines and protected from audience polarization on streaming platforms. Our paper demonstrates how digital distribution fundamentally transforms market dynamics for creative products—while streaming generally disadvantages movies by exposing them to broader audiences, it paradoxically serves as a more favorable channel for unconventional content, amplifying the returns to product innovation through algorithmic personalization.

The Disruptive Dethronement Process in Multi-Sided Markets

TIM
Kazuhiro TAKAHASHI (Doshisha University)
Prior research examining disruptive innovation in the context of multi-sided markets and ecosystems has shown that disruptive innovation in these contexts entails processes different from those in traditional markets. Specifically, the literature has demonstrated the emergence of cooperative relationships between incumbents and disruptors in these contexts, leaving their direct competition largely unexamined. To fill this gap, this study examines the process through which disruptive multi-sided platform (MSP) providers enter mainstream multi-sided market segments and overtake incumbent MSP providers through an in-depth case study of the Japanese travel agency industry, specifically focusing on Rakuten Travel and Jalan net as disruptive MSP providers. This study identifies a new source of entry barriers and a potential problem faced by disruptive MSP providers pursuing a disruptive path and elucidates an effective strategy for addressing these challenges: engaging repeatedly in switch-inducement activities and support activities for supply-side users of incumbent MSPs. The findings of this study provide new insights into the functions of support offered by MSP providers to supply-side users.

The Economics of Innovation Speed in Digital Platforms: A Theory of Persistent Multi-Domain Search

TIM
Sebastian Niederberger (Bocconi University)
We develop a theory of innovation in digital platform markets where content persists indefinitely and generates cumulative returns through network effects and algorithmic amplification. Unlike traditional innovation models where knowledge obsolesces and paradigms are abandoned, digital platforms enable parallel exploitation across multiple domains with zero marginal cost. We formalize the “stacking” mechanism whereby faster within-domain search speed accelerates cross-domain exploration, creating strategic complementarity. The model endogenizes both search speed and exploration decisions, showing how platform characteristics fundamentally alter optimal innovation strategies. Our analysis reveals substantial overproduction due to attention externalities alongside significant underexploration due to knowledge spillovers. Extensions incorporate audience dynamics, algorithmic learning, and platform competition. The theory explains empirical patterns including the rapid adoption of speed-enhancing features, winner-take-all dynamics, and persistent portfolio expansion strategies in digital markets.

When Do Product Changes Stimulate User Engagement? The Role of User Communities

TIM
Qiujie Qiu (Stockholm School of Economics); Roxana Turturea (Stockholm School of Economics); Holmer Kok (Stockholm School of Economics)
Product changes have become a central strategy for firms operating in digital markets. By refining, removing, or adding components, firms can improve existing products in response to technological change and evolving user needs. However, product changes involve an inherent trade-off. While it can renew product value and stimulate user engagement, it may also disrupt established usage patterns and impose adjustment costs on users, shaping how strongly these benefits are realized. Using a longitudinal dataset of weekly observations from 37,844 video games on Steam over a ten-year period, we find that product changes are, on average, positively associated with user engagement. However, the magnitude of this association varies systematically with user community characteristics. The positive association is stronger for products with more familiar user communities, but weaker when users allocate attention broadly across alternative products or when the user community is more highly concentrated. These findings highlight demand-side conditions that shape how product changes translate into user engagement.
