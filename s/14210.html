Session overview

Normative stakeholder theory holds that firms should be managed for the benefit of all stakeholders, broadly defined as any actor who affects or is affected by the firm. Yet recent developments in normative and positive stakeholder theory have focused predominantly on contributing stakeholders—those who make firm-specific investments in production and innovation—while leaving under-theorized the governance of non-contributing stakeholders. These stakeholders may control essential resources required for economic value creation (e.g., land, regulatory permits, political consents), yet some of them also bear concentrated harms from firm activity and thus may lack incentives to cooperate. This problem is particularly salient in permitting policy. Expansive stakeholder enfranchisement introduced through land-use and environmental regulations in the 1980s and 1990s has been associated with persistent cost overruns, delays, and economic value destruction, with adverse implications for collective welfare. These dynamics are not confined to infrastructure projects: AI factories and other platform-based businesses, real estate development, mining, warehousing and logistics, and large-scale manufacturing and processing operations routinely encounter non-contributing stakeholders who exercise de jure or de facto control (and even veto power) over resources essential to economic value creation. This symposium creates a forum to debate how stakeholder theory can better account for the management of these non-contributing stakeholders. To advance this conversation, the symposium brings together Robert T. Miller, whose recent work challenges stakeholder theory to engage seriously with insights from welfare economics—particularly the limits of preference aggregation—alongside scholars of stakeholder governance and property rights. Together, the panel examines the conceptual limits, normative tensions, and governance issues that must be addressed if stakeholder theory is to move beyond moral aspiration toward analytically rigorous and actionable frameworks for the management of non-contributing stakeholders. A central message of this symposium – highly relevant to scholars across the OMT, STR, and SIM divisions, we argue, – is that neither unrestricted stakeholder control nor firm unilateralism is viable; effective governance arrangements must balance the internalization of harms borne by non-contributing stakeholders against the societal benefits of constraining stakeholder hold-up and preserving investment incentives.
