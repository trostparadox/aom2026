Racial Dynamics in Corporate Leader Activism: BLM Support and Director Career Impact

SIM
Seung-Hwan Jeong (University of Georgia); Maurice J. Murphy (University of Georgia); Joanna J Reddick (University of Georgia); Ann Mooney Murphy (Stevens Institute of Technology)
This study examines the predictors and career consequences of corporate director activism following the Black Lives Matter movement. Using data on S&P 1500 directors, we find that Black directors were significantly more likely to engage in activism than their peers. We also find evidence of allyship, as female directors were more likely to support the movement than males. Crucially, the professional costs were unequal: Black directors who engaged in activism faced increased shareholder dissent and loss of committee chairships, while other directors experienced no such penalties. These findings highlight that while identity drives activism, minority leaders uniquely bear the career risks.

When Banks Favor Black Board Membership? Evidence from the Black Lives Matter

SIM
Henry Huang (Yeshiva University); Yi Tang (The University of Hong Kong); Chong Wang (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Xuefei Yu (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Beyond shareholders, how do social movements reshape other stakeholders’ evaluation of firms? We focus on one critical secondary stakeholder—banks— and theorize that social movements reshape banks’ risk management logic, prompting them to silently reassess firms through both legitimacy risk and default risk lenses. Following the Black Lives Matter (BLM) movement, banks, though not direct participants in activism, interpret firms’ board racial diversity as a signal of social alignment that mitigates potential legitimacy risk, while at the same time inferring lower default risk from firms with stronger social support. We argue that firms’ conformity with movement-salient norms influences banks’ credit decisions. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design on 13,960 U.S. bank loans from 2008 to 2022, we find that firms with more Black directors benefit lower loan spreads following local BLM protests, and this effect spills over to other minority groups as well. Our findings reveal how secondary stakeholders engage in silent resource allocation, embedding social legitimacy into financial risk assessments.

From Stance to Substance: Conditions Shaping When Symbolic CEO Activism Translates into Firm Action

SIM
Ghina Chammas (HEC Paris)
While prior research treats firms’ symbolic and substantive responses to stakeholder concerns as distinct alternatives, we know little about when symbolic gestures are followed by substantive follow-through. This paper studies how issue-level contentiousness shapes whether CEO sociopolitical activism is paired with subsequent corporate social responsibility (CSR) action. Drawing on institutional theory and research on stakeholder complexity, we argue that issue contentiousness exerts a non-linear influence on follow-through: moderate levels of stakeholder disagreement encourage substantive action, whereas high levels of contention constrain CEOs’ willingness and ability to commit organizational resources. We further show that this relationship depends on whether contentiousness is stable or shifting over time, and whether the issue is materially related to the firm’s core business. Using a dataset linking CEOs’ activism to issue-specific CSR practices among S&P 500 firms from 2014 to 2019, we find an inverted U-shaped relationship between issue contentiousness and substantive follow-through. This decline at high levels of contentiousness is attenuated when disagreement is still changing, but steepens when activism targets material issues. Our findings advance research on decoupling and nonmarket strategy by highlighting issue contentiousness as a dynamic condition shaping symbolic–substantive alignment, and by identifying when executive voice is most likely to translate into organizational action.

Transparent Politics: How Managerial Perceptions of Shareholder Demands Shape Political Disclosure

SIM
Debdeep Chatterjee (Concordia University); Hadi Shaheen (College of Charleston)
Firms face competing pressures when deciding how much to reveal about their political activities. Concealment protects against stakeholder backlash, preserves strategic flexibility, and shields sensitive relationships, but political opacity creates accountability gaps that increasingly concern investors and other stakeholders. We examine whether shareholder activism can compel greater political transparency in firms’ nonmarket behavior. Using panel data on 438 large U.S. firms from 2011 to 2020, we show that firms receiving political shareholder proposals increase their corporate political disclosure scores. We further argue and find that this relationship is contingent on executive political cognition. When CEOs are ideologically misaligned with the sitting administration, firms are less responsive to shareholder pressure for transparency, as perceived political threat promotes defensive postures that favor concealment over accommodation. Conversely, when executives perceive high political uncertainty, firms are more likely to respond to shareholder pressure, using disclosure to reduce information asymmetry and signal effective governance. These findings indicate that identical stakeholder demands can yield divergent transparency outcomes depending on how top managers interpret and respond to the political environment.

Translating Social Issue Ideas, Meanings, and Practices in Changing Problem Contexts

SIM
Chloe R. Cameron (Western University)
Organizations are increasingly expected to engage with social issues, yet efforts to translate such concerns into sustained organizational change often falter. Drawing on the Scandinavian institutionalist perspective on translation theory, this study examines how ideas, meanings, and practices related to social issues become organizationally actionable, and how related translation processes unfold as organizational problem definitions and priorities change over time. Based on a longitudinal qualitative study of neurodiversity employment initiatives, the analysis shows that social issue related translation objects travel across organizations not as singular ideas, but as bundles of value rationales, categorizations, and prescribed practices. These bundles enable initial organizational action by aligning social issues with recognizable organizational concerns, while simultaneously constraining how enactments can expand without further translation work. The study further advances translation theory by showing how a mission-driven social enterprise functioned as a central site for the production of translation objects, highlighting the role social enterprises can play in shaping how social issues are defined and enacted beyond their own operational boundaries. Finally, the study helps to conceptualize how value understandings of social issue related translation objects need to be resituated within recipient organizations to be sustained as initial problems used to anchor them shift.
