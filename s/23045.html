The Making of Athletic Bodies: Organizational Body Work in High Performing Sports

CMS
Suvi Heikkinen (University of Jyväskylä); Marke Kivijärvi (University of Jyväskylä)
This paper explores the multifaceted role of the organizational body work of top athletes, who continuously work in the realms of rivalry. Drawing on a sample of 34 interviews with men and women active top athletes, we examine how these high-ranking individuals engage in organizational body work and the constitution of their social identities at work in high performing sports. Our theorization builds on the emerging strands of literature on organizational body work and rivalry within organization and management studies. We introduce a novel, high performance context, that due to its distinct nature offers opportunities to develop the empirical understanding of organizational body within highly pressured, competitive organizational settings. Based on narrative research, we identified three forms of organizational body work that both women and men engaged with, including personal, interpersonal, and systemic body work. Our empirical results contribute to the dynamics of rivalry and 'embodied' labor. Taken together, we discuss the complexity and challenges faced when the top athletes are called up to organizational body work and the consequences for organizations in high performing sports and beyond.

A Critical Review of Home-Work Dynamics: A Global South Perspective

CMS
Harish Shetty (XLRI Xavier School of Management); Vivek Soundararajan (University of Bath); Andrew Crane (University of Bath)
Research on home–work dynamics has grown substantially, focusing primarily on how boundaries between home and work are affected and managed. However, despite the accumulation and synthesis of a large body of work, there has been little examination of how these dynamics have been studied and what underlying assumptions and exclusions shape prevailing understandings in this field. This paper offers a critical review of home–work dynamics by foregrounding a Global South perspective as an epistemic intervention. The review problematizes existing literature by identifying its narrow empirical scope, elite and segmented spatio-temporal framings, and its individualised, apolitical treatment of relationality. These limitations constrain our understanding of how the relationship between home and work unfolds in the Global South. In response, we propose three alternative conceptual pathways: (1) epistemic plurality, advocating for diverse ways of knowing; (2) marginal and ambiguous spatio-temporality, highlighting the complex, fluid, and often precarious interactions between home and work; and (3) dynamic, situated relationality, emphasizing a power-sensitive, contextualized, and grounded understanding of home–work relations. Together, these pathways call for more inclusive and context-sensitive scholarship that attends to the diverse and underrepresented realities of workers in the Global South.

Intersectional Labour Regimes: Re-Theorising Work, Caste, and Organisation from India

CMS
Aparna Mira Venkatesan (Independent Researcher)
Contemporary Indian organisations frequently allocate work according to entrenched caste, gender, class, and language distinctions. However, organisation studies scholarship lacks a robust vocabulary to theorise how these patterns are produced and stabilised within firms. Although intersectionality has made significant contributions to the field, its application has primarily addressed identity, experience, and representation, rather than the organisational structuring of labour. This paper introduces the concept of intersectional labour regimes as a decolonial organisational framework, repositioning intersectionality from a descriptive tool of social location to an explanatory framework for how organisations allocate, value, and discipline labour through historically embedded hierarchies. Drawing on Ambedkarite and Dalit–feminist political economy, Indian labour sociology, and responding to calls within critical organisation studies to re-radicalise intersectionality, the paper develops a four-dimensional model specifying allocative and regulatory regimes through which labour is intersectionally coded. The model theorises regimes of skill attribution, visibility and legibility, mobility, and respectability and risk, and is elucidated through brief illustrative vignettes. The contribution is twofold: it provides a material, caste-centred re-theorisation of intersectionality for organisation studies, and reconceptualises ostensibly neutral organisational categories such as merit, professionalism, and culture fit as caste-coded infrastructures. The paper concludes by outlining a decolonial research agenda for examining labour regimes in multilingual, postcolonial workplaces.

Work Intensification and Mental Health Impacts : Women Academics Speaking Out

CMS
Hadar Elraz
The research project investigates an under researched literature pertaining to women academics work and mental health experiences in UK higher education. Aiming to examine the work and mental health experiences of academic women in HEI, the paper draws on qualitative methods through interviews with forty-three female academics who were willing to talk about the interface of their working lives and mental health experiences. Reflexive thematic analysis reveals three interrelated themes related to experiences of intensified work and mental health impacts; pastoral care and mental health; institutional support for mental health and disclosure. The study offers empirical, practical and theoretical contribution to the understanding of the roles of women academics and their mental health impacts

Dalits and Nomads: Marginalities in the Sugar Industry in a Neoliberal Economy

CMS
Rajesh Mokale (UPES School of Business); Ram Mahalingam (University of Michigan); Ranjeet Nambudiri (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Patturaja Selvaraj (Gettysburg College); Nobin Thomas (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
In India’s sugar belt, the demand for disposable, low-cost labour is met largely by Dalits (formerly untouchables) and nomads, whose structural marginalization renders them particularly vulnerable to seasonal, physically demanding employment. Yet the contemporary labour regimes are not historically isolated: the long co-evolution of caste, agriculture, and social hierarchy since the transition from nomadic to settled life continues to shape how work, space, and status are allocated. Our study shows how relations between castes, mediated through the stigmatization of mobility and touch, have produced both the untouchability of nomads and the nomadification of Dalits in modern India. To trace these intertwined processes of marginalization, our paper excavates the historical and moral foundations of caste-based labour. We analyzed the neoliberal labor practices in the sugar industry of Maharashtra state of India to understand: A) Whether neoliberal practices in the sugar industry really empower nomadic Adivasis and Dalits? and b) How the sugar economy creates precarity for these groups? Based on an ethnographic study of 57 participants in Sangli district of Maharashtra State in India, we found the production of precarity in 3 ways: (1) Family based labor system (Koyta) continues not to empower nomadic Adivasis; however, they ended up experiencing newer forms of precarity; (2) Work in sugarcane harvesting reproduce inequalities by shifting the marginal position of nomads and Dalits, such that displaced nomadic communities embody the cultural prescriptions of Dalits, while Dalit communities embody nomadic displacement to serve in sugar industry as seasonal labor; (3) Family based labor system develops challenges for the nomadic women who face gendered precarity at intersections of caste and gendered society impacting on their health and habitat. Thus, we argue that neoliberal practices do not contribute to the re-distribution of wealth but lead to the redistribution of marginality. In doing this, we contribute to the literature on the precarity of untouchable and nomadic laborers in the sugar economy at the intersections of tribal and caste society.
