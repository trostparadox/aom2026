No Way Out: The Impact of Passive Institutional Ownership on Green Innovation

STR
Marlene Herz (Goethe University Frankfurt)
Institutional investors increasingly claim to leverage their influence within public companies to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and align business models with a net-zero economy. Yet, it is also questioned whether a large subgroup of these investors is actually capable of influencing managerial decisions, given that they are passively invested in these companies, which prevents them from selling their shares and exerting credible pressure on the management. I contribute to this debate by examining whether and how passive investors promote green innovation within their portfolio firms. For that purpose, I use an instrumental variable approach for a sample of Russell 3000 constituents between 2003 and 2021. The findings reveal that a 1\% increase in passive institutional ownership increases the number of green patents by 1.8%. Notably, this effect is less attributed to direct monitoring on behalf of passive investors but more to their ability to curb the influence of short-term-oriented shareholders through strategic voting within shareholder meetings. These results contribute to studies at the nexus between corporate finance and strategic management by underscoring the vital role of long-term oriented investors in influencing green innovation strategy.

Agency Exposure, External Auditing, and Green Breakthrough Innovation Under Compliance Regimes

STR
Xintong He (Peking University)
Green breakthrough innovation is critical for ecological transformation but faces high uncertainty, externalized returns, and weak market incentives. Drawing on Resource Dependence Theory, we conceptualize green breakthrough innovation as a compliance-induced response embedded in supervisory relationships with regulatory agencies and external auditors. Using panel data from Chinese A-share manufacturing firms (2015–2020), we measure agency exposure via disclosed regulatory interactions and identify breakthrough innovation through green patent analysis. Results show that agency exposure significantly promotes green breakthrough innovation by enhancing firms’ institutional adaptability and resource mobilization. However, this positive effect is significantly attenuated when firms are audited by Big Four accounting firms, as the heightened requirements for information traceability and procedural accountability reorient compliance logics and constrain agency-based incentive effectiveness. This study highlights how compliance regimes structure the boundaries of organizational innovation under conditions of regulatory complexity and institutional multiplicity.

Losing or Gaining the Edge: How Political Connections Shape Green Innovation

STR
Marlene Herz (Goethe University Frankfurt); Na Zou (University of Bath)
What motivates firms to pursue green innovation? Drawing on social identity theory, we argue that corporate political connections embed firms in partisan networks that shape strategic attention and legitimacy around the green transition, altering both the opportunities and pressures firms face. Using panel data on U.S. public firms (2001–2022) and a difference-in-differences design exploiting plausibly exogenous resignations of politically connected directors, we find asymmetric effects by ideology: losing liberal ties reduces green innovation in climate adaptation technologies, whereas losing conservative ties increases green innovation, especially in climate mitigation. These effects depend on local politics: the decline after severing liberal ties is stronger in Republican-governed states, while the increase after losing conservative ties is stronger in Democratic-governed states. Mechanism evidence is consistent with distinct pathways: losing conservative ties is followed by higher environmental enforcement intensity (buffering), whereas losing liberal ties is followed by fewer green procurement contracts (binding). Overall, we show how the ideological orientation of political ties and local political context jointly shape firms’ green innovation strategies.

Sustainability Through Openness: Knowledge Recombination Practices for Circular Innovation

STR
Reeti Agarwal (Jaipuria Institute of Management); rsha ali alghafes (Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University); Asha Thomas; Anushree Luukela-Tandon
To shift to a circular economy (CE), firms need to coordinate distributed knowledge across organizational boundaries. Using Knowledge-Based View (KBV), this study examines how open innovation (OI) practice, namely breadth and depth, facilitates the capability to develop Circular Knowledge Recombination Capability (CKRC) through Transactive Memory Systems (TMS) and knowledge-sharing (KS) mechanisms. A survey-based research design was used for data collection utilizing constructs validated in previous studies; the collected data were analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM). The findings show that both OI breadth and depth significantly improve CKRC, but through different pathways: OI breadth has a direct effect that is partially mediated by TMS, whereas OI depth has a TMS development effect on CKRC only. Because TMS has a significant positive effect on both KS and CKRC, TMS is confirmed as a cognitive coordination mechanism. KS positively affected CKRC, but KH as a moderating factor was not significant, suggesting that mature cognitive structures can mitigate the negative aspects of KH. This study contributes to KM and OI literature by framing TMS as a dynamic capability for activating open innovation into circular innovation knowledge. In practice, the findings emphasize that firms must develop cognitive knowledge architectures that map competencies, institutionalize knowledge sharing, and achieve a trade-off between breadth of exploration and depth of relational commitments to enable circular transformation.

Strategizing Circular Value Creation: Effectual Practices and Knowledge Pathways in Open Innovation

STR
Ankit Mehrotra (Jaipuria Institute of Management); Safiya Alshibani (Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University); Raj Mahto (The University of New Mexico); William McDowell (Florida Gulf Coast University)
Firms looking to transition toward a circular economy often confront persistent uncertainty while striving to balance innovation, sustainability, and profitability. This study explores how effectuation - conceptualized as a higher-order entrepreneurial logic - enables circular value creation (CVC) through knowledge-based mechanisms and how a paradox mindset influences these relationships. Anchored in Effectuation Theory, the Knowledge-Based View (KBV), and Paradox Theory, the research advances an integrated model that connects entrepreneurial cognition, organizational learning, and circular innovation. Drawing on data from 294 professionals across manufacturing and technology sectors, the study applies Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) to examine the proposed relationships. The findings show that effectuation significantly enhances both Open Innovation Capability (OIC) and Sustainability-Oriented Learning Capability (SOLC), which subsequently foster circular value creation. These capabilities act both in parallel and sequentially, suggesting that effectual reasoning promotes circular outcomes primarily through capability transformation rather than deliberate strategic design. Moreover, the paradox mindset amplifies the Effectuation – SOLC - CVC pathway, illustrating how comfort with contradiction strengthens sustainability learning under conditions of uncertainty. The study enriches theoretical understanding by linking entrepreneurial cognition, knowledge-based capability development, and paradoxical thinking within the circular economy domain. From a practical standpoint, it underscores the value of nurturing effectual reasoning, fostering open collaboration, and embedding sustainability-oriented learning systems that help firms transform uncertainty into regenerative value.
