Deviance by Design: How Grant Ecosystems Create the Conditions for Their Own Exploitation (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Ruey Heng Arthur Hu (National University of Singapore); Shi-Ying Lim (National University of Singapore)
Extant research has frequently acknowledged the presence of deviance in grant programs. Phenomena like “grant mills” and “zombie projects” are often mentioned but left unexplored beyond the need to empirically exclude them as outliers. Our inductive study explores this gap by utilizing data from archival court records and primary interviews, including one with an ex-convict, to understand how deviance emerges and persists in grant programs. Beyond uncovering previously undocumented forms of deviance, we forward an ecosystem model that explains how competitive dynamics and normalization can lead to the institutionalization of deviant practices in grant programs. Importantly, our model also reveals how non-core stakeholders seeking to benefit from grant programs and grantor interventions to curb deviance can counterintuitively trigger cascading effects that reinforce the normalization of deviant practices. Our findings pose several contributions to grant research. Of note, the embedded and persistent nature of deviance forwarded suggests that excluding identifiable outliers (e.g., grant mills), as prior studies have done, may be insufficient to avert systematically biased observations.

Entrepreneurial Resourcefulness in Total Institution

ENT
Sadek Showkat (Loyola University Chicago); Oana Branzei (Ivey Business School); Alan D. Meyer (University of Oregon)
Against the odds and rules that explicitly prohibit entrepreneurship within this total institution, a cluster of refugee camps housing over one million Rohingya refugees forcibly displaced from Myanmar by military troops has become a hotbed of entrepreneurial activity. When camp authorities or accidental fires burn down their ventures, entrepreneurs rebuild amid recurrent adversity. This paper interweaves reflexive, in-situ ethnographic accounts with textual and visual representations of refugee entrepreneurs’ evolving relationships with total institutions to reveal how entrepreneurs transform the adverse contexts they inhabit in remarkable, recurrent, and so far untheorized ways. Exploring how entrepreneurs interact with total institutions, we inductively theorize a cyclical process of place revocation and reclaiming. We show how forcibly displaced individuals come to understand place as revocable—something they may not own, yet can temporarily “resource.” We conceptualize and visualize revocable places as small-scale, easily accessible spatial settings that can be claimed, modified, or used for entrepreneurial activities but whose existence is rendered precarious by the threat of repeated dispossession. We show that when place comes to be understood as revocable, two novel types of place-based resourcefulness emerge and entwine as refugee entrepreneurs oscillate between complying with and defying the constraints of the total institution.

Law, Innovation, and Entrepreneurship

ENT
Mark C. Suchman
This article links law to two core elements of economic and social change: innovation (new ideas) and entrepreneurship (new ventures). It first maps the legal environment for innovation and entrepreneurship across multiple bodies of law and multiple modes of impact. It then synthesizes frameworks from sociolegal and organizational theory to examine: (a) how legal fields translate ambiguous rules into institutionalized organizational practices; and (b) how organizational fields evolve through punctuated cycles of innovation and entrepreneurship. The many analogies and linkages between these accounts suggest a broadly generalizable model of techno-legal regimes aligning, drifting, and realigning through loosely coupled cycles of incrementalism, discontinuity, ferment, and consolidation. The discussion closes by exploring coupling/decoupling mechanisms and sketching implications for practice, theory, and method. The thesis throughout is that law operates not as an exogenous constraint, but as an endogenous, coevolving force within overlapping legal and industrial fields.

Seizing the Master's Tools: Entrepreneurial Co-Optation Under State Repression

ENT
Adam K. Frost (University of Southern Denmark); Na Zou (University of Bath); Rick Hunt (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Our study challenges the Baumolian assumption that hostile institutions inevitably divert entrepreneurial talent into unproductive or destructive activities, we argue that a repressive state’s architecture of control paradoxically generates systemic frictions, such as allocative inefficacies and information asymmetries, that may serve as the raw material for productive entrepreneurial action. Drawing on unique archival data from prosecuted cases of ‘speculation and profiteering’ in Maoist China (1960s–1970s), we develop a process model of entrepreneurial co-optation, which exhibits a recursive, bidirectional dynamic. First, through tactical appropriation, entrepreneurial actors seize the state’s own tools—its seals, documents, and infrastructure—and repurpose them to construct alternative pathways for value creation. Second, as these ventures resolve functional failures, they trigger strategic incorporation: state actors, balancing ideological mandates against operational realities, pragmatically absorb the venture’s utility while maintaining formal prohibitions against its structure. Our findings reveal how this interplay produces a hybrid order where ‘productive’ value creation is structurally dependent on institutional subversion. This study contributes to institutional theory by demonstrating how the reciprocal capture of administrative tools and economic functions sustains the resilience of the very regime designed to suppress them.

Unveiling the Mechanisms and Necessary Conditions Underlying the Emergence of Power Law Distribution

ENT
Bisan Abdulkader (LUISS Guido Carli University); Christian Lechner; Anna Mina (LUMSA University); Christian Linder
We theorize how peripheral entrepreneurs become field-defining outliers by triggering feedback processes that yield heavy-tailed, i.e., power-law distributed outcomes. Two temporal pathways operate. The evolutionary “many-to-few” path (from outsiders to outliers) starts with broad variation; audience filtering and amplification gradually concentrate success in a few ventures. The leadership “one-to-many” path (from outliers to outsiders) emerges when a pioneer triggers cascades that reset evaluative criteria and rapidly expand network attachment. Through agent-based modelling, we advance a double-sided governance view of category emergence: early endorsers act as de facto rule-makers who codify participation thresholds, structuring subsequent selection environments and raising the odds that an outlier crystallizes. Our research explains how agency links micro action to field-level concentration via four mechanisms, attention-allocation cascades, legitimacy construction, resource-mobilization cascades, and imitation–innovation cycles, that create reinforcing loops moving ventures from peripheral experimentation to central recognition. Our framework explains power-law outcomes and informs research on category emergence, attention and legitimacy, and governance of nascent fields.
