Session overview

Self-disclosure is increasingly prevalent at work, yet its consequences remain mixed and poorly understood without attention to context. Building on the enthusiastic engagement generated by the past three editions of this symposium, this fourth consecutive edition advances research on self-disclosure by examining how contextual factors shape when, how, and with what consequences individuals share or conceal personal information across organizational settings. Drawing on five papers that span multiple levels of analysis and methodologies, the symposium examines self-disclosure among CEOs in peer advisory groups, supervisors and employees in boundary-blurring work relationships, employees managing concealable stigmatized identities, leaders disclosing personal flaws, and leaders sharing errors. Together, the papers highlight how relational conditions (e.g., trust, psychological safety), structural features (e.g., hierarchy, firm size, career stage), and identity-related constraints influence disclosure decisions and their outcomes. Findings show that self-disclosure can foster authenticity, trust, belonging, and learning under supportive conditions, but may also elicit guardedness, perceived intrusiveness, or strain when contextual cues signal risk. By integrating diverse perspectives on disclosure content, actors, and contexts, this symposium offers a multilevel and methodologically diverse account of self-disclosure at work and identifies key directions for future research on its relational, psychological, and career implications.
