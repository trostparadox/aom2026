When Passion Helps and Hurts: A Dual-Process Model of Obsessive Passion and Performance

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 4:00PM–5:00PM
Pennsylvania Convention Center — Poster Wall 1

Author: Katelyn Taylor Onkst University of Oklahoma

Session overview

Organizations often praise intense dedication, assuming such passion naturally translates into superior performance. Yet research on obsessive passion (OP), a rigid, non-volitional form of engagement, shows inconsistent links to work outcomes, yielding positive, negative, and null effects. Rather than reflecting theoretical noise, this inconsistency signals a blind spot: OP research often relies on cross-sectional or short-window designs, limiting insight into how OP processes unfold over time. Drawing on the Dualistic Model of Passion (DMP) and Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, I propose a multistage, dual-path model explaining how OP simultaneously initiates performance-enhancing and performance-depleting processes that unfold at different temporal rates. Specifically, OP initially sustains high effort through persistent goal striving, while concurrently activating rigid persistence that suppresses recovery and boundary management, leading to failed resource recovery. Over repeated cycles of engagement, these processes cumulatively deplete psychological resources, producing emotional exhaustion and delayed declines in work performance. At the interpersonal stage, I theorize that supervisor passion valuation – conceptualized through supervisors’ passion orientations – biases how objective performance information is attended to, weighted, and translated into subjective performance evaluations, creating what I term the “Passionate Pygmalion Effect.” Finally, I argue that career adaptability moderates the progression from rigid persistence to failed recovery, slowing the accumulation of resource loss rather than eliminating effort. By foregrounding temporal asymmetry, self-regulatory erosion, and evaluative bias, this framework resolves contradictions in OP research, extends COR theory by specifying how passion undermines recovery rather than merely increasing demand, and integrates a novel social-cognitive mechanism into performance assessment theory.
