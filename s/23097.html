Cannot Take Credit: The Stereotype Penalty in Human-AI Collaboration

HR
bowei hou (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); Hai-Jiang Wang (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); Guanglei Zhang
While existing research broadly shows that AI can significantly enhance employee performance, it remains unclear whether this performance improvement translates into fair organizational rewards (e.g., bonuses, promotions). Drawing on attribution theory and the stereotype perspective, we develop and test a theoretical model of performance reward in Human-AI collaboration. Across two experimental studies, we found a “stereotype penalty effect”: managers recommend significantly fewer rewards for low prior performers compared to high prior performers when Human-AI collaboration performance level is identical. Moreover, even when low prior performers surpass high prior performers due to collaboration with AI, they still receive lower reward recommendations. Further analysis indicates that internal attribution (attributing outcomes to the human) serves as a mediator: high human-AI collaboration performance decreases reward recommendations for employees with low previous performance by reducing the performance attribution to the human.

Employee Taking Charge in AI-Driven HRM - A Social Exchange Perspective

HR
Rahman Khan (Paris School of Business); Kashif Ullah Khan (Wenzhou-Kean University); Athar Hameed (Westminster International University in Tashkent); Jeoung Yul Lee (emlyon business school); Ghulam Murtaza (KEDGE Business School); Muhammad Zeshan
Drawing on the social exchange perspective (Blau, 1964), we propose that AI-driven HR practices reduce psychological ownership, which, in turn, lowers the tendency to take charge. However, organizations that remain transparent in their processes are less likely to reduce employees' psychological ownership, thereby overcoming the challenge of low taking charge at work. This research employs a mixed-methods approach, utilizing three studies with vignette experiments and time-lagged surveys to test the hypothesized model. The findings of a vignette study (Study 1) conducted with 142 employees in China confirmed that individuals in a high AI-driven HRM condition are significantly less likely to take charge than those in a low AI-driven HRM condition. Additionally, results from the 2x2 vignette-based study (Study 2), which included 190 employees, showed a significant interaction between organizational transparency and AI-driven HRM in predicting psychological ownership. Specifically, individuals in the high AI-driven HRM-high organizational transparency condition had significantly higher psychological ownership than those in the high AI-driven HRM-low organizational transparency condition. Our two-wave survey of 377 Chinese employees (Study 3) confirmed that AI-driven HRM is negatively related to employee taking charge at work. Furthermore, employee psychological ownership towards organizations significantly mediates the relationship between AI-driven HRM and taking charge at work. Also, it was found that organizational transparency significantly buffers the negative impact of AI-driven HRM on psychological ownership, which, in turn, leads to higher levels of taking charge at work.

The Myth of Neutrality in AI-Base Talent Selection

HR
Ricardo Herrera Martínez (IPADE Business School); Yvette Mucharraz Y Cano (IPADE Business School); Karla Cuilty (IPADE Business School)
Talent selection mechanisms for preventing discrimination are a legitimate concern for candidates, recruiters, and line managers. Machine learning processes underlying artificial intelligence often appear to mimic human behavior. We inquired about the replication of documented gender bias in talent selection. Our contribution provides a comparative analysis method among large language models (LLMs). We employed an experimental design to test LLMs’ responses when selecting the most suitable candidate for a C-level position. We considered the résumés of six candidates (three women and three men) created prior to the widespread use of artificial intelligence to prevent AI-assisted résumé construction. We defined a job profile and a prompt, and asked fourteen LLMs to assess the candidates. To identify gender bias, we compared outcomes across 50 iterations under two conditions: (a) résumés containing gendered names (modified for anonymity) and pronouns, and (b) résumés where gender could not be identified. We aggregated the outcomes generated by the LLMs, calculated the corresponding means, and conducted Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Levene’s, and paired Student’s t tests, yielding statistically significant results. The findings suggest that LLMs may reproduce human gender bias, underscoring the need for caution when using AI-driven selection tools in human resource management processes.

AI-Mediated Externalization: AI as a Cognitive Partner for Facilitating Tacit Knowledge Transfer

HR
Shinhee Park (University of Southern Mississippi); Sachini Edirisinghe (University of Southern Mississippi); Vishal Arghode (University of Southern Mississippi)
This study investigates the critical role of AI-mediated mentoring in mitigating the loss of know-how that occurs when experts retire. Given that tacit knowledge is intrinsically personal and intuitive, it remains challenging to articulate and frequently departs with the individual, thereby posing a significant organizational risk. Moving beyond conventional mentoring frameworks that predominantly emphasize social and relational dimensions, this study reconceptualizes AI as a cognitive partner and introduces the concept of AI-mediated externalization. Through active support such as reflective prompting, pattern recognition, and narrative mapping, AI facilitates a cognitive activation phase during which mentors externalize their implicit mental models. This process converts private intuition into semi-explicit representations, including structured documentation and reusable knowledge artifacts, thereby enhancing the precision and transparency of knowledge transfer processes. As a result, organizations are able to expedite learning cycles, mitigate knowledge attrition, and cultivate a recursive dynamic of innovation and adaptability. Notwithstanding these benefits, the study delineates distinct cognitive and ethical limitations. AI currently lacks emotional depth, cultural sensitivity, and intuitive capacity necessary for profound, sustained coaching relationships, and overreliance on technological mediation may result in a decline in human cognitive engagement. Furthermore, the disclosure paradox suggests that mentee trust is contingent upon the manner in which AI participation is disclosed. Nonetheless, this research establishes essential theoretical and practical groundwork for the fields of Knowledge Management (KM) and Human Resource Development (HRD) by illustrating how collaborative human-AI interactions can safeguard critical organizational knowledge. Keywords: AI-mediated externalization; tacit knowledge; cognitive partner

Does AI Adoption Reduce Overtime? Empirical Evidence from Annual Reports and Satellite Data in China

HR
Anqi Hu (Renmin University of China); xueyan li (Renmin University of China)
As a core driver of the new technological revolution, artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping firms’ labor organization and employment patterns. Yet, whether AI adoption mitigates excessive overtime remains insufficiently explored. Drawing on data from Chinese A-share listed firms (2012-2019), this study develops an innovative measure of AI adoption using annual report text analysis and employs satellite nighttime light data to proxy overtime intensity. Using fixed effects, instrumental variable, and propensity score matching models, we find that AI adoption significantly reduces firms’ overtime intensity. The effect operates mainly through optimizing human capital structures by reducing low-skill employment and increasing demand for high-skill labor. The moderating analyses show that this effect is stronger among small and medium-sized enterprises and remains robust across industries and regions with varying technological and regulatory contexts. These findings suggest that AI not only enhances productivity but also alleviates labor intensity by upgrading firms’ human capital composition, offering new insights for labor management and policy research.
