When Digital Talent Drags Productivity: Geopolitical Risk in Japanese Overseas Subsidiaries

STR
Lok Ting Chan (National Taiwan University); YUTAO BIAN (National Taiwan University); Cheng-Wei Wu (National Taiwan University)
We examine why an increase in digital talent sometimes hampers rather than enhances subsidiary productivity under geopolitical risk. Building on institutional theory and the multipolar geo-strategy perspective, we conceptualize such risk as an enduring institutional condition that structures legitimacy rather than a transient external shock. We integrate Revelio’s digital human-capital analytics with Toyo Keizai to construct a 6,735 subsidiary-year panel of Japanese MNEs across 30 host countries from 1989 to 2022. Panel results show that digital-talent investment is followed by productivity declines, with the negative effect intensified under higher host-country geopolitical risk and stronger host–regional convergence. These findings demonstrate that digital transformation is not solely a capability-building undertaking but a legitimacy-mediated process in which institutional frictions suppress the realization of performance gains from new digital competencies. Our study further advances this conversation by arguing subsidiaries as interpretive intermediaries that must reconcile the competing demands of geopolitical risk and technological advancement, illuminating why digital talent can generate efficiency losses when legitimacy criteria diverge across institutional arenas. We therefore propose a legitimacy–efficiency paradox and introduce a dual-legitimacy mechanism linking headquarters and host-country evaluative systems, reframing multinational adaptation as the alignment of organizational practices with multiple institutional logics in an increasingly politicized, multipolar world.

Do Foreign Firms Erode Domestic Firms' Value Capture? The Double-Edged Role of Business Groups

STR
Gaurav G B (Indian Institute of Management Lucknow); Lakshmi Goyal (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Jihye Yeo (NEOMA Business School); Alexander Eapen (The Australian National University); Rejie George (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
A central question in strategy research is how firms create and capture value in ongoing competition cycles. Among the many shocks that trigger such cycles, the entry of foreign firms is a consequential one for firms in emerging markets. We investigate how foreign competition shapes domestic firms’ markups, and specifically, how business group affiliation moderates this. Studying Indian manufacturing firms from 2001 to 2019, we find that business group affiliates experience greater markup erosion than standalone firms when facing foreign competition. Group resources enable aggressive strategic responses -- capacity expansion, product launches, and pricing strategies -- that paradoxically amplify value capture losses. However, within-group heterogeneity matters: larger groups face steeper markup declines, while R&D-focused groups defend margins relatively better through innovation. Therefore, business group affiliation represents a double-edged sword in competitive dynamics.

Theoretical Logics of Diversification and Firm Performance Distribution

STR
Po-Yang Cheng (The Ohio State University); Michael J. Leiblein (The Ohio State University)
Research on the relationship between diversification and corporate risk has produced persistently mixed findings. We argue that these inconsistencies reflect an overlooked fact. Firms do not manage diversification following a single, uniform logic as assumed in many prior studies. Rather, they follow a mix of multiple logics. We distinguish between two competing theoretical logics—portfolio diversification logic and real options logic—portfolio diversification logic emphasizes passive risk pooling and predicts variance reduction, whereas real options logic emphasizes managerial flexibility and active resource redeployment. This paper argues that managers applying these theories will differentially invest in diversification and realize markedly different outcome distributions. Our results show that, relative to firms purely employing portfolio diversification logic, firms governing diversification by real options logic report higher corporate risk and greater upside potential as well as greater downside risk. By demonstrating that diversification shapes the performance distribution differently depending on a firm’s dominant theoretical logic, this study reconciles mixed findings and highlights how misaligned assumptions about managerial intent contribute to confusion about diversification outcomes.

Corporate Diversification and Consumer Boycotts

STR
Young Hou; Jiaxin (Richard) Li (UCLA Anderson School of Management); Christopher Poliquin (UCLA Anderson School of Management)
Consumers increasingly boycott firms for sociopolitical issues. When the targeted firm is diversified, consumer boycotts may remain concentrated on the focal product or spill over to the broader portfolio. Leveraging the Bud Light boycott, we study portfolio spillovers and link insights from corporate diversification to corporate sociopolitical involvement research. Using a structural demand framework, we estimate a large decline in willingness-to-pay for Bud Light and heterogeneous spillovers across other products in AB InBev's portfolio post-boycott. Counterfactual simulations indicate that losses would've been larger under a stylized umbrella-branding exposure, suggesting that individual branding may help localize backlash and reduce portfolio-wide damage.

Impact of Industry Dynamism and Munificence on Industry Structure

STR
Pallav Bose (KJ Somaiya Institute of Management Mumbai); Swarup Kumar Dutta (Indian Institute of Management Ranchi); Faisal Mohammad Ahsan
This study examines how industry dynamism and munificence shape concentration and competitive structure in emerging markets. Using secondary data on 2,163 Indian industry-year observations (2000–2020), we investigate these environmental effects on industry evolution relative to developed-market patterns. Our key finding is a paradox: while Indian industries overall move away from consolidation following liberalization, opposing environmental forces and sectoral characteristics create distinct trajectories. Specifically, industry dynamism drives consolidation by amplifying uncertainty and favoring larger incumbents, while munificence encourages fragmentation by enabling new entry and specialization. However, manufacturing industries resist these environmental pressures, maintaining consolidation despite liberalization and munificence. This sectoral resilience reflects manufacturing's structural characteristics—capital intensity, entry barriers, and clustering effects—that fundamentally limit how environmental conditions shape concentration. Service industries, conversely, respond flexibly to environmental shifts, fragmenting under munificence and consolidating under dynamism. These findings reveal that industry structure is not merely a function of environmental conditions but is deeply rooted in sector-specific economic logics that persist across changing contexts. Our results contribute three insights to strategic management literature: (1) emerging market industry evolution follows distinct patterns diverging from developed economies, (2) environmental effects operate contingently based on sectoral characteristics, and (3) manufacturing and service sectors require fundamentally different strategic approaches in dynamic, resource-rich environments. These insights have critical implications for firms developing competitive strategy in emerging markets and for policymakers designing industrial policy in contexts where sectoral heterogeneity shapes responses to environmental change.
