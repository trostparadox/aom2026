An Emotional-Cognitive Process of Professional Identity Adaptation to Intelligent Technologies

CTO
Sona Gachayeva (University of Stirling); Daria Morozova (Leiden University)
Drawing on a longitudinal, in-depth qualitative study, this study develops a process model of professional identity reconstruction in response to emerging intelligent technological disruption. The findings show that technology-induced identity disturbance triggers intensified identity work unfolding through exploration, reaffirming human centrality and re-anchoring identity standards, normalisation, and identity redefinition, each shaped by interrelated cognitive and emotional mechanisms. Three key insights emerge: first, emerging intelligent technologies generate identity disturbance by challenging the core meanings of professional work, requiring professionals to adapt their identity in response; second, identity reconstruction unfolds through a dynamic emotional-cognitive process, extending predominantly cognitive accounts of responses to identity threats; and third, as AI becomes integrated into work, professionals redefine the foundations of their identity, moving from manual, human-exclusive know-how toward a form of human-AI complementarity in which value is located in human oversight (judgement, emotional understanding, contextual interpretation) and accountability (the non-transferable responsibility for outcomes). The findings position adaptation to intelligent technologies beyond a matter of tool adoption, but as a fundamentally identity-relevant process through which professionals renegotiate who they are and how they matter in an evolving technological landscape.

Large Language Models and the Token-Based View of Work

CTO
Paul Merritt (Cornell University)
Tokens are atomized text units, which are germane to Large Language Models’ (LLMs)’ statistical calculations. Given the widespread use of text in all manner of work-related activities, understanding the subtleties of how LLMs manipulate tokens is critical. To do so, I develop a “token-based view” to theorize how Large Language Models (LLMs) transform work-related activities. The token-based view suggests that LLMs’ processing of tokens is 1) probabilistic (subject to statistical error rates) 2) preference-aligned (reshaped to match users' sociocultural expectations rather than accuracy) and 3) mediated (invisibly transformed by vendor policies and content filters). I then illustrate how the token-based view can shift evaluation processes in labor market settings, where job applicants are generally judged upon three different criteria: human capital (e.g., KSAOs: knowledge, skills, abilities and other characteristics, or education), cultural capital (e.g., group-based behavioral codes or cues), and social capital (e.g., relations or networks). The token-based view suggests human capital and cultural capital are the most vulnerable to LLM-based token manipulation, while social capital processes are somewhat insulated. Other conditions may also influence the mechanisms of the token-based view, including the nature of the task, information availability, prompt compatibility, and LLM settings. The token-based view has theoretical implications for scholarship at the nexus of algorithms and humans, labor market processes, and the future of work.

Digital Monitoring and the Reconfiguration of Control and Expertise in Manufacturing

CTO
Cristian Negrutiu (Bucharest University of Economic Studies); Christian Fieseler (BI Norwegian Business School); Kateryna Reiby (Oslo New University College); Mihail Dumitru Sacala (Bucharest University of Economic Studies); Anca Bogdan (Bucharest University of Economic Studies); Mihaela - Cornelia Dan (Bucharest University of Economic Studies); Miruna Mazurencu Marinescu Pele (Bucharest University of Economic Studies)
Algorithmic monitoring systems promise “real-time transparency” by translating work into metrics, dashboards, and alerts. Yet in practice, intensified visibility rarely produces unambiguous control. It generates interpretive disputes, gaming, and new accountability dilemmas, especially when the same data are used to coordinate operations and evaluate people. We develop a process theory of how organizations make algorithmic visibility actionable and legitimate. Drawing on a qualitative study of a shopfloor monitoring platform deployed across multiple manufacturing plants and its vendor ecosystem, we show that implementation unfolds through four interdependent dynamics: (1) monitoring infrastructures reformat production into shared performance objects that invite ongoing intervention rather than post-mortem explanation; (2) these objects become contested as actors manage attribution, fairness, and surveillance imaginaries, producing strategic opacity inside the transparency regime; (3) organizations stabilize an envelope of acceptable visibility through layered work that combines normative framing, governance constraints, and practice-based calibration and verification; and (4) as platforms extend from monitoring toward AI agents and “virtual engineering,” envelope work intensifies to preserve human accountability while selectively delegating analytic labor to machines. Our model contributes to research on algorithmic management and quantification by reconceptualizing visibility as a duality of transparency and strategic opacity, stabilized through envelope construction. It also advances human–AI collaboration research by specifying how accountability and expertise are reorganized when monitoring systems become agentic, and why “human-in-command” arrangements are not residual preferences but enabling conditions for AI extension in operational settings.

What Makes a Job Stay Around? Occupational Identity of AI-Related Jobs

CTO
Zeyu Chen (Stevens Institute of Technology); Jingyi Sun; Justine Herve (Stevens Institute of Technology); Denghui Zhang (Stevens Institute of Technology)
The rapid diffusion of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has triggered a proliferation of occupations, yet only some solidify into durable categories while others fade. Integrating category viability theory with research on occupational identity and path constitution, we conceptualize viability as a function of coherence (convergence on an interpretable shared bundle of skills and tasks) and two dimensions of distinctiveness: cross-sectional distinctiveness (differentiation from peer occupations) and temporal distinctiveness (updating relative to pre-AI institutional baselines anchored in O*NET 2019). Using large language models (LLMs) to extract skills and tasks from a panel of U.S. job postings (2020-2024), we construct the measures of occupational identity for 145 AI-related clusters, and find that coherence exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with viability: occupations flourish when they are structured enough to be recognizable but flexible enough to adapt. Further, we observe a negative interaction between cross-sectional and temporal distinctiveness. While differentiating from peers and updating against institutional baselines are individually beneficial, increasing both dimensions reduces viability. These findings extend category viability to dynamic occupational categories, introduce temporal distinctiveness as a distinct mechanism through which digital work evolves from institutional baselines, and offer scalable, content-based measures for tracking the evolution of occupations in fast-moving labor markets.

Acceptance Under Constraint: Rethinking Technology Acceptance in AI-Enabled Recruitment

CTO
Te-Yi Lin (Tatung University); Cheng-Wen Yao
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in organizational recruitment processes, yet job seekers typically lack meaningful choice over whether these systems are used. This study examines how technology acceptance operates under such non-volitional conditions, where participation in AI-mediated recruitment is imposed rather than voluntarily adopted. Drawing on and extending the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2 (UTAUT2), we reconceptualize behavioral intention as acceptance under constraint—job seekers’ willingness to continue participating in AI-enabled recruitment despite limited agency. Survey data from 276 job seekers exposed to an AI-based asynchronous video interview system were analyzed using hierarchical regression and equivalence testing. Results show that performance expectancy and habit are the primary drivers of acceptance, reflecting instrumental justification and adaptive normalization. In contrast, effort expectancy, social influence, and hedonic motivation exhibit weak or uncertain effects, while facilitating conditions retain a meaningful positive influence as a baseline-enabling factor. These findings identify important boundary conditions of dominant technology acceptance models and highlight how acceptance processes reconfigure when technology use is imposed. The study advances research on AI and HRM by foregrounding job seekers as constrained users of algorithmic systems and contributes to a more context-sensitive understanding of acceptance in high-stakes, algorithmically mediated employment settings.
