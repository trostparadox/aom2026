Beefing IT Up for Innovation? Dual-Mode Open Source Collaboration and Digital Technology Innovation

TIM
Siqi Wang (Tianjin University); Wenqing Wu (Tianjin University)
Open source collaboration (OSC) has become an increasingly important paradigm through which organizations access external technological resources and interact with distributed developers in open source communities. Although openness is central to contemporary digital innovation strategy, it remains unclear how multidirectional OSC generates innovation benefits. We develop an integrative theoretical framework that links IT affordance theory with the paradox of openness to explain how two distinct modes of OSC, inbound and outbound OSC, affect organizations’ destabilizing and consolidating digital technological innovation (DTI). We further argue that these effects are contingent on temporal and spatial separation in the openness tradeoff, operationalized as technology cycle time (TCT) and industry concentration (IC). Using a panel dataset of Chinese startups’ OSC on GitHub from November 2008 to September 2024, we find that (a) inbound OSC is positively related to consolidating DTI, and outbound OSC is positively related to destabilizing DTI; (b) TCT and IC moderate the relationship between both inbound and outbound OSC and varying destabilizing and consolidating DTI. We contribute to open innovation literature, and expand the acquisition perspective of IT affordance theory. We also advance the understanding of open paradox by uncovering how temporal and spatial separation shape the DTI consequences from OSC.

Capturing Value in Open Innovation Ecosystems: The Mediating Role of Dynamic Capabilities

TIM
John Pritchard (University of Denver); Paul M Olk (University of Denver); Kyle Ehrhardt (University of Colorado-Denver/Anschutz Medical Campus)
Open innovation research generally assumes that all firms can access and create and capture value from open-source knowledge. While value creation has received considerable attention by open innovation researchers, less studied is whether firms capture value from open innovation and if they do, what if any mechansims lead to value capture. We evaluate the value capture process by drawing from a dynamic capabilities perspective and examine whether value capture in an ecosystem requires additional mechanisms beyond access to external knowledge. We test our hypotheses using data on 486 memberships in open innovation projects across multiple industries, comparing those engaged in Linux Foundation open innovation projects with those which are not, and examining their dynamic capabilities. The results support both a direct and a mediated relationship, suggesting that firms benefit from participating in open innovation but to capture greater value, they need to invest in external knowledge acquisition and in internal knowledge transformation.

Do OSS Licenses Change in Response to Declining OSS Project Activity?

TIM
Zhenzhen Chen (Zhejiang University); Lanhua Li (Zhejiang University); Guoqiao Liu (Zhejiang University)
Abstract: Open-source software (OSS) projects have emerged as critical infrastructure for software innovation and production in the digital age, and their long-term development depends on the effective implementation of governance mechanisms. However, how OSS governance mechanisms dynamically adjust throughout a project’s evolution remains insufficiently explored. Previous studies have extensively investigated the initial selection of OSS licenses as core governance mechanisms, yet they predominantly conceptualize these licenses as inherent and immutable institutional arrangements that shape stakeholder behavior, largely neglecting the determinants and decision-making processes underlying OSS license changes. Since OSS projects are sustained by multiple OSS stakeholders with heterogeneous interests, their long-term development hinges on the continuous alignment of governance mechanisms with the changing interests and expectations of their contributors. Drawing on a stakeholder interest alignment perspective, this study conceptualizes fluctuations in project activeness as a key signal of shifting stakeholder interests and examines whether and how such changes trigger project owners’ decisions to modify their licenses. Our findings reveal that declines in project activeness significantly increase the probability of license changes. This relationship is further influenced by whether or not the project is organization-owned and by greater disparity in contributors' platform tenure, while contributors’ contribution disparity shows no significant moderating effect. Our study contributes to the literature on OSS governance by enhancing understanding of the dynamic adjustment of OSS governance mechanisms, examining how evolving stakeholder interests shape governance decisions, and elucidating how project ownership and contributor heterogeneity influence the design and implementation of governance decision processes.

Managing the Dark Side of Open Innovation in Electric Vehicles: Challenges and Mitigation Strategies

TIM
Reeti Agarwal (Jaipuria Institute of Management); rsha ali alghafes (Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University); Asha Thomas; Anushree Luukela-Tandon
Open Innovation (OI) has become central to the growth of the electric vehicle (EV) industry, yet its adoption also exposes firms to hidden challenges. While OI promises accelerated development and broader collaboration, it often generates risks that remain underexplored in existing research. Grounded in systems theory and resource dependence theory, this study draws on qualitative insights from respondents recruited through Prolific Academic to examine how employees perceive the darker side of OI at the micro (team), meso (organization), and macro (society) levels. The research identified five critical challenges of OI in the EV sector, such as resource sharing constraints, boundary coordination issues, power asymmetry tensions, innovation pathway ambiguity, and risks of brand dilution. At the micro level, teams struggled with resource bottlenecks and coordination frictions. At the meso level, organizations faced unequal power relations among partners and a lack of clarity in collaborative innovation pathways. At the macro level, systemic risks such as supply chain disruptions, dependency on external actors, and reputational threats emerged. To address these, firms employed a focused set of strategies, including supplier diversification, contingency planning, transparent communication, and stronger contractual safeguards. At the ecosystem level, collaboration with policymakers and public engagement helped mitigate broader systemic risks. The findings highlight how EV firms strike a balance between openness and control, offering practical insights for building resilience and sustaining effective innovation partnerships.

Navigating Network Orchestration for Formal and Informal Open Innovation

TIM
Nicholas Schwarz (RWTH Aachen University)
Research summary: Network orchestration encompasses activities firms undertake to initiate and manage collaborative relationships with external partners in the context of open innovation (OI). Previous research identified network orchestration as enabler for firms to overcome the lack of hierarchical authority over external partners and extract value from OI. However, existing research does not fully reflect the heterogeneous nature of OI and offers a limited understanding of how network orchestration enhances innovation performance for different types of OI, such as formal and informal OI. Drawing on a dataset of 165 German firms, this study investigates how network orchestration affects the relationship between formal respectively informal OI and innovation performance. The study shows that network orchestration enhances the effect of formal OI on innovation performance, but it does not find such an effect for informal OI. Furthermore, it shows that the success of formal OI is enhanced by maintaining stable relationships with external partners and an active approach to managing network structures. In contrast, successful informal OI is characterized by serendipitous exchange with various stakeholders that provide access to diverse resource pools, without the need to actively manage network structures.
