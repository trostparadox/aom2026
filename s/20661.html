Creation or Augmentation? How AI Involvement Shapes Perceived Ownership and Ethical Judgment

Friday, Jul 31 2026 · 10:00AM–11:00AM
Philadelphia Marriott Downtown — Poster Wall 11
OB

People

Authors: Matthew A Lisonbee Cornell University; Hannah Pearl Sher Cornell University; Robert Sternberg Cornell University

Session overview

Abstract: The rapid adoption of generative AI has created normative uncertainty about when AI-assisted outputs still “belong” to a human producer and whether that producer has crossed a moral line. Drawing on psychological ownership theory, we propose a two-dimensional account of AI engagement: Creation, where AI generates the core substance of a product, and Augmentation, where AI refines human-generated work. This is the first study to examine whether different levels of ownership are perceived when AI creates work versus when AI augments it, and whether ownership declines at a discrete “moral cliff”—a clear moral boundary at which ownership collapses. Using a mixed experimental design (N = 311 Cornell students), participants role-played one of three contexts (software developer, marketing professional, or student) and evaluated vignettes depicting graded AI involvement. Within subjects, participants rated ownership across multiple levels of AI Creation (7 scenarios) and Augmentation (6 scenarios) using 7-point scales. Linear mixed-effects models showed ownership declined substantially with increasing AI involvement, more steeply for Creation than Augmentation, with strongest effects in the student context. Model comparisons provided no evidence of a sharp moral cliff; instead, ownership declined smoothly, consistent with a slippery-slope pattern. These findings suggest judgments about the moral acceptability of AI-assisted work depend less on overall AI use than on whether AI substitutes for authorship or augments human effort, with implications for organizational AI governance, academic integrity norms, and responsibility attribution in human–AI collaboration.
