Conceptualizing Platform Enshittification: Definitions, Types, and Dynamics

TIM
Gianluca Centraco (University of Turin); Gabriele Santoro (University of Turin)
Digital platforms are widely celebrated for creating economic, functional, emotional and social value, yet public discourse increasingly points to a darker trajectory in which everything is getting worse. In this article, we conceptualise platform enshittification as a cumulative, path-dependent process through which a platform’s business model and governance progressively and deliberately redistribute value across sides in ways that favour the platform and selected insiders, while systematically degrading service quality, fairness and/or trust for other stakeholders. Adopting a retroductive, theory-building approach grounded in illustrative cases of widely used platforms, we integrate insights from stakeholder theory, economic theories of switching costs and network effects, research on platforms and digital ecosystems, and work on the attention economy and dark patterns. We advance three contributions. First, we introduce enshittification as a distinct construct that specifies when and how platform evolution crosses a normative threshold from value creation to value extraction. Second, we identify four ideal-typical forms of enshittification—price–value distortion under lock-in, experience and attention degradation, governance and fairness erosion, and data and privacy exploitation—each tied to different resources and business model logics. Third, we theorise a three-phase dynamic from attraction to optimisation to extraction, showing how structural power, investor pressures and design choices jointly enable late-stage enshittification. We conclude by outlining implications for platform governance, regulation and future research on the dark trajectories of digital business models.

From Memes to Innovation: Conditional Effects on User Innovative Behavior

TIM
Hsiao-Han Tao (National Chengchi University); Kuo-Feng Huang (National Chengchi University); Chunhsien Wang (National Chiayi University)
Online communities have become important sources of user innovation, yet we still know relatively little about how digital interaction translates into individual-level innovative behavior. Drawing on social cognitive theory, this study examines how internet meme, which a prevalent form of digitally embedded engagement influences user innovative behavior through inspiration, and how this process is conditioned by political ideology. We conceptualize internet meme as users’ involvement with symbolic digital content. Using survey data from online community members and a regression-based moderated mediation approach, we find that internet meme positively affects user innovative behavior indirectly through inspiration. Moreover, political ideology systematically moderates this psychological transformation process: the inspirational and innovative effects of internet meme are stronger among users with more liberal ideological orientations and become significantly weaker as ideological orientations grow more conservative. By identifying internet meme as a novel antecedent of user innovation, uncovering inspiration as a key micro-level mechanism, and demonstrating ideological heterogeneity in innovation outcomes, this study advances technology and innovation management research on the micro-foundations of user innovation.

When Superstars Discourage the Crowd: Reassessing the Superstar Effect in Innovation Contests

TIM
Zhongzhi Liu (Zhejiang Gongshang University); Zhao Liu (Hunan University); Xuejin Yu (Hunan University)
Innovation contests increasingly rely on a small number of highly successful participants – often referred to as superstars – who dominate rankings and attract attention. Prior research has produced mixed findings regarding whether superstar participation motivates or discourages other competitors, largely focusing on average effects. We argue that such average effects obscure substantial heterogeneity in how non-superstar solvers respond to superstar participation. Drawing on expectancy theory, goal-setting theory, and social comparison theory, we propose that, on average, superstar presence tends to discourage non-star solvers by reducing perceived attainability of success, but that this effect varies systematically with solver skill, experience, and task complexity. Using data from a large-scale programming-contest platform, we test these arguments through solver-level analyses. Econometric results show a negative average association between superstar presence and non-star solution quality, while moderation analyses reveal that this discouragement is weaker for higher-skill solvers and stronger for more experienced solvers and more complex tasks. To further explore response heterogeneity, we supplement the analysis with a machine-learning approach that reveals substantial dispersion in individuals' reactions to superstar participation. Together, these findings reconceptualize the superstar effect as a heterogeneous motivational mechanism rather than a uniform outcome driver and offer implications for the design and governance of innovation contests.

Who Contributes the Most? How Specialization and Knowledge Similarity Shape Online Collaboration

TIM
Robert Janjic (University of Zurich)
The production and development of knowledge is increasingly carried out by large, digitally enabled groups of volunteers. Given the flexible nature of participation in most of these communities, an important question that arises is which members will be the most likely to provide substantial contributions. Scholars have argued that both an individual’s level of specialization as well as their knowledge overlaps with other collaborators are important determinants of the ability to meaningfully contribute to a project. While prior research studied those dimensions only in isolation, the present study explores how both jointly affect participants’ productivity. Based on data on content additions to article pages on Wikipedia, the empirical analysis reveals an important interdependency between specialization and similarity: only when editors have experience in distant knowledge domains will a narrow focus enable larger contributions. Interestingly, however, this relationship is itself contingent on the informational resources of the entire group. When teams are diverse, specialists, on average, add more content to a page, regardless of how similar they are to other members. These findings have important implications for existing theory and highlight the multidimensional nature of collaboration in online spaces.
