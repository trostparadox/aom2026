Governing Social Sustainability in Built Asset Management: A BIM-Enabled Management Framework

SIM
Yujie Dong (The University of Manchester); Amir Rahbarimanesh (The University of Manchester); Saeed reza Mohandes (The University of Manchester); Richard Kirkham (The University of Manchester)
Social sustainability has emerged as a central concern in management scholarship and practice, particularly in asset-intensive sectors characterised by long lifecycles, public accountability, and diverse stakeholder demands. Yet within built asset management, social sustainability is often addressed in fragmented and largely qualitative ways, limiting its integration into managerial decision-making and governance processes. This study develops a management-oriented conceptual framework for governing social sustainability in built asset management. Drawing on an integrative synthesis of the sustainability performance measurement, asset management, and digitalisation literatures, the framework systematically links social sustainability metrics to strategic asset management goals, while conceptualising Building Information Modelling (BIM) as an enabling information infrastructure that supports assessment, integration, and communication rather than as a prescriptive technological solution. To enhance credibility and practical relevance, the framework is supported by an illustrative pilot study involving senior academic and industry experts in BIM, asset management, and social sustainability. The pilot provides exploratory evidence on the clarity, relevance, and prioritisation of the proposed social sustainability metrics, and informs targeted framework refinement. Rather than advancing a hypothesis-testing model, this study contributes to the Social Issues in Management literature by reframing social sustainability as an operational and governable performance dimension within long-term asset management, and by demonstrating how digital information capabilities can support responsible, transparent, and stakeholder-oriented managerial decision-making under conditions of complexity and uncertainty.

When Helping Turns into Time Theft: Exploring Employees’ UPOB, Work Entitlement and Meaningfulness

SIM
Huei-Ying Chen; Yu-Yu Chang (National Cheng Kung University); Salsabila Salsabila (National Cheng Kung University)
Unethical pro-organizational behavior (UPOB) acts intended to advance organizational interests through ethically questionable means have been extensively examined in terms of their antecedents, yet their psychological and behavioral consequences for actors remain underexplored. Integrating social exchange theory and equity theory, we theorize that engaging in UPOB may lead employees to experience heightened work entitlement, a context-dependent belief that they are owed special treatment due to their discretionary sacrifices. This work entitlement, in turn, predicts time theft as a covert means of restoring perceived equity. We further propose that work meaningfulness attenuates this relationship by reducing the motivational appeal of entitlement-driven deviance. Two complementary studies, a four-wave experience sampling field study (N = 547) and a scenario-based experiment (N = 485), offer convergent support for our model. By identifying entitlement as a dynamic mechanism linking UPOB to workplace deviance and highlighting the buffering role of work meaningfulness, this research extends actor-centric perspectives on organizational ethics.

When Good Money Meets Market Logic: How Grants and Venture Capital Shape NTDs Drug Development

SIM
Yurun Tian (Tsinghua University); Jiehao Zhang (Tsinghua University)
Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) are a class of illnesses that disproportionately affect impoverished populations and continue to pose significant challenges for global public health. Because patients who rely on NTDs treatments generally lack purchasing power, private investment in NTDs drug development has remained limited. As a result, funding from government agencies and philanthropic foundations has become the primary source of financial support for NTDs research and development, most commonly in the form of grants. Despite substantial amount in grant funding targeted at NTDs, however, progress in the development of new NTDs drugs remains modest. This study investigates the organizational mechanisms underlying this paradox by examining U.S. biopharmaceutical startups using data from PharmaProject and Crunchbase. We find that although grants facilitate the development of NTDs drugs, they can also attract venture capital (VC) investment by signaling the quality and potential of the recipient firm. However, the entry of VCs may shift the firm’s focus toward non-NTDs projects, as their financial return orientation and equity-based governance rights give them greater influence than non-equity-based grants. Our findings highlight the unintended consequences of well-intentioned funding and underscore the importance of aligning ownership structures with mission-driven objectives. By showing how the interaction between non-profit and for-profit funders shapes firms’ innovation trajectories, this study contributes to the literature on grand challenges in domains that are socially important yet commercially neglected.

The Public Strikes Back: How Ethical Sentiment Influences the Financial Consequences of Drug Pricing

SIM
Antonio Garzon-Vico; Krithika Komalapati; Arsalan Shahid (University College Dublin); Jan A. Rosier (University College Dublin)
This study investigates whether profit-maximizing actions perceived as ethically questionable—specifically, increasing drug prices—ultimately benefit or harm firms. We focus on public sentiment as a mediating mechanism linking these actions to financial performance, using data from both news media and social media platforms. Applying advanced sentiment analysis alongside key financial indicators (ROA, Tobin’s Q, and future stock returns), we find that the impact of negative public sentiment depends both on its source and on the timespan considered. In the short term, negative sentiment expressed in business, finance, and legal outlets—as well as on social media—is associated with improved firm performance, suggesting that some stakeholders may interpret aggressive pricing as a sign of market strength. By contrast, negative sentiment from political and scientific news outlets is correlated with declines in performance, pointing to the influence of stakeholder identity and perceived legitimacy. Over the longer term, firms facing less negative sentiment show higher abnormal stock returns, indicating that the financial consequences of ethically contentious pricing practices may turn more negative over time. Our findings contribute to the “does good ethics pay?” debate by indicating that the financial effects of questionable corporate behaviour are shaped by who expresses the ethical sentiment and the timespan.

More Women, More Power? Rethinking Emancipation in Collective Enterprises?

SIM
Asma Zafar (Brock University); Maria Paola Ometto (California State University-San Marcos)
Inspired by the increased interest in how entrepreneurship affects society, the goal of this paper is to shed light on an unanswered query at the nexus of research on collective forms of enterprise and that on entrepreneurship’s emancipatory potential: (When) does women’s participation in communal entrepreneurial activity enable their liberation from restrictive gender norms? We address this question by grounding our theoretical and empirical work in the country context of Brazil, a nation known for its historical subjugation of women and its currently high incidence of collective enterprise. Drawing upon the organizational demography perspective, we suggest that the relationship between women’s involvement in communal forms of entrepreneurial activity and their sociocultural emancipation is likely to depend upon the degree to which females are represented within certain organizational positions. We test our hypotheses using primary survey data collected on approximately 3,500 Brazilian collective enterprises. Our analysis sheds new light on when women’s participation in communal entrepreneurship reveals that more women in these organizations do not translate into greater emancipation from traditional gender roles, whereas more women coordinators do. We propose and run additional models, discuss the role of embeddedness in patriarchal norms, and propose that, when dis-embeddedness occurs through connections with individuals with distinctive socialization and participation in collective enterprises, women are empowered.
