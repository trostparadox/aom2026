Session overview

Leadership development is ultimately a process of identity construction, yet most programs are designed as if it were merely a matter of skill acquisition. This session examines how communicative practice, employee feedback, executive education experiences, and formative adversity shape leader identity, developmental intentions, and grit. Drawing on identity theory, constructivist qualitative methods, and multi-method longitudinal designs, these papers offer a richer, more human account of what it takes to form leaders and not just train them.

Constructive Synthesis: A Communication-Centered Pedagogical Framework for Leadership Development

MED
Laura Nicole Miller (Assumption College); Michael Lewis (Assumption College)
Despite significant investment in leadership development programs in higher education, a persistent knowing–doing gap remains: students understand leadership in theory but struggle to enact it professionally. This conceptual paper introduces constructive synthesis, a pedagogical framework that reframes leadership development as a communicative process requiring audience awareness, personal investment, and identity rehearsal. Constructive synthesis builds on and extends experiential, case, constructivist, and transformative learning approaches by embedding leadership development in high-stakes, public-facing tasks that require students to communicate insight with real consequences. This paper contributes to the management learning and education (MLE) literature by proposing a replicable framework for advancing leadership development pedagogy. We argue that public-facing, consequential communication should be treated not as a supplemental activity but as a core feature of leadership development. We offer a case study of a master’s-level capstone course through which constructive synthesis emerged. Finally, we present this framework as a replicable, high-impact approach to fostering communicative credibility, leadership identity, and real-world readiness across the leadership development curriculum.

Bridging Action and Reflection: How Professional Identity Develops in Executive Education

MED
Pasi Aaltola (University of Jyväskylä); Eric H. Kessler (Pace University); Ari Manninen (University of Jyväskylä)
This paper examines how executive education shapes the professional identities of experienced leaders. Drawing on qualitative analysis of 57 reflective learning evaluation essays written by Executive MBA participants, we examine how executives make sense of their learning. Building on identity-based perspectives on leadership development and the concept of outsight, the study empirically identifies three interrelated process stages through which executive learning unfolds: exposure to new perspectives, experimentation with new ways of acting, and sensemaking that integrates these experiences into an evolving leadership identity. The findings show how executive learning is grounded in action and reflection within participants’ ongoing leadership practice, rather than in role transitions. By extending outsight-based identity development to later career stages, the study contributes to research on leadership development and professional identity as well as to the practice of executive education.

One Step Back and Two Steps Forward: The Impact of Leadership Development on Leader Identity

MED
Andromachi Athanasopoulou (Queen Mary University of London); Amanda Woods Herron (The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center); Jo Alanis (Rice University); Eric Middleton (The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center); Katelyn J. Cavanaugh (The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center); Courtney L Holladay (The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center)
Drawing on leader development and identity theories, we examine how leadership development programs and formative leadership experiences shape leaders’ self-views—specifically self-awareness, leader developmental efficacy, and leader identity. Using a multi-method design in a large U.S. healthcare organization, we combine longitudinal survey data from 122 leaders with in-depth interviews from a subset of 51 participants. Quantitative findings show that leadership development participation reliably increases self-awareness while leader developmental efficacy remains unchanged. Qualitative analysis reveals that leader identity formation is a negotiated social process shaped by others’ perceptions, validation, and contextual pressures, as well as leaders’ own identity claims. We identify multiple forms of leader identity shifts—personal and interpersonal development shifts, leadership potential signaling, and identity regression—and distinguish between direct identity shifts driven by the environment and indirect shifts arising from leaders’ efforts to shape others. Our findings challenge the view of leader self-views as predominantly intrapersonal and refine leader developmental efficacy theory by showing that developmental readiness unfolds over time. This study advances theory on leader identity enactment and offers practical insights into why leadership development may initially unsettle, rather than immediately strengthen, leaders’ sense of self.

When Does Developmental Feedback from Employees Mobilize or Reduce Leader Development Intentions? (WITHDRAWN)

MED
Pisitta Vongswasdi; Bart Dietz
Leadership development is inherently voluntary, making the intention of leaders to develop themselves key. Thus, leadership development effectiveness depends importantly on which leaders have this intention. We argue that employee-feedback towards leaders is instrumental in driving these intentions. In this study, using a 6-country, 3-wave survey amongst 70 leaders and 490 of their employees, we show that receiving negative developmental feedback from employees (i.e., follower ratings lower than self-ratings) leads to lower development intentions for leaders. We also demonstrate that this effect is moderated by leader core self-evaluation: When leaders have high core self-evaluations (CSE), negative developmental feedback from employees enhances their development intentions. Yet, for leaders with low CSE, negative developmental feedback from employees reduces their development intentions. Apparently, depending on CSE, leaders that most need it may be the ones least mobilized to improve. We discuss practical implications and offer suggestions for further research.

Divergent Paths from the Same Home: Social Identity Processes and Grit Development Among Siblings

MED
Andrea Shellenberger (Case Western Reserve University)
This qualitative study explored how and to what extent social identity groups influence the development of grit in individuals with siblings. The problem of practice addressed was the limited understanding of how belonging and identity formation outside the family, particularly among those who experience early adversity, shape perseverance and long-term resilience. Using a constructivist qualitative approach and the Gioia methodology, data were analyzed to identify patterns in participants’ understanding and response to trauma, belonging, and identity. Four aggregate dimensions emerged, including Social Identity Groups as Sources of Belonging, Trauma Adversity and Meaning-Making, Reframing Responsibility and Resilience, and Divergent Sibling Outcomes. Findings suggest that participation in meaningful social groups, such as faith communities, educational institutions, mentorship networks, and peer teams, can re-anchor identity, restore agency, and strengthen grit development despite early instability. The study contributes to theory by bridging social identity theory (Ashforth & Mael, 1989) with grit research (Duckworth, 2016) and offers practical implications for educators, mentors, and community leaders seeking to build resilience through belonging-based interventions.
