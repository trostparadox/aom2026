Platform Identity Formation: Escaping the Incumbent Identity Trap

STR
Paavo Ritala (LUT University); David Sjödin; Marin Jovanovic (Copenhagen Business School); Vinit Parida
Industrial incumbents in B2B markets increasingly launch digital platforms to extend their reach and orchestrate ecosystems. Yet many struggle to build a distinctive platform identity because the platform venture is interpreted through, and constrained by, an entrenched product-based organizational identity and legacy technology architecture. Drawing on the mirroring hypothesis and research on identity-challenging technologies, we examine how incumbents escape this “incumbent identity trap” while preserving the reliability of the legacy core. We build on a longitudinal single-case study of a global maritime technology incumbent (Shipcorp) and its digital subsidiary (Digicorp), tracing the platform initiative over eight years through 65 interviews, observations, and extensive archival data. We identify a three-phase process of platform identity formation: incumbent identity dominance, platform identity dissonance, and platform identity distinctiveness. Our findings show that identity formation hinges on resolving a “broken mirror” condition—misalignment between an emerging platform organization and a legacy-mirrored organization–architecture configuration. The incumbent first undertakes organizational separation by creating a digital unit, advancing an open ecosystem framing but also generating organizational entanglement and identity ambivalence among customers and partners. A second shift—architectural separation—re-grounds the platform in a modular infrastructure and associated commercialization routines that support an ecosystem-orchestrator framing, stabilize a distinct ecosystem-orchestrator identity, and enable scalable ecosystem integration. We contribute to organizational identity and platform strategy by theorizing strategic separation as a sequencing mechanism through which incumbents resolve the broken mirror problem and achieve optimal distinctiveness in platform identity formation.

Firm Agency, Network Effects and Platform Competitive Dynamics

STR
Georgios Papachristos (Northeastern University); Fernando Suarez (Northeastern University)
Today’s economy is increasingly characterized by the proliferation of platforms, where the value to users can grow exponentially due primarily to direct and indirect network effects, which explains the size and industry dominance of many platforms. In theory, late movers in platform industries should face unfavourable odds, but anecdotal evidence from many industries suggests this is often not the case. To enhance our understanding of competitive dynamics in platform industries, this paper explores the strategies and conditions under which a second mover in a platform industry is likely to succeed. We focus on two of the most important strategic decisions for a follower: how early or late to enter, and how much to differentiate its platform from early entrants, particularly when this differentiation strengthens the firm’s network effects. In this paper, we develop a simulation model and use it to explore the relationship between the performance of a later mover to a platform market and its investments in technology and in creating firm-specific network effects. Our results show that in environments of weak industry-wide network effects, a second mover can afford to enter one to five years after the first mover and still manage to ultimately dominate the market, with one of these two strategies: secure a large advantage in platform technology performance over the first entrant, or achieve, through innovative features, firm specific network effects of significantly higher strength than the first mover. Our results provide useful guidelines for scholars and managers as to when – and how – a fast second or late more strategy may work in platform industries.

Optimal Distinctiveness in Open-source LLM Ecosystem: Competitive versus Complementary Benchmarks

STR
Jianping Lu (Dalian University of Technology); Rongkang Ma (Dalian University of Technology); Chen Jingrong
How do firms achieve optimal distinctiveness when facing multiple benchmarks? This research develops a dual-benchmark framework to examine how ecosystem complementors strategically position their products when facing competitive benchmarks versus complementary benchmarks. Drawing on optimal distinctiveness theory and ecosystem literature, we examine the heterogenous effects of strategic distinctiveness from competitive versus complementary benchmarks on product performance and the moderating role of complementary benchmark dominance. Analyzing a unique dataset of 29,486 open-source large language models from 2022 to 2025, we find that competitive positioning (distinctiveness from category prototype) exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with product performance, while complementarity positioning (distinctiveness from sourced base model) demonstrates a U-shaped relationship with product performance. Moreover, when complementary benchmark dominance is high, the inverted U-shaped curve for competitive positioning steepens and the U-shaped curve for complementarity positioning flattens. This research extends optimal distinctiveness theory by demonstrating that optimal positioning patterns depend fundamentally on benchmark type and advances understanding of how benchmark characteristics shape positioning outcomes in ecosystems where organizations navigate both competitive and complementary benchmarks simultaneously.

Modeling Long-Term Platform Sustainability: An Owner-Centric Paradox Perspective

STR
Kenan Guler (Baylor University); Zeki Simsek (Clemson University)
Platform ecosystems characteristically embody persistent contradictions that can both enable and threaten long-term sustainability—the ability of platform owners to maintain long-term value creation and participant engagement while balancing competing demands. Existing theory largely treats these contradictions as trade-offs to be solved in explaining why some platforms thrive and others fail. In this paper, we develop a paradox perspective that explains platform sustainability as a function of how platform owners proactively embrace paradoxes—contradictory but interrelated elements that exist simultaneously and persist over time—and dynamically orchestrate system-level mechanisms to manage them. The associated model specifies how such paradox-embrace manifests as dynamic orchestration of management mechanisms, and how this orchestration, in turn, impacts platform sustainability. The model also theorizes that several structural and technological features of platform ecosystems moderate these impacts. Our theorizing contributes to the platform literature by moving extant understanding toward a more holistic, dynamic account of platform ecosystem paradoxes. It also enriches paradox theory by showing how paradoxes that span multiple organizational boundaries might be managed by platform owners to sustain these complex architectures, which are yet to be theorized from a paradox perspective.

Platform Brokering and Network Effects Beyond Scale: The Case of Virtual Food Banking Platforms

STR
Fuaad Coovadia (University of Oxford)
Platform strategy leans on a simple recipe: add users to unlock network effects. However, many platforms can’t simply scale their user base because target populations are limited, as in the case of platforms who connect organisations rather than individuals, or when scaling is in tension with the platform’s mission. We argue that platform owners can still produce network effects by brokering the structure and strength of ties among existing users. We define these as relational network effects and distinguish them from size-based network effects. Using an inductive, multiple case study of virtual food bank platforms - which connect food businesses with surplus food for donation to charities - we show how platform owners engage in brokering to facilitate engagement over the donation and collection of surplus food. This study, then, expands scholarship on network effects, how platforms enact brokering, and adds to understanding of this increasingly proliferate, yet understudied, type of platform tackling the dual challenge of reducing food waste and food insecurity.
