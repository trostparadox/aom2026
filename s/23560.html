Be Extreme, Don’t Be Mediocre – How Customer Knowledge Contributes to Supplier Learning

TIM
Xinying Qu (University of Hartford); Xin Deng (The University of Manchester)
Suppliers increasingly rely on customers as sources of knowledge for innovation, yet little is known about how the structure of customer knowledge shapes supplier learning. We challenge the prevailing linear view that either specialization or diversity is uniformly beneficial. Drawing on organizational learning theory, we argue that supplier learning is lowest when customer knowledge is moderately concentrated and highest when customer knowledge is either highly specialized or highly diversified. Using a panel of U.S. publicly listed firms from 2003–2017, we measure supplier learning through patents that cite customers’ patents and assess customer technological concentration using a CPC-based Herfindahl-Hirschman Index. Our results reveal a robust U-shaped relationship between customer technological concentration and supplier learning, which is further amplified by technological similarity and vertical relatedness between suppliers and customers. These findings advance research on interorganizational learning and customer-driven innovation.

Continuous External Knowledge Search and Firm Innovation Performance: A Systems Perspective

TIM
Maarten Cerpentier (ETH Zürich); Grace Liu (ETH Zürich); Martin Wörter (ETH Zürich)
Prior research on firm search strategies for external knowledge has focused on the contemporaneous innovation effects of the breadth and depth of knowledge search in one period, offering limited insight into how continuous, multi-year search strategies matter for innovation. Building on systems theory, we argue that continuous external knowledge search yields heterogeneous outcomes depending on the breadth and depth of search and source stability, i.e., whether the same knowledge source types are being replaced or remain in use over time. We find that firms benefit from using the same set of knowledge source types over time (“stable-source search breadth”), enhancing knowledge access and absorption. However, they benefit from switching over time the intensity with which they use these knowledge source types (“episodic search depth”), mitigating lock-ins while refreshing interpretive lenses. Using panel data from the Swiss Innovation and Digitalization Survey, we find that stable-source search breadth and episodic search depth positively relate to firm innovation performance. Overall, we identify a systems-theoretical mechanism linking multi-year continuous knowledge search to firm innovation outcomes.

Spill In to Spin Out? External Knowledge Usage and Employee Entrepreneurship

TIM
Shirish Sundaresan (Georgia State University); Paige Clayton (Georgia Institute of Technology)
This study explores a novel pathway through which knowledge spillovers from outside a firm’s boundaries can influence the creation of employee spinouts. Using data on innovation in the biotechnology and life sciences industry, we empirically examine how different types of external knowledge spillovers, which we term spill-ins, can shape employee-inventors’ knowledge context and influence the likelihood that they engage in employee entrepreneurship. We differentiate between technological knowledge spill-ins and scientific knowledge spill-ins and find that technological spill-ins are associated with a higher hazard that an inventor leaves to form or join a new technology venture while scientific spill-ins are associated with a lower hazard of inventor spinout. We perform additional mechanisms tests that provide evidence that spill-ins influence employee entrepreneurship through their effects on market knowledge, the value of potential ideas, and the need for complementary resources.

When Outsiders Matter: How Newcomers' Network Ties Influence Breakthrough Innovation

TIM
Hui Yang (Iowa State University); Michael Deane Howard (Iowa State University); Rose Ji Youn Kim (University of Kentucky)
Who brings the boundary-spanning knowledge that enables breakthrough innovation, and when does that knowledge actually contribute to breakthroughs? Drawing on knowledge recombination theory and research on the not-invented-here syndrome, we argue that organizational newcomers— inventors who migrate between firms—are critical carriers of heterogeneous knowledge unavailable through internal search, but that visible markers of their prior network positions can trigger resistance that attenuates their contributions. We distinguish two forms of prior visibility: status-based visibility (star scientist designation and eigenvector centrality), which signals competitive threat and activates identity-protective resistance, and control-based visibility (betweenness centrality), which is associated with behavioral patterns that violate normative expectations about newcomer conduct. Using patent data from the artificial intelligence field (1976–2020), we find that newcomer presence is positively associated with breakthrough innovation, defined as patents in the top 1% of forward citations within their technology category. However, both status-based and control-based visibility negatively moderate this relationship. These findings advance research on inventor mobility by identifying network-based boundary conditions for when external knowledge translates into breakthrough outcomes and contribute to the not-invented-here literature by specifying characteristics of knowledge carriers that activate resistance.
