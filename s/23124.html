The Illusion of Irreparability: People Underestimate the Tendency to Repair Awkward Interactions

CM
Elizabeth Nguyen Trinh; Nadav Klein
Awkward moments are common in both everyday life and the workplace, and prior research has found that people tend to overestimate how awkward certain social interactions will be. However, missing is an understanding of how people respond behaviorally when awkward moments occur, and whether people accurately anticipate these responses. We propose that people not only overestimate awkwardness but also fail to appreciate that the typical response to awkwardness is acting to repair it, a phenomenon we term the illusion of social irreparability. This miscalibration may cause people to avoid valuable social interactions out of fear that awkwardness will persist unaddressed. Across four experiments, we demonstrate that people’s prediction-experience gap is larger following opportunities for reparative responses than during the awkward moment itself. Experiment 1 shows that participants exhibited larger forecasting errors in the feedback task where reparative behaviors occur following a public singing performance than during the singing itself. Experiments 2A and 2B reveal that people underestimate the extent to which both speakers and audience members engage in reparative behaviors following an embarrassing faux pas. Experiment 3 demonstrates that people underestimate their own reparative responses when a stranger awkwardly interrupts their conversation. These findings suggest that beyond overestimating awkwardness, people fail to appreciate the self-regulating nature of social interactions, leading them to avoid interactions that would resolve more smoothly than anticipated.

Origins of Bias in Perceived Social Norms from Social Network Formation (WITHDRAWN) (WITHDRAWN)

CM
Jennifer Dannals (Yale School of Management)
Perceptions of social norms affect individual action but are often biased. The extent of such bias may depend on the manner in which people with different attributes come to be connected in social networks. Here, we study 2160 people in 120 experimentally-generated networks while they play a public goods game and observe others’ behavior. After each decision, people report their perception of the average community behavior (the social norm), and we measure the actual behavior, locally and globally in the network. We find that when networks are structured with high similarity (propensity to connect with others who contribute similar amounts to the public good) and "main-effect” attraction (propensity to connect with others who contribute a lot to the public good) central individuals form significantly more biased perceptions of the social norm of contribution. Norms may be misperceived in communities, especially by individuals in particular locations and for particular attributes.

The Different Psychologies of Mode and Mean in Social Norm Perception

CM
Jinyi Kuang (University of Pennsylvania); Jennifer Dannals (Yale School of Management)
Across literature, descriptive social norms are commonly defined and theorized using one of two statistical descriptions: the most frequent behavior (mode) or the average behavior (mean). While both capture central tendency, we propose that they convey distinct distributional information with meaningful behavioral consequences. Specifically, conveying norms with information about the mode is more associated with a binary conception of behavior and thus leading individuals to infer frequency distributions with a stronger central density. In contrast when individuals receive norm information that describe what people do on average, they imagine frequency distributions with wider dispersion. Across seven experiments and one supplemental study (N = 3,011), we demonstrate this effect across multiple paradigms (Studies 1a-1b) and contextualize the description of distribution mode and mean within other quantifiers such as “majority” (Study 2). We further find evidence for our proposed mechanism of dichotomization (Study 3) and that the difference in perceived distribution elicited by information about theon mean or mode prompts changes in behavioral intention (Study 4) and actual behavior (Study 5). Finally, we demonstrate that laypeople do not appear to capitalize on this effect when asked to craft persuasive nudges (Study 6) and discuss its implications for theory and practice.

Decision-Makers Overestimate the Reputational Costs of Necessary Evils

CM
Michael White (Columbia Business School); Stacia King (Stanford University)
Decision-makers must often choose whether to commit necessary evils (i.e., cause harm to produce a benefit), such as allocating punishments or redistributing resources. Existing work using classic ethical dilemmas (e.g., trolley problems) suggests that people react poorly to necessary evils and condemn those who commit them. However, four preregistered studies (total N = 1,622), using both survey experiments and incentive-compatible games, reveal that targets judge decision-makers who commit a broad range of everyday necessary evils more positively than decision-makers expect. Decision-makers overestimate how much emotional harm targets experience and underestimate how much instrumental value targets recognize. As a result, decision-makers overestimate the reputational costs of performing necessary evils, which can serve as a barrier to performing them. This research highlights the theoretical importance of studying social-moral phenomena (such as instrumental harm) in everyday contexts and provides practical insight into how to encourage decision-makers to perform societally beneficial, but seemingly harmful actions.

When ‘Me’ Needs ‘You’: Other-Focus Licenses the Benefits of Self-Focus in Initial Conversations

CM
Yaoxi Shi (Imperial College London); Hanne Kiri Collins (University of California Los Angeles); Shereen J. Chaudhry (University of Chicago)
First conversations are pivotal for forming impressions, yet individuals often face a paradox: talking about oneself in these conversations can foster liking but also risk appearing self-centered. We propose that this paradox arises because research has typically examined self- and other-focused behaviors in isolation. Across multi-method studies including recalled initial conversations and natural language processing of live get-acquainted conversations, we show that other-focused conversational behavior (i.e., encouraging others to talk or talking about the other person) consistently increases liking and connection intentions, and it moderates the effect of self-focused conversational behavior (i.e., talking about oneself) on these interpersonal outcomes. Specifically, self-focused conversational behavior increases liking when paired with high other-focus, but backfires when other-focus is low. We further theorize that this occurs because self-focus is perceived differently depending on the level of other-focus. By studying the joint effect of self-focused and other-focused conversational behaviors in natural dyadic conversations, this research reconciles mixed findings on talking about oneself and sheds light on how individuals can more effectively navigate the challenge of forming positive impressions in initial encounters.
