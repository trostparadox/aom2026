Session overview

“Gaslight” is a term that originates from a 1938 stage play named Gaslight by Patrick Hamilton, which was later adapted into a movie with the same name in 1944, directed by George Cukor (Sweet, 2019). In both the play and movie, the husband tries to distort his wife’s perception of reality, convincing her that she is insane in an attempt to steal her jewels (Gass & Nichols, 1988). This phenomenon is a type of emotional and psychological manipulation in which the perpetrator (gaslighter) tries to convince the target (gaslightee) that their perceptions, beliefs, and reactions are unfounded and unreal to gain control and power over the gaslightee (Abramson, 2014; Aurangzeb et al., 2023; Spear, 2019). Gaslighting occurs in relationships in which power imbalance exists (Jones, 2023; Sweet, 2019), and is typically a covert, sustainable behavior that makes the victim increasingly reliant on the gaslighter’s interpretation of reality (Jones, 2023; Sweet, 2019). Considering the embedded power asymmetry characteristic of gaslighting, gaslighting is likely to occur in supervisor and subordinate dyads. In organizations, supervisor gaslighting behavior happens when they distort their subordinates’ perceptions of reality and make them question their own competence and perceptions (Kukreja & Pandey, 2023). To date, gaslighting has been primarily explored in psychology literature and has recently extended to other disciplines, such as education, medicine, philosophy, political science, and sociology. However, this phenomenon stays largely underexplored in the organizational context. Research around supervisory gaslighting has demonstrated that gaslighting is most likely to happen in conditions of role conflict (Kukreja & Pandey, 2023) and is associated with reduced job satisfaction and affective organizational commitment (Fulcher & Ashkanasy, 2023). Supervisors may use different tactics to gaslight their subordinates, such as questioning the gaslightee’s memory of work events, refusing to engage in conversations with the gaslightee, belittling the gaslightee’s feelings, experiences, and capabilities, denying that something occurred or pretending to forget the event, or shifting their blame to the victim (Omran & Yousafzai, 2023). Relying on the existing definitions, supervisory gaslighting is distinct from other types of supervisory mistreatment since it systematically ruins the target’s confidence in their perception of reality and makes them feel crazy (Abramson, 2014). Supervisory gaslighting can occur through the mentioned gaslighting tactics and create a sustained cycle of covert manipulation that is hard for the victim to break, differentiating gaslighting from more episodic or situational mistreatments (Hyde & Grieve, 2018; Hershcovis et al., 2020). Despite the recent work on this topic, there is little consensus among management researchers regarding how supervisory gaslighting is conceptualized, defined, and distinguished from other types of supervisory mistreatments (e.g., abusive supervision or bullying). For instance, lacking a clear conceptualization of the topic hinders researchers from developing a robust and precise scale for this phenomenon. Additionally, there is still confusion about the intentionality of gaslighting behavior, raising the question of whether the supervisor is aware of the mistreatment they are causing. Considering the extensive attention this phenomenon has recently received in practice, such as the word “gaslighting” being named Merriam-Webster's 2022 Word of the Year (Merriam-Webster, n.d.), there is a notable need for clarification in the conceptualization of supervisory gaslighting. This proposed panel symposium would bring together prominent scholars who study gaslighting or other types of workplace mistreatment constructs in an attempt to build a more coherent foundation for supervisory gaslighting. We aim to accomplish the following goals: (1) foster discussion surrounding a definition of supervisory gaslighting, (2) highlight key distinctions from other forms of established workplace mistreatment constructs, and (3) shape a clearer research agenda for the field of management. We believe that addressing the existing ambiguities in this topic and advancing the scholarly dialogue on supervisory gaslighting can advance future empirical and theoretical investigations.
