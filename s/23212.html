Female Entrepreneurs’ Growth Intention and the Actual Growth: a Qualitative Study

ENT
Dan Huang (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Christoph Hienerth (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Gouya Harirchi (University of Udine)
This study aims to investigate the relationship between antecedents of growth intention and subsequent actual growth among female entrepreneurs, based on the Theory of Planned Behavior. Drawing on 55 semi-structured interviews with German and Chinese female entrepreneurs, we challenge the prevailing assumption of a strong correlation between growth intention and actual growth (a proxy for behavior). Instead, we propose a dynamic, contextually embedded model wherein entrepreneurial cognition and intentions evolve over time. The intervening conditions of endogenous perceived behavioral control and exogenous objective constraints (economic downturn and shortage of partners) shape the changes in female entrepreneurs’ growth intentions from high to low. In contrast, conditions of opportunity and responsibility influence the changes in their growth intentions, transitioning from low to high, across the entrepreneurial lifecycle. Regional differences between Germany and China highlight the influence of institutional and cultural contexts. This preliminary study contributes to the existing research gap on growth intention – actual growth behavior gaps and creates a paradigm for future entrepreneurial growth intention studies.

How Does the Shadow Economy Affect Company Growth?

ENT
Arnis Sauka (The Baltic International Centre for Economic Policy Studies (BICEPS)); Talis Putnins (Stockholm School of Economics in Riga); Reinis Krists Dimants; Roberts Valts Varslavs
This study explores the effect that a firm’s involvement in the shadow economy has on its access to capital and subsequent growth. Although shadow economies account for a substantial share of total economic activity worldwide, their effects are poorly understood due to the challenges in observing shadow economic activity. We explore this research gap by combining a unique survey-based dataset containing firm-level estimates of shadow economy activities with detailed financial data from three European Union countries during the period 2009-2024. We find that a one percentage point increase in the extent to which a firm misreports its activities reduces the firm’s leverage by 0.2% – 0.8% and, consequently, its employment growth by 0.00003 to 0.00007 percentage points. We estimate that, in aggregate, shadow economy practices have reduced employment growth in Latvia, Lithuania, and Estonia by 72 to 152 thousand work opportunities over the period from 2011 to 2022. This translates into a 39% to 83% lower growth rate and €659 million to €1,399 million in lost GDP.

Scaling Smart: How Different Growth Trajectories Impact Mental Distress in Startups (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Ellani Amirian (KU Leuven); Robin John Clerckx (KU Leuven); Veroniek Collewaert (Vlerick Business School); Bart Leten (KU Leuven)
Research on high-growth firms predominantly portrays growth as harmful to employees, with negative effects shown to intensify as firms grow faster. This uniform view on growth leaves little room for variation in how different configurations of growth shape employee mental distress. Drawing on Conservation of Resources theory, we argue that the configuration of growth—whether firms expand sales without proportional hiring (sales-driven), hiring without matching sales growth (employment-driven), or both in balance—determines resource gains and losses that, in turn, increase or reduce employee mental distress. We use longitudinal administrative data linking 2,328 Dutch high-growth startups to 98,139 employees (2007–2023) with a staggered difference-in-differences design, and find that sales-driven growth increases mental distress, balanced growth reduces it, and employment-driven growth has no significant effect. Our findings emphasize that sustainable high growth requires aligning firm expansion with employee resource preservation, offering both theoretical and practical insights for entrepreneurship, strategy, and organizational behavior research.

The Innovation Paradox in High-Growth Firms

ENT
Pourya Darnihamedani (Nyenrode Business University)
High-growth firms (HGFs) account for a disproportionate share of job creation and sales, yet evidence on whether innovation drives high growth remains mixed. We argue this ambiguity stems from treating innovation as a single construct and neglecting the coordination constraints that characterize high-growth contexts. Using firm-level panel data from the Business Environment and Enterprise Performance Survey (BEEPS), we disaggregate innovation into product, process, marketing, and organizational types and estimate their associations with HGF sales growth. We further test whether open innovation collaboration and the degree of novelty condition these relationships and compare results with non-HGFs. All four innovation types are positively associated with HGF sales growth, with organizational innovation exhibiting a stronger effect than product innovation. Open innovation strengthens the process innovation–growth relationship, whereas novelty shows no systematic moderating effect. Process and organizational innovation are not significant for non-HGFs, suggesting these are distinctive drivers of high-growth episodes.

Understanding Entrepreneurial Firm Growth: A Systematic Literature Review

ENT
David Sharma (University of Louisville)
Firm growth is widely recognized as an idiosyncratic and context-dependent process shaped by multiple factors. This study advances the firm growth and leadership literatures by systematically exploring how and why top executives influence firm growth outcomes. Drawing on a systematic review of 75 scholarly articles between 2011 to 2025, the study positions top executives as central actors whose characteristics, motivations, and strategic choices shape growth trajectories. The findings of the study reveal that executive demographic attributes, human capital, psychological traits, and entrepreneurial orientation jointly shape and enable risk-taking, innovation, and strategic decision-making. These effects are further conditioned by contextual factors such as governance structures, incentive systems, resource availability, and organizational culture. The review also highlights heterogeneity in growth outcomes across family and non-family firms, emphasizing the role of socioemotional wealth, legacy preservation, and leadership succession. By offering a nuanced perspective on executive-driven growth, this study contributes to an understanding of growth mechanisms and identifies promising avenues for future research on leadership, governance, and entrepreneurial growth of firms.
