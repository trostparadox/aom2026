Military-Governed Market Economies: Extending Varieties of Capitalism and Institutional Theories

IM
Anton Klarin (Curtin University - Perth); Songduan Fan (Curtin University - Perth); Bowen Hu; Pi-Shen Seet (Edith Cowan University); Qijie Xiao (Monash University)
This study introduces the concept of military-governed market economies (MGMEs) as a distinct variety of capitalism shaped by coercive coordination mechanisms. Drawing from comparative data from nine countries, we demonstrate that MGMEs diverge from liberal, coordinated, and authoritarian market economies by structuring business-state relations through military dominance rather than market logic or strategic collaboration. By extending Hall and Soskice’s Varieties of Capitalism (VoC) framework and institutional theory, the study conceptualizes coercive authority as a fourth institutional pillar. We reveal how coercion and repression evolve into enduring modes of economic coordination, transforming conflict into institutional order. This paper contributes to development research by introducing a new typology of military-governed market economies, integrating political economy, institutional analysis, and cross-national data. By identifying coercive authority as a distinct institutional pillar, the study enhances understanding of how military-led governance structures shape development outcomes and hinder institutional reform. Keywords: military regime; autocracy; dictatorship; varieties of democracy; coercive control; institutional theory

Decoding the State: Micro-Foundations of Strategic Repair Under Domestic Institutional Change

IM
Sichang Liu (Tilburg University); Róisín Donnelly (Tilburg University)
This study investigates how firms navigate the tension between domestic institutional change and global strategy. We challenge the view that firms automatically follow state policies; instead, we propose that state-oriented FDI is a conditional strategy driven by the need to restore resource access disrupted by institutional shocks. Integrating the logic of institutional escape with state-directed internationalization, we argue that the anti-corruption campaign creates an imperative for firms to signal loyalty through BRI investments, thereby mitigating domestic uncertainty. Crucially, we unpack the micro-foundations of this process, positing that the efficacy of this legitimacy repair strategy hinges on the executive’s decoding capability. Our empirical analysis of Chinese firms reveals that anti-corruption enforcement significantly drives BRI location choices, but this effect is heterogeneous: it is amplified by executive political connections and attenuated by foreign nationality. These findings indicate that responding to state narratives requires specific cognitive embeddedness. We advance international business literature by elucidating how firms utilize cross-border investments to resolve domestic institutional misalignment and determining the boundary conditions of such strategic responsiveness.

Turning Down the Heat: Foreign Ownership, Informal Experience, and Populism in Tax Enforcement

IM
Ximing Chen (The University of Sydney); Huan Zhang (The University of Sydney); Jing Yu Yang (The University of Sydney)
We examine how foreign firms navigate the liability of foreignness (LOF) in the tax enforcement domain of developing economies—a setting where weak institutions grant tax agents broad discretionary power. Extending international business research on regulatory institutions, we conceptualize tax enforcement as a distinct form of regulatory risk. We argue that informal entry experience—operating unregistered before formalization—equips foreign firms with tacit institutional knowledge, local embeddedness, and legitimacy that attenuate enforcement risk post-registration. Moreover, this mitigating effect strengthens in countries with strong populist discourse, which valorizes “ordinary” economic actors and reframes informal legacy as a signal of grassroots alignment rather than deviance. Analyses of 41,712 firms across 44 developing countries (2006–2023) support our arguments. By integrating informality, populism, and regulatory enforcement into the liability of foreignness framework, our paper advances a multi-actor understanding of LOF and implies that foreign firms can strategically engage informal experience and political narratives to mitigate state-based regulatory risk.

The Sovereign Shield: Geopolitics, Asset Specificity, and Rejection of the Stateless Escape (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Kaustubh Ghanekar (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
Does the digital age allow multinational enterprises (MNEs) to escape the Westphalian order through "stateless" technologies? This paper investigates how geopolitical conflict influences MNEs' choices between Free and Open-Source Software (FOSS) and proprietary technology. Integrating internalization theory with geopolitical risk research, we challenge the "sanctuary hypothesis" the idea that firms flee to the digital commons to avoid state coercion. Using a longitudinal dyadic panel of 10,519 firm-product observations in the global digital entertainment industry (2015–2024), we find that heightened geopolitical tension systematically decreases the likelihood of adopting FOSS. Rather than seeking "stateless" freedom, firms gravitate toward proprietary stacks that offer "institutional cover" and legal certainty provided by the state. Furthermore, we identify a "golden handcuffs" effect: high asset specificity in licensed middleware creates structural inertia, making firms with high technical debt less responsive to political shocks. Our results suggest that in a fragmenting "splinternet," MNEs do not flee to the commons; they retreat to the fortified harbor of the sovereign.

Reading the State's Mind: Geopolitical Disruption and Heterogeneous Firm Response

IM
Keun Woo Jeong (University of Pennsylvania); Yajie Wang (University of Pennsylvania)
In the face of major geopolitical disruptions, firms operating in affected markets must determine how to interpret and comply with rapidly shifting political stances adopted by the state. This study examines how differences in political capital, proxied by state ownership, shape firms’ compliance behavior following severe geopolitical shocks. Comparing state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and private firms, we analyze how varying degrees of political embeddedness affect firm operations in geopolitically disrupted markets. Using comprehensive monthly firm–product–level trade data covering the universe of Chinese exporters, and exploiting North Korea’s nuclear tests as an exogenous geopolitical disruption, we study firms’ trade patterns surrounding an abrupt escalation in international conflict. We find that aggregate trade declines sharply following the shock, but this decline is driven primarily by private firms with weaker political capital, which reduce exports not only in sanctioned categories but also in legally permissible humanitarian products, consistent with overcompliance under political uncertainty. In contrast, SOEs, leveraging stronger political ties and access to state information, maintain relatively stable trade relationships, indicating more calibrated compliance. These differences are most pronounced among firms located closer to the political center where political scrutiny is greatest, and in areas governed by career-concerned mayors. We discuss implications for geopolitics and firm compliance research.
