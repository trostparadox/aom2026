Fraud or Fuel? A Meta-Analysis of Workplace Impostor Thoughts and Work Outcomes

MOC
Yuxuan Zhi (Peking University)
The prevailing view treats the impostor phenomenon as detrimental, yet recent research suggests it may also confer benefits. To reconcile this tension, we conduct a meta-analysis examining whether, and under what conditions, workplace impostor thoughts predict key work outcomes. Synthesizing empirical evidence from 36 studies comprising 41 independent samples (N = 14,586), our results indicate that workplace impostor thoughts are positively related to burnout and counterproductive work behavior (CWB), but show nonsignificant relationships with task performance and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB). Moderator analyses further reveal that the relationship with burnout is amplified in healthcare settings and in samples with higher female composition, while its effect on CWB is buffered by more collectivist cultures. Importantly, measurement choice helps explain these nonsignificant relationships for performance and OCB. Whereas trait-based measures (e.g., Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale, CIPS) are reliably linked to maladaptive outcomes, workplace specific measures (e.g., Workplace Impostor Thoughts Scale, WITS) are associated with adaptive behaviors, including higher OCB and lower CWB. Overall, our findings support a compensatory perspective, suggesting that when conceptualized as a workplace cognition, impostor thoughts can trigger motivation that offsets, and in some domains reverses, negative outcomes.
