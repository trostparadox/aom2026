How Wide Is Too Wide—And for Whom? The Effect of Pay Range Width on Hiring Success

HR
Felix Bölingen (LMU Munich School of Management); Felice B. Klein (Boise State University); Qing Gong (Kennesaw State University); Valeria Alterman (University of Miami); Rohan Karri (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Recent U.S. mandates require employers to include pay ranges in job postings, yet these ranges vary widely. We theorize that firms’ disclosed range width influences hiring outcomes by sending two signals to candidates: wider ranges can signal greater risk of pay inequity and deter applicants, yet they may also signal stronger pay-for-performance practices and attract applicants. Based on these two signals, we hypothesize an inverted U-shaped relationship between pay range width and hiring success, where hiring success initially rises as ranges widen but declines once they become very broad. We further propose that peak hiring success occurs at narrower widths and the slope after the turning point is more negative for groups historically exposed to pay discrimination. These groups may weigh inequity risk more heavily and view pay-for-performance less favorably than reference groups. Using job postings from over 11,000 employers in pay-transparency states (2021–2025) linked to subsequent hires’ online profiles, we find support for our hypotheses. This research advances pay transparency literature and signaling theory, highlighting how the same pay information disclosure is interpreted differently by job seekers, depending on their labor market experience. Further, the results provide actionable insights for organizations navigating pay transparency mandates.

Academic Leaders and Executive-Employee Pay Disparity: A Meritocracy Ideology Perspective

HR
Danyang Zhu (Jiangnan University); Yan Wu (Fudan University)
How does firm leaders’ academic experience influence executive–employee pay disparity? Drawing on the meritocracy ideology perspective, we argue that firms led by academically experienced leaders exhibit greater within-firm vertical pay disparity, as such experience fosters a meritocratic ideology that frames compensation differentials as legitimate reflections of talent and effort. In turn, these leaders show less inclination to consciously address pay inequality. We further contend that firms with higher innovation intensity, younger leaders, and greater resource slack amplify the relationship between leaders’ academic experience and executive-employee pay disparity. Our study advances compensation management research by identifying leaders’ academic experience as a crucial antecedent and by highlighting meritocracy ideology as an underlying mechanism.

How and When Pay Volatility Impacts Gig Workers’ Performance: Evidence from a Daily Diary Study

HR
Mengyu Mao (Zhengzhou University); Wladislaw Rivkin (Trinity College Dublin, The University of Dublin); KENI SONG (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management)
The rapid expansion of the gig economy has exposed gig workers to substantial pay volatility driven by uncertain work requests and algorithmic pricing. Although prior research has documented the negative consequences of pay volatility for worker’s well-being and turnover, little is known about how pay volatility affects gig workers’ day-to-day performance among those who remain active in platform work. Drawing on Conservation of Resources theory, we develop a within-person model linking daily pay volatility to gig workers’ next-day pay. Using experience sampling data from 101 ride-hailing drivers across 747 days, we find that pay volatility depletes workers’ subjective vitality after work, which in turn reduces their ability to extend work hours the following day, resulting in lower next-day pay. We further identify daily pay level as an important boundary condition: when daily overall pay is higher, the negative effect of pay volatility is attenuated. Overall, our study has advanced the understanding of how daily pay volatility impair gig workers’ performance and suggests that gig platforms take measures to reduce the negative impact of pay volatility on gig workers to ensure the sustainable development.

How Negative and Frequent Performance Feedback Shapes Merit Pay Effects on Job Performance

HR
Hanbo Shim (The University of Texas at Arlington); Xiao Xiao (The University of Texas at Arlington); George Benson (The University of Texas at Arlington); So Ri Park (Rutgers University)
Merit pay—base pay increases contingent on prior performance—is arguably the most widely used compensation practice, but its motivational effect on job performance is weak or inconsistent compared to those of other incentives. In the present study, we develop a dual-stage moderated mediation model explaining when and why merit pay motivates performance, depending on negative performance feedback and performance feedback frequency. Building on compensation activation theory, we argue that merit pay activates employees’ affiliation and competence-mastery motives, shaping leader–member exchange (LMX) and thriving in learning, respectively. Negative performance feedback yet conveys incongruent evaluative cues that would undermine the compensation activation processes of merit pay. Drawing from feedback intervention theory, we propose that the effects of LMX and thriving in learning on performance depend on performance feedback frequency, which makes employees’ attention toward self- versus task-level processes more salient. Using data from 451 employees in a large consulting firm in North America, we found that negative performance feedback attenuates the effects of merit pay on LMX and thriving in learning. Moreover, performance feedback frequency moderates the effects of these mediators on job performance in opposite directions. Overall, our work highlights performance feedback as critical boundary conditions for merit pay.

How Pay-for-Performance Complexity and Pay Information Uncertainty Drive Pay Information Exchange

HR
Xiaoyue Wu (Nanjing University); Kong Zhou (Nanjing University of Science and Technology)
As pay-for-performance (PFP) systems become increasingly prevalent and complex, employees face growing difficulty in obtaining and interpreting pay information. Although some organizations have expanded pay information disclosure policies, disclosed information is often partial or aggregated, limiting employees’ ability to make meaningful pay comparisons. Drawing on uncertainty reduction theory, we examine when, why, and how employees proactively exchange pay information with coworkers. We conceptualize coworker-to-coworker pay information exchange as a domain-specific behavior and distinguish between pay information seeking and pay information sharing, which differ in their risks and benefits. Through a three-wave field survey, we propose that employees selectively choose exchange partners, seeking pay information from coworkers with low influence over them while sharing pay information with coworkers with high work similarity. Further, we found that PFP complexity heightens pay information uncertainty and motivates pay information exchange, particularly among employees with low epistemic motivation. This study advances research on pay information disclosure, compensation systems, and employee information exchange by highlighting discretionary, employee-level responses to complex PFP systems.
