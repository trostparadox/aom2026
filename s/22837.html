Read the Media Room: Environmental Underperformance, Greenwashing, and Green Innovation

SIM
Junggeun Kim (Yonsei University); Taewoo Roh (Hanyang University); Pankaj C. Patel (Villanova University)
Literature on green innovation and greenwashing has often assumed the two strategies as mutually exclusive and paid little attention to when firms pursue both strategies as a dual-path approach. Drawing on a sample of S&P 1500 firms, this study examines how environmental performance feedback drives both green innovation and greenwashing. This study also reveals divergence in environmental strategies hinges on how the firm perceives the external elements. Such divergence arises from differences in risk management processes shaped by the reach of media sources covering firms’ environmental issues. This result suggests the central role of media in steering firms toward genuine rather than deceptive strategies. This study contributes to BTOF and risk management literature by bridging them to explain firms’ green strategic pathways between deceptive and genuine responses. We also suggest that paying attention to the media can lead practitioners to make better decisions regarding environmental strategies.

When Wallets Tighten, Green Talk Rises: A Natural Experiment on Sustainable Consumption Sentiment

SIM
Ouiam Kaddouri (EMLV Business School, France)
Drawing on Consumer Value Theory and Behavioural Reasoning Theory, we examine the effect of economic downturns on sentiment toward green products using a natural experiment across two European shocks—the COVID-19 recession and the energy crisis. We analyse 19,306 Reddit posts (2018–2025) with an ensemble sentiment model (VADER–TextBlob–FinBERT) and econometric tests. Contrary to conventional assumptions, downturns are associated with small but significant increases in positive green sentiment, with the strongest lift in symbolic domains. Perceived economic burden is not a stable negative predictor once category structure is modelled, and effects are stronger in the acute COVID window than in the chronic energy crisis. Theoretically, we extend CVT by showing that expressive/identity benefits can offset sacrifice salience in public discourse and refine BRT by highlighting a medium-contingent mechanism: consumers may “talk green” even when budgets constrain “buying green.” Managerially, our results recommend a dual framing: identity-led positioning for symbolic categories, and explicit, near-term economic value for utilitarian categories. Sustainability discourse can remain resilient—sometimes counter-cyclical—under economic stress.

The Symbolic/Substantive Interplay of Corporate Environmental Sustainability and Proactive Behavior

SIM
Jisun Kim (State University of New York Polytechnic Institute); He Gao (University of Delaware); Luiz F. Mesquita (Arizona State University); Glenn Hoetker (Melbourne Business School, University of Melbourne)
Does it suffice for an organization to articulate an environmental purpose, or must it also demonstrate it through concrete action? Drawing on research on impression management, organizational purpose, and corporate environmental sustainability (CES), we address this dilemma with a theoretical model in which CES vision (i.e., symbolic action) and CES practices (i.e., substantive action) impart the effects of aligning interests (organizational pride) and of reducing information asymmetry (organizational goal clarity) between the top leaders and their members and enhance the members’ aggregate proactive behavior. Testing hypotheses on a large multi-year survey data with 24,915 managers from 302 Brazilian companies, complemented by an online experiment with U.S. managers, we find that both organizational pride and organizational goal clarity mediate the effect of CES practice on members’ aggregate proactive behavior. Surprisingly, we find no evidence of these mediating effects for CES vision statements. CES vision, however, creates the context that positively moderates the effect of CES practices on organizational goal clarity, which in turn affects members’ aggregate proactive behavior. We draw implications for CES-related management research and practice.

Supply-and-Demand for ESG Assurance: The Stakeholder and Legitimacy Lens

SIM
Géraldine Broye (EM Strasbourg Business School); Christian Ott (EM Strasbourg Business School); Nohade Hanna Nasrallah (Excelia Business School); Maretno Agus Harjoto (Pepperdine University)
Drawing from the literature, which conceptualizes audit quality as the outcome of audit effort, we examine ESG assurance quality through the lens of ESG assurance effort, defined as the observable means and resources mobilized during the assurance engagement. We adopt a supply-and-demand framework to explain variation in ESG assurance effort within a mandatory ESG reporting setting. Using a hand-collected panel dataset of ESG assurance statements issued by all French firms listed in the CAC All Tradable Index between 2013 and 2023, our study is the first study that introduces three direct input-based measures for ESG assurance effort: (i) the size of the assurance team, (ii) the duration of the assurance engagement, and (iii) the number of interviews conducted. We find that assurance effort is a co-produced outcome jointly shaped by client-firm demand and assurance-provider supply. Both parties’ incentives, rooted in stakeholder pressures and legitimacy concerns, significantly and independently contribute to the rigor of ESG assurance engagements. Examining the regulatory change from the Grenelle II Law to the EU Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD), we find that, while the additive co-production mechanism remains stable across regimes, the relative strength of demand increases under the NFRD.

Another Way Out: How Firms Leverage CSR Alternatives to Offset Their CSR Concerns

SIM
Junbum Jo (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology); Tae-Hyun Kim (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology)
Prior CSR literature suggests that firms respond efficiently to social demands through symbolic CSR. However, symbolic CSR assumes that firms respond to social demands through CSR that correspond to the same social dimension. We challenge this assumption by proposing a concept of CSR alternative, which refers to as substantive CSR to address CSR concerns in other social dimensions at relatively low cost. Drawing on the strategic and institutional view of CSR, we first argue that, to offset a high-cost CSR concern (e.g. underrepresentation of minorities), firms may increase their commitment to CSR alternatives (e.g. corporate philanthropic activities). We further propose that such tendency becomes more salient during the period when institutional pressure on CSR alternatives is greater. We tested the hypotheses using 1,622 firm-year observations of top 200 U.S. law firms in 2001-2016. We found that law firms with large hierarchical gaps in racial diversity tend to increase their pro bono commitment. This tendency is more pronounced during 2001-2008, when institutional pressure toward increased attention to pro bono commitments was high. Our research contributes to the literature on CSR, institutional theory and diversity, providing insights into how firms respond to various social demands by leveraging cost-efficient CSR alternatives.
