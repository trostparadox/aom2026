Born into Risk? The Role of Intra-Family Learning and Entrepreneurial Experience

ENT
Yanhong Ding (Nanyang Technological University); Hongxin Xie
This study explores how family background—specifically, sibling structure and parental entrepreneurial experience—shapes entrepreneurs’ risk-taking propensity, and how these effects vary with entrepreneurial experience. Drawing on a dynamic learning perspective, we theorize that entrepreneurial risk-taking is influenced by the interplay of three mechanisms: intergenerational learning from entrepreneurial parents, social learning through sibling interactions, and experiential learning from prior ventures. Leveraging China’s One-Child Policy as a quasi-natural experiment, we analyze data from approximately 2,500 entrepreneurs to disentangle the dynamic effects of family socialization and venture experience. We find that novice entrepreneurs exhibit relatively similar risk-taking propensity regardless of parental or sibling backgrounds. However, among experienced entrepreneurs, those raised with siblings and those having non-entrepreneurial parents display significantly higher risk-taking propensity. This study contributes to a dynamic view of entrepreneurial learning by showing how experience moderates the influence of early-life socialization, transforming experiences into entrepreneurial risk-taking propensities.

Swinging for the Fences: Strategic Risk Taking in Entrepreneurship

ENT
James Ostler (Willamette University); William R. Forster (Oregon State University)
Entrepreneurial entry persists in environments characterized by extreme uncertainty, intense competition, and high failure rates, yet existing theories struggle to explain why disadvantaged entrants routinely pursue high variance strategies that appear to increase the likelihood of failure. We address this puzzle by introducing the concept of strategic risk, defined as variance in relative performance that arises endogenously from competitive interaction rather than from preferences over financial outcomes. We develop a formal model showing that when survival de pends on outperforming rivals rather than achieving positive absolute returns, risk neutral entrepreneurs rationally choose variance amplifying strategies. Under these conditions, what appears as risk seeking behavior is a structurally induced response to competition. Crucially, risk and the availability of variance amplifying strategies become enabling conditions for entrepreneurial entry. In the absence of risk, disadvantaged entrants cannot rationally compete with incumbents, and entrepreneurship is endogenously suppressed. By reconceptualizing risk as a structural feature of competitive markets rather than a psychological trait of entrepreneurs, this paper reframes uncertainty from an obstacle to be overcome into a condition that makes entrepreneurship and the radical innovation it produces possible.

When Startups Scale Too Soon: Entrepreneurial Growth Timing and Misconduct Risk

ENT
Frederik Uwe Sell (RWTH Aachen University); Sebastian Kruse (RWTH Aachen University)
This paper examines whether early scaling increases startups’ misconduct risk and whether venture capital (VC) moderates that risk. While early scaling is known to elevate performance risk, its compliance implications remain underexplored. Drawing on Organizational Legitimacy Theory and the Resource-Based View, we argue that early scaling intensifies pressure to meet stakeholder expectations before internal controls mature, raising incentives to cut corners. We further investigate whether this risk differs by severity, differentiating between superficial cases, often treated as routine business costs, and severe violations with potentially existential consequences, and whether VC investors’ governance mitigates these risks. To test these arguments, we assemble a novel dataset of 46,859 U.S. startups by merging Crunchbase with WageScape job-posting data and linking firms to regulatory enforcement actions from Good Jobs First. Our results show that startups scaling within 24 months of founding face a significantly higher probability of post-scale misconduct. This relationship holds for both superficial and severe cases and is robust to alternative severity definitions and specifications. In contrast, we find no consistent evidence that VC involvement moderates the relationship. Overall, we contribute by showing that early scaling entails measurable integrity costs across severity levels, extending research on scaling risks to startup compliance.
