Authors: Li-Fang Chou National Cheng Kung University; Ming-Yen Lee Chung Yuan Christian University

Session overview

As humanoid robots evolve from executing mechanical tasks to providing socio-emotional companionship, they increasingly blur the boundary between machines and humans. Although organizational research has primarily focused on trust and AI adoption, we know little about how perceiving humanlike minds in autonomous agents "spills over" to influence how people treat others. Drawing on mind perception theory and the Inclusion/Exclusion Model (IEM), this research investigates whether perceiving higher levels of humanlike mind in robots leads to assimilation-induced dehumanization of human employees. Specifically, we propose that when autonomous agents are perceived as possessing greater socio-emotional capabilities (particularly along the experience dimension of mind), individuals increasingly attribute humanlike qualities to these nonhuman entities. However, because AI agents are generally perceived as possessing less overall mind than humans, an assimilation process occurs in which humanness judgments of actual people converge toward the lower humanness standards of robots, resulting in dehumanization of employees. Across two sub-studies with 65 full-time workers and 556 participants (403 university students and 153 full-time workers), we found that perceiving companion robots (vs. parkour robots) as having higher mind predicted greater support for dehumanizing employee treatment (e.g., substandard benefits, surveillance). Furthermore, this effect was moderated by work status (Z-generation students vs. non-Gen Z workers), conscientiousness, and innovation adoption . This research extends dehumanization research to managerial decision-making contexts and highlights the psychosocial risks of increasingly anthropomorphized AI in organizations.
