A Merry Heart Goes All the Day: Entrepreneurial Well-Being, Emotion-Focused Coping, and Persistence

ENT
Amelie Karolin Gschwinder (University of Bern); Artur Baldauf (University of Bern)
Why do some entrepreneurs persist in self-employment despite chronic uncertainty and stress while others disengage? We argue that entrepreneurial well-being impacts persistence by influencing how entrepreneurs cope with adversity. Integrating broaden-and-build theory with entrepreneurial persistence literature, we propose that entrepreneurial well-being broadens cognitive repertoires and builds enduring psychological resources, reducing reliance on maladaptive, emotion-focused coping and, in turn, promoting persistence in self-employment. Conversely, entrepreneurial ill-being narrows thought-action repertoires, increases emotion-focused coping, and undermines persistence. We test our hypotheses using panel data from the Midlife in the United States (MIDUS) study, tracking 347 entrepreneurs across three waves (1995–1996 to 2013–2014). Longitudinal analyses support our predictions: entrepreneurial well-being is positively associated with persistence, entrepreneurial ill-being is negatively associated with persistence, and emotion-focused coping mediates these relationships in the theorized directions. We reposition entrepreneurial well-being from outcome to antecedent, distinguish well-being from ill-being, and identify coping as the pathway translating psychological states into long-term engagement. In doing so, we advance research on entrepreneurial persistence by highlighting well-being as a central psychological resource for sustaining entrepreneurial careers. We further discuss implications for building entrepreneurial capabilities, the design of venture support programs, and the formulation of interventions.

Debt Easing and Entrepreneurial Participation: Evidence From Student Loan Relief

ENT
Joonho Oh (KDI School of Public Policy and Management); Jiayi Bao
Entrepreneurial entry depends not only on opportunities and individual abilities but also on the economic constraints individuals face when evaluating career options. While entrepreneurship policy research has primarily emphasized firm-level interventions, recent studies suggests that welfare-enhancing policies may indirectly shape entrepreneurial careers by easing financial insecurity. However, existing evidence largely focuses on high-skilled, high-income individuals, leaving unclear whether such mechanisms operate for individuals with greater economic needs. This study examines whether student loan relief influences entrepreneurial participation by relaxing financial constraints. We exploit the 2015 expansion of Maine’s Educational Opportunity Tax Credit, which extended student loan relief to self- employed individuals. Using the U.S. Current Population Survey data, we implement a difference-in-differences design with a synthetic control. We find that the policy expansion significantly increases self-employment among individuals eligible for the loan relief. The increase is concentrated among individuals facing greater economic and labor market constraints. We further examine the characteristics of post-expansion entrepreneurial entry. Although much of the increase occurs in unincorporated self-employment, average business income rises following the expansion. Together, these findings show that student loan relief can broaden entrepreneurial career choices by easing financial constraints, particularly for individuals with greater economic needs.

Disability and Subjective Well-Being: Is Self-Employment a Cure or a Harm? (WITHDRAWN) (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Bach Nguyen (University of Exeter)
This study investigates the impact of employment, solo self-employment, and employership on the subjective well-being (SWB) of individuals with disabilities through the lens of self-determination theory. Using a Difference-in-Differences analysis of UK panel data (2014-2020), we find that solo self-employment mitigates the negative impact of disability on SWB, while employership exacerbates it. The negative effects of employership are less severe for those with physical (vs non-physical) disabilities and higher financial competencies, but worsen with stronger social ties. The findings offer theoretical insights into occupational choices and well-being, contributing to inclusive entrepreneurship literature and policy implications for disabled individuals.

Is the Hybrid Path to Entrepreneurship Superior?

ENT
Siran Zhan (UNSW Sydney); Liwen Zhang (University of Macau); Qiyue Zhang (University of Macau); Yan Lin (University of Macau)
This study investigates the benefits and costs of a hybrid path to entrepreneurship using a conservation of resource (COR) lens. We theorized and showed that compared to those entering directly from employment, those entering via hybrid entrepreneurship invested more time in their ventures, leading to larger venture size but higher time pressure and reduced well-being. Time investment mediates the relationship between entry mode and venture performance, while time investment and time pressure serially mediate the relationship between entry mode and well-being. Active hobby engagements may attenuate the adverse effects. Contributions to hybrid entrepreneurship, entrepreneurial well-being, and COR theory are discussed.

The Effects of Migrants’ Self-Identification and Social Inclusion on Their Small Business Success

ENT
Serghei Musaji (University of New Brunswick); Qiong Wu (Stockholm School of Economics); Julio O. De Castro
While migration relocates the body, it does not always relocate the soul. Many migrants continue to identify strongly with their country-of-origin decades after relocation, while others never fully embrace their host society. Although previous research often assumes the benefits of dual rootedness (particularly for transnational business contexts), the research seldom examines how migrants subjectively identify with home and host contexts—or how these orientations shape business performance. Drawing on role transition theory and survey data from 364 migrant entrepreneurs across four major destinations in Western Europe, we argue and find that migrants’ self-identification influences their entrepreneurial success, and that this relationship is shaped by the extent to which they feel socially included in the host society. Specifically, we find that home-country attachment enhances performance under high inclusion, while being less beneficial, and, potentially, detrimental under perceived exclusion. These results reveal the contextual nature of identity’s impact on entrepreneurship. The implications are discussed.
