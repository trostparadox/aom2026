Do Platform Investors Build Better Platforms? Evidence From Incumbent Developer Portals

TIM
Michael Amroudi (TU Dortmund University)
Incumbent firms across industries increasingly pursue digital platform strategies, yet many struggle to launch successful platforms. Prior literature predominantly focuses on mature ecosystems, leaving limited understanding of the early-stage phase in which incumbent platforms are particularly fragile. Building on organizational learning theory, we argue that incumbents can mitigate early-stage challenges by accessing specialized external knowledge, particularly through investments in platform startups prior to launching their own platforms. We further argue that the effectiveness of such pre-launch learning is contingent on other platform-specific learning sources, namely a platform’s openness and the density of other incumbent platforms within the same industry. We compile a novel dataset of 341 developer portals launched by global public incumbents between 2017 and 2024. Our findings show that pre-launch investments in platform startups are positively associated with subsequent platform engagement, highlighting such investments as a valuable mechanism for platform growth. However, this effect is weakened in open governance models and even reversed when firms operate in industries with more mature platform activity, indicating substitutive dynamics across external learning sources. Our study contributes to platform and organizational learning literature by introducing the role of platform investments in early-stage platforms and substitutive dynamics with other external sources. Moreover, we provide practical insights for incumbents and platform startups.

Opening the Door to the Product Environment: Signaling for Success or Inviting Inefficacy?

TIM
Rafaela Igler (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf)
In early-stage fundraising, venture teams and investors alike are bridging gaps of high uncertainty as information asymmetry defines the early days of the relationship. Signals sent by ventures aim to mitigate the information gap and therefore increase chances for successful fundraising. Especially in high-technology environments, where the product is at the center of the venture, the question arises – what impact do signals related to the product environment have on a venture’s funding success? Initial Coin Offerings (ICOs), leveraging blockchain technology, have become an established form of raising funds for the crypto community, an environment inherently high-tech and early-stage. Grounded in signaling and human capital theory, our empirical research based on a dataset of more than 1,400 ICOs between 2016 and 2022 shows that openness on the product environment negatively impacts ICO funding success. We further find that this effect can be mitigated or turned when the ICO team has functional tech, marketing and investment expertise.

Origin Endowments: An Empirical Exploration of Entrepreneurial Ideas and Venture Outcomes

TIM
Suresh Kotha (University of Washington); Ming Zhu Wang (University of Washington)
Entrepreneurial ideas do not emerge in a vacuum; they arise through origin processes that embed ideas in specific contexts and crystallize into origin endowments—bundles of information, resources, relationships, and constraints that ventures inherit at founding. We theorize how origin endowments vary systematically along four dimensions (information structure, resource intensity, legitimacy foundations, and governance complexity) and argue that these profiles generate idea-level path dependence in venture growth trajectories. We develop predictions across three ideation pathways (i.e., prior work experience, unmet user needs, and experimentation/research) and examine how founder human capital conditions these relationships. Empirically, we combine PitchBook venture-level data and valuations with large language model–based coding of founder origin narratives and education for 26,057 U.S. ventures (founded 1990+), documenting systematic origin pathway differences in milestone progression, including moderating effects of founder PhD training.

Selling the Future to Survive: How the Silicon Valley Bank Collapse Reshaped Startup Innovation

TIM
Jibin Jun (Seoul National University); Hyo Kang (Seoul National University)
This study recasts cluster resilience by examining how specialized infrastructure failure reshapes the trajectory of startup innovation. Exploiting the 2023 Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) collapse, we analyze venture-backed startups’ patenting (2018–2024) using multinomial difference-in-differences. We find a non-monotonic “donut-hole” pattern of vulnerability: innovation contraction peaks in an intermediate “near-ring” (5–20km), while the cluster core remains buffered. Beyond scale, we document a directional retreat; exposed startups liquidate frontier exploration (e.g., AI) to stabilize viability through continuation-based exploitation—trading future options for present survival. Furthermore, triple-difference tests reveal an exposure paradox: ex-ante “stronger” startups —those with superior funding and capabilities—experience the sharpest declines. This illustrates how deep embeddedness in specialized infrastructure, while accelerating growth in stable times, transmits fragility during disruption. We reframe ecosystem resilience as a joint function of spatial exposure and structural dependence, showing that infrastructure shocks reshape not only the scale but the fundamental direction of technological search.

The Alignment Paradox in CVC-Backed Ventures: An Empirical Study of Innovation Performance

TIM
Jiayi Ma (Zhejiang University); Fuxin Lin (Zhejiang University); Xiaobo Wu
Corporate Venture Capital (CVC) is widely recognized as a catalyst for innovation, but its impact is paradoxical when alignment becomes excessive. The alignment paradox arises when close ties between corporate investors and investees facilitate knowledge transfer while simultaneously restricting innovation by reducing diversity and limiting autonomy. Using a dataset of 627 Chinese high-tech ventures (2010–2021), we analyze the interplay between knowledge spillovers, strategic constraints, and risks. The findings suggest that CVC investment generally enhances investees’ innovation performance, with technological similarity further amplifying this effect by facilitating efficient knowledge transfer. However, excessive proximity in investor portfolios, business domains, or geographic location creates constraints that limit access to diverse knowledge and heighten the risk of knowledge leakage. These insights reveal the “alignment paradox”, where certain dimensions of proximity foster innovation while others hinder it. This study advances corporate innovation management literature and offers practical guidance on balancing alignment and knowledge exchange to sustain CVC’s role in technological progress.
