Algorithmic Governance and Workers’ Multihoming: Evidence from Sequential Ride Assignments

TIM
Rosario Silva Froján (IE University); Snehasis Despande (IE University)
Digital platforms increasingly rely on independent sellers and service providers whose participation is voluntary and flexible. In many platform settings, these actors can multihome across competing platforms, posing a strategic challenge for platforms seeking to retain participation and sustain competitive advantage. Existing research has largely conceptualized multihoming as a long-term strategic choice, paying limited attention to how platforms influence multihoming when participation decisions are frequent. This paper examines how algorithmic governance affects multihoming behavior in a ride-hailing platform. We study algorithmic tasks bundling through sequential ride assignments, which increase work continuity and reduce idle time without explicitly restricting multihoming. Using proprietary daily panel data on nearly 5,000 drivers across five cities, we exploit within-driver variation in exposure to the “sequential assignments”. We find that drivers assigned to sequential rides are less likely to multihome on the following day. This effect is fully mediated by increases in work intensity and earnings and depends on local competitive conditions, weakening as competition increases. Our findings show how algorithmic governance shapes high-frequency participation decisions and identify important competitive boundary conditions of its effectiveness.

Improve Platform Efficiency by Limiting Complementor Choice: Evidence from PIN-Match in Ride Hailing

TIM
Mengwei Qu (The University of Connecticut); Bowen Lou (University of Southern California); Chen Liang (The University of Connecticut); Milan Miric
Platform companies have often benefited by providing customers and complementors with choices and used sophisticated algorithms to match different sides of the platform. Yet, complementors may often end up strategically in an attempt to seek individual advantage, but doing so may undermine the efficiency of the whole platform ecosystem. Platform companies may in turn benefit from using simpler matching algorithms that are more difficult to manipulate. In this paper, we investigate how the introduction of Uber’s simplified PIN-based matching, rather than the sophisticated algorithm that relied on pre-match proximity and bonuses impacted the efficiency of the platform as a whole. Utilizing granular trip-level data with precise pickup locations, we find that platform providing a simpler matching algorithm and removing information that would allow drivers to act strategically resulted in 4.50% reduction in wait times, 9.86% increase in driver earnings, and 8.7% decrease in platform incentives expenditures for drivers, all suggesting that drivers were no longer able to act strategically, while the entire platform was more efficient and better off on average. Our findings contribute to research on platform governance and algorithmic management by demonstrating how information restriction, rather than monetary incentives, can realign self-interested agents with system-wide efficiency. We offer important field-based empirical evidence that altering information design can reduce complementor opportunism and coordination inefficiencies in decentralized digital markets.

Multihoming Speed During Technological Transitions: A Real Options Perspective

TIM
Daria Demyanova (University of Bologna); Claudio Giachetti (University of Bologna)
Technological transitions increase uncertainty and confront firms with timing dilemmas about whether to move early or defer strategic action. Drawing on real options theory and the technological change literature, we examine how such transitions shape strategic timing in platform-based industries, where complementors decide whether and when to expand across competing platforms. We focus on complementors’ speed of platform multihoming, defined as the pace at which firms extend their offerings from a focal platform to additional platforms, and investigate why technological transitions do not exert uniform effects on this timing. We conceptualize multihoming speed as the outcome of a shifting trade-off between an option to defer—preserving flexibility while platforms and standards remain unsettled—and an option to grow—using early cross-platform moves to build learning, positioning, and expansion rights while competitive positions remain fluid. This integration implies no single transition effect on multihoming speed. Instead, complementors’ pacing reflects how this trade-off evolves over the transition, shaped by transition type—competence-enhancing versus competence-destroying—and by prior transition experience, in terms of experience depth and breadth. We test our arguments using event-history analyses of video game publishers as complementors in the U.S. console gaming industry from 1993 to 2024.

Platform Owner Acquisition of Enabling Technologies and Complementors’ Platform Choice

TIM
Xi Wang (Rice University); Haiyang Li (Rice University); Prashant Kale (Rice University)
This study advances current understanding of how technology interdependence between platform owners and complementors shapes complementors’ platform choices by examining platform owners’ acquisitions of third-party enabling technologies. Such acquisitions create a fundamental tension: they reduce coordination costs by improving compatibility between the platform and the technology, but they also increase complementors’ dependence by giving the platform owner control over both assets. We argue that greater dependence on the acquired technology leads complementors to introduce more products on the acquiring platform because integration improves compatibility and development efficiency. However, when complementors are simultaneously highly dependent on the platform, the resulting concentration of dependence heightens perceived lock-in risks and discourages them from further introducing products on the acquiring platform. Using developer-level data from the video game console industry (2010–2020), we examine developers’ responses to Microsoft’s 2015 acquisition of the Havok physics engine. We find that developers with higher pre-acquisition dependence on Havok are more likely to release games on Microsoft after the acquisition. In contrast, developers with higher dependence on both Havok and Microsoft become less likely to introduce games on Microsoft, but more likely to multihome across competing platforms. These results demonstrate that technology acquisition can both attract and deter complementors, depending on their dependence on the technology and the platform, highlighting the importance of accounting for technology–platform interdependence in platform-based markets.

Too Open to Thrive? How Platform Openness Shapes New Entrant Performance

TIM
Yue Wang (Not Associated); Anthea (Yan) Zhang (Rice University)
Increasing the level of openness can facilitate value creation on a platform by offering a diverse collection of complementary goods that attract and retain users. However, whether complements themselves can benefit from a more open platform design remains elusive. As most studies have investigated how and when platform openness benefits or hurts incumbent complements, new entrants that are most directly involved and greatly impacted by openness designs are long overlooked. To address this gap, this paper examines the performance implications of platform openness on new entrants. I propose two underlying mechanisms: the selection effect and the competition effect. The former arises as openness induces an influx of low-quality complements, which reduces their performance. The latter describes a large volume of newcomers intensifying the competitive pressure, resulting in lower performance. To empirically test the mechanisms, I use an openness policy change on Steam, which removed entry barriers for game developers and was followed by a sharp increase in the number of new games, particularly low-quality ones. Leverage this unique setting, I employ a novel empirical design to disentangle the selection effect and competition effect, revealing the performance implications of platform openness through each mechanism respectively. Findings show a negative net effect of increased platform openness on new entrants’ performance, and both competition and selection contribute to the results. This paper extends literature on platform governance strategy and on-platform complement competition. Managerial implications for entrepreneurs and platform designers are discussed.
