Organization Resilience Through Stability and Flexibility: Preparing for Future Market Downturns

SIM
Yu-Hsiang (John) Huang (Georgia College and State University); Andrew Paul Bryant (University of North Carolina Wilmington); Jennifer Griffin (Loyola University Chicago)
International tourist hotels play a critical role in Taiwan’s hospitality industry but faced a substantial downturn due to COVID-19, testing resilience. We posit that pre-existing stability limits losses while flexibility enables adaptation, leading to stronger recovery. This study tests resilience theory across 51 hotels (2020–2022), finding those with prior stability and flexibility outperformed rivals, even when controlling for size and price. We make two contributions. First, embedded traits like stability and flexibility, while in tension, mitigate downturns. Second, we define resilience as a stronger recovery toward a new normal, not a return to prior conditions as might be required with environmental or technological disruptions. Managerial and scholarly implications are discussed.

Carrying Others, Breaking Self: Ethical Provisioning and Resource Depletion in Crisis Economies

SIM
Asghar Afshar Jahanshahi (IPADE Business School)
When economies falter, those who care for others often break first. In many Global South contexts, chronic macroeconomic volatility including inflation, currency erosion, and rising living costs, creates persistent resource scarcity for employees, offering a critical setting for examining how financial obligations shape workplace behavior. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory and ethical responsibility perspectives, this multi-wave study investigates how employees’ financial responsibility load-the number of dependents they feel morally and financially obligated to support-affects job performance through perceived economic hardship and displaced aggression, and whether these processes differ by gender. Three-wave data were collected from 243 employees in Iran, a context marked by prolonged instability and household-level resource depletion. Supervisor-rated performance was obtained in a forth wave. Results show that financial responsibility load indirectly reduces job performance through heightened perceived economic hardship and increased displaced aggression. A sequential loss pattern also emerged: heavier responsibility intensified hardship, which escalated aggression, ultimately undermining job performance. Moreover, men who perceived greater hardship exhibited significantly stronger increases in aggression than women, reflecting gendered cultural expectations around financial provision. Together, these findings reveal a self-reinforcing ethical vulnerability, employees who shoulder greater moral obligations face greater psychological strain, behavioral costs, and long-term performance disadvantages. We discuss implications for ethical responsibility, organizational support, and employee well-being in environments shaped by chronic resource strain.

Keep Going When Your Community Needs You? Political Polarization and Social Enterprise Resilience

SIM
Kevin Curran (University of Amsterdam); Marton Gera (Rotterdam School of Management); Sumeet Malik (University of Amsterdam)
Social enterprises play a key role in addressing societal challenges through commercial activity, yet their dependence on broad, heterogeneous stakeholder support can make them especially fragile when external conditions shift. Prior work shows that external shocks can threaten these hybrid organizations or, at times, strengthen them by mobilizing support. This tension motivates a core question: under what conditions does the same shock reduce resilience for some social enterprises while enhancing it for others? We examine political polarization as an increasingly important external shock and theorize a conditional, double-edged effect. We argue that polarization raises dissolution risk nationally by increasing weakened support infrastructure. Yet polarization can also be associated with greater persistence in highly polarized communities, where local needs become more salient and organizational commitment becomes anchored in place-based relationships. Using the Brexit referendum as a natural experiment and longitudinal data on UK Community Interest Companies, we find that polarization increases dissolution overall but is associated with lower dissolution in highly polarized communities despite reduced profits. Our results extend research on social enterprises and reveal how political polarization can generate both fragility and localized resilience rooted in community-anchored commitment.

From Creeping Crisis to Existential Threat: Management of Prolonged Extreme Contexts

SIM
Diane Sophie Owin; Karin Kreutzer (EBS University of Business and Law)
In an era marked by prolonged and overlapping crises, organizational threats increasingly unfold as creeping crises that unfold slowly, lack clear beginnings, and evade timely recognition. Unlike for acute, time-bounded crises, traditional crisis management models, largely designed for fast-burning, high-urgency events, struggle to recognize and respond to such slowly escalating threats. This paper investigates how a creeping crisis can deepen and ultimately turn into an existential threat through the fragmented construction of urgency across organizational levels. Drawing on a three-year ethnographic study of a large, decentralized non-profit health and social care organization in Germany, we analyze how leaders and employees experience and manage prolonged extreme contexts shaped by resource scarcity, post-COVID turbulence, and systemic pressures within the healthcare system. Our findings reveal a two-phase process model. In the first phase, prolonged exposure to crisis leads to the normalization of extreme contexts, reshaping urgency thresholds through flatlining, disengagement despite recognition, and adapted urgency scales. In the second phase, we identify four recurring leadership patterns that reinforce this fragmentation: strategic detachment at the board level, responsibility displacement at central institutions, absorptive burden at location-level leadership, and resigned normalization at the team level. Together, these patterns form a reinforcing loop that undermines coordinated action and delays escalation, allowing a manageable creeping crisis to intensify into an existential organizational threat. This paper contributes to crisis management and organization studies by theorizing urgency as a socially constructed, temporally dynamic, and structurally fragmented accomplishment in decentralized organizations facing prolonged crises.

Ethical Adhocracies: Collective Memory and Moral Organization of Crisis Response

SIM
Matthew Carl Coetzee (University of Notre Dame); Jeff Cai (University of Notre Dame)
We develop a framework for understanding how organizations reconstruct moral order amid institutional collapse by introducing the concept of ethical adhocracies, improvised organizational formations through which business and civic leaders transform inherited moral repertoires into collective coordination. Drawing on 120 interviews from the aftermath of South Africa’s July 2021 unrest, the country’s most extensive civil violence since its democratic transition from apartheid, we compare two regions of Durban that faced the same crisis but produced opposing moral outcomes. In Durban North, racialized memories of past victimization fueled defensive vigilance and exclusion. In Durban West, plural memories of cooperation enabled cross-racial solidarity, de-escalation, and civic repair. Using an abductive, mixed-methods design that combines qualitative analysis with theory-guided large-language-model (LLM)–assisted corpus structuring, event extraction, and semantic embedding, we trace how moral coordination follows a pathway from collective memory to resource mobilization, action templates, and boundary outcomes of inclusion or exclusion. Our findings show that organizations can serve as the last functioning infrastructures of moral life: their interpretive labor determines whether crisis hardens divisions or renews solidarity. By conceptualizing ethical adhocracies as the organizational form of moral improvisation, this study extends business ethics beyond compliance and culture, identifying how leaders mobilize memory, infrastructure, and narrative to re-compose the civil sphere under rupture.
