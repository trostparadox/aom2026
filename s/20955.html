Session overview

Entrepreneurship is widely recognized as a driver of innovation, economic growth, and social development (Dabla-Norris, Kochhar, Suphaphiphat, Ricka & Tsounta, 2015). This recognition is largely due to the impact of startups, especially those with a focus on growth, that create innovative and transformative products, services, and processes. These activities, in turn, expand the government tax base and generate new employment opportunities (Chowdhury, Audretsch & Belitski, 2018; Sobel, 2008). However, only few new businesses transform into firms that have meaningful contribution to the economy (Kauffmann Foundation, 2016) and evidence shows that the majority of these new business are female-led new businesses (Global Entrepreneurship Monitor, 2025). Current research on women’s entrepreneurship has shown that social forces influence female and male entrepreneurship in different ways. In particular, institutions play a critical role because they are “humanly devised constraints that structure political, economic, and social interaction” (North, 1990 p.3). Informal institutions are defined by socially shared norms, which are usually unwritten (e.g., culture, values, codes of conduct), while formal institutions are clearly written rules communicated through official channels (e.g., law, policies, regulations; (North, 1990)). Informal and formal institutions are difficult to change, and individuals, especially women, have limited control over them (Brush, Bruin & Welter, 2009). Evidence shows that informal institutions in the form of culture and social norms primarily affect female entrepreneurs, while male entrepreneurs are usually little influenced by them (e.g., Bullough, Renko & Abdelzaher, 2017; Gimenez-Jimenez, Edelman, Dawson & Calabrò, 2022; Hechavarría & Brieger, 2022). Other research has shown that formal institutions in the form of family policies have a negative influence on female participation in entrepreneurship (Estrin & Mickiewicz, 2011), thereby making entrepreneurship a plan B for females in the labor market (Thébaud, 2015). Institutions also interact and have different effects on female and male entrepreneurship (Gimenez-Jimenez, Calabrò & Urbano, 2020) Evidence shows that women represent two out of five early-stage entrepreneurs (Elam et al., 2021). However, female start-up rates vary significantly across countries. Much of this variation can be explained by structural inequalities in countries’ institutions. Structural inequality refers to a situation in which dominant institutions confer advantages on some social groups but disadvantages on others (Amis, Mair & Munir, 2020). The objective of this paper symposium is to foster a discussion on women and institutions in entrepreneurship, with a particular focus on how they mutually shape and are shaped by broader contextual factors, and on where gender as a theoretical construct matters across different levels of entrepreneurial ecosystems. Because institutions constitute the “rules of the game” (North, 1990) and both produce and reproduce gender relations (Mabsout & van Staveren, 2010), they shape entrepreneurial experiences differently for men and women, while also generating heterogeneity among women entrepreneurs along lines of race and class. Given that gender is less visible at the institutional level, we believe that there are differential effects which are worthy of exploration.
