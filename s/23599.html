Black Enterprise and Cultural Economies

Session overview

This session examines how inequality shapes entrepreneurial growth, cultural production, and access to financial support. Spanning Black-owned businesses, Black music ecosystems, artist incubation, prosocial crowdlending, and discrimination in entrepreneurial finance, the papers reveal how market systems both constrain and enable marginalized entrepreneurs. The session offers practical insight into how more equitable ecosystems might be designed and sustained.

Papers (5)

A Longitudinal Analysis of Equity-Granting Incubation for Artist Entrepreneurs

DEI
Charlie Wall-Andrews (Toronto Metropolitan University)
Entrepreneurial incubators increasingly adopt equity-oriented designs to address persistent inequalities in entrepreneurial ecosystems, yet little is known about how equity-granting incubation translates into market-facing outcomes, particularly in cultural industries. This study examines the association of an equity-granting music incubator on artist-entrepreneurs’ platform performance, focusing on how structural marginalization and identity expression jointly shape post-incubation outcomes. Drawing on data from six cohorts (N = 125), we analyze changes in Spotify Monthly Listeners and Followers in the six months before and twelve months after incubation. The findings reveal a clear asymmetry in outcomes: while equity-granting incubation is associated with broad increases in audience commitment across identity configurations, gains in audience reach are more selective. Artists who both occupy marginalized positions and explicitly express identity experience the strongest and most consistent improvements across both metrics. These results suggest that equity-granting incubation operates as a conditional enabler rather than a uniform accelerator, with effects that depend on how equity is rendered legible to audiences through identity expression.

Black Business Growth: The Roles of Entrepreneur Characteristics & Discriminatory Industry Practices

DEI
Natasha R Conley (Case Western Reserve University); Diana Bilimoria (Case Western Reserve University); James Gaskin (Brigham Young University)
To date, the growth of Black-owned small businesses across the United States remains understudied. The present study quantitatively examines the factors influencing the business growth of 298 Black entrepreneurs, focusing on their individual characteristics and perceptions of discriminatory industry practices. The entrepreneur characteristics we study focus on the entrepreneur’s perseverance and faith, as well as the shared vision they are able to inculcate in their organizations, to influence their business growth. We also investigate the role of discriminatory business practices in limiting Black business growth. Our findings indicate that Black business growth is negatively associated with discriminatory business practices and positively associated with entrepreneur faith. Shared vision mediated the relationship between entrepreneur faith and Black business growth, while entrepreneurial perseverance amplified the relationship between shared vision and Black business growth and dampened the relationship between discriminatory business practices and Black business growth. The theoretical and practical implications of the study are discussed.

Rhythms and Roadblocks: Black Music’s Cultural and Economic Power in Canada

DEI
Charlie Wall-Andrews (Toronto Metropolitan University); Wendy Cukier; Mohamed Elmi (Toronto Metropolitan University)
This study examines how Black music significantly shapes Canada’s cultural and economic landscape while illuminating systemic challenges that hinder Black music professionals. A mixed-methods design integrated survey data from 1,702 respondents with market analyses, revealing that Black music routinely occupies over half of Canada’s top-streamed songs. Yet barriers—including limited funding, underrepresentation in leadership, and discrimination —undermine Black professionals’ career trajectories, creative output, and well-being. Intersectional factors particularly affect women, highlighting the need for targeted solutions. Overall, the research underscores the urgency of equitable policies, inclusive organizational practices, and anti-racist interventions to fully harness Black music’s contributions to Canada’s arts sector.

Intermediation, Marginalized Borrowers, and Social Orientation in Prosocial Crowdlending

DEI
Ronny Manos; Amir Shoham; Shlomo Yedidia Tarba (Birmingham Business School); Martin Meyer (University of Vaasa)
Advances in technology and the broader democratization of finance have shifted an increasing share of microfinance activity to lending-based crowdfunding platforms, forming part of the prosocial crowdfunding ecosystem. Some platforms adopt a pure peer-to-peer (P2P) model, while others use a hybrid approach in which P2P lending coexists with intermediated lending through microfinance organizations. P2P lending provides a direct connection between lenders and borrowers and reduces intermediation costs, raising the question of how these two models coexist and why intermediated lending remains supported. Building on research in prosocial crowdfunding, signalling theory, and altruistic and warm-glow motivations, we investigate how these dynamics unfold. Our analysis sheds new light on the role of intermediation in enhancing financial inclusion and improving access to funding for marginalized microentrepreneurs.

Evidence of Bias? A Systematic Review and Critical Assessment of the Literature on Discrimination

DEI
Ebru Sarikaya (University of Mannheim); Christoph Sajons (University of Mannheim); Bettina Müller; Lilith Burgstaller
Access to external capital varies substantially across groups of entrepreneurs, including women, ethnic minorities, migrants and other groups often considered disadvantaged. Yet whether these disparities reflect systematic discrimination remains empirically unresolved due to substantial identification challenges. In this paper, we conduct a systematic review of 85 scientific articles published between 2000 and 2023, sort them by their focus (lender or borrower behavior or market outcomes), and assess their research design, data, and inference strategy. We show that most studies rely on observational data that lack key determinants of lending decisions, preventing credible causal conclusions. Among the smaller set of experimental and quasi-experimental studies, findings remain inconclusive, with some identifying discrimination in entrepreneurial finance whereas others do not. We discuss and synthesize these results, highlight the key methodological obstacles for credible inference, and outline promising directions for future research.
