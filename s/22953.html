Speaking to Multiple Masters: How Communicative Elasticity Shapes Social Enterprise Outcomes

OMT
Hadi Shaheen (College of Charleston); Jean-Baptiste Litrico (Smith School of Business, Queen's University)
Hybrid organizations combining social and commercial logics face a fundamental challenge in communicating their dual identity to stakeholders with competing interests. While prior research has examined internal mechanisms for sustaining hybridity, we lack an understanding of how organizations manage external communication to diverse audiences who champion conflicting institutional logics. Drawing on longitudinal analysis of institutional logics of three nascent social enterprises, we extend the concept of elastic hybridity from internal organizational dynamics to external stakeholder communication. We introduce communicative elasticity as an organization's capacity to represent multiple institutional logics in external communication, enabling potential resonance with diverse stakeholders who champion competing values. We identify three communicative patterns with varying degrees of elasticity: rigid single-logic communication, sequential logic representation over time, and simultaneous multi-logic representation. Business model structure influences which institutional logic is more visible in organizational operations, thereby shaping the level of communicative elasticity required to acquire resources. Our findings reveal a paradox: business models that embed social mission most visibly create communicative rigidity that threatens organizational survival. While prior research focuses on mission drift, we identify "mission lock-in" as an equally risky yet less-studied mode. We contribute to research on hybrid organizations while raising ethical questions about the distinction between authenticity and manipulation in organizational communication.

Selling Virtue, Selling Vice: Entrepreneuring in Moralized Markets

OMT
Amrit Panda (University of Arkansas); Andrew B. Blake (Texas Tech University); Mahija Ratnoo (University of Oklahoma); Oleg V. Petrenko (University of Oklahoma); Inder Surrendar Valasai (The University of Texas at Arlington)
Markets are moral arenas as much as economic ones, yet research still tends to treat morality as an external constraint on otherwise value-neutral exchange. This paper develops a pluralist framework for moralized markets, where economic value depends primarily on contested moral meaning rather than functional attributes. We argue that moral pluralism creates systematic conditions for profit and venture creation by making the same practice appear virtuous to some audiences and intolerable to others. At the field level, we specify three mechanisms that generate moralized market conditions: moral reclassification, structural gaps, and institutional realignments. At the venture level, we theorize four modes of entrepreneurial work through which entrepreneurs translate contested moral landscapes into entrepreneurial action: moral alignment, counter-moralization, moral assertion, and moral leveraging. The framework advances opportunity theory by treating moralized conditions as structured sources of entrepreneurial possibilities, and it clarifies how entrepreneurs shape market trajectories by stabilizing, contesting, or extending prevailing moral orders.

Sequences of Intermediation: Ideological Orientations in the Evolution of Nascent Social Enterprises

OMT
Karthik Rapaka (Erasmus University Rotterdam); Tal Simons (Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University)
Founders of nascent social enterprises routinely navigate intermediaries (incubators and accelerators) sponsored by governments, corporations, academia, and philanthropies. While prior research has acknowledged the heterogeneity of sponsorship types, we know little about how engagements with multiple intermediaries could influence the means and outcomes pursued by the social enterprises over time. Hence, we ask: How do sequences of intermediary engagements shape the evolution of nascent social enterprises? We draw on a 24-months ethnographic study of an Indian education-sector incubation program and a longitudinal analysis of its cohort of eight organizations, tracing their participation across ten distinct intermediaries over the following 36 months. Our findings show that social enterprises evolve through three distinct intermediation sequences – plateauing, escalating, and leapfrogging, depending on how their relationships with intermediaries align over time. When neoliberal expectations for speed and scale clash with humanistic commitments to community relationships and democratic participation, such ideological misalignments often lead to organizational closure. However, when these misalignments recur, some enterprises in the escalating sequence still grow, but their trajectories become refracted through newer expectations for speed, reach, or efficiency. In contrast, we find that sustained alignment on neoliberal ideologies between enterprises and intermediaries generates compounded growth in the leapfrogging sequence. We show that success depends not on single moments of support but on the cumulative and evolving alignment between enterprises and the intermediaries that shape their development. We contribute to scholarship on organizational sponsorship and entrepreneurial ecosystems by showing how new enterprise legitimation unfolds not in isolated episodes but through cumulative and ideologically mediated sequences of intermediation that determine which organizations survive, adapt, or thrive.

Bittersweet Company: Relational Frames and Entrepreneurial Pursuit in a Deregulated Cocoa Industry

OMT
Amrita Saha (University of Toronto)
Why do some microentrepreneurs in emerging economies pursue new entrepreneurial opportunities after reforms lift constraints, while others do not? This study shows that the answer lies not only in material resources or motivation, but in how actors navigate the risks associated with becoming more visible to others. Drawing on an inductive, longitudinal study of smallholder cocoa farmers in Trinidad and Tobago after industry deregulation, I develop a model of how relational frames—cultural tools that guide whom to engage with in economic activities and how—shape entrepreneurial pursuit. Before deregulation, farmers relied on a hierarchical relational frame that supported predictable exchanges with economic alters and buffered them from resource-extracting stakeholders, such as criminals and corrupt authorities. Deregulation dismantled the institutional structure that supported this frame and introduced new economic alters, forcing farmers to renegotiate the social rules of exchange. Most retained the hierarchical frame, did not learn how to engage with new economic alters, and thus ultimately exited cocoa. In contrast, a small subset built relational spaces with new economic alters. Within these spaces, they revised their frame toward co creation and thus pursued and sustained new cocoa based entrepreneurial activities. This study contributes to organizational theories of entrepreneurship in the Global South by highlighting the interpretive work through which entrepreneurs (re)construct relational certainty in changing markets.

Chaotic Lifeworlds, Generational Units of Entrepreneurs, and Institutional Work (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Jianan Li (Towson University)
How do technological and cultural entrepreneurs respond to their chaotic lifeworlds and thereby refashioning institutions? To tackle these questions, we begin by arguing that chaotic lifeworlds set the parameters, interpretable in terms of the lived experience shared by a generational cohort. Specifically, drawing on material and rhetorical historiographies, we propose that chaotic lifeworlds provide emerging new resources regarding the lived experience of materiality and discursivity. Second, we employ Mannheim’s account of generational units in social change to argue that generational units of technological entrepreneurs and cultural entrepreneurs deploy those resources to respond to the ambient chaos. Finally, we discuss the interplay between these generational units of entrepreneurs and show how their symbiotic/defensive dynamics fundamentally reshape their inhabited institutions’ outlooks. Our model and representative cases demonstrate a hermeneutic cycle of an institutional becoming process and show how this cycle’s catalyst lies in the technology-culture nexus among generational units of entrepreneurs.
