An Overview Of Women’s Entreprneurship: Challenges and Spirituality in the Malian Context

ENT
DOUYON ROSALIE (Lyon Catholic University)
Women’s entrepreneurship is an important driver of development in emerging countries. This article reviews the literature and presents the results of a qualitative study on women's entrepreneurship in Mali (West Africa). The findings show that, despite numerous challenges, women persevere and are able to create their own businesses. Women face several challenges, such as the lack of funding, social representation, deeply rooted social norms, gender stereotypes, lack of training, and impostor syndrome. These factors, taken together, hinder the development of women’s entrepreneurship in the Malian context. Nevertheless, spirituality, through faith in God and religious practices, helps women persevere and embark on the entrepreneurial journey.

Navigating Norms, Expanding Boundaries: Digital Affordances and Women’s Entrepreneurial Agency

ENT
Lalarukh Ejaz (Institute of Business Administration); Vadim Grinevich (University of Bradford); Shehryar Shahid (Lahore University of Management Sciences)
Digital technologies are widely recognized for the opportunities they can open up for women entrepreneurs. Yet we know far less about how salient family logics direct women’s attention to particular digital technologies and affordances, and how this attentional focus shapes entrepreneurial action across institutional domains. This omission is striking because many constraints confronting women entrepreneurs stem from gendered identities, social stigma, and routinized family practices across societies. To address this gap, we analyze 47 interviews with women entrepreneurs in Pakistan, where a strong family logic coexists with rapid digital transformation. Integrating institutional logics and affordance theory, we show that digital affordances can operate as infrastructures of agency, enabling women to craft meaningful entrepreneurial pathways within, alongside, and at times beyond the normative contours of the family institution. We conceptualize two patterns of actionable possibility: (a), in which digital tools facilitate entrepreneurship while remaining aligned with salient family logics, and (b), in which they extend reach and unsettle normative boundaries, generating both continuity and change. We contribute to entrepreneurship theory by offering a granular account of how women entrepreneurs navigate family logics while engaging with market and other institutional domains. By situating digital affordances within a layered institutional environment, we also strengthen underdeveloped theoretical connections between institutional logics and affordance theory.

Power in the Pack: How Women’s Groups Legitimize Rural Women’s Entrepreneurship in Patriarchal India

ENT
Ambreen Saleem (GLA University); Imran Saleem
Women’s entrepreneurial activity is growing globally; however, in patriarchal rural contexts, women continue to face severe constraints. These arise from restrictive gender norms, moral scrutiny, limited mobility, weak institutional access, and intersectional stigma linked to caste and marital status. While existing research has extensively documented these barriers, considerably less attention has been paid to how rural women navigate them in practice. Moreover, women’s groups are typically conceptualised as instrumental mechanisms for accessing finance, skills, and networks, leaving their broader social role underexplored. This study addresses this gap by examining how women’s groups enable rural women’s entrepreneurship in patriarchal settings by generating collective legitimacy. Drawing on the contextualising entrepreneurship framework and feminist separatist theory, the study is based on a qualitative case analysis of 17 women’s groups in rural Barabanki, Uttar Pradesh. Data were collected through 52 semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions, and non-participant observations and analysed using abductive thematic analysis within a critical reflexive methodology. The findings demonstrate that women’s groups function as collective legitimacy devices, transforming women’s entrepreneurial activity from a moral risk into a socially sanctioned practice. Collective participation legitimises women’s public presence and mobility, neutralises stigma associated with widowhood and caste, enhances institutional credibility, improves market access, and strengthens resilience during crisis, thereby enabling sustained entrepreneurial participation. By introducing the concept of collective legitimacy, this study advances contextual and feminist entrepreneurship scholarship. It offers important policy implications for designing entrepreneurship interventions that support women’s groups as vital social institutions, not merely as financial intermediaries.

Resilience as a Relational Process: How Women Entrepreneurs Confront Gendered Entrepreneurial Stigma

ENT
Simona Grama -Vigouroux (SCBS – South Champagne Business School); ARBIA CHATMI (SCBS – South Champagne Business School); KARIM ELASRI (KEDGE Business School - Marseille); SAMBE MAME FATOU mamefatou.sambe@kedgebs.com (KEDGE Business School - Marseille)
The resilience of women entrepreneurs facing gendered entrepreneurial stigma embedded in cognitive, normative, and regulatory institutional practices remains underexplored. Drawing on an inductive qualitative study of 14 women entrepreneurs in West Africa (Ghana, Mauritania, Senegal), complemented by 878 pages of secondary data, we show that resilience is constructed through collective meaning making. It emerges from the interplay between individual factors (stigma perception, social identity, cognitive reappraisal) and community level influences rooted in formal and informal networks. We conceptualize resilience as a relational, interactional process shaped by dynamic exchanges between individual agency and context, and derive implications for women entrepreneurs, policymakers, and educators by emphasizing collective meaning making to support effective coping strategies.

The Role of Kudumbashree in Uplifting Rural Women Through Collective Entrepreneurship

ENT
Janvi Patel (Indian Institute of Management Sambalpur); Anubha Shekhar Sinha
This study examines how collective entrepreneurship operates as a structuring process in Kudumbashree, Kerala, India’s state-led poverty eradication and women empowerment programme. Drawing on qualitative data from in-depth interviews and participant observation among women from a Scheduled Tribe (ST) community, the study moves beyond outcome-focused accounts of collective entrepreneurship to explain how entrepreneurial agency is enabled and constrained through everyday practices. Using structuration theory as an analytical lens, the findings show that collective entrepreneurship unfolds through a recursive process involving institutional entry, routinized financial practices, differentiated participation, collective discipline, and engagement with external institutions. While collective arrangements lower barriers to entrepreneurial participation and stabilize livelihoods, they also generate new forms of constraint through collective liability, administrative burden, and uneven capability distribution. Empowerment thus emerges as conditional and relational, produced through repeated enactment of collective rules and resources rather than as a singular outcome. By theorizing collective entrepreneurship as an ongoing structuring process rather than a fixed organizational outcome, this study contributes to entrepreneurship and organization theory by demonstrating how empowerment and constraint are co-produced through collective action in marginalized contexts. Keywords: Collective entrepreneurship; Structuration theory; Rural women; Scheduled Tribes; Kudumbashree; Qualitative research; India
