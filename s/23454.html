Disentangling Product-Market and Supply-Chain Overlap in Coopetitive Alliance Innovation

TIM
Chiung-Yi Hwang (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University); Yong Zheng Liaw (National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University)
Why do some rival alliances spark breakthrough innovation while others fail catastrophically? Multimarket contact theory predicts that overlapping in multiple markets enables mutual forbearance through cross-market retaliation, yet cannot explain contradictory innovation outcomes. We show this stems from conflating two distinct dimensions: product-market overlap (shared customers activating observable retaliation) and supply-chain overlap (shared value-chain positions creating imitation threats). Analyzing semiconductor alliances (2005–2019), we uncover sharply contrasting curvilinear effects of supply-chain overlap. When partners serve different markets, moderate supply-chain overlap aids knowledge integration, but extreme overlap triggers invasion fears that weak cross-market deterrence cannot contain—benefits collapse sharply. When partners compete in the same markets, supply-chain overlap generates steep innovation gains as robust mutual forbearance disciplines imitation risks—benefits plateau gracefully despite redundancy. Knowledge newness amplifies risks in the former but remains manageable in the latter. These findings reconceptualize multimarket contact as multidimensional governance infrastructure, specifying when coopetition succeeds versus fails.

Green Innovation Selection Decisions in R&D Partnerships with Competitors

TIM
Thomas Draschbacher (Graz University of Technology); Anne-Sophie Fernandez; Paul Chiambaretto
In order to meet knowledge, investment, and coordination requirements in the development of green product innovation (GPI), companies increasingly choose to cooperate with competitors in so-called “co-opetitive” relationships. While much of the existing literature has focused on partner selection processes in this context, we still do not know how innovation managers select GPI projects once such partnerships have been established. Building on a distinction between different risks and benefits, we develop hypotheses regarding managers’ GPI selection rationale and test these hypotheses in a choice-based conjoint experiment in the United States. We collected 960 decisions among GPI profiles that differ in environmental benefits across lifecycle stages, development and commercialization risks and costs, and reputational potential. Our results show that, in contrast to suggestions made by the literature on conventional innovation, in coopetitive partnerships, firms strongly prefer GPI projects with low development and commercialization risks and costs, and with environmental benefits in all but the consumption phase. Further, companies prefer GPI projects that possess characteristics suitable to strengthen firms’ green identity and environmental legitimacy. These findings illustrate the necessity to rethink classical theoretical arguments in the context of sustainability and help us in refining theories of innovation project selection in R&D partnerships in general and coopetition in particular.

Is Business Similarity a Boon or a Bane for Coopetitors’ Innovation Outcomes Under Turmoil?

TIM
Kang-Ting Wang (National Taiwan University); Wenyi Chu (National Taiwan University)
This study examines whether business similarity between coopetitors is a boon or a bane for their innovation outcomes under turmoil, understood here as high environmental unpredictability. Focusing on ambidextrous innovation, we investigate how coopetitor business similarity relates to ambidextrous innovation and firm profitability, as well as the potential moderating effect of environmental unpredictability on the link between coopetitor business similarity and ambidextrous innovation. Using a multi-source archival dataset of 111 coopetitive firms, our results show that coopetitor business similarity positively affects ambidextrous innovation and that ambidextrous innovation fully mediates the connection between coopetitor business similarity and firm profitability. We further find that the advantageous effect of coopetitor business similarity on ambidextrous innovation is stronger under high environmental unpredictability. Overall, these findings highlight the critical role of business similarity in shaping coopetitive dynamics, thereby becoming an important driving force influencing ambidextrous innovation and firm profitability, particularly under environmental unpredictability.

Reconfiguring Attention in Business Groups: Digital Transformation's Impact on Subsidiary Innovation

TIM
Zhe Sun (Jilin University); Jingjing Wang (Iowa State University); Liang Zhao (University of Strathclyde); Arvids A. Ziedonis (Boston University)
Drawing on the attention-based view (ABV), we develop an analytical framework that links digital transformation, contextual attention reconfiguration, and subsidiary innovation autonomy. Using panel data from Chinese A-share listed business groups from 2013 to 2023, we examine how parent firms' digital transformation reshapes the contextual attention structure within business groups and, in turn, influences subsidiaries' innovation autonomy. Our findings reveal that parent firms' digital transformation significantly enhances subsidiaries' innovation autonomy, particularly through decentralized allocation of decision-making rights within the group and heightened collaborative innovation among subsidiaries. Moreover, patent transfers and improved resource allocation efficiency amplify the positive impact of digital transformation on subsidiary innovation autonomy. These effects are more pronounced in state-owned enterprises (SOEs), large business groups, and subsidiaries with higher cash holdings.

Repositioning Strategies in Response to Partner Technological Invasion in Strategic Alliances

TIM
Li Tian (Nankai University); Jinlei Yu (Nankai University); Rui Chen (Nankai University)
In strategic alliances, collaboration and competition coexist; this coexistence gives rise to tensions when partners engage in technological invasion. Drawing on the perspectives of strategic repositioning and coopetition literatures, this study investigates how focal firms respond to alliance partners’ invasion of their technological domains. We develop a formal model to examine the trade-offs between two repositioning strategies and argue that relational embeddedness, in conjunction with alliance network stability, jointly shapes firms’ strategic responses. Using a dataset of alliances among Chinese A-share listed firms from 2010 to 2023, this study shows that firms with low relational embeddedness are more likely to adopt a “fight” strategy by increasing patent applications in the invaded technological domain, whereas highly embedded firms tend to pursue a flight strategy by reducing patent applications to preserve relational continuity. Furthermore, alliance network stability moderates these effects by amplifying the costs of embeddedness and dampening aggressive responses. This study contributes to alliance research by highlighting the foundations of strategic repositioning under coopetitive tension and extends repositioning research to alliance dynamics.
