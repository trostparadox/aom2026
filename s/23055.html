When MNEs Can Talk, They Opt for Less Control: Temporal Alignment, Entry Motivations, Equity Control (WITHDRAWN)

IM
Liang Wen (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Jingyu Yang (The University of Sydney); Stefan Volk (The University of Sydney)
This study develops a communication-based perspective on multinational enterprise (MNE) equity strategy by introducing temporal alignment—the degree of overlap in work hours between MNE parents and subsidiaries—as a foundational enabler of intra-MNE communication. Drawing on Media Synchronicity Theory (MST), we argue that greater temporal alignment enhances the feasibility of real-time, synchronous communication, thereby reducing the need for equity-based control. Conversely, limited temporal alignment impairs convergence communication, increasing coordination challenges and driving firms to rely more heavily on equity-based control. We further posit that this relationship is moderated by the strategic motivation of the subsidiary: the negative effect of temporal alignment on equity control is stronger for knowledge-seeking subsidiaries and weaker for efficiency-seeking ones. Our empirical analysis, based on a comprehensive dataset of 218,916 subsidiaries of 5,947 Japanese MNEs (1991 to 2018), supports these hypotheses. These findings advance theories of international governance by positioning communication feasibility—rooted in daily work-time alignment—as a core determinant of MNEs’ global equity strategies.

Unveiling the Conceptual Tapestry of Institutional Dysfunctions

IM
Igor Tairan Mazzini (FURB - Fundação Universidade Regional de Blumenau); Christian Daniel Falaster (FURB - Fundação Universidade Regional de Blumenau); Manuel Anibal Portugal Ferreira; Ivan Hadlich (FURB - Fundação Universidade Regional de Blumenau)
Almost fifty years have passed since Meyer, Rowan, and Zucker laid the foundations of neo-institutionalism, which is now predominant in management studies. Many researchers have examined and expanded on the role of institutions and institutional quality in shaping economic and social outcomes and market functioning. However, scholars have used different terminology, possibly reflecting differing understandings of the multifaceted influence of institutions and their dysfunctions, what these terms mean, and how they are best evaluated. On institutional dysfunctions, a range of labels — such as institutional voids, failures, inefficiencies, deficiencies, weaknesses, barriers, and obstacles — have been used without clear conceptual distinctions, leaving the extant literature riddled with ambiguities and providing but a blurred understanding of current thinking about institutional dysfunctions, measurements, and effects. This article reviews the literature, mapping 12 recurring concepts and classifying them along a spectrum of scope, from broad notions such as institutional constraints to more specific conceptualizations, such as obstacles and barriers. We thus disentangle constructs, thereby advancing the theoretical and empirical development of institutional dysfunction.

Sub-National Variations in Labor Laws and MNE FDI Destination Decisions: Evidence from India

IM
Naga Lakshmi Damaraju (Sonoma State University); Snehal Awate; Shashwat Alok (Indian School of Business); Rohit Yadav (Indian Institute of Technology Bombay)
Labor regulations are a fundamental institutional driver of foreign investors' location selection strategies. While previous studies focused on national-level institutions, we underscore the importance of subnational institutions by investigating the effect of variations in the labor regulations across states on multinational enterprises' (MNEs) foreign direct investment (FDI) destination decisions. In contrast to the prevailing "race-to-the-bottom" approach, our evidence indicates that MNEs favor progressive laws. By decomposing labor laws into employment protection and industrial dispute resolution, we demonstrate that MNEs prefer states with strong employment protection and effective dispute mechanisms. Using subnational data on the states in India, we suggest that the state governments may not need to trade-off MNE inventors and state’s human capital. The results point out a win-win for both stakeholders of the government.

Labour Market Reform and Export Performance:Analyzing the Effects of Labour Supply Structure Change

IM
Ludan Wu (Tsinghua University); Qi Chen (Peking University)
This paper examines the effects of labour market reform, specifically the Hukou system reform, on the export performance of firms in China. Using data from over 210 cities (out of 340+) that underwent Hukou reform between 2001 and 2010, we analyze the impact of changes in labour market mobility on firm-level export outcomes. The Hukou system, which had long restricted labour mobility, began to lose its effectiveness in the 1980s, and the subsequent reform provided a more flexible labour market environment for both employers and employees. Our empirical results show that the labour market shock significantly affected firms’ export performance, with impacts observed not only on the intensive margin but also on the extensive margin. Specif?ically, firms located in reformed cities experienced a notable decline in export performance, characterized by reductions in the price, quantity, and quality of export products. Moreover, the number of export destinations and the diversity of export products also decreased as a result of the Hukou reform.
