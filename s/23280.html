A Leap of Faith: How Religion Shapes Hybrid Organizing in a Social Enterprise (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Kanwal Bokhari (University of Calgary)
Hybrid organizations balancing social and market logics operate within institutional contexts where additional logics may be highly prevalent. While hybrid organizing scholarship has productively examined how organizations manage social-market tensions, this research has largely treated broader institutional context as backdrop. We lack understanding of how prevalent contextual logics—beyond social and market—shape hybrid organizing dynamics. Drawing on a qualitative case study of Akhuwat, an interest-free Islamic microfinance organization in Pakistan, we examine how religious prevalence shapes hybrid organizing. We find that religious prevalence creates a dual dynamic: it rules out conventional microfinance models (constraining) while simultaneously providing culturally-embedded concepts, material infrastructure, and legitimacy frameworks that enable alternative approaches to sustaining dual missions (enabling). We identify how organizations leverage contextually-provided resources—distinct from organizationally-constructed mechanisms emphasized in prior research—through three processes: boundary-setting, infrastructure provision, and superordinate legitimacy. Our findings advance hybrid organizing theory by demonstrating that challenges and resources for managing dual missions vary systematically across institutional contexts based on which logics are prevalent, explaining why hybrid organizing takes different forms in different institutional settings.

Exploring Hybrid Entrepreneurs’ Navigation Strategies Over Time

ENT
Johanna Kuske (University of Cologne); Matthias Schulz (University of Göttingen); Katharina Scheidgen; Gertraud Maria Gänser-Stickler (University of Cologne); Aysha Maria Keuthen (Georg August University Göttingen); Christian Schwens (University of Cologne)
Hybrid entrepreneurs simultaneously run a business and hold a wage job. Although research suggests that this role duality can be both beneficial and detrimental, we know little about how individuals navigate their two work-related roles. This study inductively analyzes 37 interviews with 22 hybrid entrepreneurs and identifies four navigation strategies that differ with respect to individuals’ cognitions (framing of the business, prioritizing roles), behaviors (employer relationship management, time management), and goals. We coin the navigation strategies as “make both count”, “put entrepreneurship first”, “keep it light”, and “wait and see”. Moreover, we find that hybrid entrepreneurs switch their navigation strategies when experiencing changing conditions outside entrepreneurship (i.e., in the wage job or private life), (lack of) entrepreneurial success, or individual learning about their personal fit. The study contributes to research on hybrid entrepreneurs’ motives, transitions, and use of time and cognitive resources.

Hybrid Business Models and Institutional Environment: A Multi-Country Analysis

ENT
Yuly Straub (Diego Portales University); Leslier Valenzuela (Universidad de Chile); Francisco Villegas
In the face of social and environmental crises, it becomes increasingly critical to study the institutional factors that foster hybrid entrepreneurship across countries to achieve the sustainability goals outlined in the 2030 Agenda. Accordingly, the primary objective of this research is to examine the impact of the institutional environment—comprising formal and informal factors, and particularly institutional voids and quality—on hybrid entrepreneurship. Utilizing multi-country secondary data from the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM), the SDG Index, and Hofstede's cultural values for the period 2021 to 2023. Based on an extensive literature review, the hypotheses derived from the proposed conceptual model will be tested through regression analyses to quantify the effects of direct, indirect, and moderating relationships proposed in the model. This study is pioneering in quantifying the impact of institutional voids and institutional quality on hybrid entrepreneurship. This research contributes to the advancement of theoretical, practical, and empirical knowledge regarding institutional voids, institutional quality and hybrid entrepreneurship.

Hybrid Entrepreneurship and Immigration: Pathways and Typologies of Nigerian Diaspora Entrepreneurs

ENT
Ekene Okwechime (Anglia Ruskin University)
For many, hybrid entrepreneurship, i.e., where individuals maintain paid employment while developing a business, offers a pathway to navigate uncertainty, manage risk, and test market viability. This study examines hybrid entrepreneurship within the Nigerian immigrant community, drawing on 23 in-depth interviews with entrepreneurs operating across sectors in the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States. The findings identify five immigration-centric factors shaping entry into hybrid entrepreneurship: institutional navigation and adaptation, resource access and mobilisation, risk and stability management, cultural identity and market connection, and personal and emotional motivation. I also develop a typology of a hybrid immigrant entrepreneur. The study extends work on rationales for hybrid entrepreneurship by revealing how migration experiences, cultural embeddedness, and institutional adaptation influence entrepreneurial entry and decision-making. By situating hybrid entrepreneurship within the broader literature on immigrant entrepreneurship, this research offers theoretical and practical insights into how immigrants create and sustain ventures while balancing the demands of paid employment in host-country contexts.

Mentoring Hybrid Entrepreneurs: An Exploration of the LIgbo Entrepreneurship Model from West Africa

ENT
Jorge Luis Arteaga (Mississippi State University); Kenneth O. Obunadike (Mississippi State University); Joyce Komakech Nabisaalu (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Matthew W. Rutherford (Oklahoma State University)
We explore the Igbo entrepreneurship model which is a unique form of mentorship support for hybrid entrepreneurs and that has been successful across Western Africa. Through the lens of imprinting theory, we complement research on relation-based support systems with the hybrid entrepreneurship literature. Data from 120 African hybrid entrepreneurs indicate that mentorship quality raises the likelihood to transition into full-time self-employment as individuals increase their entrepreneurial skills. The implication of this finding is that hybrid entrepreneurs rely on the guidance from experienced business owners to navigate the uncertainty in less developed markets where poverty, inequality, and financial constraints dominate the entrepreneurial landscape.
