Rare Disease Drug Accessibility: MNE Strategy in Emerging Markets

SIM
Diqiu Wang (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); William P. Wan (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Daphne W. Yiu (Singapore Management University)
The transnational nature of grand challenges has heightened the role of multinational enterprises (MNEs), demanding greater cross-border responsibilities in bridging institutional differences between advanced and emerging markets. Rare disease drug accessibility highlights such challenges: treatments are constrained not only by limited development but also by prohibitive post-launch costs. In emerging markets, weak reimbursement frameworks and inadequate institutional infrastructure further exacerbate these failures in accessibility. Situated within this critical context, this study theorizes a strategic perspective on MNE responses to accessibility initiatives in host emerging countries. We propose that MNEs exhibit greater responsiveness to rare diseases, which are characterized by more acute access failures than prevalent conditions, with such responsiveness conditioned by the firm’s global status and at-risk resources. This strategic responsiveness is amplified by global status, which plays a pivotal role in converting responsibility into reputation and legitimacy gains. Concomitantly it is constrained by at-risk resources, as MNEs redeploy de-risked global assets by leveraging temporal and spatial misalignments between global product lifecycle stages and local unmet needs, thus enabling societal contributions while mitigating cross-border risks. Our findings, based on a sample of foreign-developed innovative drugs and their engagement with local access initiatives in China, help demonstrate this strategic perspective through which MNEs proactively engage with cross-border challenges.

Favoring the Foreign: Regulatory Preferences in Kenya’s Nascent Electric Mobility Industry

SIM
Katharina Eichinger (Technical University of Munich); Mohammed Bendaanane (Technical University of Munich)
Research at the nexus of nonmarket strategy and entrepreneurship examines how ventures influence regulations. However, this literature tends to view regulators as impartial actors, focusing on how ventures deploy influencing strategies while overlooking who gains regulatory access in the first place. This assumption, likely appropriate in Global North contexts with established institutions, becomes problematic in Global South settings shaped by sociohistorical characteristics (e.g., colonial past, development interventions). Through a qualitative study of twelve foreign and local ventures in Kenya’s nascent electric mobility industry, we investigate why regulators favor foreign over local ventures in regulatory formation. We find that regulators view foreign ventures as more innovative, capable, and credible drivers of the transition from petrol to electric mobility, while downplaying local ventures’ capacity. Our findings suggest that this preference stems from cognitive biases rooted in sociohistorical hierarchies and resource factors that create visibility advantages for foreign ventures. Our findings show that regulatory access inequality tends to silence local voices in regulatory processes, enabling foreign ventures to gain disproportionate influence while constraining local ventures’ ability to shape regulations. We find that local ventures respond by leveraging indigenous institutions (e.g., saccos) to persist but face limited success in overcoming the regulatory disadvantage. Our study contributes to nonmarket strategy by revealing that regulatory access, determined by regulators’ a priori preferences, interests and cognitive schemas, precedes regulatory influence, and to liability of foreignness by showing that foreignness can be an asset in Global South regulatory arenas. Our findings carry important and timely practical implications, inviting regulators and ventures to critically examine cognitive biases and reimagine development pathways that value indigenous institutions rather than defaulting to Western templates.

Policy Mix Conditions for Circular City Transitions: Evidence from Brazil

SIM
Amanda Daniele de Carvalho (University of Sao Paulo); Bruna Grandi (University of Sao Paulo); Emre Cinar (University of Portsmouth); Diego Alfonso Vazquez (University of Portsmouth); Janaina Mascarenhas (University of Sao Paulo)
Transitions towards circular cities increasingly depend on the capacity of public policies to address interconnected urban challenges related to resource use, infrastructure, land use and governance. While a wide range of public policies already touch upon dimensions relevant to urban circularity, their co-existence does not necessarily translate into coherent or sustained support for circular urban transitions. This article examines how the configuration of a national public policy mix shapes the conditions under which circular city transitions unfold. Based on a qualitative analysis of national policy documents, the study analyses policy strategies and instruments relevant to urban circularity and examines how they combine as a policy mix. The findings reveal a pattern of selective strategic concentration alongside weak alignment between strategies and instruments, resulting in fragmented and uneven forms of support for circular urban transitions. By conceptualizing these patterns as policy mix conditions, the article contributes to the literature on policy mixes and sustainability transitions by showing how national policy configurations structure the institutional context within which circular city transitions are pursued.

Building Conditions for Impact: An Adaptive Model of Impact Investing in Weak Institutional Contexts

SIM
Felipe Ignacio Symmes (EGADE Business School); Maria Fernanda Figueroa Herrera (Facultad de Ciencias Empresariales, Universidad Austral); Marina Santucci (Austral University); M. Dolores Del Rio (Austral University)
Impact Investment (II) existing research is limited in two important respects. First, most studies have not sufficiently examined the foundations for choosing traditional SMEs as investees of II over explicitly social-purpose driven organizations, which is especially observed in low-income countries. Second, scholars increasingly suggest examining how II may contribute to the institutional domain, advancing over a merely quantitative and micro-level approach on impact, where intermediaries of II such as fund managers may play a very important role. We use an inclusive market lens to examine an inductive case study of Fundación de Inversión y Empresariado Social (IES) in Bolivia. Our analysis shows how IES shifted its focus from individuals in extreme poverty toward small and medium enterprises (SMEs) with scaling and growth potential, working mainly at the governance level and market skills with funders and managers to acknowledge the growth of their organizations as well as giving them access to conventional capital markets that exclude them. Our study generates three contributions. First, this study advances the impact investing (II) literature by proposing a process model that captures how II unfolds in challenging institutional environments by simultaneously fostering both the supply of and demand for capital among conventional small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), while also supporting the transformation of the capital markets that these firms may access to. Second, it highlights the pivotal role of intermediaries such as fund managers in helping SMEs overcome structural barriers to business development in low-income and institutionally fragile contexts by not only providing financial capital and individualized managerial support to SMEs but also actively shaping more inclusive market access to these SMEs. Third, it bridges impact investing (II) with inclusive market outcomes, a contribution that is particularly salient in low-income countries characterized by profound institutional weaknesses, showing how II navigates the trade-offs between financial performance, competitiveness, and inclusion that define these contexts.

Chance, Risk Engineering, and Moral Hazard

SIM
Tobey Scharding (Rutgers Business School)
Is it ever unethical for venture capital (VC) firms to invest in very risky startups? Although firms typically use risk engineering (e.g., sophisticated portfolio diversification) to protect themselves from financial losses if a startup collapses, such tactics can create moral hazard. Scholars have addressed collective action problems related to VC strategy but not unintended bad effects like creating moral hazard. To explore these effects, I develop a novel interpretation of the philosophical doctrine of double effect, which guides decision making that produces good effects (e.g., chances of bountiful returns) alongside bad. My theorizing has three implications for VC investment strategy. First, risk engineering undermines the traditional standard of prudence governing investment decision making; thus, an alternative standard is needed. Second, firms may not pursue good effects that impose risks on third parties without trying to mitigate them. Third, mitigating risks to third parties can improve VC strategic decision making.
