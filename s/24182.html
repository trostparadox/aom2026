Looking Outside to Foster Psychological Safety and Voicing Within Chronically Unsafe Teams

MOC
Elisabeth (Bu Deul) Yang (National University of Singapore); Julia DiBenigno (Julia DiBenigno)
Those on an organization’s frontlines are best positioned to notice early signs of impending emergencies in which unvoiced concerns can escalate into death or disaster. Prior literature suggests that frontline team members’ psychological safety, a critical antecedent of upward voice, is necessary for their concerns to be promptly reported to those with authority on their teams. Yet many teams face conditions unconducive to psychological safety (i.e., member churn and rotation, steep cross-occupational hierarchy, lack of co-location). How can organizations ensure that frontline concerns are consistently raised on teams with unsafe climates? Drawing on a multi-year ethnography of a U.S. hospital, we examine how a designated outsider to the team, whom we call a voice conduit, can scaffold psychological safety such that the concerns of frontline team members (in this case, bedside nurses) are continually surfaced and elevated to those with authority (in this case, primarily physicians), even on psychologically unsafe teams. We identify key structural characteristics of the voice conduit role and the practices this role enables which render them simultaneously safer for frontline team members to share concerns with and more protected to raise frontline concerns so they are acted on by those with authority. While prior scholarship on psychological safety has focused on promoting psychological safety internally within a team, our study offers a novel structural perspective by exploring the promise of a role external to a team to scaffold frontline psychological safety and voicing on otherwise unsafe teams across an organization.
