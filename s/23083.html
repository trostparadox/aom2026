Digital HRM as Multi-Level Architecture and Governance

HR
Edvalter Holz (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute); Thomas Oliveira (INSPER Teaching and Research Institute)
Research on the digitalization of HRM is growing rapidly, yet the field remains conceptually fragmented and weakly integrated across levels of analysis. We address this problem through a systematic and thematic review of 198 peer-reviewed journal articles on digital HRM. We conceptualize digital HRM as a socio-technical configuration in which HR infrastructures and AI-enabled decision systems reshape HR strategy, HR processes, and workforce governance across strategic, functional, individual, and social levels. Our synthesis organizes the literature into three macro-themes—HR Digital Transformation (HRDT), HR-Techs/e-HRM infrastructures, and AI-based HRM—and shows that each stream privileges different value-creation assumptions while unevenly theorizing control, datafication, and accountability. Building on a level-sensitive integration, we develop an architecture-and-governance framework that explains heterogeneous outcomes by tracing how digital HRM redistributes visibility, discretion, and accountability across levels through three cross-level mechanisms—datafication, delegated judgment (automation vs. augmentation), and orchestration. We conclude with a research agenda specifying boundary conditions (e.g., governance design, data quality, HR capability maturity) linking digital HR architectures and AI-enabled HR decisions to both performance and legitimacy outcomes.

Ethical Meaning-Making from Common Good HRM: Cross-Cultural Evidence

HR
Jawad Khan (Shenzhen University); Moazzam Ali (Shenzhen University); Qingyu Zhang (Shenzhen University); Adeel Luqman (University of Dundee)
Ethical misconduct in organizations often occurs in subtle ways, but its effects can weaken collective wellbeing and organizational integrity. Therefore, organizations need to adopt strategic practices that boost employees' moral development. Common Good Human Resource Management (CGHRM) is a values-based HR approach that helps shape employees' attitudes and actions; however, its ethical impacts have not been fully explored. Based on signaling theory, we suggest that CGHRM communicates clear moral expectations, which foster employees' moral identity internalization, increasing their ethical sensitivity and encouraging ethical behavior. We also argue that ethical leadership is a key factor that strengthens the effect of CGHRM on moral identity internalization, thereby strengthening the underlying process that leads to employee ethical behavior. Evidence from an experimental vignette study in Cyprus and a multi-source field study in China supports our proposed hypotheses. These results provide valuable insights into how CGHRM promotes ethical behavior and highlight the important role of ethical leadership. These findings enrich understanding of CGHRM's ethical influences and point to meaningful opportunities for future research on the conditions under which HRM systems cultivate employee ethics.

The Impact of Socially Responsible HRM and Employee Thriving at Work

HR
Qiongyao Zhou (Shanghai University); Yuanhua Chen (Huaqiao University); Hongdan Zhao (Shanghai University); Cuiling Jiang (KEDGE Business School)
Prior work links socially responsible human resource management (SRHRM) to a variety of organizational performance outcomes, yet we still know relatively little about how SRHRM generates broader social benefits. Drawing on cognitive–affective personality system (CAPS) theory, we propose a model in which SRHRM promotes employee thriving at work by simultaneously shaping a key cognitive and affective process. Using a scenario-based experiment in France and a field survey in China—we find that SRHRM is positively associated with thriving, and this relationship operates indirectly through employees’ cognitive flexibility and harmonious passion. Moreover, employees’ dialectical thinking strengthens these indirect effects, such that the mediated relationships via cognitive flexibility and harmonious passion are more pronounced when dialectical thinking is higher. By unpacking the cognitive and affective pathways and identifying an important boundary condition, this study clarifies how SRHRM translates into employee growth and offers insight into its potential for broader societal benefit.

Who Cares and Who Doesn't? Common Good HRM Approach and the Search for Meaningfulness Through Work

HR
Maria Järlström (University of Vaasa); Essi Saru (University of Turku); Maija Viitasaari (University of Vaasa)
Global environmental and societal challenges, such as social inequality and climate change, have increased research interest in sustainable human resource management (HRM). This study examines sustainable HRM from the common good perspective, the latest development in the field, which extends the focus beyond organizational boundaries to address broader societal issues. We draw on signaling theory and sensemaking to explore the meanings individuals assign to sustainable HRM and how they make sense of meaningfulness or meaninglessness in their work. Our data consists of open survey responses of Finnish knowledge workers. Our results demonstrate how the employees find meaning or meaninglessness in their work through several sensemaking factors. We contribute to research on work meaningfulness and meaninglessness by integrating common good, signaling theory and sensemaking.

On the Reciprocal Relationship Between Pay Satisfaction and Social and Institutional Trust

HR
Na Liu (KU Leuven); Shuai Yuan (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Mengwei Li (Tilburg University)
Trust plays a critical role in fostering social cohesion. However, extant research examines trust mainly as an antecedent to organizational outcomes, with limited attention given to the reverse pathway. In fact, workplaces are key sites of social interaction, and employees' work experiences can significantly shape their broader societal attitudes. Accordingly, this study investigates bidirectional relationships between pay satisfaction and two forms of trust— social trust (confidence in others) and institutional trust (confidence in institutions). Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we propose that these relationships operate through spillover mechanisms, with life satisfaction serving as a critical mediator transforming resources gained in one domain into positive outcomes in another. Using four-wave panel data from Dutch households (2021-2024) and lag-2 Dynamic Panel Models, we provide robust causal evidence for these relationships. Our findings reveal asymmetric patterns: social trust uni-directionally predicts pay satisfaction, while pay satisfaction and institutional trust mutually reinforce each other through gain spirals, with life satisfaction mediating all significant pathways. This asymmetry suggests that social trust functions as a stable moral orientation shaping workplace perceptions, whereas institutional trust operates as a dynamic resource responsive to workplace experiences. Our research offers important implications for practitioners. Employees' experiences at work have consequences not only for organizational outcomes but also serve as a potential mechanism for broader social repair.
