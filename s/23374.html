Neither Chance nor Design Alone: A Pluralistic Theory of Innovation Ideation

STR
Murad A. Mithani (Rutgers University-Camden); Ipek Kocoglu (Kean University)
Serendipity – an unexpected discovery – is often contrasted with intuition-driven ideation (hunches) and structured search (guided investigative efforts within defined problem spaces). This study examines how serendipity interacts with hunches and structured search in the course of major technological discoveries. Through three historical case studies: the rotary kiln for manufacturing Portland cement, the automatic bottle blowing machine, and the plain paper copying machine, we analyzed the overlaps and interplay among the three ideation approaches. Our findings show that serendipity both capitalized on and influenced other ideation approaches. When combined with hunches, serendipity tended to redirect or reaffirm innovative efforts; when paired with structured search, it either redirected innovative efforts or enhanced outcomes. In cases of nested serendipity – multiple and overlapping serendipitous events – innovative efforts were simultaneously redirected and reaffirmed. We also identified the emergence of minor hunches i.e., “hunchettes” that, though individually insignificant, collectively produced transformative effects, as well as “phantom paths” that facilitated transitions from one ideation approach to another. Overall, our findings suggest that serendipity operates as an iterative, non-linear process that thrives in organizational environments open to its dynamic interaction with hunches and structured search.

Why Luck Matters: Defining, Dimensioning and Categorizing its Strategic Importance

STR
Richard J. Arend (University of Southern Maine)
Luck matters in strategy and in entrepreneurship. Competitive advantage arises from luck because performance heterogeneity can only be produced by earlier heterogeneity, and that only comes from luck – either inherited or currently-experienced. New luck – a type of heterogeneity where a venture can have an advantage that incumbents cannot – when sufficiently large (relative to other factors), can lead to new entry. We provide needed clarity to the concept of luck – through a new definition, a comprehensive dimensioning, and a detailed typology – to increase its understanding. We speak to its influence on firm heterogeneity, on the standard innovation process, on attributions, and on behaviors while it is being experienced. And, we highlight the real but underplayed limitations of its control and the challenges in its empirical study.

Searching and Shaping Nascent Industries

STR
Jackson Thomas Anderson (Harvard Business School)
Nascent industries develop as pioneering firms are still learning what to do. Among many sources of uncertainty, firms face a particularly consequential challenge: determining which strategic elements to coordinate on with rivals and which to differentiate on, a decision that shapes future organizational learning and competitive dynamics. This paper develops a search-centric theory of industry shaping in nascent markets, arguing that pioneers shape industries primarily by constraining what others can explore through two mechanisms: Imposed Coordination, which locks rivals into specific shared strategic choices, and Imposed Differentiation, which locks rivals out of the shaper's choices. I model coupled search dynamics where firm payoffs depend endogenously on coordination and differentiation externalities across strategic elements. Results from computational experiments demonstrate that industry shaping is high-risk: proper alignment between shaping mechanism and strategic element characteristics can generate competitive advantage even for low performing firms, while misalignment can destroy even dominant firms' performance. Early industry leaders face additional tradeoffs when shaping, as coordination-inducing moves may erode competitive advantage even when boosting absolute performance. These findings challenge conventional strategic wisdom about differentiation in uncertain environments and reveal that in nascent industries, determining rivals' strategies is as consequential as determining one's own.

Knowing Where to Look and When it Matters: Theory, Experimentation, and Organizational Search

STR
Nety Wu (INSEAD)
How does the value of theory change as experimentation becomes cheaper? We study this question using an NK model in which organizations search through either theory-guided evaluation or trial-and-error experimentation. We show that theory plays different roles across cost environments. When experimentation is costly, theory provides unique directional value by concentrating scarce trials in promising regions of the landscape—--an advantage no exploration strategy can replicate. When experimentation is cheap, this uniqueness disappears: theory-less organizations can match theory-driven performance by adopting well-tuned exploration strategies, and theory’s role shifts from knowing where to look to automatically implementing effective exploration. With imperfect theories, cost and theory act as partial substitutes: theory reduces, but does not eliminate, the returns to experimentation. Theory fully substitutes for experimentation only when it is highly accurate, and fails when it induces premature convergence. This study contributes by clarifying how and when theory matters in organizational search, showing that its distinctive value lies in directing search under high costs and in structuring exploration when experimentation is cheap.

Search Speed, Knowledge Domains, and Growth: Evidence from YouTube Channels

STR
Sebastian Niederberger (Bocconi University); Alfonso Gambardella (Bocconi University)
The theory of this paper predicts that innovation speed increases firm performance because the quicker exhaustion of existing domains accelerates the discovery of new domains of knowledge. We provide a granular test of this theory using data from YouTube, conceptualizing creators as firms and videos as discrete innovation outputs. The introduction of YouTube Shorts provides an exogenous shock to identify the causal effect of search speed on innovation outcomes. We show that higher search speed leads to earlier exploration of new content domains, increased channel size, and improved performance. Speed is a general neglected mechanism of innovation-based performance. Among the many technologies that have raised the speed of innovation, artificial intelligence today is likely to have unprecedented implications, encouraging firms to discover systematically new domains.
