How CVC Investor Attributes Influence Who Ultimately Acquires the Venture?

ENT
Zhuo Chen (China Europe International Business School); Tieying Yu (Boston College)
Despite extensive research on the benefits CVC investors provide to the ventures they fund and how CVC investments affect ventures’ exit outcome, we know little about how CVC investors influence which third-party corporations ultimately acquire these ventures. Building on the endorsement literature, we propose that the attributes of a venture’s CVC investor serve as informative signals that shape potential acquirers’ assessment of the venture. We argue that greater technological proximity between a potential acquirer and the CVC’s parent firm increases the likelihood that the potential acquirer will acquire the CVC-backed venture. This endorsement effect is contingent: it weakens when potential acquirers possess alternative information sources and strengthens when the CVC signals strong commitment through lead-investor status. Based on a sample of 319 CVC-backed U.S. high-tech ventures acquired by third-party firms, we construct a matched case-control dataset that contains 14,435 venture–potential acquirer observations. Using a conditional logit model, the empirical results provide strong support for our hypotheses. This study contributes to the CVC literature by shifting attention from real-options and investor-venture dyads to the endorsement role of CVC attributes in shaping the match between CVC-backed ventures and potential acquirers. We also advance research on venture exit by demonstrating how corporate investors' attributes differentially influence which potential acquirers ultimately acquire the CVC-backed ventures.

Managerial Experience and Discretion in CVC: Determinants of Follow-on Investment Decisions

ENT
Rose Ji Youn Kim (University of Kentucky); Mukund Chari (University of Colorado-Boulder); Haemin Dennis Park (The University of Texas at Dallas)
Partnerships between established firms and new ventures through corporate venture capital (CVC) have become increasingly prevalent. Because CVC investments are primarily strategic, follow-on investments are critical for realizing corporate investors’ strategic objectives. However, divergent views about partnership value may exist within organizations, making managers central in advocating for ventures and securing internal support. Managers’ investment experience is therefore likely to shape follow-on investment decisions but its influence is ambiguous. Greater investment experience enhances managers’ standing and credibility, increasing their ability to champion the ventures they oversee. However, the experience may heighten managers’ awareness of partnership challenges and prompt them to voice concerns, potentially reducing support. We suggest that managerial discretion clarifies when investment experience influences follow-on investments. When managers possess greater discretion, experience increases follow-on investment because concerns are perceived as manageable rather than deal-breaking. Drawing on the managerial discretion literature, we examine individual, organizational, and external sources of discretion. Using data on 73 CVC managers from 13 firms investing in 366 startups between 2007 and 2017, we find that investment experience increases follow-on investment when (a) managers hold high rank, (b) there has been no CEO turnover at the parent firm, or (c) the portfolio company is younger.

Parenting for Survival: Corporate Entrepreneurial Orientation and CVC Portfolio Failure

ENT
Mario Marcel Bihler (TU Dortmund University); Steffen Strese (TU Dortmund University)
While research on Corporate Venture Capital (CVC) primarily focuses on success, the drivers of portfolio failure remain largely underexplored. We address this gap by investigating how a corporate parent’s entrepreneurial orientation (EO) affects its CVC portfolio failure rate. Drawing on the resource-based view (RBV), we examine the theoretical tension between "EO-as-advantage" and "EO-as-experimentation" and test how this association is moderated by the corporate parent's R&D capabilities and capital intensity. Based on 470 firm-year observations from S&P 500 firms (2001-2019), we employ a fixed effects fractional logit regression. We find that a corporate parent’s EO significantly reduces its CVC portfolio failure rate, supporting the "EO-as-advantage" perspective. This failure-reducing effect is strengthened by the corporate parent’s R&D capabilities but weakened by its capital intensity. Our study advances CVC research by systematically analyzing failure antecedents beyond the common survivorship bias, contributes to the EO literature by providing a contingent perspective on the advantage-versus-experimentation debate, and extends the RBV by highlighting the necessity of aligning strategic orientations with the corporate parent’s underlying resource configuration.

Strategic Alliances for CVC Syndication: How Partner Characteristics Drive Cross-industry Investment

ENT
Timiry R Tian (University of Wisconsin–River Falls); Jing Zhang (Old Dominion University); Chris H. Willis (University of Mary Washington); Jay O'Toole (Old Dominion University)
This study investigates the formation of corporate venture capital (CVC) syndicates, especially how partner characteristics drive cross-industry investment decisions. Unlike prior research, which has focused primarily on dyadic relationships, we focus on triadic structures when two CVCs jointly invest in a startup, which raises questions related to potential impact of competition between the CVCs’ parent corporations. By integrating knowledge-based and relationship-based views of alliance formation with coopetition theory, we propose that complementary industry knowledge and prior syndications among CVC partners promote cross-industry investments. In contrast, industry similarity among CVC partners and the extent of a partner’s prior investment experience in the startup industry tend to discourage such triadic syndication. We find support for these hypotheses analyzing 3,031 deals of 155 CVCs of S&P 500 parent corporations targeted at 2,165 startups between 2001 and 2022. Our findings contribute to the coopetition and knowledge-based alliance formation literature by revealing the important role of industry-specific and partner-specific knowledge for triadic CVC syndication.
