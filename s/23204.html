Exceptional but Exhausted: Women Founders and Venture Closure in Male-Dominated Industries

ENT
Elena Lizunova (Brigham Young University); Jennifer M. Merluzzi (The George Washington University)
Using large-scale administrative data from the French SINE survey of new ventures linked to the SIRENE business registry, along with interviews with entrepreneurs in France and the United States, we examine how gender incongruity between founder gender and the gender-typing of their venture’s industry shapes post-entry venture closure in male-dominated industries. Accounting for selection into industries, founder human capital and early venture characteristics associated with success, we find that women-led ventures in male-dominated industries--despite being comparably qualified and performing similarly to men-led ventures—are significantly more likely to close within the first five years. Interview evidence suggests that women founders in these industries face heightened scrutiny, inadequate support and relational isolation, creating sustained pressure to overperform. Over time, these conditions culminate in emotional exhaustion that increases the likelihood of venture closure even among actively engaged founders. Consistent with this account, post hoc analyses show that women founders in male-dominated industries engage in at least as much, and often more, venture-building effort than men. These findings shift attention from entry to post-entry venture survival, showing that gender incongruity penalties can persist even among highly qualified women leading viable ventures--and complicating narratives of entrepreneurship as an escape from labor-market inequality.

Gender Bias in Entrepreneurship: How Attitudes and Cognition Shape Evaluations

ENT
Banu A. Goktan (University of North Texas at Dallas); Gonca Gunay (Istanbul Bilgi University)
Despite growing female participation in entrepreneurship, gender bias remains pervasive. Across two studies with participants from Türkiye, we examined how gender–authority attitudes (GAM) and implicit person theories shape evaluations of male and female entrepreneurs under gender–behavior congruence and incongruence. Results show that pro-male evaluation bias is modest yet remarkably resilient. At the same time, the mechanisms underlying this bias are not uniformly visible in global Male - Female difference scores. Target-level analyses indicate that GAM predicts bias selectively and is most evident when a male entrepreneur violates masculine role stereotypes by enacting communal, relationship-oriented behavior. Implicit person theories matter primarily under these stereotype-incongruent male profiles. Role congruity effects are likewise profile-specific rather than global. These findings refine role congruity theory and extend implicit theory research by clarifying when attitudes and cognitive mindsets translate into biased entrepreneurial judgments and by showing why broad bias indices can mask the mechanisms driving profile-level evaluations.

Growing Side by Side, Yet Women are Kept Aside? Examining Gender Disparities in ESO

ENT
Dhruvi Naik (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Hetal Manish Jhaveri; Abrar Ali Saiyed (University of Glasgow); Stephanie A. Fernhaber (Butler University); Christina Theodoraki (Aix-Marseille University)
Entrepreneurial ecosystems (EEs) are commonly framed as gender-neutral, yet increasing evidence suggests that Entrepreneurship Support Organizations (ESOs) may reproduce structural biases that disadvantage women. This study examines whether ESO selection processes shape gender inclusivity within nascent Indian ecosystems. Using qualitative insights from interviews with entrepreneurs and ecosystem experts, we identify techno-centric innovation logic and embedded masculine norms as dominant selection influences. We then employ fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fs/QCA) across twenty ESOs to assess how five selection criteria - background, resource constraints, innovation, sustainability, and scalability - combine to drive startup selection. Innovation and sustainability emerge as core, necessary conditions, while other factors function as peripheral. However, these seemingly neutral criteria systematically privilege technology-intensive ventures, contributing to stark male overrepresentation within incubated cohorts and reinforcing a gendered “illusion of inclusivity.” By integrating Entrepreneurship Enablement Theory with feminist perspectives, the study reveals how ESOs can act as selective gatekeepers rather than universal enablers, offering theoretical and policy insights for designing more gender-inclusive entrepreneurial ecosystems.

Spiral Trajectories: Adaptive Strategies of Brazilian Women Entrepreneurs in Later Life

ENT
Eloisa Elena Shinohara (FATEC); Vania Maria Jorge Nassif; Edmundo Inacio Junior; Victor Silva Corrêa (Paulista University)
This study investigates adaptive strategies developed by Brazilian women entrepreneurs aged 50 and older, analyzing how they transform life experiences and accumulated knowledge into resources to overcome challenges related to the intersectionality of gender and age in the Brazilian context. The qualitative research, exploratory and descriptive in nature, was conducted with 19 women entrepreneurs aged 52 to 70 through semi-structured interviews. Data were analyzed using Atlas.Ti® software, resulting in four inductive categories: motivations for senior entrepreneurship, gender- and age-specific challenges, resources and competitive advantages of maturity, and overcoming and adaptation strategies. Results reveal that motivations transcend the traditional necessity-opportunity dichotomy, manifesting as hybrid and frequently catalyzed by biographical disruption events such as divorce, job loss, or illness. Data demonstrate that ageism operates in a sector-specific manner, manifesting explicitly in traditional corporate markets, while sectors such as education and specialized niches value experience and maturity. The main theoretical contribution lies in the Spiral Trajectories Framework, a model that integrates four intersectional axes—motivational, contextual, temporal, and strategic—demonstrating how disruption events function as catalysts for professional reinvention. The framework evidences the cumulative nature of these trajectories, where each cycle of disruption-reflection-strategy-reinvention elevates the level of available resources and competencies. This study contributes to the literature on senior women's entrepreneurship by providing insights for the formulation of more inclusive public policies and support programs sensitive to age and gender specificities in the Brazilian context.

Women in Social Entrepreneurship as Emancipation and Empowerment

ENT
Tra Tran-Thien (University of Strasbourg)
Social entrepreneurship has increasingly been recognized as a pathway to women’s emancipation, yet little is known about the emancipatory mechanisms operating within patriarchal contexts. Drawing on data from women social entrepreneurs in Vietnam, this study explores diverse strategies by which women navigate gender-related constraints across both family and entrepreneurial spheres. We unpack the dimensions of social entrepreneurship that foster women’s freedom, autonomy, and broader social change. The study introduces the emancipatory model as a form of emancipatory social entrepreneurship that empowers women through familial negotiation and the cultivation of male allyship. It offers both theoretical and practical insights to inform efforts supporting women-led initiatives and engaging men as partners in advancing gender equality.
