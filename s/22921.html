Reconceptualizing Organizational Roles

OMT
Elisabeth Holien (BI Norwegian Business School); Thorvald Haerem (BI Norwegian Business School)
We revisit core assumptions in organizational role theories, which traditionally view roles as either formal positions or social constructions. We reconceptualize roles as dynamic, routine-embedded phenomena that emerge through interdependent enactment. Drawing on classic organization theory, contemporary approaches to organizing, and research on routine dynamics, we apply the distinction between ostensive and performative aspects to the study of roles. The ostensive aspect includes the structures that constrain and enable action, and the performative aspect reflects situated interactions where these roles are enacted and changed. In this perspective, roles are relational patterns central to routine performance. Role specialization and change thus emerge endogenously through repeated interaction among interdependent actors, both within and across organizational routines. Roles are relational patterns constituted through ostensive-performative dynamics, vital to organizational stability and change. We discuss theoretical implications for organizational design, coordination, and routinization.

Shuffle Then Deal: Misfit as an Endogenous Mechanism to Formalize New Structures

OMT
Lisa Ellen Cohen (McGill University); Huong Le Bui (McGill University)
This study investigates how person–job misfit can lead to the creation, alteration, and eventual formalization of jobs. Through inductive analysis of interview data about 19 cases of misfit, we find that misfit leading to structural change emerge from two distinct hiring practices: hiring for immediate but undefined needs which we label the Unanticipated Future Pathway; and hiring for defined future needs which we label the Anticipated Future Pathway. On Unanticipated Future Pathway, new hires took on any additional tasks that the organization need and they eventually became over worked and confusion about responsibilities emerged. On the Anticipated Future Pathway, managers assigned tasks to new hires, who eventually expressed frustration about not doing the work they were hired to do. At the same time, managers complained that they were paying these employees too much for the work that they were doing. On both pathways, jobs were altered in response to these problems. On the Unanticipated Future Pathway, the initial jobs were formalized and other new jobs were added. On the Anticipated Future Pathway, jobs were often eliminated and tasks redistributed across other existing jobs. By documenting how misfit can drive job formalization, we marry theories of job and organizational design to problematize dominant insights about the relationship between task organization and employment practices. Rather than being driven by deliberate managerial organizational designs, employment practices can shape the way these designs emerge and persist in organizations by motivating managers and job incumbents to temporarily tolerate misfit.

Rethinking Autonomy: How Organizational Controls Shape Knowledge Sharing in Self-Managing Teams (WITHDRAWN)

OMT
Beauregard Berton; Saeedeh Ahmadi (University of Amsterdam)
This study investigates whether and under what conditions the implementation of self-managing teams enhances employee knowledge exchange. While self-management is often promoted as a vehicle for improved motivation and collaboration, empirical evidence remains inconclusive. Drawing on a quasi-experiment in a leading Dutch health insurance firm, we examine how two informal organizational control mechanisms, peer monitoring and managerial output control, shape employees’ likelihood to share knowledge. Using multilevel modeling with data from 233 employees nested in 61 teams, we find that contrary to our expectations, employees in teams where self-management was implemented were less likely to share their knowledge. Moreover, while peer monitoring showed no significant moderating effect, managerial output control mitigated the negative relationship. These findings challenge the view that increasing autonomy at the team-level inherently supports individual knowledge sharing behaviors. Finally, our quasi-experiment emphasizes the importance of organizational controls in designing teams, contributing to our understanding of organizational design and knowledge exchange.

Balancing Knowledge Expansion and Managerial Demand Through Shared Knowledge

OMT
Moh Hosseinioun (Northwestern University); Hyejin Youn (Seoul National University)
How can firms expand their knowledge scope without expanding managerial hierarchy? Integrating specialized expertise increases coordination and agency costs, typically requiring additional managerial layers. We argue that firms can balance this trade-off through shared knowledge. Building on a nested model of specialization in which differentiated expertise rests on shared foundational skills, we theorize that shared knowledge functions as an organizational infrastructure that mitigates coordination and agency costs as firms expand their knowledge. We distinguish between lateral shared knowledge among peers and vertical shared knowledge between managers and subordinates, and argue that the latter plays a distinct and more scalable role. Using more than 300 million U.S. job postings from 2010 to 2022, we show that firms' knowledge scope expands through recombination around a shared core of skills, and that managerial demand responds primarily to knowledge diversity rather than firm size. Consistent with our theory, vertical shared knowledge is associated with fewer managerial positions, greater delegation, and effects that strengthen as knowledge scope grows, substantially larger than those of lateral shared knowledge.

Not Just a Job: The Process and Consequences of External Stakeholder Collaboration in Designing Jobs

OMT
Sara Mahabadi (University of Alberta); Lisa Ellen Cohen (McGill University); Arvind Karunakaran (Stanford University)
In this paper, we ask how internal actors and external stakeholders (e.g., investors, consultants, and regulators) collaborate in designing jobs. Designing jobs is crucial for organizations but invokes substantial risks and challenges that internal actors might overcome by collaborating with external stakeholders. Using a startup accelerator as a strategic-research setting and data from observations and interviews, we develop a process model of such job-design collaborations that happen at the hiring interface. First, collaborators jointly recognize problems that could be resolved by designing jobs and that entrepreneurs need help in this process. Second, they generate and implement models to allocate work to jobs through co-creation and brokering. Finally, in some cases, the entrepreneurs refine collaboratively designed jobs after they are put in place. This process enables not only job design, but also initiates the hiring process, resolves the triggering problems, and changes other jobs. These jobs endure and influence subsequent structures. Further, engagement in the process allows collaborators to access knowledge and develop relationships they rely on later. Thus, job-design collaborations produce more than single jobs: they provide scaffolding on which structures continue to be built. Our model broadens conceptualizations of how and when jobs are designed, who participates in the process, and what job design does.
