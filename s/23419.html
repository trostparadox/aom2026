Differential Effects of Entrepreneurial and Circular Bricolage on Venture Performance

ONE
Israa Thiab (Rochester Institute of Technology); Clyde Eiríkur Hull (Rochester Institute of Technology)
This study examines whether entrepreneurial and circular bricolage have differential effects on performance across circular and conventional business models. While prior research establishes bricolage as beneficial for resource-constrained ventures, we lack understanding of how bricolage effects vary by business model type, and validated measures of circular bricolage remain underdeveloped. We introduce and validate a measure of circular bricolage by adapting the Davidsson et al. (2017) entrepreneurial bricolage scale to capture domain-specific resourcefulness in sustainability-focused contexts. Using multi-group structural equation modeling, latent profile analysis, and response surface analysis on 439 ventures (147 circular, 292 conventional) from the United States and Myanmar, we find significant differences in how bricolage types influence performance. For conventional ventures, both entrepreneurial bricolage (ß = .669, p < .001) and circular bricolage (ß = .619, p < .001) show strong positive effects. Circular ventures show a different pattern, with entrepreneurial bricolage (ß = .601, p < .001) exhibiting stronger effects than circular bricolage (ß = .415, p < .001). Multi-group path difference tests confirm these variations are statistically significant (??² = 20.97, df = 2, p < .001). Latent profile analysis identified six naturally occurring bricolage configurations, with "Dual Bricoleurs" (high on both types) achieving dramatically superior performance (?² = .572). Response surface analysis reveals that circular ventures with high circular bricolage but low entrepreneurial bricolage perform worst—a pattern we term the "capability trap," wherein circular ventures emphasize capabilities already inherent to their business model rather than developing the complementary entrepreneurial flexibility they critically need. We contribute to strategic management by identifying this capability trap as a phenomenon explaining why specialist bricolage fails without generalist entrepreneurial bricolage. We contribute to entrepreneurship theory by demonstrating that business model type serves as a boundary condition for bricolage effectiveness and by identifying distinct strategic bricolage archetypes. We contribute measurement tools by providing a validated instrument for assessing domain-specific resourcefulness applicable beyond sustainability contexts to any constrained environment.

Exploring the Asymmetric Impacts of Globalization on Ecological Footprints: A NARDL-Based Analysis

ONE
Muhammad Sonail (China University of Geosciences (Wuhan))
The relationship between globalization and environmental sustainability remains a critical area of research in environmental economics, particularly in the context of rapidly developing economies. Building on the environmental Kuznets curve theory and examining globalization's multifaceted impact, this study investigates the asymmetric effects of globalization on ecological footprints (EF) in India and Pakistan, utilizing time-series data from 1991 to 2018. The Nonlinear Autoregressive Distributed Lag (NARDL) cointegration approach is employed to explore the long-term and short-term dynamics of globalization’s influence on EF, integrating insights from the theory of sustainable development. The findings reveal that globalization exerts significant asymmetric effects on EF, with India experiencing a stronger and more pronounced impact than Pakistan. In alignment with the environmental Kuznets curve, negative shocks to social globalization—such as those related to cultural and social integration—result in a more significant increase in EF than positive shocks in both countries. Additionally, economic globalization, particularly through increased trade and industrialization, consistently exacerbates environmental degradation. These results suggest that while globalization can foster economic growth, it often does so at the expense of environmental sustainability. Based on the theoretical framework of sustainable development, the study proposes policy recommendations to mitigate these adverse effects, including the adoption of sector-specific regulations, promotion of clean energy technologies, and the advancement of sustainable industrial practices. These strategies aim to achieve a balanced approach to growth and sustainability, ensuring that globalization supports, rather than undermines, ecological well-being.

Passion and Wind of Change: Uncertain Path to Adopting a Less Competitive Sustainable Technology

ONE
Stephane Eric Collignon (West Virginia University); Henrik Sternberg (Iowa State University); Shazbah Shafi (Chalmers University of Technology); Martin Svanberg (RISE Research Institutes of Sweden)
Diesel cargo ships are efficient and reliable but fluctuating crude oil prices and climate concerns lead to questioning using this technology. In the past 50 years, attempts to re-adopt sails to fully or assist propelling cargo ships have been made. So far, these attempts represent an insignificant number of cargo ships. We propose to explore what leads some ship owners to keep trying to adopt sail technologies on their cargo ships. We use an inductive grounded theory approach to analyze interviews and conferences given by executives of ship-owning companies. Our analysis led to formulating a theoretical model, in which passion is the main instigator. This model, that we compare to a cyclone path, is our main contribution. Like a cyclone, the path of wind technology adoption is uncertain, construed of cyclical patterns, and gains momentum toward becoming a societal phenomenon. We use this model to expand on existing literature. Passion literature, upper echelon theory, and the socio-technical perspective are discussed under the prism of our findings.

Context-Conditioned Sustainability Strategies in HEIs: Lessons from the Global North and South

ONE
Jeferson Silva Henrique; Sourabh Aggarwal (IAE BUSINESS SCHOOL - AUSTRAL UNIVERSITY)
This study adopts an exploratory research design to examine how Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) implement sustainability strategies in pursuit of sustainable development (SD). Treating HEIs as organizational actors, we analyze campus-based sustainability initiatives through the theoretical lenses of Base of the Pyramid (BoP), Creating Shared Value (CSV), and regenerative strategies. The empirical analysis draws on secondary data from the 2023 Times Higher Education (THE) Impact Rankings, focusing on four cases: two top-performing HEIs from the Global North and two ranked institutions from the Global South. The findings reveal systematic differences in strategic orientations, reflecting uneven institutional capacities and contextual constraints across regions. These findings advance a context-sensitive understanding of sustainability strategy by demonstrating how institutional capacity conditions the strategic logics through which HEIs respond to universal sustainability expectations. Theoretically, the study underscores the limitations of universal sustainability models and highlights the need for context-sensitive approaches that recognize the Global South’s dual role in economic development and resource preservation. Socially, it challenges conventional notions of “world-class” universities by foregrounding contributions to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including poverty reduction, gender equality, and employment. Practically, the study offers actionable insights for integrating sustainability into HEI strategy and advances an underexplored domain in sustainability research.

Governance Structures and Environmental Performance in Mission-Driven Organizations

ONE
Mahinda Wijesiri (Independent Researcher)
Given mixed evidence on whether diversity in governing bodies translates into environmental outcomes, we examine how governance structures relate to environmental performance in mission-driven organizations across varied national settings. We find that more diverse governance structures are associated with stronger environmental practices, and that this association is shaped by national governance quality. These findings offer implications for policymakers, regulators, and practitioners by showing when governance design is more likely to support environmental objectives in mission-driven organizations.
