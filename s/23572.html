Does Customer Participation Improve New Product Development Performance? A Meta-Analysis

TIM
Yating Zhou (Zhejiang Normal University); Changfeng Wang (Zhejiang Normal University); Yanglei Cao (Zhejiang Normal University)
Firms increasingly rely on new product development (NPD) for competitive advantage. However, prior empirical findings remain sharply divided on whether customer participation improves NPD performance. Moreover, inconsistencies in conceptualizations and analytical perspectives across studies have limited a coherent understanding of the relationship. This meta-analysis of 174 independent effect sizes evaluates the effect of customer participation on NPD performance, compares participation modes across NPD efficiency and effectiveness, and tests moderators. The results indicate that customer participation positively relates to NPD performance. Both information sharing and co-development are beneficial, but their effects vary across performance dimensions: co-development is more strongly associated with NPD efficiency, whereas information sharing is more conducive to NPD effectiveness. Further analyses show that the positive impact of customer participation is more pronounced in emerging economies, high-technology industries, and studies using subjective measures. In addition, absorptive capacity strengthens the effect of customer participation on NPD performance, whereas R&D intensity is not a significant moderator. These findings provide meta-analytic evidence that helps reconcile inconsistent results and offer implications for aligning customer participation modes with contextual conditions to enhance NPD performance.

Emerging Resistance to the Digital Transformation of Public Transport by Passengers with Dementia

TIM
James Rupert Fletcher (University of Bath)
The digital transformation of public transport is prone to resistance. Among the discontents of such transformation, older people and those living with cognitive impairments are repeatedly identified. Stereotypes assuming their innate resistance to technological change are widespread, but research shows that older people with dementia can have rich digital lives. This paper reports findings from an ethnography of public transport digital transformation with older passengers living with dementia in Greater Manchester, UK. Combining interviews, photography, poetry, route maps and contextual statistics, four cases illustrate the emergence of resistance to digital displays, cashless exchange, e-ticketing and live routing. They reveal subtle, nuanced and contextually contingent resistances that emerge from digital transformation disrupting passenger ecobiographies – the rootedness of their lives in longstanding ecosystems. Findings show that everyday resistance can be small, transient and relational, and should be taken seriously as an indicator of potential injustices. Future initiatives should meaningfully include older and disabled users throughout to increase alertness to ecobiographical disruption and emerging resistance.

Evaluative Roles, Fear, and Usage Experience in Shaping the Social Acceptance of Robotaxis

TIM
Xiaofan Chen (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Lixian Qian (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University)
Robotaxis—AI-driven autonomous vehicles operating within platform-based ride-hailing services—have moved from pilot testing to early-stage commercial deployment in China. Despite increasing technological maturity, their diffusion has been accompanied by persistent public fear, posing a critical challenge to wider deployment. Adopting a socio-technical lens, this study advances a dual-role evaluation perspective and positions fear as a central emotional mechanism shaping social acceptance of robotaxis. It distinguishes between user-centric fear, related to personal use of the technology, and society-centric fear, concerning broader societal consequences. Using a mixed-methods approach, 30 interviews were conducted to identify the threats leading to each form of fear. A survey of 602 respondents (302 experienced and 300 inexperienced users) was then employed to examine the proposed research model. The results show that threats regarding safety, time efficiency, privacy, and liability elicit user-centric fear, whereas threats related to employment, equity, and infrastructure increase society-centric fear. Both forms of fear suppress social acceptance. Mediation analysis indicates that several threats influence social acceptance primarily through fear, underscoring the pivotal role of emotional processing in robotaxi deployment. Moderation analysis reveals that robotaxi-riding experience reshapes fear–acceptance relationships: user-centric fear is more influential among inexperienced individuals, whereas society-centric fear becomes more salient among experienced users. These findings highlight the importance of emotion-sensitive and stage-aware management strategies for the socially responsible deployment of AI-enabled mobilities.

Harnessing Stakeholder Wisdom: A Startup's Guide to Innovation Success

TIM
Elisa Thomas; Tatiana Iakovleva (University of Stavanger)
Startups face unique challenges in managing stakeholders' knowledge for product innovation, especially given their resource constraints. While frameworks such as lead user innovation (von Hippel, 1986) and responsible research and innovation (Owen et al., 2013) emphasize the value of stakeholder engagement, they often overlook the practical realities startups encounter. This study addresses this gap by examining how startups manage stakeholder knowledge for product innovation. Drawing on qualitative case study research involving four Brazilian startups at various stages of product development, this paper explores how startups structure their knowledge exchange processes and integrate external insights into their innovation activities. Our findings highlight that both tacit and explicit knowledge exchanges are critical for innovation success, requiring distinct management structures and routines. Additionally, startups prioritize external knowledge based not on quantity but on its alignment with their internal strategy and mission, emphasizing the need for a targeted and strategic approach to stakeholder engagement.

Understanding Green Orientation in Crowdfunding of Entrepreneurial Ventures

TIM
Rakesh Kumar Pati (Deakin University); Niharika Garud (University of Melbourne)
In this research, we build theory on how green orientation signals can shape crowdfunding success of entrepreneurial ventures. We also explore boundary conditions that shape this relationship and limit the benefits that the green orientation brings to ventures, such as venture characteristics (project duration, venture stage, and venture innovativeness). We test our proposed hypotheses through two empirical studies, first being a sample of 148 energy and green technology entrepreneurial ventures from the Indiegogo crowdfunding platform and second study being a series of three experiments where data was gathered from 117 crowdfunders. The empirical evidence from both studies finds significant positive effect of green orientation signals on crowdfunding success of entrepreneurial ventures. Particularly, as theorized in this article, ventures with longer project duration on crowdfunding platform and high innovativeness leverage the benefits of green orientation signals significantly more than those with shorter duration and low levels of innovativeness, respectively. Additionally, we find that the effect of green orientation signals on crowdfunding success is much stronger among ventures in advanced stages than ventures in early stages. We contribute to the crowdfunding literature by highlighting that there is a bright side as well as dark side of green orientation depending on venture characteristics. Implications of our findings on research and practice are also discussed.
