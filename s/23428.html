Partnering for the Future: Human-Generative Artificial Intelligence Boundaries in Public Management

PNP
Dermot Breslin (Queen's University Belfast); Sarah Hudson (Rennes School of Business); Joanne Gardner Le Gars (Rennes School of Business); Anna-Sofia Yanson (Università della Svizzera Italiana); Stephane Paquelet
This study explores how decision-making boundaries between humans and Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) are defined in public management. Drawing on a qualitative study of a French municipality planning department implementing a GAI-augmented decision-support application, task, cognitive, and relational boundaries are seen to shape human-GAI collaboration, resulting in a typology of distinct coevolutionary relationships. Task boundaries are characterized by an automation threshold beyond which human judgment is warranted to maintain accountability and legitimacy for non-routine or high-stakes decisions. Human and GAI cognitive boundaries are conceived as either distinct and separate in a dualistic sense, or as a blurred and interdependent duality. Regarding human-GAI relationships, boundaries are seen to be governed by antagonistic, competitive or mutualistic interactions. Integrating these dimensions reveals a typology of six co-evolutionary human-GAI decision-making hybrids. The findings advance research on AI-augmented decision-making generally and extend recent research on AI as a social and institutional actor in public-sector decision-making.

Trapped in the Digital Net: Consequences of Monitoring and Digital Overload for Civil Servants

PNP
Tingting Song (Shanghai Normal University); Chao Pan; Zheng Jiang (City University of Hong Kong)
While digital technology empowers public sectors worldwide, increasing their flexibility and efficiency, the negative impacts of digital technology on civil servants are neglected largely. Based on the Job Demands-Resources model, this study investigated the underlying mechanisms by which the negative effects of digital technology usage influence the subsequent psychology and behavior of civil servants. Data were collected from 614 civil servants, with results revealing that electronic monitoring and digital overload were positively correlated with turnover intention and negatively correlated with cooperative willingness. The above relationships were mediated by job insecurity. Public service motivation moderates the influence of electronic monitoring and digital overload on job insecurity. This research contributes to the understanding of digital technology usage, providing valuable insights into preventing and managing electronic monitoring and digital overload. The implications underscore the necessity for targeted interventions that foster public service motivation, ultimately fostering a healthier work environment for civil servants.

The Digital Native Trap: How Digital Technologies Imprint Social Enterprise Scaling

PNP
Jessica Sasso Lopes (emlyon business school); Christof Brandtner (emlyon business school)
This paper examines the conditions under which novel technologies enable scaling in social enterprises, which we understand as growth in mission-oriented activities. Building on imprinting theory, we argue that digitalization—the adoption and use of digital technologies—holds transformative potential for scaling, but its outcomes are contingent on the prevailing technology at the time of an organization’s founding. Our contextual view of digitalization proposes that the effect of digitalization on scaling depends on whether organizations are digital natives, founded in the digital age and imprinted with digital technologies from inception, or technological incumbents, founded before digital technologies became widespread and later adapting to them. Using a unique dataset of administrative and survey data tracking social enterprises in the San Francisco Bay Area in the period between the Great Recession and COVID-19, we analyze the association between digitalization and scaling trajectories over a decade. Our findings show that digitalization is generally associated with an increase in scaling; however, digital natives do not benefit from digitalization to the same extent as technological incumbents—a counterintuitive result we term the digital native trap. We explain this effect as an early overreliance on the affordances of digital technologies, which may eclipse the need to develop the affordances necessary to overcome liabilities of newness. Bridging literatures on imprinting, digitalization, and scaling, we contribute to social entrepreneurship and organizational research by revealing the heterogeneous effects of digitalization and by showing how technological imprinting shapes organizations’ ability to leverage digital technologies for social impact.

The Dual Effects of Digital Government on Public Engagement

PNP
Xinru Sun (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Xiaofei Zhang (Harbin Institute of Technology); Feng Guo (Tianjin University)
Public engagement is essential for governance effectiveness. Although its dynamics have been reshaped by the rapid advancement of digital technologies, little is known about how digital government influences public engagement. Grounded in the expectancy-disconfirmation model, this study investigates the effect of digital government on public engagement—a dual-dimensional construct comprising behavioral participation and affective satisfaction—and examines the moderating role of government responsiveness, operationalized as response speed and length. Using a panel of 4,381 city-year observations from 337 Chinese cities (2011 to 2023) and a staggered difference-in-differences approach, we find that digital government has a dual effect on public engagement: it promotes citizens’ participation in governance while reducing their satisfaction. However, faster and more detailed responses can counteract the decline in satisfaction, even though extremely detailed replies may slightly dampen participation. These findings provide valuable theoretical and practical implications for understanding and managing digital-era public engagement.

The Impact of Social Media Fatigue on Volunteer’s Online Group Discontinuous Intention

PNP
Chi-Shiun Lai (National Yunlin University of Science and Technology); Guan-Lun Chen (National Yunlin University of Science and Technology)
Non-profit organizations (NPOs) use volunteer online groups to facilitate volunteer communication and task coordination. Drawing on cognitive load theory, this study examines the antecedents of online group discontinuous intention of volunteers and regard social media fatigue as a mediator. Integrating social identity theory, person-group fit is further introduced as a moderator. Survey results show that information overload, social comparison, and fear of missing out increase both low and high levels of discontinuous intention through social media fatigue. Moreover, person-group fit mitigates the effect of social media fatigue on low, but not high, discontinuous intention. It indicates that when discontinuous intention varies in degree, the mitigating effect of person-group fit differs. These findings extend research on social media fatigue in NPO contexts and offer managerial implications for volunteer group management.
