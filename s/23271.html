Becoming Embedded After Displacement: How Refugee Entrepreneurs Rebuild Economic and Social Ties

ENT
Sanita Rugina (Maastricht University); Ester Eomois (Estonian Business School)
Despite growing research on refugee entrepreneurship, we still know little about how refugee entrepreneurs actively become embedded in host-country contexts, particularly under conditions of ongoing war, family separation, and caregiving responsibility. Existing studies often conceptualize embeddedness as an outcome or structural condition, paying limited attention to the agentic, emotional, and relational practices through which refugees reconstruct economic activity, identity, and belonging over time. Drawing on 44 in-depth interviews with Ukrainian female refugee and experts, this study examines how refugee entrepreneurs become embedded in unfamiliar institutional, market, and social environments. The Ukrainian wartime displacement offers a critical empirical setting, as women disproportionately carry responsibility for entrepreneurship, caregiving, and family stability while navigating uncertainty about permanence, return, and future orientation. Our analysis shows that refugee entrepreneurs engage in sustained embeddedness work - intentional and ongoing practices aimed at situating themselves and their ventures in the host country. This work comprises three interrelated dimensions: agency enactment, contextual adaptation, and identity alignment. While all participants engaged in these practices, differences in emotional engagement, family configurations, and degrees of liminality shaped how embeddedness work translated into outcomes. We identify four embeddedness pathways—protective liminal, ethnic anchor, selective functional, and integrative home-making—demonstrating that embeddedness is a dynamic, heterogeneous, and deeply personal process rather than a uniform trajectory.

Beyond Static Ties: Navigating Evolving Community Embeddedness in a Century-Old Asian Family Firm

ENT
Rebecca Chunghee Kim (NUCB Business School, Nagoya University of Commerce & Business); Hsi-Mei Chung (I-Shou University); Artemis Chang (Queensland University of Technology); Lu Zhao (Queensland University of Technology)
Existing scholarship often views community embeddedness in family firms as a static condition, neglecting how its underlying values dynamically emerge, evolve, and ultimately co-create shared value across generations. Addressing this critical gap, this study employs a longitudinal qualitative case analysis of a five-generation Taiwanese family firm, utilizing in-depth interviews with family leaders and stakeholders, complemented by social network visualization. We uncover a novel, four-stage dynamic process model—Minimal, Emerging, Expanding, and Maturing embeddedness—that reveals its evolution from non-interactive ties to mutually constitutive relationships. Our key finding is that embeddedness transitions from a peripheral element to a central mechanism for shared value creation, often propelled by "necessity" for sustainable growth. This model significantly advances theoretical understanding of dynamic community embeddedness, reframes stakeholder engagement, and offers crucial insights for long-term firm sustainability and regional resilience for global family firms beyond Asian society.

Beyond The Pandemic: A Comparative Study of Business Group Recovery and Family Succession Mode

ENT
Yu-Hsuan Hung (National Taiwan Normal University); Min-Ping Kang (National Taiwan Normal University); Chwo-Ming Yu (Chang Gung University)
This study examines the performance differences between family business groups (BGs) and non-family BGs under the impact of the COVID-19 crisis, with a particular focus on the influence of family governance structures, especially succession mechanisms. While previous research has primarily compared single-family firms with non-family firms, it has often overlooked the heterogeneity among family businesses and the unique challenges to BGs posed by the pandemic. Conducted in the context of emerging markets, this study considers the impact of institutional voids on BGs and explores how family BGs leverage internal markets to navigate external challenges. Using a Difference-in-Differences (DID) model with two-way fixed effects (TWFE) estimation; the analysis encompasses 296 observations from 46 family business groups and 36 non-family business groups during the pandemic crisis. The findings reveal that non-family BGs demonstrated significantly improved performance compared to family business groups during crisis period. Additionally, among family business groups, those with primogeniture succession exhibited better performance compared to those with non-primogeniture succession. This research challenges the conventional view of family businesses as a homogenous category, highlighting the critical role of governance structures in times of crisis. By exploring the resilience of family business groups through the lenses of institutional voids and internal market mechanisms, this study offers novel insights into their response during crisis. It further underscores the importance of family governance in achieving superior performance during macroeconomic shocks, advancing the literature on family business governance and crisis resilience.

Perpetuity on Loop:The Quadruple Bottom Line and Family Firm Sustainability Amid Institutional Voids

ENT
Tulsi Jayakumar (S.P. Jain Institute of Management and Research); Natalia A. Vershinina (Audencia Business School)
Family firms (FFs) dominate the global business landscape, yet their approach to sustainability remains an underexplored topic, particularly in emerging economies characterized by weak institutional environments. This paper develops and applies a Quadruple Bottom Line (QBL) framework that extends the conventional Triple Bottom Line (TBL) of People, Planet, and Profits by introducing Perpetuity — a supraordinate logic rooted in socioemotional wealth — that undergirds family ownership and transgenerational vision in such contexts. Through a single-case, longitudinal study of “IndMovers,” a multi-generational family firm in the Indian logistics industry, we show how the family’s Perpetuity Logic integrates and reconciles competing economic, social, and environmental logics. The QBL comprises two interlinked loops: (1) a sustainability outcomes loop where family logic drives TBL performance through identity-consistent choice, frugality-innovation complementarity, reciprocity, and intergenerational modelling; and (2) a feedback loop where TBL achievements reinforce SEW, renewing the family’s moral authority and long-term orientation. By analyzing how institutional voids in labour, credit, regulatory, and infrastructural domains activate family-based private-order governance, we reveal how FFs transform constraints into sustainability enablers. The study contributes to theory by positioning Perpetuity as an institutional logic that organizes how family firms “do well, do good, and endure,” advancing understanding of sustainability and paradox management in FFs within weak institutional contexts.

Social and Environmental Practices as Resilience-Building Mechanisms in Family Firms

ENT
Mohamed Ouiakoub; Elias Hadjielias (Cyprus University of Technology)
This article examines how social and environmental practices (SEPs) contribute to the development of organisational resilience in family businesses (FBs). While prior research highlights the importance of sustainability and long-term orientation in FBs, the mechanisms through which SEPs are translated into resilience capabilities remain insufficiently theorised. Addressing this gap, the study explores how SEPs shape resilience-building processes over time and support organisational responses to repeated and heterogeneous crises. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative analysis of a large family business operating in a developing economy, the article shows that SEPs play a central and structuring role in family business resilience through interconnected reactive, proactive, and reinforcing dynamics. Specifically, SEPs enable immediate crisis responses while simultaneously fostering long-term preparedness grounded in sustainable innovation, institutionalised governance, and intergenerational orientation. The analysis further reveals reinforcing feedback loops through which crisis-related learning strengthens SEP structures and routines over time. Building on these findings, the article introduces the concept of SEP-driven family business resilience, conceptualising resilience as a cumulative and processual dynamic shaped by family influence, territorial embeddedness, and societal responsibilities.
