Disagreement is Not the Problem: How Entrepreneurial Teams Make Disagreement Work

ENT
Hassan Imam (Roehampton University); Syeda Nimra Batool (Royal Holloway - University of London)
Early-stage entrepreneurial teams operate under persistent uncertainty and routinely confront divergent views about strategy, priorities, and execution. While disagreement is pervasive in such teams, its implications for innovation and entrepreneurs’ satisfaction remain poorly understood. Drawing on information processing theory and Self-Determination Theory, this study examines how entrepreneurial teams engage with disagreement and how these engagement processes shape both team innovation and founders’ evaluative satisfaction in early-stage ventures. Using multi-source, three-wave survey data from founders and co-founders of 247 technology ventures, we examine constructive controversy as a team engagement process and assess how its effects unfold through team innovation, contingent on task conflict intensity and shared leadership. The results show that constructive controversy is positively associated with both team innovation and entrepreneurs’ satisfaction, but these effects are bounded. Specifically, the relationship between constructive controversy and innovation follows an inverted U-shaped pattern across levels of task conflict and is strengthened when leadership influence is broadly shared within the team. By shifting attention from the amount of disagreement to how disagreement is engaged and governed, this study advances a process-oriented account of entrepreneurial team dynamics. Overall, the study shows that the psychological viability of early-stage entrepreneurship depends on the micro-social processes through which teams transform disagreement into forward movement under uncertainty.

How Empathy Shapes Resilience: Empirical Evidence from Entrepreneurial Teams

ENT
Geng Yao (Shaanxi Normal University); Lei Hongzhen (Shaanxi Normal University); Lan Juanli; Yan Yihang
Against the backdrop of rapid development of emerging technologies, start-ups face survival crises and even bankruptcy due to their inherent “liability of newness.” Consequently, fostering resilience within entrepreneurial teams becomes crucial for counteracting this vulnerability. Drawing on the motivated information processing theory, this paper develops a model of resilience formation, positioning empathy as an antecedent variable and team interaction processes as mediators. Analyzing survey data from 82 entrepreneurial teams through path analysis and Bootstrap mediation effect tests, the empirical results demonstrate that both cognitive empathy and affective empathy significantly enhance team resilience by improving the levels of fact-oriented and emotion-oriented team interactions. This finding offers a novel perspective for understanding the formation mechanism of resilience in entrepreneurial teams and broadens the approach to enhancing teams’ capacity to cope with complex environments.

How Expertise-Based Authority Amplifies the Benefits of Heterogeneity in Entrepreneurial Teams

ENT
Wei Chi (Tsinghua University); Xu Huang (Hong Kong Baptist University); Yumei Chen (The Chinese University of Hong Kong)
How founding team diversity translates into innovation remains an unresolved challenge. Although prior research emphasizes integration processes enabled by formal power structures, it provides limited insight into how informal hierarchies operate within entrepreneurial teams to shape innovation outcomes. We focus on expertise-based authority—the informal and collectively recognized right to influence decisions based on domain-specific competence—as a critical contingency influencing whether and when founding team diversity translates into innovation. We theorize that the innovative value of diversity depends on its interaction with expertise-based authority, and that this relationship is further contingent on virtual collaboration and task problem ambiguity. Using two complementary empirical studies—a cross-sectional analysis of startup teams and a longitudinal study of top management teams in high-growth firms—we show that the combination of high diversity and expertise-based authority enables founding teams to achieve superior creativity and innovation performance. These effects are amplified in highly virtual settings and under high problem ambiguity. This study contributes to research on entrepreneurial team innovation by identifying expertise-based authority as an important contingency that conditions the effects of founding team diversity.

Trust and Founder Stability: Dyadic Effects on Satisfaction and Turnover Intentions

ENT
Christian Toni Wimmer (Technical University of Munich)
Founding teams are central to new venture creation, yet many ventures are destabilized by breakdowns inside the entrepreneurial team. Trust is widely viewed as a key relational resource that enables coordination under uncertainty, but prior work has not clearly distinguished how trust operates within co-founder dyads and whether trust imbalances matter beyond overall trust. Drawing on social exchange theory and interdependence perspectives, we develop a dyadic model that separates actor trust (a founder’s trust in the co-founder), partner trust (being trusted by the co-founder), and trust asymmetry (differences in trust within the dyad). We argue that actor trust represents a socioemotional investment with implications for relationship quality and withdrawal motivation, whereas partner trust functions as a relational signal that can shape how founders evaluate their collaboration. We further theorize that trust asymmetry disrupts reciprocity and may undermine dyadic functioning. We test these ideas in early-stage new venture teams consisting of two active co-founders and analyze the data using actor-partner interdependence models that account for dyadic non-independence. Findings show that founders’ own trust is a consistent predictor of relationship quality and lower withdrawal motivation, while being trusted shows more outcome-contingent associations. In contrast, trust asymmetry provides limited incremental explanatory power once actor and partner trust are considered, suggesting that imbalance may be weaker, conditional, or harder to detect in early-stage teams. Overall, this study clarifies distinct trust pathways in founder dyads and integrates “trusting” and “being trusted” into a unified framework, offering a more cautious assessment of trust within entrepreneurial teams.

Founding Team Composition and Investment Outcomes: The Mediating Role of Strategic Positioning

ENT
Jungsoo Ahn (State University of New York at Stony Brook)
Building on signaling and capability perspectives, we develop a mechanism-based account of how founding team composition shapes investment outcomes through early strategic positioning. Using a dataset of U.S. blockchain ventures (2009–2022), we examine two positioning choices: industry scope and value-chain positioning. Mediation analyses show that mixed teams combining technological and business expertise secure funding faster than specialized teams, and that this advantage is partially explained by mixed teams’ broader industry scope. We also find that team composition predicts value-chain positioning: mixed teams are more likely than specialized teams to adopt upstream positioning. Value-chain positioning, in turn, shapes early investor sorting: upstream ventures attract a higher share of financial-logic investors in the first financing stage, whereas downstream ventures align more with corporate-logic investors. Overall, the study clarifies how founding team capabilities translate into strategic positioning choices and, in turn, heterogeneous funding trajectories and investor types in an emerging technology ecosystem.
