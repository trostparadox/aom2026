Scale and Scope in Reverse Morris Trusts

STR
Emilie Feldman (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania); Constance E. Helfat (Tuck School of Business)
Reverse Morris Trusts (RMTs), also known as spinoff mergers, are little studied yet economically significant. RMTs differ from standalone spinoffs in one primary respect: the spinoff immediately merges with another company. Prior research suggests that changes in scale and scope due to M&A improve performance. However, research has focused on ex post rather than ex ante (potential) benefits and on gains from scope rather than scale. This study provides a framework that decomposes the ex ante benefits of M&A into those connected to scale, scope, cost reductions, and revenue enhancements, and applies it to RMTs, for which granular data are available. Results show that the potential benefits of scale from cost reductions are more positively associated with shareholder value than the potential benefits of scope.

Corporate Scope Adjustments via Acquisitions and Divestitures as Responses to Rivals Being Acquired

STR
Jens-Christian Friedmann (Rotterdam School of Management); Korcan Kavusan; Louis Mulotte (Tilburg University)
Research in competitive dynamics and corporate strategy suggests that firms adjust when the competitive landscape shifts, yet we know little about how focal firms reconfigure their corporate scope when a rival is acquired by a third party. Drawing on the awareness–motivation–capability framework, we theorize that rival acquisitions constitute salient structural shocks that increase focal firms’ awareness of changed competitive conditions, intensify their motivation to respond, and facilitate the capability to implement scope-changing actions by shaping stakeholder expectations. We argue that focal firms may respond through scope expansion (acquisitions) or scope contraction (divestitures), and we develop hypotheses about when each response is more likely. We predict that acquisition responses are stronger when the focal firm overperforms the acquired rival, when investor reactions to the rival acquisition are favorable, and when the third-party acquirer is also a competitor of the focal firm. Divestiture responses are stronger when the focal firm underperforms the rival, when investor reactions are unfavorable, and when the third-party acquirer is not a competitor. We find support for our predictions using data on U.S. public firms. The study links competitive response to corporate scope activities and offers guidance for executives navigating industry consolidation.

Should I Stay or Should I Go: Startup Repositioning Following Rival Acquisitions

STR
John McKeon (Syracuse University)
This paper studies how startup acquisitions impact the positioning of rival startups. On one hand, the acquisition of a startup may put rivals at a competitive disadvantage, incentivizing positioning away from the acquired firm. On the other hand, a startup acquisition may send positive signals of future demand on the acquisition or product market, incentivizing rivals to position closer to the acquired firm. I compile a novel dataset of historical snapshots of startup websites over time and construct a measure of distance between startups' positions on the product market. Applying a matched difference-in-differences framework, I find that following the acquisition of a rival, startups move away from the acquired rival (differentiate themselves), on average. The effect is stronger for startups that have not raised venture capital funding and in older markets. These findings highlight the role of the M&A market in shaping the direction of entrepreneurial innovation, with implications for entrepreneurial strategy and antitrust policy.

Reconfiguring the Scope of the Firm Through Corporate Sell-Offs: A Systematic Literature Review

STR
Glenn Hauser (University of Bern)
Sell-offs are a widely used mechanism for restructuring the corporate portfolios of multi-business firms and have attracted substantial scholarly attention over the past decades. Yet, this evidence remains fragmented across disciplinary silos and thematic streams. As a result, the sell-off literature lacks integration which impedes the consolidation of a distinct research domain and the accumulation of knowledge. To address this gap, we conduct a systematic literature review of 87 articles published between 1981 and 2025. We organize the field across antecedents, outcomes, moderators, and drivers of shareholder value, and consolidate largely stand-alone findings into overarching themes to develop a comprehensive framework. Our results reveal that evidence is most consistent for antecedents with sell-offs being strongly associated with financial pressure, weak firm performance, and organizational complexity. While short-term market reactions to announcements are robustly positive, evidence on completed sell-offs and long-term outcomes is mixed and remains underdeveloped. We therefore challenge the prevailing view that sell-offs, and divestitures more broadly, are inherently value enhancing, especially in the long run. Finally, we identify key gaps and priorities for future research and derive practical implications for managers and boards.
