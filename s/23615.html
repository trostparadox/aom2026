Session overview

This session explores work, community, and belonging among people whose relationship to organizations or labor markets is shaped by vulnerability, displacement, or contested recognition. The papers address entrepreneurs with disabilities, stay-at-home mothers, identity transitions among displaced fishers, diaspora organizations, and indigenous community resilience. Together, they show how work can function both as a source of dignity and as a site of exclusion, ambiguity, and struggle.

Support or Obstacle? The Role of Digitalisation in the Lives of Entrepreneurs With Disabilities

DEI
Sara Csillag (Budapest University of Economics and Business); Carmen Svastics; Zsuzsanna Gyori
Despite a growing interest in digitalisation and disability at work, entrepreneurship appears to be a less frequently researched area. Drawing on qualitative interviews with 19 entrepreneurs with disabilities, this study examines the interplay between digitalisation, disability and entrepreneurship in a Central and Eastern European country, Hungary. The lens of the study was the Bio-psycho-social Model of Disability (ICF model), which provided the opportunity to explore the biological, psychological and social factors at play. The analysis of findings revealed a dynamic relationship between bodily functions, activities, participation, as well as environmental and personal aspects within the entrepreneurial context. Thus, while it is argued that digitalisation holds great promise for all, imperative digitalisation may be regarded not only as an opportunity but also a challenge, where outcomes depend largely on the coaction of individual and contextual factors.

Not Working, Still Judged: How Moralized Work Shapes Stay-at-Home Mothers’ Lives

DEI
Yufan Deng (Southwestern University of Finance and Economics); Dishi Hu (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
Stay-at-home motherhood is often treated as a private family choice or a temporary career interruption. Yet in work-centered societies, paid employment functions as a moral marker of responsibility and social contribution, leaving mothers who step out of paid work subject to continued moral evaluation. This article develops a process model to explain why stay-at-home mothers who share similar circumstances diverge in how they relate to work, community, and social life over time. Drawing on research on moralization and work meaning, we conceptualize motherhood and paid work as coexisting moralized domains that generate recurring moral encounters. We introduce moral credit states and regulation orientations to explain how recognition and deficit are experienced and managed. By reframing marginalization as moralized organizational positioning, this framework extends work-family and inequality research and offers new insight into participation at the boundaries of employment.

Crossing the Mental Fence: Identity Conflict and Narrative Strategy in Livelihood Transition

DEI
Siqi Cao (Hainan University)
Livelihood transitions in rural and coastal peripheries constitute profound ontic shifts rather than mere occupational changes. While existing research frequently prioritizes economic or ecological metrics, it often obscures the nuanced, liminal experiences of transitional subjects. To address this gap, this paper examines the process of identity conflict and reconstruction through a longitudinal qualitative data of fishers on West Island, Sanya, China. The findings reveal that land tenure insecurity acts as a critical catalyst, dismantling the material and symbolic foundations of the traditional fishing identity. We identify a threefold typology of identity conflict characterizing this liminal phase: (1) Doing: the friction between the fisher’s habitus of autonomy and the institutional discipline of service work; (2) Understanding: a symbolic rupture between self-perception and external stigmatization; and (3) Being: structural exclusion that traps individuals in low-skilled positions, prolonging their liminal status. Although fishers employ emotionally resonant and rationally functional narratives to legitimize their transition, we find that these discursive strategies are often undermined by a lack of social validation, leading to hidden mental health costs. We conclude that sustainable rural transitions require governance frameworks that transcend economic compensation to address the psychological toll of displacement and safeguard the dignity of marginalized communities.

Diaspora and Homeland at Work: Israel-Palestine and the Jewish Organization

DEI
Edith Pick (Brandeis University)
Diaspora organizations can be seen as spaces where national identities are negotiated, expanded, and contested, and as transnational sites where organizational members hold overlapping and multiple frames of belonging and loyalty. This framing suggests the potential to extend diversity debates beyond the us/them binary logic of the nation-state. This qualitative study explores representations of Israel-Palestine in Jewish organizations in the UK, that broadly identify with a liberal Zionist orientation. It examines how nationhood and attachment to “homeland” are constructed, negotiated, and managed in everyday organizational life, with particular attention to moments of ambiguity and dissent. It shows how, as employees navigate diasporic ambivalence, diversity and inclusion discourses work to shape and often limit political-ideological differences. It contributes to diversity and organization studies by conceptualizing nationhood and homeland attachment as DEI questions and by advancing a diaspora organizational lens on how nationalism and belonging are negotiated in workplaces. It also highlights the ambivalent role of communities in DEI work, showing how community engagement can operate both as a source of ethical grounding and as a gatekeeping mechanism that constrains organizational capacity to accommodate difference.
