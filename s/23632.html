Multiple Liminality, Mobile Communitas: Management Consultants Walking Both Sides of the Street

MC
Jeanette Hartley (The Open University); Maria Rita Tagliaventi (University of Bologna); Giacomo Carli (The Open University)
We explore how management consultants experience and manage multiple, overlapping forms of liminality across their consultancy, client, and personal domains. We adopt a qualitative, interpretivist research design involving 28 management consultants drawn from three UK-based consultancies of varying size and market focus. Data collection was primarily based on semi-structured interviews, supported by organisational documents. Data analysis followed the Gioia methodology (Gioia, Corley and Hamilton, 2013), employing inductive reasoning to develop a grounded, process-oriented model. Management consultants locate themselves at the intersection of competing expectations of their consultancy, their clients, and their personal life, thus enacting multiple liminality. They form mobile communitas, i.e., spontaneous alliances that clients, members of their internal consultancy networks, family and friends, which are nested and fluctuate over time and space. These communitas provide emotional and cognitive support that helps consultants engage with, and make sense of, their multiple liminality. Prior research on management consultants has examined different sources of liminality separately, resulting in a fragmented rather than integrated understanding of the phenomenon. By focusing on consultants’ lived experiences of multiple liminality, this paper deepens insight into how this condition is interpreted and managed at the individual level and how it relates to organisational-level liminality.

Neither Employee Nor Consultant: Identity Negotiation In Fractional Leadership

MC
Toni Haugh (Drexel University); Rajiv Nag (Drexel University)
This study develops a grounded theory framework based on an in-depth analysis of the lived experiences of professionals joining an emerging occupation - fractional leadership—wherein senior executives are hired part-time by small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) to provide strategic leadership without the full cost of retaining full-time executives. Positioned within the contingent work framework, fractional leaders differ from traditional gig and freelance workers due to their integrated organizational roles. Our findings reveal that fractional leaders are motivated by the desire for agency, autonomy, and impact, while community networks play a crucial role in sustaining their professional and personal journeys. Fractional leaders face unique challenges, including financial instability and career uncertainty, yet their proactive community engagement differentiates them from other contingent workers. This study underscores the potential of fractional leadership to enhance organizational dynamics within SMEs. It highlights the importance of further research into the evolving role of fractional executives in the broader landscape of contingent work.

The Triadic System of Founder’s Syndrome: Integrating Founder, Governance & Stakeholder Perspective

MC
David J Westover (Western Michigan University)
Founder’s Syndrome (FS) is a phenomenon whereby an organization’s founder refuses to give up their disproportionate influence within the organization, which over time can inhibit its growth and maturation. FS has been credited with the dissolution of hundreds, if not thousands, of organizations over the years. Based on the number of studies conducted over the past thirty years, it would appear that most researchers have overlooked the importance of Founder’s Syndrome. This might be due to its amorphous nature or because it is a widespread problem with a very limited research base from which to work. This article seeks to review the current state of research on the subject, synthesize the disparate theoretical perspectives, and reconceptualize FS as a multistakeholder phenomenon. With this understanding, there are many avenues for solid research that can be explored to expand the knowledge base about this important effect and how it impacts newly founded organizations.

Careers at the Margins of the Gig Economy: A Review and Framework for Low-Skill Gig Work

MC
Ankur Kushwaha (FLAME University); Yusuf Hassan (Heriot Watt University)
The rapid expansion of the gig economy has created large-scale employment opportunities while simultaneously raising concerns about the long-term career prospects of workers engaged in low-skill, platform-based work. Prior research has examined issues such as precarity and instability, relatively little attention has been paid to how prolonged engagement in low-skill gig work shapes workers’ career trajectories. Drawing on the miner-prospector continuum, this study adopts a prospector lens to review the gig economy literature. We develop an integrative classification of gig work based on work arrangements and skill requirements. Our analysis shows that workers in low-skill gig work often experience uncertain and fragile career paths. Opportunities to move upward within platforms are limited, and options outside the platform are frequently scarce, increasing the risk that workers become stuck in their career. Building on these insights, we propose a practitioner-oriented framework that demonstrates how platform organizations can create meaningful career growth opportunities for gig workers by using existing organizational structures and platform-based business models.

A Narrative Analysis of Women Directors' Sandwich Generation Experiences

MC
Roshona Porter (Western Michigan University)
This dissertation examines how women in director-level positions and above narrate and make meaning of their experiences navigating dual caregiving responsibilities—simultaneously caring for children and aging parents—while maintaining strategic organizational leadership roles. Despite extensive research on work-family conflict and motherhood in the workplace, scholarly attention to elder care and dual caregiving among women leaders remains limited. Preliminary autoethnographic research identified five leadership-specific complications that distinguish sandwich generation experiences in leadership contexts from those in non-leadership roles: strategic decision-making under cognitive duress, professional visibility paradox, career momentum interruption, identity integration challenges, and organizational context gaps. This study employs narrative analysis methodology to examine how women leaders construct meaning from these experiences, manage competing role demands, and navigate organizational contexts that may inadequately support their caregiving realities. Through in-depth interviews with women directors experiencing dual caregiving, this research aims to contribute theoretical insights about the intersection of gender, caregiving, and leadership while identifying organizational support gaps that disproportionately affect women in senior roles.
