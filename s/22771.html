Social Movements and Shifts in Legal Punishments for Contested Practices

SIM
Emily Murray Ulrich (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania); Mary-Hunter McDonnell (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania); Timothy Werner (The University of Texas at Austin); Brayden G. King (Northwestern University)
How do social movements reshape organizational fields through law? Sociological research shows that movements influence legislation, jurisprudence, and legal mobilization, yet less is known about how movements shape the punishment of misconduct once liability is established. This omission is consequential because punishment is a central mechanism through which societies express and enforce moral boundaries. We theorize that social movements intensify legal punishment by heightening the moral salience of contested practices and activating collective emotions—especially moral outrage—among lay publics charged with enforcing the law. In civil litigation, these dynamics are most visible in punitive damages: discretionary sanctions that allow juries to translate condemnation into material penalties. We further argue that movements’ effects on punishment vary with local conditions that shape engagement and receptivity to movement claims. We test these arguments using a difference-in-differences analysis of jury verdicts in employment discrimination cases adjudicated before and after the mainstream emergence of the #MeToo Movement in October 2017. We find that punitive damages in sexual harassment cases increased substantially following #MeToo, particularly in jurisdictions with greater movement-related social media discourse and partisan alignment with the movement. Together, these findings show how social movements transform collective moral judgments into coercive legal sanctions.

Female CEOs and Firm Stakeholder Orientation: An Empirical Investigation

SIM
Nowmi Nowrin (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); Michael A. Abebe (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley)
There is a longstanding scholarly interest in understanding the link between gender diversity in the upper echelons (senior executives and directors) and firm outcomes, with a particular emphasis on the impact of female CEO appointments on firm performance. While research has shown a positive relationship between female CEO leadership and firm performance, less is known regarding the mechanisms through which women CEOs improve firm performance. In this study, we seek to address this gap in the literature by exploring one such mechanism—stakeholder orientation—through which female CEOs enhance firm performance. Drawing from stakeholder theory and the literature on women and prosocial behavior, we argue that, compared to their male counterparts, female CEOs are more likely to emphasize an inclusive and collaborative leadership style that considers the needs of the firm’s diverse stakeholders. Further, we propose that such a leadership approach, in turn, contributes positively to the financial performance and reputation of the firm. Using both a large-scale empirical study and a content analysis of CEO communications among S&P 1500 firms from 2014 to 2023, we found support for our predictions. Implications for research and practice are discussed.

Board Sustainability Expertise: How Shareholder and Employee Reps Shape Supervisory Board Practice

SIM
Jan Marvin Apel (Leuphana University of Lüneburg)
This paper examines how sustainability expertise is defined, distributed, and translated into influence on parity codetermined supervisory boards in large German listed corporations, and how shareholder and employee representatives jointly shape sustainability monitoring as European sustainability regulation and scrutiny intensify. Thirty-six semi-structured interviews with supervisory board members in parity codetermined listed corporations, analysed abductively through an integrated resource dependence and resource-based perspective to trace how directors construct sustainability expertise, how it is allocated across shareholder and employee benches, and how it becomes consequential in board work through formal and informal governance channels. Sustainability expertise is constructed less as an individual credential than as a collectively developed capability combining regulatory literacy with sector-specific operational understanding. Shareholder representatives tend to supply externally oriented regulatory and capital-market knowledge, whereas employee representatives contribute firm- and workforce-grounded implementation insight and longer time horizons. Codetermination can therefore broaden the board’s knowledge base, but influence depends on whether expertise is channelled through committee mandates, agenda-setting authority, and informal cross-bench coordination. Boards vary in whether these mechanisms confine expertise to defensible compliance or enable strategic integration. The study is context-specific to large German parity codetermined listed corporations with a mandatory two-tier system. Future research could compare corporate governance regimes and link board sustainability expertise to organisational outcomes under evolving European sustainability regulation. This paper opens the boardroom black box by theorising sustainability expertise as a dynamic, collective board capability and showing how codetermination, by bringing together distinct shareholder and employee resource bases, conditions whose expertise counts and how it is converted into influence.
