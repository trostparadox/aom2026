Clarity Before Ambiguity: Attribution and Linguistic Adaptation During Micro-Role Transitions

ENT
Daniela Defazio (University of Bath); Vincenzo Buttice (Politecnico di Milano)
Individuals often navigate micro-role transitions, temporarily enacting new roles while still inhabiting existing ones. During such transitions, negative performance feedback is often visible but difficult to interpret because it may reflect resistance to the newly enacted role, spillover from established roles, or unrelated factors. As a result, individuals face attributional uncertainty that complicates efforts to adapt communication. We develop a theory of when negative performance feedback becomes actionable in identity-incongruent micro-role transitions. We argue that such feedback generates pressure to adapt, but triggers adaptation only once actors achieve attribution clarity that the reaction stems from resistance to the newly added role. We further argue that attribution clarity arises when feedback is distinctive to the new role enactments or persists across consecutive messages. Once attribution clarity is achieved, individuals adapt their communication by making their language more semantically ambiguous, allowing them to maintain alignment with audience expectations while enacting multiple roles. We use computational linguistic analysis to quantify semantic ambiguity and test these arguments using a longitudinal dataset of 4,286 social media communications by 142 creative and social entrepreneurs during crowdfunding campaigns and leverage an exogenous engagement shock to strengthen causal inference. By theorizing attribution clarity as a conditional mechanism for action, this study advances performance feedback research and explains how individuals adapt communication during micro-role transitions.

Living the Dream: How Entrepreneurs Manage Role Multiplicity in Lifestyle Ventures

ENT
Valeria Cavotta (Baruch College, CUNY); Scott L. Newbert (Baruch College, CUNY)
Although lifestyle entrepreneurs (LEs) create ventures to pursue their passion for an activity or place, they are often underprepared and/or reluctant to perform the managerial tasks that are essential to operating lifestyle ventures. To better understand the implications that this entrepreneurial role has on LEs, we draw on role identity theory and entrepreneurial well-being research to qualitatively explore on what bases LEs derive their lifestyle role identity, how they manage their lifestyle and entrepreneurial roles, and how their engagement with these roles affects their well-being. Findings from in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 36 LEs reveal three main insights. First, they show that the lifestyle role identity is a function of both the LE’s passion for and their knowledge of the activity or place around which the venture was founded. Second, our findings suggest that the autonomy and client interactions that result from engaging in the entrepreneurial role can have both positive (enriching, enhancing) and negative (constraining, challenging) effects on LEs’ lifestyle role identity. Third, we find that role multiplicity in lifestyle ventures can have both positive (thriving, deepening) and negative (satisficing, sustaining) effects on LEs’ well-being and that any negative effects may be attenuated by enacting coping strategies (allocation, selective accommodation).

The Proper Order of Goods: Judgment and Integrity in Lifestyle Entrepreneurship (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Robin Holt (University of Bristol Business School); Christian Garmann Johnsen (Copenhagen Business School); Stratos Ramoglou (University of Bristol)
Lifestyle entrepreneurship—creating new ventures to engage in activities that entrepreneurs find inherently meaningful—hinges on a fundamental tension. Although the ends pursued by lifestyle entrepreneurs are noneconomic, such as the intrinsic rewards of pursuing one’s passion and achieving fulfillment through engagement in meaningful activities, sustaining a new venture requires increasing attention to economic demands. This tension often leads lifestyle entrepreneurs either to lose their passion or to neglect requirements essential to venture viability. Yet, despite this pervasive tension, some lifestyle entrepreneurs manage to achieve fulfillment and preserve their craft-based identity while simultaneously securing economic ends—and even thrive through particularly demanding processes of scaling. To address this puzzle, we draw on Alasdair MacIntyre’s account of practical reasoning, which explains how humans can sustain integrity and lasting flourishing in the face of real-world pressures of the kind involved in lifestyle entrepreneurship. The core of our MacIntyrean framework is that human flourishing is achieved when the pursuit of external goods (such as financial power and institutional legitimacy) does not displace internal goods (such as craft mastery and meaningful work). Crucially, this framework offers insight into the judgments that prevent such displacement. Applying MacIntyre to lifestyle entrepreneurship, we (1) deepen theoretical understanding of its core tension as an institutional push toward a reversal in the order of goods, and (2) theorize the judgment processes through which lifestyle entrepreneurs can resist the subordination of internal goods to external ones. Beyond addressing a central paradox in the lifestyle entrepreneurship literature, we argue that conceiving lifestyle entrepreneurship as an ideal-typical context for maintaining practice integrity allows us to revisit core assumptions about mainstream entrepreneurship. In doing so, we problematize the assumption that profit is invariably an end rather than a means even in for-profit venturing, and show how the study of lifestyle entrepreneurship sheds valuable light on the very practice of business venturing as, most fundamentally, an activity in search of human flourishing.

The Struggle to Become a Founder: Role and Personal Identity Tensions in Early-Stage Social Ventures

ENT
Claudia Moura Romero (Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile); Julio A. Pertuze (Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile); Julian Andres Diaz Tautiva (Universidad Andrés Bello)
We examine how identity tensions shape founders’ trajectories during the early, uncertain stages of social venture formation. While prior research highlights the role of founder identity in sustaining hybrid ventures, it largely focuses on entrepreneurs who have already consolidated the entrepreneurial role. We instead study identity formation during the liminal phase, when founder identity remains emergent and negotiable. Drawing on a five-year longitudinal study of 17 solo founders in Chile, we combine interviews, surveys, and configurational analysis (fsQCA) to identify four identity pathways. Three—role-based misfits, altruistic idealists, and split professionals—are associated with venture discontinuation, while integrated hybrids are linked to venture survival. Disengagement arises through distinct mechanisms, including ideological incompatibility, insufficient agentic motivation despite prosocial commitment, and competition from entrenched professional identities. In contrast, integrated hybrids navigate similar role tensions by combining self-enhancement and self-transcendence values. We show that founder identity transition involves more than adopting a new role; it requires alignment between personal values and role expectations, advancing a multidimensional, configurational perspective on founder persistence in social entrepreneurship.

The Role of Personal Identity in the Adoption of and Transition of a Role Identity

ENT
Rama Krishna Reddy Kummitha
Based on a longitudinal study conducted in the social entrepreneurship setting, this paper examines the micro-foundations that drive the interest of aspiring social entrepreneurs (ASEs) and their role adoption. We emphasise that personal identity not only influences their role salience but also seeks for role transition when actors perceive that such a transition allows them to achieve the goals associated with their personal identity—more so than their engagement with the initial chosen roles. This paper builds theory around the social entrepreneurial initialization process and shows how ASEs embrace and transform their roles. Early research has considered the entrepreneurial roles to be somewhat static, whereas our findings highlight that role selection and transition is determined by personal identity coupled with entrepreneurial attributes that are shaped and reshaped by the interactions between the self and actors in the environment, which enforces identity reflexivity on the focal actors. We make three theoretical contributions to the social and identity literature.
