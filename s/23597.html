Do Machines Make Firms Less Responsible? Automation and the Erosion of Firm-Employee Relations

TIM
Dongil Daniel Keum (Columbia Business School); Simeng Wang (Columbia Business School); Nandil Bhatia (Columbia Business School)
By reducing firms’ dependence on human labor, automation increases firm bargaining power and weakens incentives to invest in firm-employee relations. We show that higher automation is associated with lower employee-related CSR scores, deterioration in employee-related corporate culture, increased framing of employees as costs, greater labor rights violations, and more negative employee reviews of senior leadership and work-life balance. These negative effects are stronger in routine-intensive industries, in labor markets where workers have fewer outside options due to high unemployment or greater labor market concentration, and in years with lower electricity prices. Our findings caution that automation affects not only the quantity of jobs but also firms’ treatment of employees and the quality of employees’ workplace experience.

From Technostress to Empowerment: AI’s Role in Supporting Autistic Workplace Inclusion

TIM
Paul A. Raddatz (The University of Memphis)
This paper develops a theoretical model examining how AI technologies impact the workplace experiences of autistic employees through the dual lens of technostress and coping mechanisms. While AI integration presents unique challenges for autistic individuals due to their distinct cognitive processing styles and sensory sensitivities, it simultaneously offers unprecedented potential for workplace support and inclusion. Drawing on technostress theory, cognitive theories of autism, and emerging research on AI in organizations, the model explicates how AI functions as both a source of stress and a coping resource. The model identifies five key AI-induced technostressors particularly relevant to autistic employees and explores how these may disproportionately affect their well-being compared to neurotypical colleagues. The model also considers individual-level factors (such as AI literacy and autism trait severity) and organizational-level factors (including inclusive culture and leadership support) that moderate the effectiveness of AI as a coping mechanism. This theoretical integration advances management literature by extending technostress theory to incorporate neurodiversity considerations, challenging implicit assumptions of neurotypicality in existing models, and providing insights for developing inclusive technological implementations. The model has significant implications for organizations striving to leverage AI technologies while supporting workplace neurodiversity and creating truly inclusive environments.

Innovation After Rejection: The Role of Ownership Assignments and Misalignments

TIM
Wei Wei (Imperial College London); Paola Criscuolo (Imperial College London); Martine Haas; Bárbara Larrañeta (Pablo de Olavide University)
What shapes employees’ willingness to continue innovating after their organizations reject their ideas? Developing an ownership perspective on innovation engagement, we propose that rejections are likely to reduce employees’ subsequent innovation activity, but this effect may be mitigated or amplified depending on whether ownership rights to the rejected ideas are assigned to the employee or to the firm. We further argue that ownership assignment will have more influence when outcomes do not align with employees’ expectations, with opposite effects for two types of misalignments: unexpected losses, when ownership is unexpectedly assigned to the firm, and unexpected gains, when it is unexpectedly assigned to the employee. We test our hypotheses using a proprietary longitudinal dataset of employee invention disclosures in an R&D-intensive Fortune 500 firm operating across countries with variation in intellectual property rights and find evidence for the predicted effects of both ownership assignment and ownership misalignments. We discuss the implications of our findings for research on employee innovation, idea ownership, and organizational fairness.

Technological Self-Efficacy and Healthcare Workers’ Occupational Well-Being in Smart Healthcare

TIM
Wei Zhang (Sichuan University); Chu Qin (Sichuan University); Min Wu (Sichuan University); Zihan Hu (Sichuan University)
This study examines how technological self-efficacy affects healthcare workers’ occupational well-being in smart healthcare environments. Drawing on social cognitive theory and self-determination theory, a mixed-methods approach is adopted. A quasi-experimental design compares an AI technology– assisted group with an ordinary technology–assisted group using survey data from 123 healthcare specialists across four public hospitals in Sichuan Province, China, supplemented by semi-structured interviews with 22 physicians. The results indicate that technological self-efficacy significantly enhances occupational well-being, with stronger effects observed in AI-assisted settings. Occupational resilience mediates the relationship between technological self-efficacy and occupational well-being. Perceived technological substitution negatively affects occupational well-being but positively moderates the relationship between technological self-efficacy and occupational resilience, particularly in the AI-assisted group.
