Where You Are or Who You Know? Location Versus Network Effects on INVs' Access to Venture Capital

IM
Jukyeong (Judy) Han (McMaster University); Minyoung Kim (The Ohio State University)
This study investigates the net effects of international new ventures’ (INVs) location and network ties on accessing venture capital investments, and the differences therein. Drawing on the information diffusion literature, we develop a theoretical framework on the unique roles of INVs’ location and network in diffusing ventures’ information, thereby facilitating ventures’ access to venture capitals’ investment. For this purpose, we introduce and contrast two types of liability of outsidership (LOO): LOO to a relevant location and LOO to a relevant network. We maintain that location diffuses INVs’ information by facilitating direct interaction between ventures and venture capitalists and, consequently, the flow of information is typically localized in geographic space. On the contrary, network diffuses information in a more indirect but farther-reaching manner than that of location via such channels as word-of-mouth and referrals between venture capitalists. Results of empirical analysis with a dataset of 2,474 INVs located in 66 countries show that location-only ventures can access the largest amount of venture capital investments followed by network-only counterparts, and those with neither of them. We also find that the difference in the amount of venture capital investments between location-only and network-only ventures increases over time.

Network Position, Ownership Structures, and Business Group Internationalization

IM
Dinesh Prabhu (Not Associated); Karthik Dhandapani (Indian Institute of Management Tiruchirappalli); Mukundhan K.V. (Indian Institute of Management Tiruchirappalli); Surender Munjal (Aston University)
This study examines why affiliates within business groups display divergent internationalization behavior, arguing that heterogeneity arises from the interaction between affiliates’ network positions and the hierarchical governance structures that bind them. Drawing on identity domain theory and the literature on embeddedness, the paper theorizes that apex affiliates, those occupying central positions in intra-group director networks, become custodians of the group’s domestic identity, leading them to prioritize internal coordination and domestic legitimacy over foreign expansion. Conversely, non-apex affiliates, positioned more peripherally, possess greater strategic autonomy and thus bear the burden of international venturing that builds group-level intangible assets. Using a 14-year panel of 277 Indian BG-affiliated firms, the study finds robust evidence that affiliate centrality negatively predicts internationalization. Furthermore, promoter ownership and the control–cash flow rights wedge amplify this negative relationship through distinct mechanisms: concentrated promoter ownership heightens risk aversion around apex firms, whereas a larger wedge encourages promoters to externalize risk by restraining apex affiliates while enabling selective experimentation by non-apex affiliates.

Compensating for Technological Resources Gaps: Network Centrality and Foreign Establishment Modes

IM
Gabriele Galli (University of Innsbruck); Roberto Urbani (LUISS); Louis Mulotte (Tilburg University)
Classic Resource-Based View logic predicts that only firms with strong technological resource endowments possess the technological and organizational capabilities needed to build new operations from scratch, yet empirical evidence shows that firms with low technological endowments sometimes choose greenfield investments rather than cross-border acquisitions, contradicting conventional expectations. This study develops a relational extension of the RBV to explain this tension. We argue that board network centrality functions as a network-enabled resource that can compensate for technological gaps. Using a sample of 797 European publicly listed firms observed between 2013 and 2023 and 16,771 cross-border greenfield and M&A investments, combined with annual board interlock networks, we find evidence consistent with this compensatory mechanism. Firms with high technological endowments are more likely to choose greenfields, consistent with RBV arguments, but more central firms—regardless of technological strength—also display a higher propensity to enter via greenfields. Crucially, network centrality exerts its strongest pro-greenfield effect among technologically weak firms, enabling them to undertake larger greenfield commitments and smaller, more selective acquisitions. These findings highlight how external, relational resources reshape establishment mode choices and resource commitment, offering a more complete understanding of how firms mobilize internal and external capabilities to support international expansion.

Multinational Enterprise Failure and Alliance Network Spillovers in the Home Country

IM
Thi Ha Trang (Lucy) Dang (National Chung Cheng University)
This study explores the market reactions and long-term value implications of multinational enterprise (MNE) failures on alliance network survival firms in the home country, offering fresh insights into the intersection of networks, organizations, and international business. Analyzing 24 bankruptcies in the mining industry from 2001 to 2021, spanning 14,980 alliances across 24 networks, the findings reveal that MNE failures impose significantly greater adverse effects on the short and long-term stock performance of survival firms in the home country than non-MNE failures. Moreover, the study identifies critical distinctions between distress and announcing events. Distress events lead to a 1.3 percent decline in firm value while announcing events show no significant loss (0.0 percent), suggesting that networks buffer survival firms through enhanced information access and resource redistribution. Finally, the centrality of the failing MNE within the network further amplifies these dynamics, intensifying both negative and protective effects. While higher centrality fosters short-term resource benefits, it exacerbates long-term negative contagion effects, deepening survival firms’ underperformance. These findings illuminate the dual role of networks as both safeguards and conduits of risk, advancing our understanding of how organizational failures cascade through interconnected firms.
