Beyond Corporate Entrepreneurship: Leadership Discretion, Bureaucratic Paradoxes,and Employee Agency

ENT
Marcos Hashimoto (Bradley University)
Purpose This study investigates how bureaucratic structures interact with employee agency to shape entrepreneurial behavior in established organizations. Building on institutional theory and agency theory, we develop and empirically refine a multilevel model explaining how autonomy, rules, paradoxes, and cognitive heterogeneity influence intrapreneurial behavior. Design/methodology/approach We conducted a three-month field experiment across ten organizations (N = 50 employees), where supervisors progressively relaxed select bureaucratic rules and controls. Following the intervention, employees and supervisors participated in semi-structured interviews. Analysis employed the Gioia methodology to inductively derive a paradox framework and a four-type employee typology. Findings The study surfaced four paradoxes governing bureaucratic–entrepreneurial dynamics: (1) rules constrain yet enable action, (2) bureaucracy limits discretion but also produces entrepreneurial behavior, (3) internalization of rules reduces the necessity of rules, and (4) uniform structures increase the chances to hide agency differences. We also identified four employee types—A (Competent Conformist), B (Constraint Contributor), C (Accomplished Intrapreneurs), and D (Structure-dependent Performer)—each expressing unique responses to autonomy and constraint. Originality/value The research contributes: (1) a revised paradox-based theoretical model integrating institutionalization, agency, and entrepreneurial action; (2) empirical microfoundations demonstrating how employee heterogeneity shapes paradox navigation; and (3) actionable insights for managing autonomy in bureaucratic environments.

Beyond the Lone Intrapreneur: Team-Based Upward Influence and Leaders’ Intrapreneurial Engagement

ENT
Yingya Jia (Shanghai University); Xiaoyu Wang (Tongji University); Shuping Yue (Shanghai University)
As AI transforms industries, intrapreneurship—employees pursuing innovative opportunities within established organizations—has gained increasing prominence. However, prior research has predominantly conceptualized intrapreneurship as an individual-level phenomenon, overlooking the team-based and hierarchical contexts in which such behavior unfolds. Drawing on followership theory and an upward influence perspective, this study examines when and how team members’ intrapreneurial behavior shapes team leaders’ intrapreneurial engagement through leaders’ psychological activation of role responsibility. We use experience sampling data from 50 R&D teams in China and finds that leaders high in extraversion or perceiving strong team emotional support increased their intrapreneurial behavior when members’ engagement is low. By framing intrapreneurship as a collective team process, this research moves beyond the “lone hero” narrative, emphasizing hierarchical reciprocity and temporal dynamics.

Decision-Making Paradoxes in Business Families: A Response Surface Analysis

ENT
Clemens Christian Krueger (Zeppelin University); Reinhard Prügl (Vienna University of Economics and Business)
Business families face inherent paradoxes arising from the intersection of kinship and enterprise logics, yet how they navigate these tensions remains poorly understood. Drawing on paradox theory and Response Surface Analysis of 418 German speaking business families, we examine how families configure three core decision making paradoxes: hierarchy versus autonomy, rationality versus relationships, and formality versus informality. Findings reveal that different paradoxes exhibit fundamentally different optimal patterns. The rationality relationships paradox shows a linear additive pattern where embracing both poles maximizes performance. The hierarchy autonomy paradox exhibits a U-shaped pattern where moderate balance underperforms clear commitment. Among family characteristics, family governance emerges as the strongest predictor of proximity to optimal configurations. These findings extend paradox theory by demonstrating context specificity in paradox navigation, showing that not all tensions should be managed identically. This study opens the black box of business family decision making and identifies governance structures as key enablers of paradox capability.

Empathy and Cofounder Relationship Satisfaction: An Accuracy and Bias-Based Perspective

ENT
Sukriti Hari (LM Thapar School of Management, Thapar Institute of Engineering and Technology); Raja Singaram (University of Galway); Sarnish Kour (LM Thapar School of Management, Thapar Institute of Engineering and Technology); William B. Gartner (Babson College)
Placed in a high-stress context while building startups, cofounder often experience intense relationship issues with each other. Drawing from extant research on situational empathy, we performed two experimental studies: i) a quasi-experimental entrepreneurial simulation in a laboratory setting (N=42, 21 dyads) and ii) a field study (N=260 individuals, 130 dyads). Using the truth-bias approach to examine expressed and perceived empathy among cofounder dyads we tested several hypotheses. We find that empathic understanding between cofounders is positively related to their relationship satisfaction and this relationship is negatively mediated by the level of relationship conflict they experience. Interestingly, an inflated perception of the empathy received from a cofounder serves the cofounder relationship better than an accurate perception of the same. We discuss the implications of our findings to show how they contribute to advancing our understanding of cofounder relationship dynamics and its contribution to new venture development.

From Initiative to Innovation: Intrapreneurship & Paradox Mindset

ENT
Aníbal López (ISEG); Tomé Guerreiro D O Salgueiro (University of Delaware)
Although organizations often praise proactive employees for engaging in intrapreneurial behaviors, we know little about the psychological mechanisms that explain this relationship and its boundary conditions. We adopt a cognitive perspective and argue that proactive personality positively predicts intrapreneurial behaviors because proactive employees rely on a paradox mindset. We also take a trait-interaction approach and propose that this relationship is stronger for individuals with lower perfectionism. We tested our hypotheses using a time-lagged design with 382 full-time employees, surveyed at three points over one month. The results supported our model: proactive personality related positively to intrapreneurial behaviors through paradox mindset, and this indirect effect was significantly stronger at lower levels of perfectionism. Our findings extend paradox theory to micro-level intrapreneurial phenomena and show how perfectionism can inhibit the psychological capacities needed to navigate organizational contradictions.
