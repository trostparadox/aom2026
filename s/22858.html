Do Authentic Leadership Behaviors Actually Benefit Leaders?

OB
Wilma Tay (Case Western Reserve University)
Using an experimental vignette, this study examined whether a leader’s admission of a mistake, in line with authentic leadership expectations, incurs positive outcomes for leaders and whether these outcomes are equivalent across leader gender. The authors hypothesized that admitting a mistake would have positive consequences for leaders and that gender may still shape evaluative outcomes for leaders even when they act authentically. Additionally, the authors hypothesized that the positive implications of admitting a mistake would be greater for men than for women. Results showed that when leaders act in line with expectations for authentic leadership, they are evaluated favorably, even in situations where they should be penalized. No significant differences were found when the leader's gender was manipulated. Overall, the findings support the idea that leader behaviors consistent with authentic leadership ideals have positive consequences for leaders. The implications of the findings for research and practice are discussed.

A Calling to Arms: Three Root Causes of Counterproductive Leadership Among U.S. Army Civilians

OB
Megan Marie Pekol-Evans (Marymount University); John Jamison (Marymount University); Andrew Hall (Marymount University); Daniel Stimpson
Intuitively, U.S. Army leaders are thought to be those in uniform. However, Army civilian leaders also fill many senior military leadership roles and typically have fewer leadership development opportunities during their careers than their uniformed counterparts. This grounded theory study began as an exploration of the process of developing effective civilian leadership competencies, but, as the study progressed, it led instead to an exploration of the process that leads to counterproductive civilian leadership. The findings of this exploratory grounded theory study show three different pathways to counterproductive behaviors in Army civilian leadership development, with conditions extremely sensitive at each level: self-development pathway of employee factors (e.g. individual attitudes and behaviors), an operational influence pathway of supervisor factors (e.g. influencing attitudes and behaviors) and an institutional influence pathway of institutional-level factors (e.g. strategic level policy and attitudes). This study’s theoretical coding uncovered a conducive environment for the emergence of counterproductive leadership in organizations. These findings are novel, suggesting that both individual employee behaviors and patterns at the supervisor and organizational levels contribute to the emergence of counterproductive leaders in the workplace.

Reluctant Leaders: A Process-Based Perspective

OB
Hao Zhao (China Europe International Business School)
Prior research on leadership emergence tends to assume that people are willing to lead and has neglected people who are reluctant, even when they are offered the leaders’ positions. We develop a process model based on expectancy theory and cognitive dissonance theory to explain why individuals experience reluctance, why organizations push them to accept the role, and the cognitive and behavioral consequences. We also explore whether reluctant leaders are effective once on the job. The model extends prior work by identifying both individual- and organizational-level antecedents, as well as emotional and behavioral outcomes of reluctant leaders, and offers theoretical and practical insights into interventions that can help reluctant leaders transition into more effective leaders.

Big-X of Leadership: Deriving Characteristic Leader Behaviors Using AI-Based Semantic Embeddings

OB
Peter Yih-Tong Sun; Marc H. Anderson (Iowa State University); Sudong Shang (Griffith University)
Leadership research has produced an extensive array of leadership styles, many of which exhibit substantial conceptual and empirical overlap. Although prior work has attempted to define a full range of leadership behaviors by combining prominent styles and introducing additional dimensions, the field lacks a clear, systematic account of the characteristic leadership behaviors that underlie this proliferation of constructs. This fragmentation has prompted calls to abandon leadership styles altogether; however, doing so risks discarding a substantial body of theoretically and empirically grounded work. In response, this study seeks to uncover a comprehensive set of characteristic leadership behaviors derived directly from existing leadership measures. We first systematically reviewed 123 leadership styles used over the past two decades. From this review, scale items from 42 leadership styles were retained, as they adhered to established scale development criteria, to represent a broad and diverse behavioral domain. Leveraging a lexical-semantic foundation, we applied AI-based semantic embeddings to cluster leadership scale items based on their semantic proximity. This process yielded 14 distinct clusters, each representing a characteristic leadership behavior. Expert judgment was then used to label and validate the clusters based on their most representative items. This study provides a data-driven foundation for consolidating leadership styles into a coherent behavioral framework, offering a pathway toward greater theoretical clarity and cumulative progress in leadership research.

Leader Boundary Spanning as an Enabler of Member Self-Regulation: A Multilevel Investigation

OB
Shaoyang Li (Monash University); Mariano L.M. Heyden (Monash University); Véronique Ambrosini (Monash University)
Work teams increasingly depend on boundary spanning behaviors to manage relationships with external interest groups. However, prior research reveals a paradox: leader-directed boundary spanning fails to meet external expectations, while member-initiated boundary spanning can make leaders feel threatened, prompting them to suppress it. Drawing on social identity theory of intergroup leadership and social cognitive theory, we develop a multilevel model explaining how leader boundary spanning behavior can motivate member boundary spanning without undermining members’ boundary spanning self-efficacy. We propose that leader boundary spanning operates through two mechanisms: (1) directly motivating member boundary spanning by exemplifying prototypical boundary spanning behavior, and (2) strengthening the relationship between member boundary spanning self-efficacy and behavior by signaling which intergroup relations are cooperative and strategically relevant. We tested the model with survey data from 126 leader-member dyads nested in 44 teams. Results indicate that leader boundary spanning positively influences member boundary spanning and strengthens the boundary spanning self-efficacy–behavior link. These findings address the organizing paradox and advance understanding of how teams can organize boundary spanning in dynamic environments.
