Overconfidence as an Operational Asset: Evidence from Workplace Accidents

STR
Suman Banerjee (Stevens Institute of Technology); Vikram Nanda (The University of Texas at Dallas); Mark Humphrey-Jenner (UNSW Sydney); Pawan Jain
Workplace accidents impose substantial human and financial costs, yet we know little about how executive cognition shapes operational risk. We examine whether CEO overconfidence—a bias typically associated with aggressive investment—can yield strategic benefits in operational reliability and employee safety. We argue that overconfident CEOs are more likely to undertake forward-looking, partially irreversible \emph{strategic modernization commitments} (capital renewal, technology adoption, and system redesign) that reduce reliance on outdated, accident-prone production systems. Our argument does not require that safety be an explicit managerial objective. Instead, accident outcomes provide a concrete, economically meaningful \emph{performance dimension} through which the effectiveness of modernization strategy is revealed. Using workplace accident data from the Right-to-Know Network and OSHA matched with U.S. public firms from 1994–2015, we find that firms led by overconfident CEOs experience 2–4\% fewer serious accidents and 1–2\% fewer accidents per employee. The effect is concentrated in accident-prone industries and financially constrained firms—settings where modernization is most consequential and most tempting to defer—and it attenuates when labor institutions are strict or governance tightens under SOX. We further show that workplace accidents negatively predict subsequent firm valuation and operating performance. Together, the findings extend behavioral strategy by showing that executive biases can generate domain-specific benefits through strategic resource allocation and capability building, and they contribute to ESG research by linking leadership traits to a concrete “S” outcome with clear economic consequences.

CEO Cognitive Complexity and Strategic Change: The Role of Social and Governance Contexts

STR
Chuan Qin; Angel Sharma; Chandra Bhushan Dubey (Oklahoma State University)
How CEO cognitive complexity influences strategic change presents an unresolved theoretical tension. On the one hand, cognitively complex CEOs can interpret competitive dynamics from multiple perspectives and integrate diverse information; on the other hand, such complexity may generate ambiguity, slow decision making, and strategic overcomplication. Drawing on upper echelons theory, we argue that CEO cognitive complexity is, on average, negatively associated with strategic change due to over-deliberation, confusion from competing perspectives, and delays in action. We further theorize that this relationship is contingent on CEOs’ relational and governance contexts. Using a longitudinal sample of S&P 500 firms from 2004 to 2023, we find that CEO cognitive complexity reduces strategic change, but that this negative effect is weakened by larger CEO networks and mitigated—and in some cases reversed —by higher board independence. Our findings help reconcile competing perspectives on CEO cognitive complexity by identifying boundary conditions under which it constrains or enables strategic change.

The Cultural Origins of Workplace Safety: Evidence from CEOs’ Rugged Individualism Imprint (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Lee Biggerstaff (Miami University); Xiaoran Ni (Central University of Finance and Economics); Jin XU (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); David Yin (Miami University)
This study examines how CEOs’ cultural backgrounds influence workplace safety outcomes. We focus on “rugged individualism” – a cultural ethos of self-reliance and personal autonomy rooted in the American frontier experience – as a key facet of CEOs’ early-life imprinting. Using establishment-level injury data from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) spanning 2002–2011, we find that firms led by CEOs from regions with a strong frontier heritage experience significantly fewer workplace accidents. A one-standard-deviation increase in a CEO’s frontier exposure is associated with roughly a 7% reduction in injury rates relative to the mean. This effect is robust to a host of controls and persists under quasi-exogenous CEO succession events, supporting a causal interpretation. We identify two mechanisms for this influence: greater safety-oriented investments and more sustainable employee workloads. Moreover, the impact of CEO individualism on safety is most pronounced when it contrasts with the local culture – in establishments located in regions with historically low frontier exposure – suggesting that leader values matter most when they introduce a new cultural emphasis to the organization. Our findings highlight the deep and lasting influence of cultural imprinting on executive decision-making, extending upper echelons theory and offering novel insights into how informal personal values shape critical organizational outcomes like workplace safety.

Framing Strategic Change: Persuading Audiences in Offensive vs. Defensive Changes

STR
Jueun Kim (York University); Majid Majzoubi (York University)
Organizational change often provokes resistance because it disrupts audiences’ established beliefs. To mitigate such resistance, firms use framing strategies —selective language that shapes how audiences interpret change. This study examines how the effectiveness of framing depends on the logic of change, distinguishing between offensive and defensive change. We argue that these types of change shape audience concerns about necessity and feasibility, determining which framing strategy is most effective. Two 2×2 experiments show that, for offensive change, continuity framing increases investor support by bolstering perceived feasibility, and why-focused framing increases support by elevating perceived necessity. Contrary to predictions, discontinuity and how-focused framing do not reliably increase support for defensive change. We discuss boundary conditions—failure-cue dominance, ceiling/floor constraints with credibility erosion, and interpretive heterogeneity—that may limit framing effects in defensive contexts.
