Validating the Self-Efficacy in Preserving Socioemotional Wealth Scale: Evidence from Australia

ENT
Sadhana Singh (Macquarie University); Patrick Raymund Matutina Garcia (Macquarie University); Laramie Tolentino (Macquarie University); Francesco Chirico (Macquarie University)
This paper validates the Self-Efficacy in Preserving Socioemotional Wealth (SE-SEW) scale in the Australian context. Originally developed and validated in India, this nine-item instrument comprises three dimensions: self-efficacy in strengthening family identity and emotional attachment (SE-IE), self-efficacy in preserving dynastic succession (SE-DS), and self-efficacy in maintaining family values (SE-FV). Data were collected from 190 family members employed in their family firms using a time-lagged research design. First, we cross-validate the SE-SEW scale by confirming its factor structure and establishing measurement invariance as well as convergent, discriminant, and incremental validity. Second, drawing on Social Cognitive Career Theory, we develop and test a nomological network. Confirmatory factor analyses support the superordinate multidimensional factor structure of SE-SEW, consistent with the original Indian validation. The scale demonstrates strong psychometric properties, including positive associations with affective commitment and discriminant validity from Ownership, Management and Control, Transgenerational Orientation, and family business self-efficacy. Furthermore, SE-SEW exhibits incremental validity by predicting work-related attitudes and behaviors beyond family business self-efficacy and mediates the effects of emotional intelligence and family member support on work engagement, organisational citizenship behaviour, and in-role job performance. Theoretical contributions and practical implications are discussed. Keywords: socioemotional wealth, self-efficacy, family business, scale validation, Australia

Beyond Uniform Socioemotional Wealth: Dimensional and Contextual Influences on Family Business Exit

ENT
Joyce Komakech Nabisaalu (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); STEPHEN Charles MUKEMBO (University of Missouri-Columbia)
Family business owners face a tension between preserving socioemotional wealth (SEW) through family-centric exit strategies and pursuing financially optimal market-based approaches. Drawing on SEW theory and Behavioral Agency Theory, we examine how different SEW dimensions influence five exit strategy preferences and how financial stress moderates these relationships. Using multinomial logistic regression on survey data from small family businesses across 12 US states, we find that SEW’s influence varies significantly across its dimensions. Emotional attachment of family members and the renewal of family bonds through a succession orientation strongly deter market-based exit strategies, while family identification shows no statistically significant effect. Critically, owners’ perceived financial stress systematically weakens SEW’s influence on exit decisions, making families more receptive to external exit strategies they would typically resist. Post hoc analyses reveal that these effects are more pronounced among business founders and purchasers than among inheritors, suggesting that active business creation generates deeper psychological attachments. Our findings reveal that family business exit decisions depend on which SEW dimensions are salient and how contextual pressures alter decision-making reference points.

Family Firm Generational Succession and the Mixed Gamble of International Entry Mode Choice

ENT
Yize Qin (Tongji University); Yuqing Shen
This study investigates how generational succession influences family firms’ choice between wholly owned subsidiaries (WOS) and joint ventures (JV) when entering foreign markets. Drawing on socioemotional wealth (SEW) theory and the mixed-gamble perspective, we challenge the view of SEW as a unitary construct by distinguishing between restricted SEW, which focuses on family control and influence, and extended SEW, which emphasizes firm growth and longevity. We argue that generational succession alters the family firm’s mixed gamble calculus: while founding generations prioritize restricted SEW and favor WOS to maintain unfiltered authority, second-generation successors prioritize extended SEW and are more inclined toward JV to leverage partners’ complementary resources. Furthermore, we identify prosperity as a critical boundary condition, proposing that high R&D intensity and performance above social aspirations diminish the second generation’s propensity for JV, shifting their preference toward WOS to protect firm-specific knowledge assets or demonstrate independent competence. Analyzing a sample of 11,054 overseas subsidiaries established by 353 listed Chinese family firms across 67 host countries between 2009 and 2023, our empirical results provide robust support for these hypotheses.

Socioemotional Wealth and Stakeholder Engagement: Do Family Firms Care About Their Stakeholders?

ENT
Zhibiao Zhang
Scholars have long debated whether socioemotional wealth (SEW) promotes or inhibits proactive stakeholder engagement (PSE) in family firms, with empirical evidence yielding mixed results. This paper posits that this ambiguity arises from treating SEW as a monolithic construct. Drawing on intergroup relations theory, it proposes that distinct SEW dimensions trigger divergent social categorization processes. Specifically, we argue that family-oriented dimensions (e.g., family control and dynasty) foster “ingroup favoritism” restricted to the family, thereby inhibiting broader engagement. Conversely, firm-oriented dimensions (e.g., binding social ties) expand the ingroup boundary to encompass nonfamily stakeholders. Crucially, the paper theorizes a configuration effect: the presence of strong firm-oriented dimensions can transform the nature of family-oriented goals, facilitating a recategorization of nonfamily stakeholders as “family” (a superordinate identity). This theoretical framework resolves the tension between family-centric and stakeholder-centric behaviors, offering a nuanced explanation for the heterogeneity in family firm social performance and advancing a more granular understanding of SEW.
