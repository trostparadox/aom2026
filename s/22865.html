Childhood Adversity and Materialistic Values as Antecedents of Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction

OB
Daniel Judson Beal (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University); Jill M. Sundie (Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University)
Basic psychological need satisfaction is an essential motivational component of work and life, and deficits in these needs have important implications for psychological and organizational outcomes (Van den Broeck et al., 2016). The current study takes a step back to examine distal factors that can influence current levels of psychological need satisfaction. We present an initial examination of childhood adversity and material values as a key mechanism linking early experiences outside of work to current psychological need satisfaction in a large, nationally representative sample of working adults within the US. Our theoretical approach relies on research from biomedical, evolutionary psychological, and consumer behavior literatures to advance the notion that material values represent a critical mechanism linking adverse experiences in childhood to need satisfaction in adulthood. Findings were largely supported and point out several counter-intuitive effects linking childhood adversity to organizationally-relevant motivations in adulthood, thereby identifying a potentially rich and unexplored area in motivation and organizational behavior.

Masking Misfit: Interplay of Red Tape and Need for Autonomy, Autonomy Need Frustration, and Facades

OB
Jialei Gu; Ke Zhang; qiwei liu (Beijing Normal University); Xiao-Hua Wang (Beijing Normal University); Ran Bian
While extensive literature has well documented the antecedents of facades of conformity (FOC) on the individual level, we suggest to extend it by exploring how an interaction between individual and contextual factors may lead to FOC. From a P-O misfit perspective, we illustrate how red tape perception and need for autonomy interact to produce employees’ misfit experience, and prompt them to resort to create facades as a coping strategy. Through a SDT motivation mechanism, we further explain the particular mediating role of autonomy need frustration in the relationship between the proposed interaction and FOC. First, a two-wave survey (N = 210) shows that red tape is positively related to FOC, and need for autonomy amplifies this positive relationship. Further, an experiment manipulating red tape (N = 275) replicates the findings in the first study, and demonstrates that the proposed interaction positively influences FOC via autonomy need frustration. Our study contributes to the existing literature regarding FOC’s antecedents by providing a novel P-O misfit perspective, and revealing the underlying SDT motivation mechanism of FOC. For the practitioners, we expect to illuminate the importance of optimizing rule efficiency and fostering a need-fulfilling environment to promote employees’ identification with the organization.

Empowering Leadership Needs (Un)Fulfillment: An Individualized Leadership Perspective

OB
Lan Jin (Kyung Hee University); Ke Zhang (Shandong University); Minyoung Cheong (Kyung Hee University); Chou-Yu (Joey) Tsai (State University of New York at Binghamton)
Even leadership styles typically regarded as desirable may lose their effectiveness if they fail to fulfill followers’ needs. Grounded in individualized leadership theory and emphasizing follower self-worth, this study examines how the (mis)alignment between followers’ empowering leadership needs and the empowering leadership they receive, referred to as empowering leadership needs (un)fulfillment, shapes their sense of self-worth, which in turn influences their taking charge behavior and quiet quitting. A two-wave field study of 277 employees revealed that both the presence and level of alignment between followers’ empowering leadership needs and what they received (i.e., empowering leadership needs fulfillment) enhanced their perceptions of leader support for self-worth. This, in turn, promoted followers’ taking charge behavior while mitigating quiet quitting. By exploring the phenomenon of empowering leadership needs (un)fulfillment through a classical yet underexplored theoretical lens, this study offers a nuanced understanding of how empowering leadership influences followers’ psychological and behavioral responses.

What We Do And How We Do It: A Dual Pathway Model Of Work Autonomy and Wellbeing

OB
Wei Jee Ong (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology); Bowen Shi (National University of Singapore); Ran Bian
Autonomy is widely recognized to be a key job resource and driver of positive work attitudes and wellbeing. Existing literature typically suggests autonomy has generally positive effects on various indicators of wellbeing. However, individuals often exercise autonomy in different ways and in different domains of work. This research proposes a distinction between two types of autonomy – autonomy over the outcomes and processes of work – and suggests that they are differentially related to wellbeing outcomes. Integrating self-determination theory and control theory, we theorize that these types of autonomy fulfill distinct psychological needs and thereby affect wellbeing in distinct ways. We argue that outcome autonomy fosters eudaimonic wellbeing by supporting authentic self-expression, while process autonomy enhances hedonic wellbeing by improving the perceived manageability of workload. We find support for our model across two studies – a behavioral experiment using an online sample (Study 1) and a multiwave survey of university alumni employed in diverse occupations (Study 2). Results suggest that outcome autonomy and process autonomy are uniquely linked to eudaimonic and hedonic wellbeing respectively. Consequently, this research offers a new framework to understand different types of autonomy and their consequences in organizations.
