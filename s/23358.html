R&D Search Vs Technology Catchup: A Behavioral View of Direction of Technology Search

STR
Lakshmi Goyal (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
Building on the premise that performance shortfall influences firms to engage in problemistic search, this study suggests that emerging economy firms prefer technology catchup to R&D search as a behavioral response to performance shortfall. In addition, this study theorizes that policy instruments referred to in the national innovation policies that enable R&D focus managerial attention towards R&D search and away from technology catchup. Hence while they weaken the relationship between performance shortfall and technology catchup, they strengthen the association between performance shortfall and R&D search. I test and find empirical support for my predictions on a sample of Indian manufacturing firms from 1992 to 2018 and perform several additional tests which further support my predictions. This study advances research on the direction of technology search, the problemistic search behaviors of emerging economy firms, and the important role played by innovation policies and institutions in shaping problemistic search direction.

Aspiration-Based Cognitive Frames and Adaptive Responses to Stretch Goals

STR
Mouxuan Sun (Dalian University of Technology); Meihan Liu (Dalian University of Technology); Miles M. Yang (Macquarie University); Chengye Han (Dalian University of Science and Technology)
This study examines how organizations respond to performance shortfalls under escalating stretch goals, moving beyond traditional “failure = search” models to emphasize the interpretive role of cognitive framing. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm and managerial cognition perspectives, we employ a mixed-method design that integrates a controlled simulation experiment (Study 1) with in-depth executive interviews (Study 2). Study 1 provides causal evidence that defensive frames channel resources toward exploitative innovation, while growth-oriented frames foster exploratory reallocation, demonstrating that identical shortfalls can produce divergent outcomes depending on interpretation. Study 2 extends these insights by showing that frames are socially embedded in organizational structures, cultures, and histories, and that they can persist through reinforcing cycles or shift following shocks and managerial interventions. Together, our findings extend behavioral strategy by positioning framing as a dynamic, context-dependent mediator of performance feedback, and by identifying conditions under which stretch goals foster rigidity or renewal.

When Firms Stop Reaching: Underperformance and Nonlinear Aspiration Adjustment

STR
Haobang Chen (Tsinghua University); Donghong Li (Tsinghua University)
The way organizations set performance targets is critical to organizational decision making. The performance feedback literature posits that underperformance leads to subsequent target reductions. However, we argue that boosting targets can help underperforming firms gain positive expectations and external support, but it also increases the risk and expense of future failure. We thus propose a nonmonotonic response to performance deficits, in which businesses initially boost, then eventually lower, successive performance targets as the magnitude of underperformance increases. Using annual management forecasts as observable proxy for performance targets, we present strong evidence for this inverted U-shaped relationship. Furthermore, we demonstrate that this nonlinear pattern collapses into classic downward adjustment when companies have more slack, more stable external support, or less performance variability. Our study combines the backward-looking and forward-looking perspectives on aspiration, emphasizing firms' proactive role in aspiration formation in response to performance feedback.

Not All Experiences are Created Equal: How Managerial Success and Failure Shape Problemistic Search

STR
Wendy Juyoung Cheon (The University of Arizona); Songcui Hu (The University of Arizona)
The Behavioral Theory of the Firm posits that performance shortfalls relative to aspirations trigger problemistic search, often leading firms to increase risk-taking. However, despite longstanding recognition that managerial experience shapes cognition and decision-making, research has paid limited attention to how different patterns of managerial experience influence firms’ responses to performance shortfalls. We examine how managerial success and failure experiences shape risk-taking under performance shortfalls, focusing on the amount and concentration of experiences. Drawing on research on cognitive biases and experiential learning, we theorize that accumulated experiences have opposing effects depending on whether they are successes or failures. As performance falls below aspiration levels, greater accumulated successes amplify firms’ propensity to increase risk-taking, as managers become overconfident in closing performance gaps through risky strategies. In contrast, greater accumulated failures dampen this propensity by fostering managerial underconfidence. Conversely, experience concentration—the extent to which experiences are narrowly distributed across domains—has opposite effects. Concentrated success experiences constrain risk-taking responses to performance shortfalls, whereas concentrated failure experiences encourage greater risk-taking by enhancing cognitive capacity. Empirical analyses provide general support for our predictions. This research advances behavioral strategy and problemistic search by clarifying how managerial experiential structures shape firms’ risk-taking responses to performance shortfalls.

Carrots or sticks: The Impact of Environmental Regulation and Incentives on Firm Innovation

STR
Jingwen Li (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Yong Jin (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Xin Xu (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Dong Wu (Zhejiang University)
Challenging the traditional assumption that organizational search is inherently proactive and goal-oriented, this study investigates "regulatory-induced boundary spanning"—a context where firms are compelled to acquire distant knowledge by institutional mandates. We theorize that while regulatory pressure forces the physical acquisition of compliance assets, firms often choose "rational non-integration" due to high coordination costs and uncertain returns, resulting in a lack of innovation. We hypothesize that innovation-contingent government incentives are required to provide the "return certainty" needed to activate these dormant knowledge stocks. Using a triple-difference design leveraging China’s water quality monitoring network and the 2008 High and New Technology Enterprise policy, we find that regulatory pressure alone fails to spur innovation. However, when combined with strong incentives, it significantly boosts patent output. Furthermore, we uncover a critical temporal mechanism: innovation is maximized when regulation precedes incentives, whereas simultaneous implementation leads to resource congestion.
