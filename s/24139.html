Peacekeepers, National Identity and Identity Work

CMS
Angela McGinn (Maynooth University); Andrew D. Brown (University of Bath)
Based on an interview study of 48 Irish Defence Force officers we analyze how members of multinational peacekeeping teams draw on their national identity to author both positively valenced, desired, and negatively valenced, undesired, occupational identities. In exploring the importance of national identity as a resource for building occupational identities, our study makes two principal sets of contributions. First, we argue that identity work is not infrequently self-disparaging, that people may hold contrasting versions of their occupational identities, and that selves are narratively complex in a way previously rarely considered. Second, we theorize the relationships between desired and undesired identities and the extent to which undesired identities may be (mal)adaptive. Undesired identities, we argue, are not merely detrimental but also means for self-insight. Moreover, because self-doubt is intrinsic to the human condition, while undesired identities can pose threats to those people desire, they cannot simply be defused or disregarded; rather, they must be coped with in pursuit of livability. In making these contributions we add to understanding of how work identities are fed not just by internal organizational discourses but by those pertaining to national identity.
