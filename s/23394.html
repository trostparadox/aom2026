Challenging the Marketization of Public Goods and Services

ONE
Catherine (Cat) Summers (University of Michigan); A. Wren Montgomery (Smith School of Business, Queen's University); Rosalie Luo (Ivey Business School)
In response to growing marketization, we develop a multi-stage model of community-led challenging of marketization, showing how communities contest unjust market institutions and disentangle market forces from the provision of public goods and services. Our model is based on findings from a longitudinal qualitative study that traces the changing role of the market in essential public services as residents of Detroit rallied for justice and fought for access to affordable, clean water over several years. We provide three evidence-based, actionable insights for actors engaged with or leading communities that are challenging marketization. First, initial community stasis can be overcome by unveiling a justice-sensitizing artifact that exposes distributive injustice. Second, actors can bridge local and global political issues by linking to and engaging supportive global organizations, frames, and narratives through a justice connection. Third, leaders can facilitate justice convening to engage the public in conversations about values, morals, and the meaning of public goods and services. Our insights offer a way forward for communities struggling with the undermining of cherished values, shared resources, and longstanding public institutions. Community-led challenging of marketization helps create conditions that support the dismantling of overarching systems that perpetuate environmental and social injustice.

How Environmental Labels Reshape Markets Over Time: Evidence from France’s Repairability Index

ONE
Jonathan Schubert (TU Dortmund University); Julie Saesen (Technical University Dortmund); Tessa Christina Flatten (TU Dortmund University)
Environmental labels aim to encourage sustainable consumption, yet research still lacks evidence on whether they shift demand in durable-goods markets, where purchase cycles are infrequent and consumers face substantial information frictions. We investigate this question by studying France’s mandatory repairability index for smartphones, which requires firms to disclose standardized repairability scores, and a later repair subsidy. Using multi-country, model-level panel data and an augmented difference-in-differences design, we show how repairability disclosure reshapes durable-goods demand, how its effects evolve over time, and how organizational characteristics condition their strength. We introduce brand equity as a moderator of environmental labeling effectiveness and show that high-equity brands respond less strongly to repairability disclosure. Empirically, we highlight that the repairability index increases the sales-weighted repairability of smartphones sold in France, that its effect grows cumulatively as consumers and firms adjust, and that the repair subsidy sustains, but does not intensify, this trajectory. Our findings demonstrate that environmental labels can shift durable-goods markets, albeit unevenly, and illuminate the behavioral, temporal, and organizational mechanisms through which environmental information policies advance circular-economy transitions.

P2P Resale’s Missing Sellers: What Holds Them Back?

ONE
Jan Thomas Frecè (Bern University of Applied Sciences); Nadine Gurtner (Bern University of Applied Sciences); Deane L. Harder (Bern University of Applied Sciences); Jan Keim (Bern University of Applied Sciences); Claus Noppeney (Bern University of Applied Sciences)
Peer-to-peer (P2P) resale markets play a crucial role in the circular economy by extending product lifespans through reuse. However, many consumers do not resell unused items—a behavior that remains insufficiently understood, as existing research primarily focuses on buyers and overlooks the cognitive mechanisms influencing non-professional sellers’ decisions. Taking a cognitive perspective, we draw on qualitative interviews with 20 consumer sellers and non-sellers to identify six overarching categories (comprising 21 subcategories) of cognitive distortions that shape both retention (whether to keep or dispose of items) and disposal decisions (whether to sell, gift, donate, or discard). Specifically, no-demand assumption, attachment-based retention, and temporal retention extension distortions affect retention, while transaction effort fallacy, utility misjudgment, and community convenience distortions affect disposal decisions. We trace how these distortions emerge and interact throughout consumers’ item disposition process. By uncovering these cognitive distortions, we move P2P resale scholarship beyond buyer behavior at the point of sale to the upstream, pre-sale decision processes that prevent potential sellers from entering the market, thereby constraining the supply of reusable goods and limiting circular-economy value retention through reuse. Our findings reconceptualize P2P reselling as a complex cognitive activity and offer actionable insights for increasing participation in P2P resale markets.

The Rise of Eco-Friendly Pop-up Stores: Green Brand Loyalty and Green Corporate Image as Mediators

ONE
Jihyun Park (Yonsei University); Minseok Kim (Temple University); Taehyung Kim (Korea University of Technology and Education)
As environmental awareness grows, companies are increasingly integrating sustainability into their strategies, with eco-friendly pop-up stores emerging as dual-purpose tools for marketing and recruitment, particularly in South Korea. Our study investigates how experiences in eco-friendly pop-up stores influence South Korean job seekers’ application intentions, drawing on the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework. Specifically, we examine the mediating roles of green brand loyalty (GBL) and green corporate image (GCI), and how perceptions of environmentally sustainable interior design (ESID) affect job-seeking behavior. A mixed-methods of sequential explanatory design was employed. Study 1 involved a survey of 180 job seekers with bootstrapping to test indirect effects. Study 2 consisted of semi-structured interviews with 15 participants, analyzed thematically and integrated with the quantitative findings. Results show that GBL serves as a crucial mediator, while GCI does not. The findings highlight eco-friendly pop-up stores as effective offline recruitment platforms and offer implications for green HR strategies.
