Ethical Leadership and External Whistleblowing Intentions: Fear of Retaliation Under Pressure

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 4:00PM–5:00PM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 7
SIM

People

Authors: Nikolina Duka; Jovana Milic University of Montenegro; Sonja Muratovic University of Montenegro, Faculty of Economics; Jovana Popovic University of Montenegro; Ivana Predrag Perovic

Session overview

Whistleblowing is vital for detecting wrongdoing, yet employees must weigh ethical duty against retaliation risk. Ethical leadership may increase willingness to report and reduce fear of retaliation—both potentially shaping intentions to report externally. We propose a dual-path model where ethical leadership relates to external whistleblowing intentions directly and indirectly via fear of retaliation. As performance pressure heightens perceived stakes and uncertainty, it may limit leaders’ ability to reduce retaliation fears, creating a conditional indirect effect. We tested this first-stage moderated mediation model using self-report survey data from 340 employed individuals in Montenegro, drawn from both public and private sector organizations and representing diverse demographic and occupational backgrounds. Results supported the proposed conditional process: ethical leadership related to lower fear of retaliation, but this relationship weakened as performance pressure increased. Fear of retaliation was positively related to external whistleblowing intentions, and ethical leadership also showed a positive direct association with external whistleblowing intentions. The indirect effect via fear was negative and became weaker (less negative) as performance pressure increased, supporting first-stage moderated mediation. These findings extend ethical leadership research by showing how performance pressure shapes leaders’ capacity to alleviate retaliation fears, with implications for when employees consider external reporting.
