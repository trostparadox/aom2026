Executive Education Reimagined: Skills, Transformation, and the Limits of the Classroom

Session overview

Executive education faces mounting pressure to deliver transformative rather than merely transactional learning, yet its pedagogical architecture is surprisingly undertheorized. This session interrogates what executive education is really doing: proposing a three-layer skills architecture beyond hard/soft binaries, synthesizing evidence on active learning across global programs, theorizing how entrainment enables consciousness-raising, confronting the power dynamics of learner positionality, and exploring AI-augmented coaching as a democratizing force in negotiation education. If you believe executive education can do better, this session is for you.

Active Learning in Executive Education: A Systematic Review of Uses, Potentials, and Limits

MED
Brauer Brayan Koren (Universidade Positivo); Marcello Romani-Dias (Fundação Getulio Vargas); Joao Lins Pereira Filho; Aline dos Santos Barbosa (Fundação Getulio Vargas); Franciany Cristiny Venâncio Dugonski (Universidade Positivo)
As Executive Education (EE) programs continue to expand globally, active learning methodologies have become a central pedagogical response to demands for effectiveness. However, existing research tends to focus on a limited subset of active learning techniques and to examine them in isolation, resulting in a fragmented understanding of their pedagogical contributions. To address this gap, this article aims to analyse how active learning methodologies are used in EE by comparing their recurring pedagogical potentials and limits and by elucidating the trade-offs embedded in instructional choices. The analysis is based on a PRISMA-guided systematic literature review of 126 articles from high-quality journals. The review identifies a broad set of active learning approaches in EE, with case-based methods, action learning, and simulations and role play emerging as the most prominently discussed in the field. Other experiential approaches, including coaching and mentoring, storytelling, project-based learning, and service-learning, are also examined. Case-based approaches support the analysis of complex organizational dilemmas while risking simplification. Action learning highlights tensions between action and reflection, while simulations and role play foster behavioral competencies but face institutional constraints. Overall, the study brings forward pedagogical and institutional implications, enabling an integrated understanding of active learning in EE through recurring trade-offs.

An Entrainment Lens on Consciousness-Raising in Executive Develoment

MED
Danny Sandra (IESEG School of Management); Wim Van Lent
Consciousness-raising activities in executive development programs are increasingly used to deepen leaders’ self-awareness, empathy, and sense of responsibility. Yet, these activities regularly evoke discomfort, and transformative learning theory offers limited guidance for explaining how such experiences translate into durable shifts in meaning-making. Particularly, it tends to leave pacing and sequencing under-theorized, and provides limited guidance on how embodied, relational, and contextual conditions shape learning. Addressing this gap, we emphasize entrainment as lens for analyzing and understanding how rhythms of attention, affect, interaction, and meaning-making adjust through coupling over time. Using the Integral Entrainment Matrix, we examine consciousness-raising experiences in executive education across across intra-, inter-, extra-, and exter-entrainment to elucidate how pacing, coupling media, and multi-level dynamics support transformative learning. The analysis suggests that transformative learning is more likely during entrainment, which describes the adjustment phase that precedes synchronization between individuals and their context, and that pedagogical designs that coherently engage multiple entrainment dynamics yield stronger learning outcomes. Theoretically, our analysis connects the literatures on transformative learning and organizational entrainment, and suggests the importance of temporal precision for transformative learning in executive education. Furthermore, it elaborates five design principles for structuring consciousness-raising interventions and supporting re-entry into organizational rhythms.

Navigating Learner Positionality in Executive Management Education: A Conceptual Framework

MED
Yemisi Bolade-Ogunfodun (University College London); Roopa Nagori; Esta Zixuan Qi; Lebene Soga
Executive education focuses on building capacity in mid-to-senior professionals through intensive programs designed to integrate academic theory with professional practice. While the benefits of such programs for practitioners and organisations are well-documented, the literature remains largely silent on the challenges executive learners face when navigating the distinct, often contradictory roles of organizational leader and academic learner. Grounded in experiential learning theory and research on positionality, this paper investigates the underlying mechanisms of academic (dis)engagement within this learner cohort. We frame executive learning as a process shaped by the simultaneous enactment of intertwined professional and learner positionalities, and their ongoing interpretation. We argue that sensemaking and identity work function as interpretive mechanisms, as executive learners draw on conflicting frames of reference, and different patterns of engagement emerge from the negotiation of these positional demands. The paper contributes a new conceptual framework which re-frames engagement as an outcome of the positionality-driven sensemaking process, illuminating the practical implications for psychosocially informed curriculum design and academic support. We propose tailored interventions that mitigate the tensions of intertwined positionality, to foster deeper and more sustained academic engagement for the experienced professional.

AI as a Coach in Negotiations: Harnessing Artificial Intelligence to Guide, Augment, and Democratiz

MED
Michael W. Morris (Columbia Business School); Xin Lucy Liu (Columbia Business School); Ryan Shea (Columbia University); Zhou Yu (Columbia University)
Artificial intelligence promises to reshape negotiation education, not by replacing human judgment but by augmenting it. While recent research related to this topic varies on many dimensions, a salient dimension that organizes this review is temporal—whether the coaching occurs before, during, or after the negotiation of a deal. This temporal framework also elucidates differences between the projects presented at the AI Negotiation Summit in March 2025. These projects drew on AI for empowering negotiators with pertinent legal facts, enacting negotiation strategies, classifying types of agreements, and drawing lessons from tactical improvements. Used carefully, the power of AI to help negotiators performance and learning can extend the benefits of negotiation training to those who need it most. Yet it also raises questions about authenticity, bias, and ethics. Drawing analogies to autonomous driving technology, we argue that the optimal uses of AI coaching lay in complementarity: AI coaching should expand human capacity for rigorous preparation, active perspective taking, and creative problem solving—while leaving voice, agency, and responsibility where they belong: with human negotiators. We argue that it can also level the playing field in extending educational access to less privileged groups.
