It Does Not Happen in a Vacuum: Mental Illness as a Relational Axis of Inequality in organizations

Friday, Jul 31 2026 · 10:00AM–11:00AM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 3
DEI

People

Author: Jon Echeverria ESADE Business School - Ramon Llull University

Session overview

Despite growing attention to workplace mental health, employees with mental illness continue to face stigma, disadvantage, and exclusion at work. Organizational responses remain reactive and individualized, offering support primarily after diagnosis or disclosure while leaving structures of inequality unaddressed. This paper challenges prevailing approaches by theorizing mental illness as a relational and organizationally produced axis of inequality, rather than solely an individual health condition or wellbeing. We argue that mental illness operates as a hidden yet consequential dimension of diversity that intersects with existing DEI concerns around normativity, voice, and legitimacy at work. Drawing on a grounded theory study of 39 in-depth interviews with employees who have experienced mental illness, we examine how organizational norms, expectations, and relational processes shape inequality, exclusion, and voice. Our analysis identifies five interrelated mechanisms through which inequality is produced and sustained. First, organizational contexts amplify suffering through everyday micro-interactions and symbolic practices, rendering mental health initiatives reputational rather than transformative. Second, dominant norms of productivity, emotional stability, and autonomy—aligned with the ideal worker paradigm—operate as disciplinary standards that marginalize deviation. Third, discourses of individual responsibility and privatization displace organizational accountability and produce silence and moral distancing. Fourth, standardized and paternalistic support practices undermine autonomy and generate epistemic injustice by silencing employees’ experiential knowledge. Finally, biomedical framings essentialize mental illness, transforming diagnosis into a fixed identity marker that legitimizes unequal treatment. By foregrounding these mechanisms, the paper advances DEI scholarship by reconceptualizing mental illness as an organizational inequality process embedded in everyday organizing.
