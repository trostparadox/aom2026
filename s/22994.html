CEO Identity and Strategic Exit from Conflict States: Roles of Tokenism and Institutional Maturity

IM
Natalia Abgaryan (University of Houston)
This study examines whether and when CEO gender and race influence multinational enterprises’ full divestment decisions in conflict-affected developing countries. Drawing on Upper Echelons Theory and research on legitimacy, tokenism, and institutional environments, we theorize that identity-based executive influence is conditioned by organizational and host-country contexts that shape strategic discretion under heightened scrutiny. Using a longitudinal sample of S&P 1500 multinational firms comprising 11,306 firm-year observations from 2014 to 2019 and multilevel logistic regression models, we find that CEO gender, but not CEO race, is systematically related to divestment behavior. Firms led by female CEOs are less likely to fully divest from conflict-affected markets, and this effect is strengthened in institutionally mature host countries. In contrast, CEO race exhibits no significant direct or conditional association with divestment. Organizational tokenism does not amplify identity-based divestment pressures in the predicted manner; instead, gender-based differences in divestment emerge only in non-tokenistic organizational contexts, suggesting that symbolic representation constrains executive discretion irrespective of CEO gender. These findings refine Upper Echelons Theory by demonstrating that identity-based effects on international strategy are selective rather than universal and that host-country institutional maturity, rather than organizational tokenism, plays a central role in enabling gender-contingent strategic discretion in conflict settings. By centering armed conflict as a persistent strategic context, the study contributes to international business research on executive decision-making under extreme uncertainty.

Foreign Divestment, Political Turbulence, and Subsidiary Technological Capabilities

IM
Ziko Konwar (University of Leeds); Han Jin (University of Leeds); Olha Zadorozhna
Political turbulence and political risk are growing globally, leading to a rising wave of divestment by multinational enterprises. However, extant research is silent on how foreign divestment affects the technological capabilities of sister host country subsidiaries that stay behind. Does the scope of knowledge integration in affiliated subsidiaries of multinational firms influence these capabilities in periods following a foreign divestment event? Our paper explores this question using literature from knowledge-based perspectives and political turbulence, to develop a theoretical framework postulating the reasons behind divestment as a determinant of technological persistence. To test this framework empirically, we create a novel, global firm-level dataset of foreign divestments spanning 78 countries from 2007 to 2024, linking divestment events with corresponding multinational parent–subsidiary pairs to examine subsidiary innovation output in post-divestment stage. Using a battery of robustness checks to estimate dynamic treatment effects, our findings reveal that the patent growth of affiliated foreign subsidiaries in host countries is weakened following complete divestment of a foreign subsidiary. This negative effect is amplified in country environments with high political risk and among country pairs characterized by stronger political affinity. We outline the implications for IB scholarship on foreign divestment and technological capabilities from these results.

When Flexibility Backfires: Stock-Market Reactions to Exit Mode During the Russia–Ukraine Conflict

IM
Nate Xu (West Texas A&M University); Fiona Kun Yao (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign); Song Chang (Hong Kong Baptist University)
Prior research has paid limited attention to the performance consequences of exit mode. We study how the mode of foreign exit—full versus hybrid—shapes stock-market reactions during the Russia–Ukraine conflict. We maintain that under strong normative consensus to withdraw, investors penalize flexibility-preserving hybrid exits relative to decisive full exits: hybrids are viewed as equivocal and leave stakeholder claims unresolved, whereas full exits reflect costly commitment. Using an event study of publicly listed multinationals with exposure to Russia, we find that hybrid exits are associated with lower cumulative abnormal returns than full exits. Furthermore, drawing on the stakeholder legitimacy-power-urgency framework, we examine boundary conditions: the penalty weakens when sentiment across the firm’s host-country portfolio is more heterogeneous (contested legitimacy of stakeholder claims) and strengthens with higher institutional investor ownership (greater stakeholder power) and better prior performance (heightening urgency). Robustness checks—alternative event windows, coarsened exact matching, and selection-correction models—yield consistent results. We contribute to research on foreign divestment under geopolitical risk by showing that the mode of exit—not merely its occurrence—conditions stock-market reactions, and that stakeholder salience helps explain when and for whom hybrid exits are most penalized.

Exit to Exist: Firms’ Selective Divestment Under Sanctions

IM
Alina Kinchinbayeva (University of South Carolina); Joao Albino Pimentel (University of St. Gallen)
This study examines how multinationals respond to third-country sanctions. Going beyond host-country political risk, sanctions increasingly reflect geopolitical contestation imposed by firms’ home governments or by third countries. Under third-country sanctions, compliance is not legally mandated but coerced through targets’ dependence on the sanction sender. Conceptualizing sanctions as coercive threats, we argue that firms’ strategic calculus is shaped by three factors: firms’ dependence on the sanctioning authority, unit redundancy, and host-country commitment. Greater firm-level dependence increases exposure to sanction enforcers, raising the costs of inaction, while unit redundancy lowers the costs of strategic action by facilitating divestment. By contrast, host-country commitment raises the costs of action by increasing the irreversibility of exit. We test this framework using a panel of French multinationals exposed to U.S. trade sanctions, a setting that allows us to observe third-country coercion without home-country legal alignment. Accounting for nonrandom sanction exposure, we find that greater dependence on the sanctioning country and unit operational redundancy accelerate divestment. The study contributes to the literature in two ways: first, by expanding the research on firm responses to secondary sanctions, and second, by advancing research on divestment–reinvestment strategies under extraterritorial coercion.

MNE Divestment to Navigate Home-Host Geopolitics: A Dual-Dependence Perspective

IM
Ziliang Deng (Peking University); Rosamond Yiyang Wang (Peking University); Frederick Yanzhe Wang (Peking University)
Some MNEs divest from host countries that have geopolitical tensions with their home country to reduce risk exposure. However, divestiture may cause a significant decline in market presence in host countries. Strategically securing the host market's interests while responding to domestic pressures is a critical issue that, however, has received insufficient attention in the literature. Drawing on resource dependence theory, we hypothesize that during equity divestment from a host country, MNEs may strategically increase the use of patent families initially sourced from a third country and later applied in the host country to manage dual dependencies on home-market political legitimacy and host-market economic interests. An empirical analysis of panel data on U.S. MNEs that have wholly or partially divested from China between 2015 and 2022 supports our hypotheses. The study develops a dual-dependence framework to better understand MNE divestment strategies amid tensions between home and host countries. It highlights the multi-locational arbitrage advantages of MNEs in the era of deglobalization.
