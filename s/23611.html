Session overview

This session examines the relationship between diversity and performance across levels of analysis, from couples and work teams to boards and firms. The papers explore social learning and creativity, social class profiles, racioethnic homogeneity and performance decay, CEO range and board diversity, and internationalization and innovation. Collectively, they show that diversity-performance relationships are contingent, multidimensional, and central to understanding organizational effectiveness.

A Bidirectional, Reciprocal Social Learning Model on Power Couples’ Role Salience and Creativity

DEI
Sonya Lin (Aston Business School); Jakob Stollberger (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Giles Hirst (The Australian National University)
Power couples have existed across centuries and industries, yet it has received little attention in organizational behavior research. Build on social learning theory and work-family literature, we propose and test a bidirectional reciprocal model, arguing that both power couples’ weekly role (i.e., family/work) salience and role modeling, influence each other, which in turn, will shape both power couples’ creativity at work and family. Our Experience Sampling Methods (ESM) analysis from 211 full-time power couple in the US collected weekly across six weeks shows that both couple members’ weekly family salience (but not weekly career salience) crosses over and impacts the respective others’ family-related creativity outcomes via social learning from their respective partner. Our paper makes significant contributions to both work-family and creativity literatures, by showing a specific work-family crossover mechanism, and showing that creativity can not only be achieved by one, but dual.

Bundles of Advantage: A Person-Centered Perspective on Social Class and Job Attitudes

DEI
Steven Kardel (The Pennsylvania State University); Carolyn Thi Dang (The Pennsylvania State University)
Social class is multidimensional, yet commonly used variable-centered research operationalizes class using only one dimension (wealth, income, education, occupational prestige) or a composite average of multiple dimensions; this does not account for how social class dimensions can uniquely combine. To address this limitation, our work adopts a person-centered perspective. Using latent profile analysis (LPA) across two U.S. nationally representative, archival studies of working adults, the Midlife in the U.S. (MIDUS) and the General Social Survey (GSS), we uncovered five social class profiles. Three profiles, comprising half of our samples, exhibited congruent advantages (all dimensions were congruently above or below average) while two profiles exhibited incongruent advantages (some dimensions were above average, others were below average). We further explored job attitudes associated with profile membership. Applying relative deprivation (RD) theory, results confirmed our predictions related to (in)congruence among class dimensions at above and below average overall levels of advantage. We observed a ceiling effect in which incremental advantage did not affect job attitudes beyond congruent above-average advantage. We found mixed results related to compensatory effects (i.e., advantages in some dimensions of class compensate for disadvantages in other dimensions). This research offers insight into workplace outcomes based on social class.

Board Diversity and Firm Performance: Exploring the Influence of CEO Personal Range

DEI
Saneesh Edacherian (University of Birmingham); Amit Karna (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Sunil Sharma (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
Past research indicates that the effect of board diversity on firm performance is equivocal. We address this issue by recognizing the multiple sources of board diversity and the effect of CEO personal range on the performance effects of board diversity. Using a dataset of 171 large Indian firms that witnessed a CEO change at least once between 2000 and 2018, we test the effect of different sources of diversity on performance and the moderating effect of CEO personal range on this relationship. Our results show that various sources of board diversity affect firm performance differently. Our results also reveal that interaction between CEO personal range and board diversity helps firms realize the potential advantages of board functional and social capital diversity but aggravates the potential challenges due to board gender diversity.

The Effect of Internationalization on Innovation: The Role of Board Diversity

DEI
Saneesh Edacherian (University of Birmingham); Amit Karna (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad); Sunil Sharma (Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad)
There is consensus among researchers that the internationalization of emerging economy multinational enterprises (EMNEs) is motivated by their intention to acquire strategic assets such as innovative technologies, broaden their expertise, and enhance their skills. Yet the amount of research on the outcomes of EMNE internationalization and the role of strategic leaders in capturing the advantages of internationalization is scarce. To address this gap, we study the relationship between EMNE internationalization and innovation performance and the impact of two types of board-level diversity on this relationship. We test these relationships on a 21-year panel of 244 firms from the Indian biopharmaceutical industry. We find that EMNE internationalization has an inverted U-shaped relationship with innovation performance and this relationship is contingent on board functional and gender diversity. Our study contributes to the EMNE internationalization and upper echelons literatures.

Is Sameness Stifling? How Group Racioethnic Homogeneity Impacts Individual Performance Decay

DEI
Derek R. Avery (University of Houston); Enrica Nicole Ruggs (University of Houston); Larissa R. Garcia (University of Houston); Horatio Traylor (University of Houston); Curtis Wesley (The University of Texas at San Antonio)
People routinely question the value of diversity for groups and the individuals comprising them. Consciously or not, such questioning reflects a default acceptance of homogeneity as the standard against which all other levels of composition are to be compared. Unfortunately, existing theory tells us relatively little about how homogeneity (particularly racioethnic) impacts workers. Accordingly, this research extends the social comparison model of competition to show that group racioethnic homogeneity corresponds to significant individual performance decline among 144 call center workers (Study 1), 1416 military cadets (Study 2), and 360 professional athletes (Study 3). Moreover, Study 3 and experimental evidence (Study 4; N = 212) indicate competitiveness decay helps account for this effect. In more homogeneous groups, individuals become less competitive over time, resulting in individual performance diminishing. These results suggest that sameness may indeed be stifling and serve to undermine individual performance.
