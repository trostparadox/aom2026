Measuring Perceived Invisibility: Development and Validation of a Three-Dimension Scale

MOC
Brian Waterwall (East Carolina University); Andrew O. Herdman (East Carolina University); Amy McMillan (East Carolina University); Leah Katell Grubb (East Carolina University); Walter Lee Grubb (East Carolina University)
In this study, we introduce and validate a new measure of perceived invisibility in the workplace—a construct that captures individuals’ subjective experience of being physically separated, ignored, and devalued. To date, scholarship has focused primarily on invisibility as a structural or task-based phenomenon and highlighted how marginalized identities or stigmatized roles are systematically ignored or devalued inside organizational systems. Unavailable, however, is an individual-level measure that captures and assesses the experience of invisibility, independent of identify or role-based assumptions. We define invisibility as the extent to which an individual or individual’s work is perceived to be unseen, ignored, and/or taken for granted. Based on this conceptualization, we propose three dimensions of invisibility: physical separation (unseen), ignored (physically present but not acknowledged), and devalued (taken for granted). By focusing on individual perceptions, this construct provides organizational scholars and practitioners with a measure to understand the antecedents and impacts of perceptions of invisibility on employee well-being and organizational effectiveness in contemporary, often digitally mediated, workplaces.

“Just a Job”: Resourcing a Job Orientation for Well-Being

MOC
Jordan Nye (University of Michigan)
The well-known work orientation framework categorizes individuals as holding a calling, career, or job orientation toward their work. Calling and career orientations – where work serves as an end in itself (as a source of experienced meaningfulness or advancement) – have both received substantial scholarly interest. Meanwhile, holding a job orientation – where work is viewed as a means to an end (for survival) – remains underexplored. Research increasingly suggests that work orientations are not stable traits but can shift over time, raising questions about the process by which these shifts occur and their impact on important outcomes of interest. Drawing on 50 interviews with U.S.-based professionals, I examine how individuals resource a job orientation in response to work-related threats to their well-being. In a society that valorizes devotion to work, individuals may undergo a process of conscious reorientation, whereby they 1) recognize how their view of work contributes to these threats and 2) in response, voluntarily choose to see work as a means to an end rather than an end in itself. I show that this cognitive shift has behavioral effects for individuals, who subsequently decenter the work domain and center the non-work domain. Ultimately, this process helps individuals reclaim their well-being. This study advances our understanding of the dynamics of work orientations by suggesting that a job orientation is not merely the opposite of a calling orientation but can serve as a strategic and protective response to work-related threats to well-being.

Redefining Busyness: The Positive Effects of Self-Imposed Busyness on Thriving and Resilience

MOC
Elizabeth Nguyen Trinh
While prior research and popular press often cast busyness in a negative light, many people actively choose to be busy. To understand this puzzle, I conducted a multistudy investigation that identifies and theorizes self-imposed busyness, or the extent to which individuals, of their own volition, occupy themselves with work-related tasks during times when they could otherwise take breaks or rest. In Study 1, a qualitative study of 39 entrepreneurs, I inductively identified self-imposed busyness as a distinct behavior. An in-person lab experiment (Study 2) isolated the causal effects of self-imposed busyness, and an experience-sampling study (Study 3) examined whether the same mechanisms explain its daily effects. Together, these studies challenge the prevailing view that busyness is uniformly detrimental by showing how and why self-imposed busyness can promote thriving and resilience. This work repositions busyness as having underrecognized benefits.

The Bright Side of Envy: Examining Leaders’ Downward Benign Envy in Organisations (WITHDRAWN)

MOC
Sabreen Kaur (Monash University); Herman Tse (Monash University); Nathan Eva (Monash University)
The existing downward envy literature has generally defined it as a negative and hostile emotion associated with negative action tendencies and outcomes for employees and organisations (e.g., abusive supervision). As a result, the current understanding of downward envy remains highly negative, thus limiting our understanding of any positive or bright side of envy. The omission is particularly troubling when considering the dual nature of envy, such as benign envy, which is a more positive form of envy associated with a willingness to ‘level up’ to the envied others, leading to positive outcomes (e.g., self-improvement). To propel the field of envy forward, we draw on social comparison theory to examine how leaders’ downward benign envy drives them to improve, such that they seek advice from their envied subordinates. Across two multi-wave online panel studies (Study 1: N = 180; Study 2: N = 163), we found consistent evidence that benign envy enhances leaders’ motivation to improve, which in turn increases their advice-seeking from envied subordinates. These findings advance the downward envy literature by positioning downward benign envy as an important leader emotion that can lead to positive outcomes while strengthening leader–subordinate dynamics in organisations.

When the Time Is Right: Labeling Dates to Motivate Donations

MOC
Jan Gerrit Voelkel (Cornell University); Robert Kuan; Lauren Eskreis-Winkler; Kevin V Zhu (University of Pennsylvania)
Many societies label certain dates to set time aside for the commemoration of an important event, individual, group or cause (e.g., Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Mother’s Day). Based on prior research on mental accounting, we theorize that labeling dates may produce a “time-is-right effect” whereby individuals who are reminded of the label on a given date (e.g., Earth Day) are more likely to donate to an organization whose mission matches that label (e.g., The Rainforest Alliance). Across nine experiments (all preregistered; total N = 10,226), including three hypothetical and six incentive-compatible experiments, we find consistent evidence for a large “time-is-right effect”; labeling dates increased donations to charitable organizations that serve causes aligned with a given date’s label by an average of 11 percentage points in incentivized studies. We further provide evidence that this effect occurs because labeling dates both makes the associated cause more salient (Study 3) and provides a justification for donating to that cause on those dates (Studies 1, 2 and 4). These findings extend a small prior literature suggesting that date labels can shape financial decisions and consumer behavior more broadly and offer new insight into why mental accounting matters.
