Disclosure Under Counsel: Law Firms and Corporate Innovation

TIM
Farzana Afrin; Muskan Chawla; Xizhao Wang (Northwestern University)
Patent attorneys play a central role in shaping patent outcomes, yet little is known about how the legal agents who draft patent applications influence disclosure quality. Using large-scale textual analysis of 1.3 million patent documents, we examine how the use of external patent law firms affects disclosure quality across patent sections and subsequent litigation outcomes. Claims drafted by external counsel are shorter, less complex, and contain fewer unfamiliar terms, with these effects being particularly pronounced for independent claims, which define the core legal boundaries of patent protection. In contrast, external lawyers use more abstract language in the summary section, making it harder for competitors to readily discern the technical contribution. Detailed description section remains unchanged. We further document that patents drafted by external law firms experience significantly lower future litigation rates. Exploiting the implementation of the America Invents Act, we show that firms increase their reliance on external legal representation following the reform, generating unintended spillover benefits for patent disclosure quality. Overall, our findings contribute to the literature on innovation disclosure by highlighting the role of legal intermediaries as an important yet underexplored channel shaping the quality of patent disclosures.

Ideology on Trial: How CEO Political Leanings Shape Firms’ Propensity to Litigate Over Patents

TIM
Ali Radfard (Aston University); Luca Pistilli (University College Dublin)
This study investigates how CEOs’ political ideology affects corporate decisions to sue for patent infringement. Integrating upper-echelons and behavioural-agency perspectives, we theorise that conservative-leaning CEOs—marked by heightened threat sensitivity and low tolerance for ambiguity— frame infringement as a looming loss and therefore favour litigation as the most rule-bound, controllable response. Liberal CEOs, by contrast, are less likely to initiate litigation. We further contend that recent firm performance amplifies this ideological imprint: when results exceed aspirations, conservative leaders become still more eager to protect hard-won gains through the courts, whereas poor performance dampens that impulse. These propositions are tested on an unbalanced panel of 1,275 US firm-year observations, obtained by linking political-donation records with patent, financial and litigation data. Results confirm that conservative CEOs significantly raise the odds of initiating a patent suit; the effect strengthens at high performance levels and weakens when performance falls below industry benchmarks. By exposing the ideological microfoundations of patent enforcement, the study extends competitive-dynamics research, demonstrates how personal values shape non-market strategy, and alerts boards, R&D managers and investors to a behavioural driver of costly patent disputes.

Patent Enforcement Under Institutional Complexity: The Case of University Patent Litigation

TIM
Sam Horner (University of Liverpool); Nikolaos Papageorgiadis (University of Liverpool); Wolfgang Sofka (Copenhagen Business School); Irene Margaret (University of Liverpool)
Patent enforcement represents a critical strategic choice for organizations pursuing competitive advantages through technological innovation. While existing research emphasizes how patent enforcement strategies are shaped primarily by legal and political institutions defining patent rights, it largely overlooks how organizations navigate pluralistic institutional environments characterized by conflicting demands. We integrate sociological institutional theory to patent strategy literature to examine how organizational responses to institutional complexity condition patent enforcement decisions. We argue that an organization’s structural position in its institutional field, resource dependence on minority logic holders, and degree of structural hybridity condition its strategic responses to institutional conflicts: defiance, acquiescence, or compromise. We test these hypotheses using data on patent litigation initiated by U.S. universities, an ideal context given universities’ inherent exposure to conflicting commercial and scientific logics. Our findings indicate that universities occupying dominant field positions more frequently adopt defiance strategies, proactively engaging in patent litigation. In contrast, universities dependent on minority logic holders or employing hybrid organizational structures are less aggressive litigators. We contribute to patent strategy research by highlighting institutional complexity as a critical determinant of patent enforcement, offering a novel explanation for increased university patent litigation despite no obvious commercial pay-offs.

Preemptive Patent Enforcement and Cumulative Innovation: Evidence from Virtual Patent Marking

TIM
Wolf-Hendrik Uhlbach (Tilburg University); Gaétan De Rassenfosse
Cumulative innovation is an important antecedent of firm performance. While firms frequently rely on patents as an appropriability mechanism, they face a tension between preserving their ability to invent cumulatively and deter imitation of commercialized products. We examine this tension in the context of Virtual Patent Marking (VPM), a provision of U.S. patent law that strengthens patent enforceability by requiring the disclosure of which patents underlie a product. Exploiting information on the timing of marking, we show that marking increases rivals’ follow-on inventions to marked patents. However, we do not find evidence that such spillovers lead to greater preemption of firms’ future inventions, instead they create opportunities for future inventions. Implications for the appropriability and vicarious learning literatures are discussed.

Stock Liquidity and Patent Litigation Settlement: An Application of Agency Theory

TIM
Can Huang (Zhejiang University); Mingdong Hu (Zhejiang University); L WANG (Zhejiang University)
Companies facing patent litigation must resolve their cases. CEOs may prefer settling and resolving litigation quickly, but shareholders may choose the opposite, generating agency issues. Blockholders (large shareholders) can use the threat of exit as a governance mechanism to address the agency problem, but its effectiveness is influenced by stock liquidity. Analyzing a sample of 6,715 patent lawsuits that involve US-listed firms and were filed between 2000 and 2015, we find that low stock liquidity makes it difficult for blockholders to constrain CEOs’ managerial opportunism through exit threats, thereby increasing the likelihood that patent litigation is resolved through settlements and reducing the duration of the litigation process. Additionally, we demonstrate that CEO duality and the quality of financial disclosures moderate the effects of stock liquidity. These findings connect stock market microstructure to corporate patenting strategies, extending the application of agency theory in patent-litigation research.
