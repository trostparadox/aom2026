Fragmented Shores in International Waters: Discrimination in Port State Control’s Labor Inspections

IM
Yi Sui (University of Toronto); Hao Zhang (Renmin University of China); Ruiying Lu (Renmin University of China)
This study examines how regional coalitions and bilateral trade relationships shape the enforcement of international labor standards in port state control (PSC) inspections. Drawing on 125,047 inspection records from seven regional PSC regimes between 2023 and 2024, we find that vessels flagged under states sharing the same regional Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) with the inspecting port state are significantly less likely to be cited for labor-related deficiencies. This indicates institutionalized regulatory leniency toward “in-group” members, despite the ostensibly uniform nature of international standards. Further analysis shows that bilateral trade dependence moderates this coalition-based effect. For non-coalition vessels, higher trade interdependence directly reduces labor deficiencies, suggesting that port states relax enforcement to safeguard economic ties. For coalition vessels, however, high trade dependence weakens the bias associated with MoU membership, partially offsetting its protective effect. These results suggest that economic interdependence can reduce discriminatory enforcement, but this may come at the cost of seafarers’ labor protections. These findings challenge the presumed neutrality of PSC regimes and contribute to the literature on complex interdependence and maritime labor governance by showing that PSC labor inspections are shaped not only by technical standards, but also by overlapping geopolitical and economic pressures.

When the Dust Doesn't Settle: Electoral Outcomes and Firm Cross-Border Investments

IM
Matteo Moesli (Zurich University of Applied Sciences); Jakob Müllner; Christopher Andrew Hartwell (Zurich University of Applied Sciences)
While international business literature recognizes host country elections as a major source of risk, scholarly understanding regarding their effect on MNE investment patterns remains limited. This study addresses this gap by examining the boundary conditions under which electorally induced political change affects foreign investment decisions. Using quarterly cross-border greenfield investments from MNEs across 80 home countries (2003-2020), we analyze how electorally induced governmental transitions shape MNE investment decisions. Contrary to prevailing theoretical assumptions, we find that political change does not systematically reduce investment activity in post-electoral periods. Instead, investment reductions only occur when incoming governments exhibit higher populist salience or possess greater political discretion to implement policy changes. These findings demonstrate that electoral effects on investment are conditional rather than universal, depending on incoming leaders’ ideological characteristics and their capacity to disrupt established policies. This differentiation offers important theoretical implications for international business research, by demonstrating that not all political transitions pose equivalent investment risks.

Intercultural Trust Dynamics in Refugee Employment Integration: The Role of Stakeholders

IM
Daniela Rivera Piedra (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Leigh Anne Liu (Georgia State University); Ashley Fulmer (Georgia State University)
Refugee integration into the workforce poses a critical challenge for international HRM and cross-cultural management, particularly when highly skilled refugees enter the host country labor market under conditions of involuntary mobility, institutional uncertainty, and cultural distance. In such contexts, conventional HR mechanisms for recruitment, screening, and skills validation are often weak or absent, increasing the perceived risk for employers and limiting access to suitable employment. Drawing on an inductive qualitative study based on 27 interviews with highly skilled refugees, employers, and intermediaries in the United States, this article examines how intercultural trust functions as a relational mechanism that enables integration into the workforce. We develop a stakeholder-centered framework that conceptualizes trust as a dynamic, triadic, and mediated process rather than a dyadic one. The results show that affective trust grounded in emotional security and care often precedes cognitive trust based on competency assessment, reversing conventional trust development sequences under conditions of institutional asymmetry. Intermediaries play a central role by translating cultural norms, validating skills, and transferring credibility between refugees and employers. This study contributes to international HRM and cross-cultural management by clarifying how trust-based stakeholder engagement enables recruitment, early integration, and access to meaningful employment for marginalized global talent.

When a Firm Becomes the Flag: The Process of Firm–Nation Identity Fusion

IM
Mahija Ratnoo (University of Oklahoma); Amrit Panda (University of Arkansas); Inder Surrendar Valasai (The University of Texas at Arlington)
Organizations can shape not only markets but also national self-understandings, yet identity research rarely explains how influence runs from firm to nation. We develop a theory of firm–nation identity fusion, a condition in which audiences treat a firm as a symbolic stand-in for the nation in a salient domain, so that judgments of the firm and judgments of the nation become tightly coupled. Building on the idea of iconic brands, we explain how some firms become national icons and why this differs from country-of-origin effects, nation branding, or flag-waving marketing: many firms borrow national symbols, but only a few come to embody the nation. Our framework specifies antecedents that make fusion more likely (e.g., emergence during nation-building periods, early state endorsement, and capability scarcity), mechanisms that produce fusion (nation-building narration, cultural symbolism, and global visibility that turns the firm into a category representative), and consequences for strategy and evaluation (expanded legitimacy, intensified scrutiny, amplified expectations, and symbolic protection that departs from ordinary market logics). We contribute by extending organizational identity upward to the national level, clarifying identity-based evaluation as a distinct audience judgment, and explaining how corporate successes and crises can become sites where collective esteem, vulnerability, and accountability are negotiated.
