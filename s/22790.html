Perceived AI-Driven Job Displacement and Career Exploration on New Workforce Entrants

CAR
Mei-Ling Maggie Wang (Tamkang University); Chun Hsien Lee; Ming-Chi Lee (National Kaohsiung University of Science and Technology); Min-shi Liu (National Chung Cheng University)
As generative AI increasingly reshapes professional work contexts, new workforce entrants are experiencing heightened existential anxiety regarding the potential for AI to undermine both their immediate employment and long-term career trajectories. Drawing on the Conservation of Resources theory, we propose that the perceived threat of AI-driven job displacement elicits career anxiety. This anxiety serves as a motivational response, mobilizing defensive career exploration to prevent anticipated loss of career resources. We further posit that this defensive mechanism is conditional; specifically, the translation of anxiety into exploration is amplified for entrants with a lower focus on opportunities, who perceive a greater resource deficit. Analysis of time-lagged data from 296 new workforce entrants demonstrates that career anxiety mediates the positive relationship between perceived AI-driven job displacement and career exploration. Moreover, this indirect effect is significantly more substantial among entrants characterized by a low focus on opportunities. These findings reveal that entrants who are pessimistic about their prospects respond to AI threats by prioritizing the avoidance of obsolescence over long-term optimization. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

The Cost of AI Adaptation: Territorial Control Loss Driving AI Loafing and Proactive Career Behavior

CAR
Beini Liu (Beijing Technology and Business University)
As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes deeply embedded in employees’ day-to-day work, many employees are expected not only to interact with AI intensively but also to work alongside AI systems that can learn from experience and continuously improve. Yet, the psychological costs of this “successful” AI integration remain underexamined. Drawing on conservation of resources theory and territoriality theory, we propose that intense AI interaction can ironically backfire when AI is perceived as highly adaptable, because employees may feel that a self-improving AI is increasingly encroaching on their discretion and ownership over valued work domains—what we term AI-caused loss of territorial control. We further theorize that this perceived loss of control prompts compensatory and defensive responses, manifested as greater AI loafing and stronger proactive career behavior. Moreover, we posit that career encouragement buffers these effects by mitigating the downstream consequences of territorial control loss. Based on data from a multi-wave field study and an experiment, we find support for our theorized model. Overall, our findings highlight a hidden cost of working with self-improving AI and identify career encouragement as a critical boundary condition for sustaining employee agency and adaptive career responses in AI-intensive work.

How and When Employee-AI Cooperation and Competition Influences Employees’ Career Behaviors

CAR
Juan Wang; Wenxiu You (Northwestern Polytechnical University); Wendong Li (The Chinese University of Hong Kong); Zhe Zhang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Yang Liu (Northwestern Polytechnical University)
The adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) in organizations is catalyzing transformations in human work. However, researchers have not reached a consensus on whether AI promotes or hinders employees’ career behaviors. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, we aim to examine how and when employee-AI task interdependence (i.e., cooperation and competition) influences employees’ career behaviors (i.e., career-oriented proactive behavior and career inaction). Using a novel eye-tracking experiment (N = 68) and a field study (N = 383), we found that employee-AI cooperation increased employees’ career-oriented proactive behavior via career adaptability, but employee-AI competition influenced employees’ career inaction via career insecurity. Furthermore, AI anthropomorphism weakened the positive relationship between employee-AI cooperation and career adaptability. AI anthropomorphism strengthened the positive relationship between employee-AI competition and career insecurity, thereby enhancing the mediating effect of career insecurity in the link between employee-AI competition and career inaction. Our research contributes to a more nuanced understanding of different kinds of employee-AI task interdependence (cooperation and competition) on employee career behaviors, shedding light on both the benefits and risks of AI adoption in the companies.

Seeing the Future or Losing It? The Roles of STARA Awareness and Career Crafting in Employability

CAR
Xiaolin Ge (Beijing Forestry University); Yujing Zhang (Beijing Forestry University); Debin Du (Beijing Forestry University); Hongfeng Song (Beijing Forestry University); Gang Wang (Beijing Forestry University); Meishu ? (Beijing Forestry University)
Rapid advances in smart technologies, artificial intelligence, robotics, and algorithms (STARA) are reshaping the nature of work and raising concerns about employees’ employability. Drawing on theory of purposeful work behavior, our study examines how individuals’ future work selves promote employability through career crafting, and how STARA awareness moderates these relationships. Using a multi-wave survey design with full-time employees, we reveal that future work self positively predicts employability through career crafting. However, STARA awareness weakens the relationship between future work self and career crafting, suggesting that technological replacement concerns can undermine the motivational power of seeing a clear future self. This research enriches understanding of how individuals sustain their employability in an era of smart technologies by highlighting both the proactive and inhibiting forces that shape career construction. Practical implications are discussed for helping employees manage technological uncertainty and maintain a sustainable career trajectory.

Facing the Future of Work: How Perceived GenAI Replaceability Shapes Employability Through Crafting

CAR
Duoran Liu (Renmin University of China); Erica Wen Chen (Renmin University of China); Jean Fan Yang; Hongkun Tang (Renmin University of China); Cathy Y. GUO (IESEG School of Management)
As generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) becomes increasingly integrated into the workplace, employees are confronted with growing uncertainty regarding the long-term sustainability of their careers. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, we propose a moderated mediation model that explains how perceived GenAI replaceability influences employees’ employability beliefs through distinct job crafting strategies. Specifically, we argue that perceived GenAI replaceability functions as a psychological resource threat that prompts both promotion-focused and prevention-focused job crafting, each of which has divergent implications for employability. Furthermore, we theorize that learning goal orientation moderates these pathways by shaping employees’ propensity to invest in or conserve personal resources. We tested our model using a three-wave, time-lagged survey of 468 employees working in GenAI-exposed roles. The results showed that perceived GenAI replaceability was positively related to both forms of job crafting. Promotion-focused job crafting enhanced employability, whereas prevention-focused job crafting reduced it. Moreover, learning goal orientation amplified the positive effect of promotion-focused job crafting and attenuated the negative effect of prevention-focused crafting. These findings contribute to emerging research on AI and careers by revealing how employees adapt to technological disruption through self-initiated changes in their work behavior and mindset.
