How Achieving Regulative Legitimacy Shapes Impression Management: A Behavioral Perspective

STR
Momoko Kurokawa (UTokyo Economic Consulting Inc.); Junichi Yamanoi (Waseda University)
This study examines how achieving regulative legitimacy—acceptance grounded in conformity with codified rules and laws—reshapes firms’ impression management (deliberate efforts to shape stakeholder evaluations) in nonfinancial reporting. We focus on symbolic communication, operationalized as the volume of voluntary, initiative-focused narratives that describe what firms are doing to improve. From a behavioral perspective, we argue that a legal benchmark creates a salient evaluation point: being below the threshold is interpreted as a shortfall (loss) and meeting or exceeding it as an attainment (gain), implying that narrative emphasis should respond more strongly to performance improvements while firms remain below the benchmark. We examine Japan’s statutory disability employment quota under the Act to Facilitate the Employment of Persons with Disabilities and link a standardized substantive outcome —firms’ actual disability employment rates—to this narrative volume. We find a clear asymmetry in responsiveness: narrative volume increases strongly as firms improve toward the benchmark while remaining noncompliant, but becomes substantially less responsive once the benchmark is met or exceeded.

Reacting to European Regulation: U.S. Corporate Political Activity in the Wake of GDPR

STR
Namil Kim (Yonsei University); Olivier Bertrand (FGV EBAPE); Marie-Ann Betschinger (HEC Montréal); Hyungseok David Yoon (University of York)
While prior studies show that firms leverage their political connections at home to shape foreign regulatory frameworks, much less is known about the reverse process, namely how influential foreign regulations affect firms’ domestic political activities. This question is particularly relevant for corporate lobbying, the most common form of political activity. Drawing from the attention-based view, we argue that firms that are exposed to foreign regulatory issues via their foreign affiliates are more likely to engage in issue-related lobbying at home to shape their home country regulatory regimes. We further argue that when the underlying issue is more salient in the headquarters’ local context and within their firm, the firms with a foreign affiliate in a country with the relevant foreign regulation are more likely to engage in issue-related lobbying. We test our predictions by examining the effect of the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) implemented in 2018 on U.S. firms’ lobbying activities at home. Using a difference-in-differences analysis on a sample of 4,874 U.S. firms from 2014 to 2022, our findings provide strong evidence in support of our predictions. These findings extend research on corporate political strategy and privacy policy diffusion by highlighting how foreign regulations abroad can redirect firms’ strategic attention toward lobbying activities at home.

Corporate Political Activity and Regulatory Enforcement: Lobbying Versus Campaign Contributions

STR
Debtanu Lahiri (Nova School of Business and Economics)
Research on corporate political activity (CPA) has long recognized firms’ efforts to shape their regulatory environments, yet much of this work treats CPA as a unified construct and evaluates its consequences through outcomes distant from the objects of political influence. Drawing on an institutional perspective, we argue that different forms of CPA are differentially suited to distinct arenas of regulatory governance and therefore have systematically different implications for enforcement outcomes. We distinguish between lobbying, which enables sustained engagement with administrative agencies during policy implementation, and campaign contributions, which primarily target elected officials during policy formation. We test these arguments using facility-level enforcement under the U.S. Clean Air Act from 2011 to 2020, linking enforcement actions and penalties to parent-firm political engagement. Consistent with our theoretical framework, lobbying is associated with greater enforcement leniency, while campaign contributions exhibit no comparable association. We further show that the enforcement effects of lobbying are stronger in conservative political environments, highlighting the role of institutional receptivity in conditioning how political engagement translates into administrative discretion. By disaggregating CPA and situating political strategies within the institutional arenas in which enforcement decisions are made, this study advances a more precise theoretical understanding of how firms shape regulatory implementation.

First-Mover Advantage Beyond Markets: Evidence from the Tribal Gaming Industry

STR
Ana Antolin (Harvard Business School)
Policymakers often face substantial information gaps when designing rules and regulations, particularly in nascent and complex industries. To address these gaps, they frequently rely on information and expertise from the very organizations that will be regulated. I argue that firms that are first to engage with policymakers—what I call policy leaders—can secure lasting competitive advantages. These advantages may emerge through conventional market mechanisms, such as earlier or less restricted market entry, or through shaping regulatory frameworks in ways that generate asymmetric benefits. I test this argument in the context of the U.S. Tribal gaming industry, leveraging the passage of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA) in 1988. Prior to IGRA, Tribes operated bingo independently of states, but IGRA required Tribes and states to negotiate regulatory frameworks for the expansion into Las Vegas– style gaming. Using a novel dataset covering more than 250 Tribes across 27 states, I distinguish policy leaders—Tribes that initiated negotiations early— from those that delayed. I find that policy leaders operate gaming facilities that are 60–70 percent larger than those of policy laggards. I also compare Tribes that entered gaming before IGRA to those that entered afterward, showing that policy timing and market entry timing operate through distinct, additive channels. These advantages persist decades later, and markets with policy leaders are significantly more concentrated, even after accounting for competitive potential. The results indicate that early policy engagement creates durable strategic benefits beyond traditional first-mover effects.

How Do Firms Influence Environmental Regulations?

STR
Joyce (Heqiao) Ma (Duke University)
This paper examines how firms participate in the federal rulemaking process and influence the environmental regulations issued by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Whereas prior research on corporate political activity has largely emphasized legislative lobbying and campaign contributions, I focus on firm engagement in rulemaking through public comment submissions. Drawing on a novel dataset that links EPA rules and firm-submitted comments, I analyze patterns of firm participation and the strategies they employ to influence regulators. Using natural language processing, I systematically characterize comment content and assess firm influence based on the EPA’s responses. The findings reveal three key insights: (1) small firms play a surprisingly active role in shaping environmental regulations, (2) commenting strategies vary systematically across firm types, and (3) both comment specificity and firm size are important predictors of whether comments are incorporated into final rules.
