Employee Sensemaking of CSR: Justice-Driven Pathways to Affective and Continuance Commitment

SIM
Marina Santucci (Austral University); Maria Jose (Majo) Murcia (IAE BUSINESS SCHOOL - AUSTRAL UNIVERSITY)
This study examines how employees interpret and respond to corporate social responsibility (CSR) by distinguishing between progressive and rational CSR activities and assessing their effects on affective and continuance commitment. Using survey data from 303 employees in Argentina and Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling, we find that progressive CSR strongly enhances affective commitment, whereas rational CSR does not yield positive attitudinal effects unless perceptions of organizational justice are considered. Organizational justice emerges as the central sensemaking mechanism through which employees evaluate the fairness of CSR, revealing employees as active, agentic interpreters rather than passive implementers of organizational initiatives. Its mediating influence varies across CSR logics, indicating that employees scrutinize CSR differently depending on its underlying orientation. Affective commitment strongly predicts continuance commitment, revealing a sequential pathway linking CSR perceptions to retention-related attitudes. The findings advance micro-CSR theory by conceptualizing CSR as a multidimensional construct with differentiated, agency-driven employee effects, and by extending CSR scholarship through empirical evidence from a Global South context.

The Leadership Dance: Unleashing Purpose and Engagement Through Relational Strategies

SIM
Kim de Haan (Nyenrode Business University); Lidewey E.C. Van Der Sluis (Nyenrode Business University); Emmerentia Nicolene Schutte (University of the Western Cape); Nico Eric Schutte (University of the Western Cape)
This study explores the incorporation of relational principles into purpose-driven leadership in knowledge-intensive organizations to improve work engagement. It presents the novel perspective of the dance metaphor (van der Sluis, 2021) to depict the dynamic interaction between leaders and followers. This method emphasises that effective leadership, like a well-coordinated dance, necessitates synchronization, responsiveness, and mutual impact, consequently augmenting organisational work engagement. The research utilised a qualitative methodology, conducting semi-structured interviews with pairs from several knowledge-intensive organizations. This method facilitates a comprehensive examination of the application of relational concepts inside dyads to enhance leadership effectiveness and job engagement, highlighting the micro-level intricacies of these relationships. The main findings indicate that relational principles are fundamentally linked to one of the M’s in the MMM (Morals, Manners, Mindset) paradigm, and that synchronizing these M’s enables effective collaboration in the workplace. It demonstrates that the most effective professional relationships function not through conventional hierarchical frameworks but through a reciprocal adaptation towards a common aspiration. This method cultivates a workplace atmosphere characterised by fluid and reciprocal leadership and cooperation, enhancing engagement and mutual support. Recommendations are made.

Employee Focused CSR and Affective Commitment: A Time-Lagged Moderated Mediation Study (WITHDRAWN)

SIM
Kamran Iqbal (University of Sargodha)
This study employs the lens of social exchange theory to propose a moderated mediation model. Specifically, it examines the mediating role of organizational trust in the relationship between employee-focused corporate social responsibility (CSREMP) and affective commitment. Additionally, it investigates how this mediation is negatively influenced by the moderating effect of job insecurity. Data were collected from 224 banking employees through three time-lagged surveys. Overall, the results provide support for the moderated mediation model. However, contrary to expectations, the findings show an insignificant direct effect of CSREMP on affective commitment. The results do confirm the mediating role of organizational trust in the relationship between CSREMP and affective commitment. Furthermore, the findings demonstrate the negative moderating effect of job insecurity on the relationship between CSREMP and organizational trust. Moreover, the moderated mediation analysis reveals that higher levels of job insecurity reduce affective commitment through the indirect effect of CSREMP via organizational trust. This study highlights the complex roles of job insecurity and organizational trust in shaping the relationship between CSREMP and affective commitment, thereby enhancing our understanding of how CSREMP can contribute to affective commitment.

Collective CSR Memory, Commitment, and Job Satisfaction: Insights from Japan

SIM
Miho Murashima (Rikkyo University)
An organization’s history of social responsibility can leave lasting impressions on their employees. This study introduces the concept of corporate social responsibility (CSR) collective memory—that is, the shared recollection among employees of their company’s past CSR activities—and investigates how it shapes employees’ perceptions of organizational ethics and, in turn, influences their work attitudes. Through a mixed-method analysis of employees at a Japanese fashion e-commerce firm, this study tested a structural model in which CSR memory affects both organizational commitment and job satisfaction directly and through perceived ethicality. Quantitative analysis using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling revealed that CSR memory significantly enhances both perceived organizational ethics and employee outcomes. In contrast, the mediating role of ethics significantly explained the link between CSR memory and organizational commitment, but not job satisfaction. To explore this difference, this study conducted qualitative interviews with employees whose ethical perceptions and satisfaction levels diverged. Thematic analysis found that collective CSR memory contributes to satisfaction by reinforcing identity-based pride and symbolic meaning, even when ethical perception is weak. This study offers a novel temporal perspective on CSR, demonstrating that how CSR is remembered—rather than merely performed—can shape a firm’s internal ethical climate and employee motivation. This study contributes to micro-CSR theory by clarifying the distinct psychological pathways through which collective CSR memory affects commitment and satisfaction. Findings also have practical implications for embedding CSR values in both organizational culture and daily work.
