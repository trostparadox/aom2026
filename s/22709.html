Decolonizing Racialized Erasure in the State 'Lunatic' Asylum (1833 – 1856)

MH
Ren Lovegood (University of Massachusetts Boston)
This paper aims to decolonize the history of racialized erasure within 19th-century state 'lunatic' asylums. Focusing on the State Lunatic Asylum at Worcester to uncover its pervasive role as a site of forced labor extraction and colonial archival violence, thereby challenging benevolent institutionalization narratives by examining the obscured lives of marginalized individuals. The study employs two critical methods: 1) object-oriented inquiry and 2) critical fabulation. Object-oriented inquiry treats administrative documents as material traces encoding power dynamics and classification systems. Critical fabulation responsibly addresses archival gaps and silences, acknowledging colonial power structures and leveraging cross-document triangulation to reconstruct obscured histories. The paper contributes to business and labor history by demonstrating how institutional systems produce racialized disability. Critical archival analysis reveals a continuum of confinement that superseded enslavement, preserving the core function of extracting utility from racialized bodies deemed economically unproductive. Furthermore, this paper critically interrogates contemporary privacy regimes, arguing their unintended consequence in business history research is to shield institutions from scrutinizing past injustices more than protecting historical subjects, thereby perpetuating traditions of exclusion and criminalization. The work concludes by calling for the use of increased critical and postmodern methods in business and labor history research as ways of giving voice to historically marginalized populations who have been silenced by the archives.

The Politics of Idea Meritocracy: Tracing How Expectancy Theory Became Received Wisdom in Management

MH
Nicholous M. Deal (Mount Saint Vincent University); Robert Lloyd (Southwest Academy of Management)
Expectancy theory is arguably among the most popular, widely-recognized motivation frameworks in all of management education. The theory has persisted for over sixty years despite decades of critique and uneven empirical support. This paper examines expectancy theory as a case of ‘idea meritocracy,’ which is the belief that only the best theories rise to curricular prominence through evidentiary superiority. Drawing on critical historical scholarship in management learning and education, and using critical discourse analysis of archival material, widely adopted textbooks, and field-level syntheses, we trace the ideological and institutional processes that have gone into producing its present-day success. Our analysis surfaces four interlocking processes (e.g., rise, critique, rehabilitation, and canonization) through which the theory accrued authority. We argue that expectancy theory’s durability is better explained by ideological alignment and pedagogical convenience than by meritocratic epistemic competition. We conclude with implications for management educators, advocating historically conscious teaching makes visible the political conditions under which ‘core’ theories become canonical.
