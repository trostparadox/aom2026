Extending HRM to the Crowd to Enhance Exploratory Innovation Using Crowdsourcing

TIM
Hsing-Fen Lee (Royal Holloway, University of London); Marcela Miozzo (King's College London); Damian Grimshaw; Uma Rani (International Labour Organization (ILO))
Organizations that gain access to distant knowledge are better equipped to carry out exploratory innovation processes. Nevertheless, exploratory innovation requires efforts of knowledge absorption, as the organization needs to develop specific mechanisms to assimilate distant knowledge and exploit it. We focus on the search for distant knowledge by soliciting solutions from crowds of external contributors. We propose an organizational mechanism, which we label “extended HRM practices,” to explain how distant knowledge from crowds of external contributors can be used effectively to enhance exploratory innovation. Drawing on data from an original survey of UK SMEs, we demonstrate the importance of extended HRM practices as an outward-looking coordination mechanism that fosters connectedness and catalyzes interconnections between internal and external knowledge for exploratory innovation. We make two major contributions. First, we extend the literature on crowdsourcing for innovation beyond the prevailing focus on processes for soliciting and filtering innovation solutions, toward an understanding of the organizational mechanisms that enable knowledge absorption for improved innovation performance. Second, we contribute to the literature on open and distributed innovation by highlighting the value of extended HRM practices, specifically those concerning performance feedback, interactions, training, continued work opportunities, and reward, to crowds of external contributors in a quest for integrating distant knowledge and realizing benefits for improved exploratory innovation. Overall, by focusing on organizational practices that can effectively connect to the crowd, this research engages with the debate on the evolving nature of the organization and its innovation capabilities.

Generating Internal Value In Research and Technology Organizations from Collaborative Innovation

TIM
Prince Gilbert FIALOR (Toulouse I Capitole University); Eric Michaël LAVIOLETTE; Amadou Lô (Toulouse Business School)
Purpose: This study examines how public Research and Technology Organizations (RTOs) adapt to appropriate and reinvest value from collaborative innovation to strengthen their internal capabilities. It focuses on how RTOs transform learning from collaboration into organizational adjustment and sustained relevance in innovation ecosystems. Design/methodology: We conducted a qualitative case study on the French RTO, CEA (Commissariat à l’énergie atomique et aux énergies alternatives), drawing on 41 semi-structured interviews, field observations, and internal project documents collected over 24 months. Using the dynamic capabilities framework, we explore how CEA reconfigures its internal processes to enhance the effectiveness of external collaborations. Findings: We identified two interrelated processes, prospecting (identifying and preparing collaboration opportunities) and feedbacking (capturing and applying lessons from projects), driving CEA’s sensing, seizing, and transforming capabilities. These practices enable CEA to align technologies with partner needs and adapt organizationally over time. However, learning remains localized, regional teams face coordination constraints, and flexibility is limited by centralized governance structures. Strengthening cross-regional learning and strategic integration is essential for organization-wide capability development. Originality/value: This study is among the first to apply the dynamic capabilities framework to a public RTO. It advances our understanding of RTO adaptation by reframing value appropriation not as a post-collaboration outcome but as an embedded, capability-driven process through which RTOs learn, evolve, and sustain strategic relevance in complex, evolving, innovation ecosystems.

Insolubility as a Goal: Joint U-I Labs for Sustaining Paradoxes Towards Pasteur-Like Research

TIM
Elise Lagasse (Mines ParisTech); Quentin Plantec (Toulouse Business School); Pascal Le Masson; Benoit Weil
University–industry collaborations (UICs) are increasingly promoted to foster innovation, yet existing research largely treats the tensions they generate as problems to be mitigated or resolved through alignment, proximity, or transfer oriented governance. Drawing on paradox theory, this paper reconceptualizes UIC tensions as enduring paradoxes that must be actively sustained to support Pasteur-like, use-inspired research. We examine joint university–industry laboratories (JUILs) as organizational arrangements designed to maintain the simultaneous presence of academic and industrial logics. Based on a qualitative study of 13 JUILs supported by the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS), drawing on 46 interviews and 117 secondary sources, we identify governance mechanisms that institutionalize the four interrelated paradoxes: belonging, performing, organizing, and learning; through dual academic– industrial leadership, shared but revisable research agendas, mutual proposal and veto rights, standardized intellectual property frameworks, bounded autonomy, or iterative steering processes aligned with doctoral research cycles. By mobilizing paradox theory in the context of UICs, the paper shifts attention from tension resolution toward the governance of insolubility, showing how JUILs operate as Pasteur-like research settings beyond technology-transfer logics while satisfying both academic and industrial stakeholders.

Open Innovation, Absorptive Capacity, and Financial Health in Turbulent Technological Environments

TIM
Derek Eldridge (The University of Manchester); Tahir M. Nisar (University of Southampton)
As technological turbulence intensifies, evidence remains limited on how firms can translate open innovation into sustained financial returns. This study examines how open innovation intensity influences firms’ long-term financial health and develops an integrated framework linking external knowledge inputs, internal capabilities, and financial outcomes. Absorptive capacity is theorized as a key mediating mechanism, while technological turbulence is introduced as a moderating boundary condition. Using firm-level panel data and fixed-effects regression models, complemented by robustness and endogeneity tests, the analysis reveals three main findings. First, open innovation intensity has a significant and positive effect on long-term financial health, reflected in improved profitability quality, cash flow stability, and debt-servicing capacity. Second, absorptive capacity partially mediates this relationship, indicating that open innovation contributes to financial sustainability by strengthening firms’ ability to identify, assimilate, and exploit external knowledge. Third, technological turbulence amplifies this mechanism: under moderate to high levels of turbulence, the positive effect of open innovation on absorptive capacity is stronger. This study contributes theoretically by extending open innovation research from short-term innovation outcomes to long-term financial health and by clarifying the mediating role of absorptive capacity and the contingent role of technological turbulence. Practically, the findings suggest that firms should balance external collaboration with sustained internal investment in learning capabilities and strategically adjust openness in response to technological volatility to achieve durable financial resilience.

Traceability and Relational Innovation in the Biomedical In-Dustry

TIM
Gregory Theyel (California State University); Glen Taylor; Izzet Sidki Darendeli (California State University)
Can traceability increase the overall capacity for innovation in a value chain? We find preliminary support for the proposition that traceability has both first-order and second-order effects on value chain innovation. The first-order effect is an increase in transaction cost efficiency. The second-order effect is the enhancement of relational innovation. Biomedical value chains are more efficient when accurate sourcing information is readily available. The complexity of regulatory requirements, especially where new product formulations are involved, acts as an incentive to have excellent tracking systems for all inputs in the value chain. This is how traceability efficiently lowers value chain transaction costs. The second-order effect goes beyond simple efficiency and individual feedback loops between organizations. It is an emergent property of the increasingly complex system of network interdependencies within the value chain system itself. This additional complexity increases the potential for unpredictable new nonlinear pathways for innovation to emerge. This is more than just the amplification and dampening effect of individual feedback loops from the increased transparency in information flows required to achieve traceability. This secondary effect is a function of effects of the overall unpredictable relational innovation potential of the value chain. Based on previous research and additional insights from our analysis, we examine the possibility of traceability leading to a first-order effect on transaction costs and a second-order effect on relational innovation capacity.
