Session overview

Institutional trust is commonly understood as a psychological state grounded in positive expectations about institutional intentions, decisions, and actions. While trust varies across individuals, organizations, and societies, prevailing explanations—such as declining legitimacy, reputational damage, or performance failures—are insufficient to fully account for contemporary trust challenges. This symposium advances a reconceptualization of institutional trust as an inside-out outcome of institutional design and dynamic processes rather than a passive response to external evaluation. We argue that trust is actively produced through internal design choices, including the delegation of authority, enactment of rules and norms, allocation of vulnerability, and evolution of decision-making processes. From this perspective, trust problems reflect systemic design failures that generate uneven, unstable, and shifting trust experiences across groups and institutional interfaces. By treating trust as a dynamic process rather than a static stock, the symposium examines how trust is produced, fragmented, and reconfigured—particularly as institutions redesign roles, redistribute risk, and incorporate algorithmic systems—helping explain why institutions can be simultaneously trusted and distrusted.
