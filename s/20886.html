Prior Losses Lead People to Make More Rational Decisions Compared to Prior Gains

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 8:30AM–9:30AM
Loews Philadelphia Hotel — Poster Wall 1
MOC

People

Authors: Jack Xinhao Yu The Hong Kong Polytechnic University; Hayagreeva Rao Stanford University

Session overview

Cognitive biases systematically impair organizational decision-making, yet few interventions successfully address multiple biases simultaneously. We investigate whether prior losses, which organizations and decision makers typically seek to avoid, can paradoxically reduce their susceptibility to widely studied biases compared to prior gains. We propose that prior losses diminish decision makers’ subjective confidence, which promotes more extensive information sampling and deliberative processing. Across six studies with students, investors, and managers, we test this account in both scenario-based corporate board decisions and incentivized investment tasks. Studies 1 to 3 find that the sunk cost bias, default bias, and outcome bias are approximately 50% weaker following prior losses than prior gains. Studies 4a and 4b manipulate experienced gains and losses in an investment task and find that losses reduce decision makers’ subjective confidence, which predicts a greater likelihood of changing one’s course of action. Employing process tracing methodology, Study 5 found that prior losses lead to sustained attention to available information and longer information searches before decision-makers commit to a choice. Whereas loss aversion research has focused on how losses influence what people choose, and loss-attention research has examined how losses influence arousal and attention allocation, our findings reveal how prior losses shape confidence, which affects the quality of the decision by regulating when information search terminates. Moreover, this research identified a domain-general mechanism for debiasing multiple organizationally relevant biases due to premature commitment to specific options. Practically, periods following organizational setbacks may facilitate more rational decision-making than those following successes.
