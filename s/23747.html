Rural Entrepreneurship, Sustainable Poverty Alleviation, and Institutional Functional Specialization

ENT
Linlin Jin (Guangdong University of Technology); daobang yang (Guangdong University of Technology); Xizi Wu (Guangdong University of Technology)
Rural entrepreneurship is increasingly positioned as a critical engine to counter the fragility of poverty exits and drive sustainable alleviation in emerging economies. Anchored in the context of China—the world’s largest emerging economy—this study investigates the causal impact of rural entrepreneurship on sustainable poverty alleviation using panel data from the 2016–2022 China Family Panel Studies (CFPS). We employ a Double Machine Learning (DML) framework to estimate causal effects while controlling for high-dimensional confounding, measuring ex-ante relapse risk via three-stage Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) and multidimensional deprivation via the Alkire-Foster method. The results, robust to Bartik shift-share instrumental variable estimation, demonstrate that rural entrepreneurship significantly suppresses relapse risk and mitigates multidimensional deprivation. Crucially, we identify a pattern of "Institutional Functional Specialization": formal institutions primarily reinforce entrepreneurship’s risk-suppressing effect, whereas informal institutions amplify its efficacy in alleviating multidimensional deprivation. Further analyses reveal distinct functional roles across entrepreneurial types and critical boundary conditions: necessity-driven and informal ventures primarily serve as safety nets against relapse, whereas opportunity-driven and formal ventures foster multidimensional inclusion. This study provides rigorous empirical evidence for entrepreneurship-driven poverty alleviation research in emerging economies, extends its theoretical boundaries, and offers critical policy insights for designing long-term sustainable poverty alleviation mechanisms.
