Does Government Regulation Affect Firm International Trade Performance?

STR
Xuemei Xie (School of Economics and Management, Tongji University); Hong Yu (School of Economics and Management, Tongji University); Zhiyan Wu (Zhejiang University)
This paper investigates the impact of government regulation on firms’ international trade performance. Using large-scale firm datasets, we show that heightened government regulation disciplines firms by reducing production intensity and thereby lowering total trade activity, while simultaneously redirecting their trade toward environmentally friendly products through increased innovation. Moreover, firms’ responses vary systematically with their adjustment capabilities and the intensity of the regulatory pressure they face. These findings provide a more nuanced understanding of the multifaceted role of government regulation in shaping firms’ trade performance and offer important guidance for firms on how to align trade activities with sustainable development goals.

Signals in the Storm: Disentangling the Spillovers of Geopolitical Shock through Dual Text Analysis

STR
Yukun Wei (Tsinghua University); Donghong Li (Tsinghua University)
The existing literatures on non-market strategy have long explored how institutional jolts affect firm value, yet how investors decode granular risk signals from complex resource configurations remains under-theorized in the shadow of geopolitical decoupling. This article investigates the market value spillovers of sanctions, such as the U.S. Entity List, through the lens of dual organizational similarity. Utilizing Natural Language Processing (NLP) to analyze both patent-text and product-text data, we provide a high-fidelity mapping of firms’ technological and product spaces to disentangle the dual pathways of geopolitical contagion. We find that technological similarity acts as a deterministic signal of structural vulnerability, triggering significant negative valuation spillovers. However, product similarity exhibits a more complex logic. While it facilitates "stigma contagion" through guilt by association, high product similarity paradoxically functions as a "strategic buffer" for firms anchored in identical technological paths. By revealing the paradox of similarity, this study advances the micro-foundations of signaling theory and provides critical insights into how the interplay between technological embeddedness and market positioning defines corporate resilience in a fragmented global economy.

When Political Capabilities Travel: Subnational Industrial Policy and Firms’ Subsidiary Entry (WITHDRAWN)

STR
Anni Chen (Southeast University)
Subnational governments increasingly deploy industrial policies that vary across jurisdictions, yet we know little about how within-country policy heterogeneity shapes firms’ domestic geographic expansion. Integrating the institution-based view with political and dynamic capabilities, we argue that expansion depends on the portability of home-grown political capabilities to potential host jurisdictions. Conceptualizing a home–host dyadic policy environment, we predict that greater industrial policy similarity lowers political learning costs and facilitates transferring configuration-specific political routines, increasing the likelihood of city-level subsidiary entry. This similarity effect is weakened by policy uncertainty at home, which shifts learning toward adaptive political capabilities, and by policy uncertainty at the host, which raises the risks of deploying home routines. Using Chinese listed firms’ city-level subsidiary entries (1998–2018) matched with 5,122 local government work reports, we find support for our hypotheses. Our study advances research on nonmarket capabilities and subnational institutions by introducing dyadic, text-based measures of industrial policy similarity and uncertainty and by theorizing when political capabilities travel across jurisdictions.

How Much Do Governments Matter? Firm-Level Performance Implications of Public and Private Management

STR
Jose Carlos Rodriguez Pueblita (IPADE Business School); Aldo Musacchio (Brandeis University); Sergio Giovanetti Lazzarini (Insper)
We theorize that firm performance depends not only on internal management practices but also on the quality of local public management. Moving beyond country-level institutional determinants of performance, we argue that capable subnational governments create “islands of capabilities” that shape firm-level productivity by facilitating complementary public goods and reducing institutional frictions. Using original measures of private and municipal management practices for over 15,000 firms and 2,458 local governments in Mexico, we show that both dimensions significantly affect and explain productivity outcomes. To identify causality, we instrument municipal capabilities using historical distances to colonial tax centers, capturing persistent institutional legacies. Our findings suggest that public management quality—rooted in historical bureaucratic capacity—matters as much as private management in explaining productivity. We also conduct analyses of heterogeneous effects and find that quality public management is especially important for small enterprises and firms not integrated into global value chains. This paper advances our understanding of institutional determinants of firm-level performance by jointly assessing the role of public and private management and considering institutional variations of governmental action even within the same country.
