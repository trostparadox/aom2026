Preparing Principals: A Causal Test of School Leadership Training as a Distributed Property

OB
Rebecka Persson (Stockholm School of Economics); Karl J. Wennberg (Linköping University); Ema Kristina Demir (Stockholm School of Economics)
Leadership training primarily targets individuals, yet distributed leadership theory views leadership as shared across actors. This study tests whether leadership training effects display distributed properties when multiple leadership team members are trained over time, distinguishing between additive effects (cumulative organizational gains as more members train) and concertive effects (multiplicative gains via collaboration). Using longitudinal data from 2009 to 2021, we analyze 18,563 person-year observations from 3,783 principals and assistant principals using dynamic difference-in-differences and multilevel approaches. Outcomes are student achievement measures and teacher ratings of instructional leadership. Results show that sequencing matters: when principals are trained first, concertive mechanisms boost student achievement. Yet teacher ratings of instructional leadership decline when principals are trained first, suggesting a redistribution of leadership across teams. Findings challenge individual-focused training evaluations and support the potential of distributed mechanisms of leadership training, highlighting the importance of strategic training order in leadership teams.

Don't Judge a Leader by Their Reluctance

OB
Bahar Moraligil (Loughborough University); Zeynep Aycan (Loughborough University); Mustafa Ozbilgin; Cihat Erbil
Organizations invest over $166 billion annually in high-potential employees (HiPos), yet many programs fail to identify the right candidates, with failure rates up to 40%. This study addresses the misrecognition of HiPos, focusing on reluctance to lead (RTL) as a key factor. RTL can lead to competent individuals being overlooked. Moderate RTL can enhance leadership by promoting power-sharing and follower self-leadership, which is characterized by humility and a servant mentality. This research investigates whether HR professionals’ HiPo selection biases against RTL harm leader effectiveness through two studies. We investigate the impact of RTL on HiPo selection (Study 1), and examine the relationship between RTL and leadership effectiveness (Study 2). By understanding RTL's role in misrecognition and its association with effective leadership, this research aims to diversify the HiPo pool, support reluctant leaders' development, and promote diversity and inclusion in leadership selection.

Project Success-Failure Experiences and Leader Development: A Decade-Long Field Study

OB
Yeun Joon Kim (University of Cambridge); Andreas Wilhelm Richter (University of Cambridge)
Drawing on Role Transition Theory, we examine the effects of project success-failure experiences on the development of leader mindsets and subsequent team performance outcomes, comparing different career transition and non-transition periods: (1) the transition from education to work, (2) the transition into a first formal leadership role, and (3) the non-transition period between these transitions. Using a decade-long field study of 132 team leaders in an East Asian fashion design company, we find that project success-failure experiences during the transition from education to work strongly predict the development of leaders’ regulatory focus mindsets. Specifically, project success fosters a promotion focus mindset, while project failure fosters a prevention focus mindset. These mindsets, in turn, predict team outcomes: a promotion focus mindset enhances team creativity, while a prevention focus mindset improves routine performance. However, project success-failure experiences during the transition into a first leadership role do not exhibit similar effects. We discuss contributions to the leader development literature.
