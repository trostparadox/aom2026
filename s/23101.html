Perceived Fit in Internships: Value Alignment, Organizational Emphasis, and P-O Fit

HR
David W. Sullivan (UNSW Sydney); Justin P. Wiegand (San Diego State University); Erica Studer-Byrnes (University of Florida)
Internships are a primary gateway to organizational entry, yet organizations receive limited guidance on how early experiences shape interns’ perceptions of person–organization (P–O) fit. Drawing on values-based fit theory and phenomenological perspectives on fit, we argue that values are most influential early in internships and that perceived fit is shaped less by exact value congruence and more by alternative forms of value alignment and misalignment. Specifically, we propose that high person–high organization value alignment and organizational emphasis on salient values play a disproportionate role in shaping early fit perceptions, which then guide subsequent fit perceptions as internships unfold. We test our theory using a four-wave longitudinal study of 188 interns placed in 150 organizations over a 12-week period. Mediated polynomial regression and response-surface analyses show that interns report higher early perceived fit when both they and the organization strongly emphasize a value, and when the organization emphasizes values more strongly than interns do. Over time, early fit perceptions increasingly shape later evaluations, reducing the direct influence of value alignment. These findings refine values-based P–O fit theory and suggest that clear, early organizational emphasis on core values is a practical and tractable lever for shaping interns’ fit perceptions.

When Teams Stumble: How Performance History Shapes Adaptive Learning Responses to Failure

HR
Yibing Zhou (Shanghai Jiao Tong University); Lei Wang (University of Science and Technology Beijing)
Organizational learning research presents a contradiction: team failure sometimes stimulates exploration, yet other times drives exploitation. We propose that teams’ performance history functions as an interpretive lens that shapes how failure is cognitively processed, creating distinct psychological mechanisms. Prior performance establishes evaluative benchmarks that transform setbacks—either as recoverable deviations or indicators of inadequacy. These interpretations mobilize different systems: performance history determines whether failure activates resilience mechanisms promoting exploration or fear mechanisms encouraging exploitation. We tested this moderated mediation framework using longitudinal, multi-source data from 168 R&D teams (1,030 individuals), combining archival performance metrics with a novel operationalization of team failure. Findings confirm our account: high-performing teams showed increased exploration via resilience-mediated processes, whereas low-performing teams demonstrated enhanced exploitation via fear-mediated processes. By showing how performance context shapes failure's meaning and consequences, this research bridges competing theoretical traditions and establishes an interpretive framework for organizational learning.

Why and When Newcomer Reputation for Creativity Triggers Coworker Ostracism

HR
Min Li (University of Science and Technology of China); Yanfen Wang (University of Science and Technology of China); Hui Jiang (University of Science and Technology of China); Dongdong Li (Shanghai University)
Prior research has predominantly focused on the interpersonal interaction outcomes of reputation for creativity from a vertical interaction perspective (i.e., interactions with leader), while overlooking the potential consequences from a horizontal interaction perspective (i.e., interactions with coworker). Drawing on social rank theory and social interdependence theory, by examining interpersonal outcomes under the horizontal interaction lens, this research builds a process mechanism to understand why and when newcomer reputation for creativity leads to coworker ostracism. Specifically, this research argues that newcomer reputation for creativity causes coworker ostracism by influencing coworker perception of future status threat, especially when coworker-newcomer have low outcome interdependence. The results of multiple studies using complementary methodologies—three experiments and one two-wave study—support these hypotheses. These findings provide a nuanced explanation for the unintended consequences of newcomer reputation for creativity and offer theoretical guidance for organizations to effectively guard against coworker ostracism.

Informal Learning as an Impression-Relevant Process: A Within-Person Investigation

HR
Yuhan Zhan (Florida International University); Catherine Kleshinski (Indiana University Bloomington); Seunghoo Chung (Korea University)
Despite the prevalence and importance of informal field-based learning (IFBL) in the workplace, we still lack a comprehensive and balanced understanding of its consequences. Drawing on an integration of impression management and social comparison theories, this study takes a more nuanced view of IFBL by examining the consequences of daily engagement in its three subdimensions- feedback-based learning, vicarious learning, and experimentation learning. Using a daily experience sampling study, we find that feedback-based learning is positively related to image maintenance concern, which in turn facilitates knowledge sharing when competition is low and is positively related to self-promotion regardless of competition intensity. In addition, experimentation learning is positively related to knowledge-based psychological ownership, which leads to self-promotion when competition is high. This study contributes to the employee learning literature by offering a more complete and balanced understanding of IFBL and by expanding the range of its consequences. Implications for future research and practice are discussed.

Changes in Newcomer Networks: Relationship Building, Adjustment, and Individual Difference

HR
Ruolian Fang (The University of Western Australia); Hong Zhang (Lingnan University)
Newcomers’ ego-networks of informal relationships with organizational insiders play a critical role in their adjustment, yet prior research has largely relied on a static view that obscures how their networks change over time, what drives such change, and with what consequences. We develop and test a dynamic social capital model that centers within-individual, change-to-change relationships among newcomers’ relationship-building behaviors (general socializing and upward relationship building), friendship network features (diversity and status), and adjustment, as well as the moderating role that newcomers’ core self-evaluation (CSE) plays in the change-to-change relationships between behaviors and friendship networks. We tested our hypotheses using two multi-wave studies of organizational newcomers. Study 1’s results show that increases in general socializing foster growth in network diversity and increases in upward relationship building facilitate gains in both network features. In turn, only increases in network diversity predict gains in role clarity, task mastery, and social integration. Study 2’s results further show that newcomers higher in CSE more effectively translate increases in relationship-building efforts into cumulative network advantage over time. Our findings contribute to scholarship on newcomer socialization and social network dynamics.
