Artificial Intelligence and the Human Side of Innovation: Employee Intrapreneurship in New Firms

ENT
Arsenii Bystrov (Higher School of Economics); Galina Shirokova (HSE University); Younggeun Lee (State University of New York, Korea)
In the face of rapid digital transformation and resource-constrained environments, new firms increasingly rely on artificial intelligence (AI) to enhance innovation and competitiveness. This study examines how AI adoption in new ventures influences employee intrapreneurship, highlighting the mediating role of learning orientation (LO) and the moderating impact of environmental hostility. Drawing on dynamic capabilities and organizational learning theories, we argue that AI empowers employees to initiate innovative projects. Using survey data from new ventures, we find that AI use positively relates to employee intrapreneurship, with LO serving as a significant mediator in this relationship. However, the benefits of AI-enabled learning are attenuated under hostile environmental conditions characterized by uncertainty, resource scarcity, and competitive intensity. These findings extend the literature on digital transformation and intrapreneurship by revealing AI as a catalyst for bottom-up innovation, contingent upon organizational learning culture and external context. For practitioners, the study underscores the importance of fostering strong learning orientations and resilience strategies to ensure that AI investments translate into sustainable intrapreneurial outcomes in turbulent environments.

Entrepreneurial Learning in the Era of Generative Artificial Intelligence

ENT
Timo Christian Eckhardt
Generative artificial intelligence (generative AI) is becoming increasingly important for entrepreneurship, reshaping how entrepreneurs acquire, process and apply knowledge. Yet we know little about the mechanisms that underlie entrepreneurs’ learning processes when generative AI is used for entrepreneurial activities. This field study, conducted in the United Kingdom, triangulates 18 semi-structured interviews from early-stage entrepreneurs, 56 chat episodes, and 384 chat interactions. Applying the Gioia methodology and an experiential learning lens, we explain how generative AI fits into the entrepreneurial learning process that supports artefact development. The result is a process model that explains how generative AI mediates the entrepreneurial learning process during entrepreneurial artefact development. During the artefact development, entrepreneurs experience an accelerating effect through increased productivity, an extension in their skill boundaries while keeping the generative AI under control through quality management. In addition, they perceive a positive influence on the psychological meta-level and follow a workflow pattern. At the core, it facilitates entrepreneurial experiences, assists in reflective observation, enhances concept building and theorising, and supports through option generation and simulation. By addressing the underexplored domain of AI enabled learning during early-stage entrepreneurship, this study moves the conversation towards how entrepreneurs bundle talking, thinking and doing through generative AI mediation when crafting pitches, business plans and other entrepreneurial artefacts. In doing so, we advance entrepreneurial learning research for the generative AI era and offer scholars and entrepreneurs guidance on how to leverage generative AI as a mediator to accelerate and deepen learning during early-stage entrepreneurship.

From Hamartia to Adaptation: Sequencing Entrepreneurial Learning from Failure

ENT
Diarmuid Smyth; Yi Jiang
We observe the processes through which serial entrepreneurs learn from failure. Entrepreneurial failure is widely acknowledged as a catalyst for learning, yet little is known about how such learning unfolds over time or why some entrepreneurs progress while others remain constrained by the emotional aftermath of failure. Drawing on in-depth interviews with 24 serial entrepreneurs across 69 failure episodes, this study identifies four lessons the entrepreneurs typically learn after failure, as well as three sequence patterns explaining how entrepreneurs move from the disruption of failure toward renewed entrepreneurial engagement. Central to this progression is the concept of Hamartia, a threshold moment of honest self-recognition through which emotional turbulence is transformed into reflective capacity. By theorizing the process characteristics underlying these learning sequences and connecting them to the learning-from-failure literature, we extend experiential learning theory and offer an explaination for variation in post-failure trajectories among serial entrepreneurs.

Learning & Personality in Entrepreneurship: A Systemic Review

ENT
Nohemi Torres Salazar (Oklahoma State University); Pier Luigi Giardino (Not Associated)
Learning is a fundamental element in entrepreneurial development and success. Nonetheless, it has not been thoroughly explored in literature, showing a lack of consensus in its definition and operationalization in empirical studies. In foundational works, learning has been theorized as a cumulative of knowledge that can influence venture survival, depicting the bounded rationality of entrepreneurs. Research in managerial psychology incorporates personality traits as a potential factor that influences learning and performance. This literature review analyzes research published on the intersection of personality traits and learning within an entrepreneurship context, with the objective of identifying the underlying mechanisms and moderators that affect the relationship between personality traits and learning. This review expands the literature areas of managerial psychology and the entrepreneurial learning literature. Future suggested directions focus on the use of longitudinal studies and the intersection of bright personality traits (BPT) and dark personality traits (DPT) to better explain the mechanisms by which entrepreneurs learn.

Self-Regulated Entrepreneurial Learning: Scales Development and Validation

ENT
Noah Bellwald; Alexander Paul Fust (University of St. Gallen); Tobias Jenert (University of Paderborn); Christoph Winkler (Iona University)
Successfully navigating uncertainty is a challenge for entrepreneurs that demands adaptive and dynamic learning processes. Self-regulated learning (SRL) provides a framework for such adaptability, yet its application to entrepreneurship (SREL) remains underexplored. This study introduces a novel SREL framework and develops three empirically validated scales aligned with the SRL phases of forethought, performance, and self-reflection. Each scale captures cognitive, metacognitive, and motivational dimensions. Tested in the U.S. and validated in Europe, Africa and Asia, the scales provide a robust tool for assessing entrepreneurial learning, advancing entrepreneurship education, and empowering entrepreneurs to grow continuously.
