Substantive vs. Strategic Green Innovation: Mitigating the Consequences of Environmental Violations

ONE
Prem Thapamagar (University of Science and Technology of China); Noor ul Amin (University of Science and Technology of China); Nisar Ahmad
Firms face intensifying regulatory scrutiny and rising stakeholder expectations for environmental stewardship, yet many continue to incur environmental violations, raising concerns about their long-term performance consequences. Prior research has focused on short-term market penalties, leaving the operating performance dynamics of misconduct underexplored. Drawing on the Natural Resource–Based View (NRBV), we argue that environmental violations gradually erode firms’ return on assets (ROA), but that substantive and strategic green innovation can buffer these adverse effects. Using an event study of 716 Chinese listed firms with violations (2005–2023), matched with non-violating peers, we analyze abnormal ROA trajectories. We find that environmental violations are significantly negatively associated with firm operating performance; however, the magnitude of this adverse effect is contingent upon the green innovation strategies pursued by violating firms prior to the violation year. Importantly, firms engaged in both substantive and strategic green innovation not only avoid this decline but, in some cases, achieve positive abnormal ROA. Our study advances theory by clarifying the temporal dynamics of environmental misconduct, and it informs managers and policymakers by showing how green innovation strategies can transform regulatory shocks into opportunities for long-term performance resilience.

When Policy Meets Capital: Stringency, Green Finance & Organizational Adoption of Low-Carbon Tech

ONE
Muhammad Umar (Lebanese American University); Manal Yunis; ADNAN SAFI (Qingdao University); Nawazish Mirza (Excelia Business School)
The transition to low-carbon technologies is ultimately an organizational investment and adoption challenge that depends on both regulatory pressure and access to green capital. We examine how environmental policy stringency and green finance jointly shape low-carbon technology adoption across G7 economies. Using Method of Moments Quantile Regression, we estimate heterogeneous effects across the adoption distribution while accounting for trade openness, energy intensity, and research and development expenditure. Results show a conditional (complementary) relationship between policy and finance rather than an additive one: policy credibility and stringency appear most effective when they activate financial channels that mobilize private green capital. The policy–finance synergy strengthens from lower to upper quantiles, with green finance becoming increasingly effective among advanced adoption contexts, while stringency influences late-adoption contexts mainly through indirect financial channel activation. Trade openness and R&D investment also support adoption. These findings highlight a sequencing logic for organizing the low-carbon transition: late adopters benefit from green finance development before stringent policies can translate into technology uptake, whereas advanced adopters gain most from coordinated policy–finance frameworks. The study offers implications for managers and financial intermediaries navigating decarbonization investments under evolving regulatory and capital-market conditions, and for policymakers designing integrated climate policy and finance architectures.

Navigating Policy Reversals: How Evaluative Shifts Reallocate Corporate Environmental Performance

ONE
Jian Hu (Singapore Management University); Heli Wang (Singapore Management University)
This study examines how state-level policy reversals that narrow the admissibility of environmental criteria in public finance and contracting reshape firms’ operational environmental outcomes. We argue that responses will diverge across environmental domains because greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are diffuse and weakly attributable, whereas toxic releases (TRI) are locally legible and more tightly sanctioned. Using a facility-year panel linking EPA GHGRP emissions and EPA TRI releases to public parent firms and exploiting staggered policy adoption in difference-in-differences and event-time designs, we find that these policy reversals are associated with higher emissions in the GHG-related outcome but no detectable average change in toxic releases. Moderator tests show no robust buffering role of firm ESG capability, while civil-society scrutiny attenuates the enactment-linked effect on toxic releases but does not condition the GHG response. These findings indicate that politicized policy reversals can produce cross-domain reallocation in environmental performance rather than uniform rollback.

The Zero-Emission Energy Trap: How Interdependencies Keep the Energy Transition Unresolved

ONE
Roxane Grüne (RWTH Aachen University); Torsten-Oliver Salge
Grand challenges such as decarbonizing national energy systems demand tight coupling among interdependent fields, e.g. renewable energy generation, grid infrastructure, and emerging complements such as hydrogen. Building on issue field theory, we examine how interdependencies between different exchange fields sustain non-settlement in an environmental issue field. Drawing on 90 semi-structured interviews (2024 - 2025) with diverse actors in Germany’s energy field that are engaged in the field of renewable energy, especially wind and solar energy, the energy infrastructure and the hydrogen field, we examine the issue field of zero-emission energy production in Germany. We analyze interdependencies, processes and challenges that occur within and between those fields through which the achievement of zero-emission energy production and the settlement of the corresponding issue field is hindered. Using a bricolage analysis combining Gioia method, abductive theorizing, and temporal bracketing, we develop a self-reinforcing Non-Settlement Model. Our model identifies three reinforcing feedback cycles - Curtailment Lock-In Effect, Subsidy Lock-In Effect and Non-Settlement Loop, showing why the issue of zero-emission-energy cannot be solved. Our contributions extend institutional theory, especially issue field theory by theorizing inter-field dependencies as a self-reinforcing feedback loops.
