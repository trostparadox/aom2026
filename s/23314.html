Does CSR Improve Financial Performance? Reevaluating Evidence from Close-Call Resolutions

STR
Jonathan Berkovitch (LUISS); Andrew King (Boston University); Giovanni Valentini (IESE Business School)
Scholars have long sought to understand the causal relationship between Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) and financial performance. A notable innovation in this quest was the use of regression discontinuity designs (RDD) to infer causality by examining how investors respond to close-call passage of resolutions to increase firms’ CSR commitments. Two influential studies reported that investors expected such resolutions to boost the focal firm’s prospects while diminishing those of competitors. However, the rarity of CSR resolutions left those findings reliant on few observations near the voting threshold, raising concerns about whether the conditions required for valid RDD-based causal inference are satisfied. In addition, prior work identified resolution passage using a simple majority rule, whereas actual company voting policies may impose different approval requirements. Finally, a subsequent study covering a different period found no significant market reaction. We replicate and extend all three analyses, re-examining their original samples before broadening the time horizon and applying company-specific passage rules. Across specifications, we find no consistent evidence that investors revalue firms, positively or negatively, following the passage of CSR resolutions. Our results offer a clearer view of the CSR-performance link and highlight both the promise and peril of regression discontinuity designs.

Backing Out-of-Place Causes: Visibility Choices in Corporate Giving

STR
Shuyao (Steven) Zhou (Instituto de Empresa, S.L.)
U.S. corporations donate either through corporate foundations, which disclose grants on IRS Form 990-PF, or via direct gifts that typically remain undisclosed. Using advocacy giving by S&P 500 firms from 2018–2024, this study examines whether firms use this channel choice to manage the visibility of socio-politically sensitive donations. Results show that firms are more likely to give directly when the recipient advocacy nonprofit’s ideology is out of step with the political lean of the recipient’s local environment. Additional analyses show stronger patterns when the recipient is larger, when the local political environment is more homogeneous, and when the environment is strongly liberal. Overall, the evidence documents systematic shifts toward lower-visibility giving in advocacy contexts where local disapproval is more likely.

Provisional Engagement with CSR: Strategic Decoupling as a Real Option

STR
Neri Min (Korea University); Junmin Park (Korea University); Changwha Chung (Korea University)
This paper investigates the persistence of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) decoupling despite the heightened transparency and associated risks in today’s business environment. While CSR decoupling—symbolically committing to CSR without aligning substantive actions—has been criticized for its potential to harm reputation, this study argues that it can be a strategic tool for firms. Integrating insights from Real Options Theory and Attention-Based View, we conceptualize CSR decoupling as a flexible strategy that allows firms to test and assess the potential business value of CSR before making further commitments, with attention shaping the likelihood of transitioning from symbolic to substantive CSR practices. Using data from 7,816 companies across 52 countries between 2011 and 2022, we provide empirical evidence that CSR-decoupled firms are likely to recouple when their corporate social performance improves. Our findings extend the classic decoupling theory by showing how transparency has shifted its dynamics and offering a new perspective on CSR decoupling as a strategic testing mechanism. This study contributes to the literature by integrating Real Options Theory and Attention-Based View into the CSR decoupling conversation, offering both theoretical and practical insights into how firms can navigate CSR commitments in contexts of intensified external scrutiny.

ESG Rating Uncertainty: Causes, Consequences and Potential Remedies

STR
Ali Bayat (University of Aberdeen); Ruini Qu (The University of Edinburgh); Zohreh Rahmani (University of Aberdeen)
This paper synthesizes the fragmented literature on ESG rating divergence and employs a Bayesian meta-synthesis to quantify its magnitude, drivers, and the efficacy of potential remedies. We estimate a latent correlation of approximately 0.45 (95% credible interval 0.24 to 0.63) for overall ESG scores but document a structural break in the governance pillar (posterior mean ˜ 0.13; 95% credible interval -0.23 to 0.56) and a structural ceiling in the social pillar (posterior mean ˜ 0.34; 95% credible interval 0.12 to 0.55). Decomposing the sources of variation via a four-dimensional framework, we find that fundamental disagreement on ESG definitions is the dominant driver (36%). We further test the efficacy of an integrated policy bundle comprising standardization and regulation, stakeholder participation, and third-party evaluation. These interventions yield only modest convergence, reducing disagreement by roughly 17% for environmental scores but only 3.3% for governance. Collectively, our results illustrate that 1) the scale of divergence is consistent with a crisis of validity; 2) the source of divergence is primarily conceptual rather than methodological or behavioral; and 3) divergence is structurally irreducible through policy. Market participants must implement strategies to mitigate the effect of the uncertainty caused by the divergence or shift toward outcome-based alternative measures.

Only the Paranoid Survive: Monitoring, Awareness, and Learning in CSR Strategy

STR
Christian Geisler Asmussen (Copenhagen Business School); Andrea Fosfuri (Bocconi University); Nicolai J. Foss (Copenhagen Business School)
Firms increasingly pursue Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) strategies to build and maintain legitimacy and reputation. However, these benefits can be undermined by the actions of network partners such as suppliers or subsidiaries. While prior research highlights high monitoring costs and weak enforcement as key challenges, we use game-theoretic modeling to highlight the role of different degrees of awareness among lead firms in value chains. Our findings reveal that: (1) credible commitment to monitoring improves both social and firm performance; (2) fully aware firms monitor selectively, while partially aware firms apply uniform oversight, enabling greater misconduct among suppliers; (3) data-driven decision making may lead to underestimation of reputational threats, which lowers CSR and economic outcomes (whereas overestimation improves both); (4) firms may fail to monitor either because they are genuinely unaware of reputational risks or because they strategically pretend to be unaware to avoid accountability, which is more likely when stakeholders are lenient or when market immaturity makes unawareness plausible.
