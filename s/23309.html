When the Watchers Grow Weary-Governance Workload of External Directors and Organizational Misconduct

STR
Krati Jain (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Manish Popli (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Mehul Raithatha (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
This study revisits the "busy boards" debate in the realm of effective governance by focusing on the cognitive workload that external directors face rather than the number of directorships they hold. We develop a multidimensional construct of governance workload that aims to capture the nonlinear and dynamic information-processing demands of external directors. Drawing on information-processing theory, we argue that higher aggregate governance workload limits external directors' cognitive bandwidth, increasing reliance on heuristics, lowering monitoring quality, and thus increasing the firm's proclivity to engage in organizational misconduct—illegitimate actions that cause material harm to shareholders. Using data on US firms from 1999 to 2023, we find that a greater aggregate governance workload of external directors increases the likelihood of organizational misconduct. However, this effect is mitigated when external directors possess longer tenures or have had prior board roles in firms engaged in misconduct. These findings extend the board busyness literature by offering a cognitive explanation for heterogeneity in governance effectiveness.

Attention Without Accountability: Online Media Visibility and Insider Trading in China

STR
Rong Zeng (University of Manitoba); Mingzhi Liu (University of Manitoba, Asper School of Business); Songlian Tang (Donghua University)
This study examines whether online media visibility disciplines or enables insider trading in the opaque setting of private in-house meetings. While prior research has emphasized the governance potential of media attention, findings on online media remain mixed—ranging from monitoring effects to pressure-induced misconduct. Drawing on agenda-setting theory, we distinguish between media visibility and media attributes to evaluate how salience and framing shape insider behavior. Using a sample of 20,089 firm-year observations of Chinese listed firms from 2012 to 2019, we find that higher online media visibility is positively associated with insider trading profitability, suggesting that visibility amplifies speculative attention rather than curbs opportunism. Furthermore, the effect is weakened when coverage is original or positively toned, but strengthened when coverage is government-oriented. These results highlight the dual nature of online media—as a source of both public attention and governance distortion in opaque information contexts. Our findings contribute to research on online media, corporate governance, and agenda-setting theory by demonstrating how online media visibility and framing jointly influence insider behavior.

Collusion or Monitor? Firm with the Co-CEO Structure and Its Financial Misconduct

STR
Chenxin LIU (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University); Lin Luo (Nanjing University); Liangding Jia (Nanjing University); runjia li (Nankai University); Yingqi Xia (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen)
Financial misconduct often originates in CEOs’ discretion over financial reporting, yet conventional governance mechanisms (e.g., compensations and board of directors) provide limited close-up visibility into executives’ day-to-day decisions. We theorize that a co-CEO structure constitutes an internal governance design that embeds peer-level mutual monitoring in routine top leaders’ work. Because co-leadership inherently combines cooperation (shared accountability) and competition (latent power rivalry and succession stakes), we argue that its misconduct-deterrent effect is contingent on dyadic configurations that shift the balance between monitoring and mutual protection. Using a 2000–2024 panel of U.S. public firms that adopted co-CEO arrangements, we show that firms with co-CEO years are associated with a lower likelihood of restatement. This deterrent effect weakens when co-CEOs have longer prior co-working experience and when the dyad exhibits a larger power gap, but strengthens for insider–outsider pairings and greater political ideology distance. Our findings position co-CEO structures as a contingent peer-check mechanism, extending corporate governance research beyond arm’s-length monitoring and enriching shared leadership theory with coopetition-based microfoundations. Furthermore, we do not argue that co-CEO structures are categorically superior to single-CEO models, or other well-established governance mechanisms. Rather, we treat co-CEO as an underexplored but existing governance design that illuminates when close-up peer monitoring constrains wrongdoing—and when it may backfire through collusion.

Actions Have Consequences: CEO Exposure to Natural Disasters and Securities Class Action Lawsuits

STR
Saloni Singh (Stevens Institute of Technology); Josep A. Tribo (Stevens Institute of Technology); Suman Banerjee (Stevens Institute of Technology)
This paper examines whether natural-disaster trauma experienced during a CEO’s formative years shapes CEOs’ risky behavior, proxied by firms’ securities class action lawsuits (SCAs). Our claim is that there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between childhood fatality exposure and risky behavior. In particular, we claim that CEOs with medium fatality exposure are significantly more likely to face SCAs, whereas those with extreme exposure are significantly less likely to do so. A second claim is that CEOs’ educational level moderates the previous connection. Higher-educated CEOs attenuate the association between trauma exposure and litigation incidence. We confirm our result. In particular, a one-standard-deviation increase in fatalities exposure is associated with an increase of about 3 pp in SCA probability at low-to-moderate exposure levels, whereas at higher exposure levels, the predicted probability declines by roughly 6 pp. We use U.S. public firms linked to CEOs born after 1955 in the US. The final sample comprises 821 CEOs, 6,617 firm-year observations, and 773 firms. These findings provide some clues about CEOs’ expected risky behavior, which may be of critical importance when recruiting a new CEO and/or providing CEOs with incentives to run a firm.

The Effect of CEO Communication Style on Corporate Misconduct

STR
Anmol Pandey (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley); Michael A. Abebe (The University of Texas Rio Grande Valley)
Given the influence and visibility of their position, the public communication of Chief Executive Officers (CEOs) has important implications for their firms. It signals what they pay attention to and is likely to motivate their subsequent strategic actions. Despite the extensive research in this area, we know less regarding how CEOs' attention to certain issues or actors, more than others, in their communication influences the occurrence of corporate misconduct. In this study, we draw insights from the attention-based view to argue that CEOs exhibit a dominant communication style in their interactions with stakeholders. Specifically, we theorize that CEOs differ in their use of a competitor-oriented versus stakeholder-oriented communication style and propose that while CEOs' use of competitor-oriented communication is associated with higher incidents of misconduct, their use of stakeholder-oriented communication will reduce these incidents. Furthermore, we argue that financial distress amplifies (weakens) the positive (negative) effect of competitor (stakeholder)-oriented communication on misconduct. Finally, we also present risk-taking strategies and corporate social performance as mediators of these baseline predictions. Our study of CEO communications in quarterly earnings calls among S&P 500 firms from 2015-2019 provides support for most of our predictions. Implications for research and practice are discussed.
