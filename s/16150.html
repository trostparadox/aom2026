The Pathway of Noble Entrepreneurship: Virtue, Moral Responsibility, and Sustainable Enterprise

Authors: Navarat Sachayansrisakul; Nattawat Ponnara

Session overview

The Social Responsibility (MSR) literature has extensively examined corporate responsibility, ethical governance, and stakeholder accountability; however, entrepreneurship research within MSR remains largely outcome-oriented, emphasizing responsible innovation and sustainability performance while under-theorizing the moral development of entrepreneurs themselves. Ethics is frequently treated as an external constraint embedded in institutions, codes, or stakeholder pressures, rather than as an internal, developmental process shaping entrepreneurial judgment, intention, and action. This paper advances a virtue-based theoretical framework that reconceptualizes entrepreneurship as a moral pathway, in which enterprise development is inseparable from the cultivation of ethical awareness, responsibility, and wisdom. Drawing on Buddhist moral philosophy, the study positions entrepreneurship as a form of livelihood that functions simultaneously as economic activity and moral practice. Grounded in the Buddha’s teachings, the paper identifies greed, anger, and delusion as unwholesome roots generating harm and unsustainability, and theorizes generosity, discipline, and wisdom as cultivated virtues that integrate inner morality with outer ethical conduct. Using a qualitative, interpretive design, the study draws on in-depth interviews with 22 Buddhist entrepreneurs across the Asia–Pacific region. Findings show that entrepreneurs who cultivate virtues demonstrate greater moral clarity, resilience, and responsibility, interpreting generosity as inclusive value creation, discipline as ethical consistency, and wisdom as long-term discernment. These virtues form what the study conceptualizes as The Noble Pathway of Entrepreneurship, a moral continuum inspired by the Buddha’s Eightfold Path. The paper further introduces the 7D–4W Model of Virtuous Entrepreneurship, theorizing responsibility as a dynamic, cultivated capability sustained through reflective practice. By shifting MSR attention from compliance to moral agency, this study contributes a process-based account of socially responsible entrepreneurship grounded in virtue ethics and sustainability.
