Data Exclusivity for Science (WITHDRAWN)

TIM
Seokbeom Kwon (Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology)
When data creators publish research during exclusive data access periods, does this encourage or discourage subsequent reuse by other scientists? This question is central to policy discourse on how proprietary data access for data creators shapes scientific progress. The conventional view suggests that greater publication by data creators signals preemption of valuable research opportunities, thereby deterring reuse. We challenge this view by theorizing that data creators’ active publication during exclusive access can encourage data reuse through dual mechanisms: signaling data value while inducing self-sorting among potential reusers toward research questions distinct from creators’ works. We test this argument using data from the European Southern Observatory, where principal investigators (i.e., data creators) receive one-year exclusive access before mandatory public release. Employing a control function approach with an instrumental variable, we find that higher publication volumes by data creators during exclusive access increase subsequent data reuse by other scientists. Applying natural language processing methods to abstracts of publications, we further show that greater creator output during exclusive access is associated with lower topical overlap between creators’ and reusers’ research, indicating that scientists with distinct research interests are more likely to reuse the data and that this self-sorting intensifies as creators publish more. These findings reveal that temporary exclusive data access can simultaneously protect creators’ research opportunities and facilitate broader data reuse, offering new insights for designing data sharing policies in science.

Do Women Ask for Less? The Gender Gap in Science Funding

TIM
Astrid Ulv Thomsen (Copenhagen Business School); Valentina Tartari (Stockholm School of Economics); Hans Christian Kongsted (Copenhagen Business School); Kris Gulati (University of California Berkeley); Lorenzo Palladini (Stockholm School of Economics)
Scientific discovery depends not only on what ideas are funded, but on how researchers themselves translate ideas into resource requests that shape teams, training, and future knowledge production. Using a unique dataset covering the full universe of funded and unfunded grant applications to two major philanthropic science funders in Europe between 2011 and 2022 - linked to peer-review scores, investigators’ prior research records, and itemized budgets - we examine where gender differences arise in the funding process. We find that female principal investigators receive 6.4–7.7% less funding per application than male investigators, a gap that is partly driven by differences at the proposal stage: women request 5.4% less funding, conditional on proposal quality and researcher characteristics, despite being more likely to receive funding. This gap is concentrated in team formation, with women systematically proposing smaller PhD cohorts and no comparable differences across other budget categories and is largest among early-career researchers. Extrapolating these estimates to the U.S. context suggests that women may be systematically requesting on the order of $1 billion less annually from major public funders such as the NIH and NSF, with implications for training capacity, knowledge production, and the cumulative organization of scientific labor.

Learning by Evaluating: Evidence from Academic Peer Review

TIM
Charles Ayoubi (ESSEC Business School); James M. Zumel Dumlao (University of Michigan); Misha Teplitskiy (University of Michigan)
The evaluation of innovative projects is an essential task for scientific and business organizations alike. While prior work has focused on the quality of evaluations and how they affect innovators, little is known about how the evaluation process affects evaluators themselves. We propose that evaluations provide evaluators with valuable learning opportunities by exposing them to relevant, cutting-edge knowledge. We test this argument in the setting of academic peer review, using administrative data from the Institute of Physics Publishing comprising 331,449 reviewer-manuscript pairs across 62 physical sciences journals between. We find that evaluating a manuscript more than doubles the likelihood of a reviewer using that knowledge, measured through citation within three years, compared to qualified evaluators who were invited but unavailable. Evaluators’ geographic and intellectual distance to the work moderate the learning effects of evaluation. While unavailable evaluators from the same country as a paper’s corresponding author have significantly higher baseline citation rates to the paper being evaluated, evaluators from different countries who reviewed the paper exhibit significantly higher citation levels, indicating that structured evaluation of external ideas can compensate for geographic boundaries in knowledge transfer. Similarly, reviewers intellectually distant from the manuscript reviewed exhibit significant learning effects from review exposure, however intellectual proximity between evaluators’ recent work and the work being evaluated is associated with stronger learning gains. Results from instrumental variable analyses exploiting the staggered rollout of an anonymization policy and within-reviewer variation corroborate these findings. These findings show that expert evaluations benefit not only organizations through efficient resource allocation but also the evaluators themselves, offering a new explanation for why experts participate in the laborious and seemingly under-incentivized work of evaluation.

Opening Knowledge Gaps Drives Scientific Progress.

TIM
Kara Kedrick (Carnegie Mellon University); Wenlong Yang (Huazhong University of Science and Technology); Thomas Gebhart (University of Minnesota); Yang Wang; Russell James Funk (University of Minnesota)
Knowledge production is often viewed as an endogenous process in which discovery arises through the recombination of existing theories, findings, and concepts. Yet given the vast space of potential recombinations, not all are equally valuable, and identifying those that may prove most generative remains challenging. We argue that a crucial form of recombination occurs when linking concepts creates knowledge gaps––empty regions in the conceptual landscape that focus scientific attention on proximal, unexplored connections and signal promising directions for future research. Using computational topology, we develop a method to systematically identify knowledge gaps in science at scale. Applying this approach to millions of articles from Microsoft Academic Graph (n = 34,363,623) over a 120-year period (1900-2020), we uncover papers that create topological gaps in concept networks, tracking how these gap-opening works reshape the scientific knowledge landscape. Our results indicate that gap-opening papers are more likely to rank among the most highly cited works (top 1–20%) compared with papers that do not introduce novel concept pairings. In contrast, papers that introduce novel combinations without opening gaps are not more likely to rank in the top 1% for citation counts, and are even less likely than baseline papers to appear in the top 5% to 20%. Our findings also suggest that gap-opening papers are more disruptive, highlighting their generative role in stimulating new directions for scientific inquiry.

The Impact of Causal Evidence on Innovation: Lessons from Randomized Controlled Trials in Surgery

TIM
Wesley Greenblatt (Harvard University); Soomi Kim (Columbia Business School)
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are often considered the gold standard for providing causal evidence, but they are not without limitations. In addition to their high costs, RCTs may produce results that are not useful or generalizable for follow-on research. Employing a difference-in-differences approach, we study how evidence generated by RCTs shapes research activities in surgery. We find that follow-on research appears to decline after the publication of an RCT, with the remaining research being of lower impact and clinical relevance. This dampening effect of RCTs is especially pronounced for high-quality trials and those that evaluate interventions with lower barriers to adoption. We conclude with a discussion of whether these effects have positive or negative implications for innovation.
