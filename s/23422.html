Collaborative Field Building Under Coopetition: Institutional Work in Regional Transition Governance

PNP
Christoph Richard Felix Nasgowitz (RWTH Aachen University)
Tackling complex public problems requires collaboration among heterogeneous actors that extends beyond mere policy implementation. Drawing on an inductive longitudinal single-case study (55 interviews, extensive observations) of Germany’s most heavily publicly funded transformation region, we show that actors collectively shape the “rules of the game” for their shared endeavor – shared definitions of the problem, agreed participation rules, workable decision interfaces, and resource arrangements that make joint action possible. Yet such collaborations rarely involve cooperation alone; actors also compete for funding, authority, and recognition, creating tensions of coopetition. We examine how these cooperative and competitive dynamics interact over time. We find that the collaboration advances through phase-linked, workable agreements that progressively make the emerging institutional field governable while shifting who has voice and access across three arenas: meaning, authority, and resources. These agreements help stabilize coordination without full consensus, but they become contested as they begin to shape who benefits and who leads.

The Recursive Interplay Between Network Governance Outcomes and Public Value Creation

PNP
Jorge Renato Verschoore (Unisinos University); Douglas Wegner
This article develops a conceptual framework to bridge the network effectiveness gap by specifying how network governance produces both intermediate and finalistic outcomes. Building on network outcomes and micro-governance research, we theorize a collaborative environment - comprising cohesion, commitment, inclusion, legitimacy, power symmetry, and trust - as an intermediate outcome that links day-to-day governing practices to the public value created by networks. We distinguish these six intermediate outcomes from three groups of finalistic outcomes: democratic, performative, and innovative public value. The framework advances three propositions: (1) collaborative-environment outcomes are analytically distinct from finalistic outcomes; (2) a stronger collaborative environment reduces coordination hazards, enables service integration, and aligns resources and behaviors, thereby increasing finalistic outcomes; and (3) finalistic outcomes recursively reshape the collaborative environment, generating virtuous or vicious cycles of effectiveness. We conclude by outlining an agenda for multi-level, longitudinal, and configurational studies of network governance effectiveness across policy domains.

Bottom-Up Knowledge Flows in the NGOs: An Abductive Study Using Nonaka’s SECI Model

PNP
Gaurav Tasiwal (Institute of Rural Management Anand); Satyendra Pandey; Prachi Bhatt (FORE School of Management); Kushal Anjaria (Institute of Rural Management Anand)
This study investigates how knowledge is created, transferred, and integrated across organizational levels in the NGO context, drawing on Nonaka’s SECI (socialization, externalization, combination, internalization) framework. Using an abductive, qualitative single-case study of an Indian NGO, using semi-structured interviews with frontline staff, community resource persons, project leads, and middle managers, complemented by focus groups, field observations, and document analysis. We find that knowledge work in NGOs follows a distinctly bottom-up configuration. Socialization is dense and continuous at the community interface, sustained by proximity, trust and shared practice, while externalization and combination become increasingly selective and constrained upstream. Rather than unfolding as a symmetric cycle, transitions between SECI phases are uneven and contingent. We identify two cross-level mechanisms: communication architecture and boundary-spanning roles, that condition these transitions by opening or closing windows for tacit-explicit conversions. Tacit insights stabilize into reusable, cross-level knowledge only when three conditions co-occur: timely codification, validation through internal or external partner channels, and alignment with organizational or policy agendas. The study highlights how operating rhythm and structural arrangements shape knowledge dynamics in resource-constrained NGOs. Absent these conditions, knowledge remains localized, person-bound and vulnerable to loss. By developing a mechanism-conditioned account of SECI tailored to NGOs, this study advances KM theory to nonprofit contexts. It explains why grassroots knowledge often circulates locally yet fails to inform organizational memory or policy and specifies the organizational arrangements under which upward knowledge conversion becomes possible. While the single-organization, retrospective design limits generalizability, the study offers propositions and operational indicators to guide future comparative, longitudinal and mixed-methods research on NGOs' knowledge systems.

Managing Resilient Purpose-Oriented Networks

PNP
Robin Hargroder Lemaire (Louisiana State University); Qian Hu (George Mason University); Jeongyoon Lee (University of Kentucky); Kate Albrecht (University of Illinois at Chicago)
This study examines the resilience of purpose-oriented networks (PONs)—consciously formed, self-referencing collectives of organizations pursuing a shared purpose—crucial for addressing complex societal challenges and delivering public services. While unpacking the complex, nested levels of networks, we address the following questions: 1) How to conceptualize PON resilience as a multi-layer concept? and 2) What are key considerations for managing PON resilience at different levels of analysis? We demonstrate multi-layer conceptualization of network resilience with the case of a Food Access PON, focusing on three layers of analysis: node, connection, and domain. For each layer, we discuss how resilience can be conceptualized, key questions to consider, and the challenges to managing resilience. Our multi-layer conceptualization of resilience offers network managers ways to mitigate potential vulnerabilities of the PON.

Social Sensing and Big Data Approaches to Gaining Insight into System-Oriented Networks

PNP
Branda Nowell (North Carolina State University); Cody Taylor (Appalachian State University); Serena Kim (North Carolina State University); Nina Blau (North Carolina State University); Eliana Isabel Sanchez Rangel (North Carolina State University); Mary Obiol (North Carolina State University); Amber LaRose (North Carolina State University)
Remote sensing is the science of collecting information about a phenomenon without being in direct physical contact with it. The sub-field of social sensing is the practice of using human-generated data to detect, interpret and understand social conditions indirectly. In this paper, we present a methodology for leveraging big data techniques and social sensing to gain insight into system-oriented networks. Scholars have long been interested in understanding policy outcomes as product of the interconnected web of actors engaged in, and relevant to complex problem and policy domains. In 2022, Nowell and Milward introduced the term system-oriented networks (SONs) as an over-arching taxonomic classification describing a group of actors defined by boundary conditions set by the researcher around a system of interest within a defined geography. Referred to in the past by many names reflecting different sub-types and disciplinary traditions including policy networks, institutional ecosystems, governance networks, and community networks, the core assumption underlying research on SONs is that the observed outcomes in community and policy settings are often the product of a complex set of actions and interactions by a collection of institutional actors operating at different scales and representing diverse interests (Klijn and Koppenjan, 2014; Koliba et al, 2017). However, because of their complex, multi-level and dynamic nature, methodological challenges in studying SONs abound and theorizing about SONs has outpaced methodological advancements. Key methodological challenges include ambiguous boundary determinations, near population level data requirements for network level analytics, missing data, data collection costs when seeking to represent large geographies, survey burden associated with network roster data techniques, and the dynamic nature of networks leading to the need for feasible tracking of change over time (for discussion see Taylor & Nowell, 2024). While limited in application to date, large data analytic techniques offer promise as a complement to existing case study approaches for studying SONs. In this paper, we review the core analytical challenges of studying SONs and investigate what insights into the institutional make up and organization of SONs can be captured through remote social sensing using big data analytic techniques. Analytical opportunities as well as challenges are discussed.
