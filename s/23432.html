When Success Equals Non-Existence: Death as Planned Obsolescence in a Nonprofit Organization

PNP
Nitesh Bhat (Azim Premji University)
Organizational death is typically considered an undesirable outcome emerging from decline, failure, or any imposed closure. Parallelly, success is associated with impact, scale, and sustainability. However, my study of SELCO Foundation, an Indian nonprofit organization, presents a contrast. In my conversations, I repeatedly heard interviewees equate organizational death or non-existence as an aspirational ideal, or as a measure of their success. Data suggest that the idea of working toward organizational death unfolds as a planned obsolescence. Such obsolescence is a deliberate and moral aspiration to not exist despite organizational viability and legitimacy. I distil a four-phase process for how employees work toward organizational death: first, the idea of organizational permanence is problematized; second, success is redefined from an ecosystem standpoint; third, exit is embedded into organizational interventions, and finally, death is normalized as an aspirational state of organizational evolution. my study extends nonprofit theory by reframing organizational death as a potentially favorable outcome and offering new insight into how social ventures define success and mission completion.

Pathway to Social Performance in Museums: An Empirical Study on Business Model Innovation

PNP
Akitsu Oe (Tokyo University of Science); Shohei Oishi (Tokyo University of Science); Mariko Watanabe (Tokyo University of Science)
This study empirically examines the mechanisms underlying business model innovation (BMI) in social purpose organizations (SPOs) from the perspective of organizational innovation (OI). Although OI refers to organizational activities perceived as subjectively novel by the focal organization, the pathways through which multiple practices of OI jointly create and enhance customer value and lead to organizational performance remain underexplored. Using covariance-based structural equation modeling on survey data collected from 394 Japanese public museums, the analysis reveals that internal process innovation facilitates technological collaboration with other organizations and indirectly contributes to museums’ social performance via enhanced visitor orientation and product innovation. The findings indicate that BMI in museums as SPOs emerges through the combination and interaction of multiple practices of OI. This study contributes to the development of BMI research in the context of SPOs, advances the theoretical integration of BMI and OI, and provides practical implications for museum management.

The Impact of Performance Feedback on Government Short- and Long-Termism in Strategic Planning

PNP
Ting Huang (Queen Mary University of London); Chengwei Wang (University of International Business and Economics)
Despite extensive research on how public organizations respond to performance feedback, little is known about how such feedback shapes governments’ temporal orientation in strategic planning. Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm, this study examines how performance feedback influences short-term and long-term planning horizons. Using panel data from 292 Chinese municipal governments between 2010 and 2019, we test a tiered performance feedback framework that differentiates performance outcomes near versus far from aspiration levels. Fixed-effects analyses show that municipal short-termism peaks near aspiration thresholds, particularly when performance falls slightly below benchmarks, while performance above aspirations is associated with greater attention to long-term planning. These findings challenge the “zone of indifference” assumption and highlight time horizon as a critical yet overlooked dimension of performance feedback responses.

Discipline or Distortion? Top Managers’ Tenure Heterogeneity and Performance Data Manipulation

PNP
Wen Xu (Central China Normal University); Linfei Li (Technical University of Munich); Mengtao Liu
While top management teams (TMTs) are central to public governance, existing research has predominantly attributed performance data manipulation to the strategic responses of individual executives facing high-powered incentives and accountability pressures. This study shifts attention to the under-theorized dimension of TMTs’ non-demographic heterogeneity, specifically examining how tenure differences within TMTs influence data distortion by shaping the allocation of decision-making primacy. Based on nighttime lights data, economic growth measures, and managers’ career-history data for Chinese cities from 2007 to 2018 (N = 3,372), Our two-way fixed-effects model yields three key findings: First, tenure overlap within the TMTs significantly serves as a constraint on data manipulation, suggesting a mutual monitoring effect. Second, the sequence of entry matters: manipulation is mitigated when the official with greater managerial discretion (Party Secretary) assumes office prior to the subordinate (Mayor), reinforcing vertical oversight; conversely, manipulation risks intensifying when the official with lower managerial discretion precedes the superior, reflecting an "entrenchment effect." Third, mechanism analyses reveal that these tenure dynamics influence manipulation primarily through credit expansion and fiscal stimulus channels. Our findings highlight that synchronizing tenure and optimizing appointment sequences are critical governance mechanisms for enhancing the integrity of government performance information.

Do Customers Reward Innovation? Experimental Evidence from For-Profit and Nonprofit Ventures

PNP
Chen Ji (Louisiana State University-Shreveport); Meng-Han Ho (National Central University); Patrick C. Dwyer (Indiana University)
Innovation facilitates organizational performance and service quality in both for-profit and nonprofit ventures. Despite the central role customers play in the entrepreneurship process, little is known about how they interpret and respond to different forms of innovation in both ventures. Understanding customers’ perceptions is essential, as their evaluations directly influence a new venture’s ability to secure critical resources and survive. This study examines how customers assess innovation signals emitted by new ventures and whether these signals meaningfully shape perceptions of organizational quality and willingness to contribute financially. Using a 3 (no vs. technological vs. managerial innovation) × 2 (for-profit vs. nonprofit ventures) between-subject experimental design, we test a mediation model linking innovation signals to customer support through perceived quality, with venture form as a contextual moderator. Results show that venture form functions as a strong identity signal that outweighs innovation, which operates as a relatively weak cue. The findings extend signaling theory by demonstrating how customers prioritize different cues when evaluating new ventures and challenge the assumption that innovation is always a decisive factor in attracting early customer support.
