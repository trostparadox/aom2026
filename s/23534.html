Leading Minds: How CEO Personality Shapes Digital Innovation

TIM
Sarah Lassek (University of Münster); Verena Rieger (University of Münster); David Lohmar (University of Münster); Vanessa Bröker (University of Münster)
Despite facing similar technological opportunities, firms often follow markedly different digital innovation trajectories. Building on upper echelons theory, the Five-Factor Model, and dynamic managerial capabilities, we propose a conceptual model linking CEOs’ openness and extraversion positively, and conscientiousness negatively, to variation in firms’ digital resource bases. Moreover, we propose that CEOs’ IT backgrounds strengthen these relationships. We utilize computational linguistics on CEO speech in Q&A sections from S&P 1500 earnings calls (2001–2023) to measure extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness among 3,946 CEOs across 1,977 firms. Our findings indicate that the effects of CEO personality on firms’ digital innovation are contingent on CEO IT background, with openness, extraversion, and conscientiousness exhibiting positive associations when CEOs possess an IT background. Moreover, personality traits influence the quantity of digital innovation output and the composition of the innovation portfolio in distinct ways. Our findings reveal systematic variations in how CEOs prioritize digital innovation strategically. They also offer novel implications for leadership selection and innovation governance, emphasizing the importance of considering CEO personality and human capital together.

Strategic Slack & Socioemotional Wealth: A Dual-Theoretical Perspective on R&D Smoothing.

TIM
Zeenat Murtaza (Queen Mary University of London); Androniki TRIANTAFYLLI (Queen Mary University of London); Georgios Kavetsos (Queen Mary University of London)
This study investigates how family governance interacts with financial conditions to sustain innovation through R&D smoothing (the deliberate stabilization of R&D investments over time). Drawing on Behavioral Agency Theory (BAT) with Socioemotional Wealth (SEW) as its core construct and the Resource-Based View (RBV), this research develops a governance-contingent framework that explains how SEW priorities and resource orchestration shape firms’ responses to liquidity changes and financial constraints. Using a dynamic panel dataset of 2,829 firm-year observations from French publicly listed firms (1997–2021) and employing a two-step system GMM estimator to address endogeneity, the analysis reveals three key insights. First, family-controlled firms exhibit lower sensitivity to liquidity changes than non-family firms which reflect family firms’ stewardship logic rooted in long-term orientation. Second, control intensity matters: firms with concentrated family control maintain stable R&D trajectories while those with low-to-moderate family control adjust investments more dynamically. Third, financial constraints reverse the governance effects on R&D smoothing in response to liquidity changes: family-controlled firms become more sensitive to liquidity fluctuations than non-family firms, with low-to-moderate family-controlled firms displaying heightened responsiveness and adopting flexible investment strategies, whereas high-control family firms sustain relatively greater innovation continuity under resource scarcity. These dynamics are most pronounced in high-technology sectors where liquidity acts as a dynamic capability. This study extends an understanding of the ability–willingness paradox in family firms by framing R&D smoothing as a governance-driven response while offering practical guidance for innovation continuity under financial constraints.

The Burden of Expertise: How Traditional Manufacturing CEOs May Stifle Automation Innovation?

TIM
Jia Guo (Dongbei University of Finance and Economics); Wenkai Zhou (Rollins College); Dongming Wang (Dongbei University of Finance and Economics); Guochao Yang (Fudan University)
The transition to smart manufacturing raises a critical question about leadership: could deep expertise in traditional methods become a barrier to adopting new technologies? This paper investigates the impact of CEOs' traditional manufacturing (TM) background on firms' automation innovation. Drawing on upper echelons and imprinting theories, we propose that TM CEOs' deeply ingrained experience may create a competency trap, leading to a preference for tangible, incremental improvements over uncertain, software-driven automation. Using a panel of Chinese A-share listed manufacturing firms, we employ a large language model to classify automation patents from textual data. Our empirical results indicate that TM CEOs are associated with a significant reduction in subsequent automation innovation. We uncover two mediating mechanisms underlying this effect: (1) a labor allocation channel, where TM CEOs favor a larger share of shop-floor workers over technical and R&D staff, and (2) a capital allocation channel, where they direct resources toward fixed assets and over-production while reducing R&D intensity. This suppression effect is mitigated by external market pressures (intense competition and powerful customers) and internal governance factors (CEO MBA education, a younger top management team, and higher managerial ownership). Furthermore, we establish the economic consequences by linking depressed automation innovation to lower future firm value (Tobin’s Q) and total factor productivity. Our findings contribute to the operations management literature by delineating the critical yet double-edged role of CEO expertise in technological transitions and by identifying the organizational levers that can unbind firms from legacy constraints.

Unpacking the Innovation Effects of CEO Ownership: The Roles of Earnings Pressure and Slack

TIM
Hai-Yen Nguyen (Southern Taiwan University of Science and Technology); Chung-Wen Hung (Southern Taiwan University of Science and Technology); Cheng-Yu Lee (National Chiayi University)
Grounded in stewardship theory, this study examines how CEO ownership influences firms’ innovation investment by highlighting long-term orientation, intrinsic motivation, and goal alignment between CEOs and organizational stakeholders. Using a panel dataset of publicly listed Taiwanese firms from 2002 to 2018, the findings show that CEO ownership serves as an important governance mechanism that encourages greater investment in innovation. The study further identifies two contingent factors that shape this relationship: earnings pressure and organizational slack. Earnings pressure—defined as the consequences of failing to meet stock analysts’ forecasts—acts as an external monitoring force that weakens the positive effect of CEO ownership on innovation investment. However, this constraining influence is mitigated when firms possess organizational slack, which provides a financial buffer enabling CEOs to maintain long-term strategic priorities despite external scrutiny.

When Compensation Difference Makes a Difference: CTO Relative Pay and Firm Innovation

TIM
Jiani Wang (Peking University); Yijiao Zhang (Peking University); Peiyuan Huang (Peking University)
Extending executive compensation research beyond CEOs, we investigate how the compensation design of key functional executives, i.e., Chief Technology Officers (CTOs), influences firm innovation. We argue that CTOs face dual comparison pressures that yield competing forces in driving firm innovation. They benchmark their compensation against external industry peers while simultaneously maintaining parity within the top management team (TMT). Using panel data on publicly listed U.S. firms in technology-intensive industries from 2000 to 2019, we find that higher CTO pay relative to external peers is associated with greater innovation output, whereas higher CTO pay relative to internal TMT members is associated with lower innovation. Moreover, these effects are more consequential as organizational complexity (as captured by industry dynamism and related diversification) increases. Our findings underscore the importance of balancing external and internal alignment in designing CTO compensation, highlighting the tradeoff between incentive benefits and coordination costs associated with pay differentials in governing firm innovation.
