Executive Function as Mechanism Link From Mental-Health Morbidity to Burnout & Soft-Skill Decay

NEUB
Dr. Douglas Peterson
Mental-health morbidity is one of the most pervasive yet undertheorized challenges facing modern organizations. Although research has explored workplace stress, burnout, stigma, absenteeism, and the emotional components of psychological strain, organizational scholarship has not fully accounted for how mental-health conditions alter managerial judgment, behavior, and performance. This article advances the thesis that executive function (EF) decay is the central cognitive mechanism linking mental-health morbidity to organizational outcomes. Drawing on interdisciplinary evidence from clinical psychology, cognitive science, neuroscience, and organizational behavior, the paper develops a model showing how common mental-health conditions degrade working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility—the core components of EF. These impairments undermine judgment accuracy, interpersonal interpretation, emotional regulation, and decision processes. The paper explains the implications for individual performance, team dynamics, and macro-level organizational functioning, positioning EF decay as a foundational construct for HR research on mental health. Practical implications for HR systems and future directions for empirical research are also proposed.

Attention Regulation in an Age of Distraction: From Paying Attention to Inhibiting Distraction

NEUB
Yi Jin Leow (Singapore Management University); Jochen Matthias Reb (Singapore Management University)
Why is sustained focus increasingly difficult in modern knowledge work, and what can organizations do beyond exhorting employees to “pay attention”? We argue that digital- and artificial intelligence-mediated workplaces have become stimulus ecologies characterized by high cue density, heterogeneity and engineered signals. Current organizational theories of individual attention rely on resource/capacity metaphors, under-specifying the mechanisms of attention and how distraction disrupts performance. Bridging organizational research with cognitive neuroscience, we integrate attentional network theory (alerting, orienting, executive control) with ecological rationality to develop a multilevel theory of attention regulation. We introduce stimulus-ecology escalation and theorize as cue competition intensifies and salience becomes decoupled from diagnosticity, the binding constraint on sustained, goal-consistent attention shifts from allocating limited resources to inhibitory governance: Executive control to maintain goals, inhibit unhelpful impulses, and enable rapid disengagement and re-engagement to focal tasks. We theorize mindfulness as a trainable form of metacognitive regulation that strengthens executive and inhibitory control, attenuating performance costs of interruptions and consequently improving job performance. Finally, we specify how person-level practices and environment-level “attentional architecture” operate as complements by reducing avoidable cue competition to preserve control for high-value conflicts. Oue framework generates propositions linking stimulus ecology, mindfulness, sustained attention, and job performance, and offers a research agenda for improving attentional regulation in distraction-rich organizations.

When Social Dominance Helps—and When It Hurts: Decision-Episode Dynamics of Status Motivation in Ada

NEUB
Hadi Mohamadpour (University of Tabriz); Sohvi Heaton (Baylor University); Farhad Farkhondeh Tale Navi (University of Tabriz); Soomaayeh Heysieatalab (University of Tabriz)
Social dominance—a motivational orientation toward influence and control—is widely observed among leaders, yet its consequences for adaptability under uncertainty remain unsettled. This matters because adaptability—the ability to recalibrate behavior in response to feedback—is essential for effective decision-making in dynamic environments. Prior research often treats adaptability as a static trait or infers it from aggregated outcomes, obscuring the mechanisms that determine when dominance helps or hinders adaptive responses. We address this gap by analyzing trial-level behavioral and neural data from 30 executives performing a monetary gambling task. Integrating dominance with feedback sensitivity (indexed by feedback-related negativity, FRN) and cognitive control capacity (beta-band power), we examine short-horizon recalibration within decision episodes. Results show that dominance amplifies post-loss risk escalation (agentic recovery), but when paired with high beta power, it suppresses loss-contingent switching, constraining error-driven learning. Greater FRN reduces risk posture, while prior gains promote strategic risk-taking. Importantly, only strategic recalibration predicts performance benefits, whereas reactive switching and volatility do not. By locating adaptability within decision episodes and linking it to motivational and neurocognitive mechanisms, this study clarifies when dominance fosters resilience—and when it reinforces rigidity.

High-Functioning Depression in Organizations: Resource and Neuroplasticity perspective

NEUB
Haesang Park (Morgan State University); Bingqing Wu (University of Wisconsin-Parkside)
This paper advances a conceptual model of high-functioning depression (HFD), defined as a pattern in which individuals sustain high levels of performance while experiencing persistent internal distress and depletion. Drawing on Conservation of Resources theory, HFD is framed as a form of resource overprotection whereby individuals chronically overinvest in work to avert perceived losses of status, competence, or belonging, thereby initiating loss spirals that gradually erode energy, vitality, and capacity for enjoyment despite continued external success. Integrating this perspective with research on neuroplasticity, the model proposes that repeated pairing of pressure and performance strengthens threat-oriented amygdala–prefrontal pathways that prioritize vigilance and control over rest and emotional flexibility, rendering HFD both subjectively necessary and resistant to change. On this basis, the paper articulates propositions linking HFD to resource overinvestment, neurophysiological overload, and interpersonal strain, and specifies relational interventions that can support the development of more sustainable neural and behavioral patterns conducive to long-term wellbeing. Practical implications highlight how leaders and organizations can redesign development and wellbeing systems to center nervous-system flexibility, recovery, and relational safety as core resources for employees experiencing HFD.
