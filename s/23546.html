Women’s Careers Reimagined: Identity, Rhythm, and Mid-Career Change

Session overview

This session rethinks women's careers by moving beyond linear models of work and advancement. The papers explore work identity, childfree women's experiences, life-course rhythms, mid-career transitions, and managerial aspirations to show how careers unfold through gendered expectations about care, time, morality, and ambition. Together, they broaden diversity scholarship by highlighting neglected pathways and experiences in women's working lives.

Papers (5)

Work Identity and the Myth of Mothers’ Lower Identity

DEI
Sara Hendrick (University of Northern Colorado); Angie Cameron Schultz (University of Northern Colorado)
This research examines the hypothesis that women’s work identity is high, regardless of parenthood status, whereas men’s work identity is contingent on parenthood. We posit that women prioritize personal fulfillment while men emphasize salary at work, and that these different motives shape work identity. Across two studies, we find consistent support for this pattern. In a nationally representative sample (Study 1), women reported stronger work identity than men, partially explained by women’s greater motivation for personal fulfillment. In a study of partnered employees (Study 2), women’s work identity was strong regardless of parenthood status, while fathers reported stronger work identity than non-fathers.

Sex Differences in Managerial Aspirations: The Role of Gendered Traits and the B5 Personality Trait

DEI
HeyIn Gang (Saginaw Valley State University)
Women remain underrepresented in senior management roles, and one possible explanation is that women have lower aspirations. In this study, we investigate whether sex differences exist in managerial aspirations, and examine the role of two sets of dispositional traits – gendered traits (masculinity and femininity) and Big Five personality traits – as determinants. Building on social role theory and the personality literature, we hypothesized that the predictors of managerial aspirations will differ between men and women. We found no explicit sex differences in managerial aspirations and masculinity, but found statistically significant sex differences in conscientiousness, emotional stability, and femininity. Masculinity was the only significant predictor of men’s managerial aspirations, whereas masculinity and both extraversion and conscientiousness predicted women’s managerial aspirations. Our findings challenge supply-side explanations for women’s underrepresentation in management and suggest that the growing androgyny among women may strengthen their leadership potential, even as traditional norms discriminating against women persist. These findings have implications for future research and practice.

“You Don’t Have Children Anyway”: Invisible Demands & Conditioned Autonomy of Childfree Women at Work

DEI
ELISA BEZERRA DE BARROS (UNIVERSIDADE FEDERAL DE RONDÔNIA); Aline dos Santos Barbosa (Fundação Getulio Vargas); Marcello Romani-Dias (Fundação Getulio Vargas)
This article examines how childfree women experience organizational life by unpacking the paradox between invisible demands and conditioned autonomy. Drawing on qualitative data from interviews with childfree women in Brazil, the study shows that while these women are often perceived as more available and flexible for career advancement, this perceived availability translates into moral and organizational pressures to absorb additional workloads and responsibilities that remain largely unrecognized. The findings further reveal how the absence of motherhood is socially and organizationally stigmatized, contributing to processes of misrecognition that constrain women’s autonomy and professional legitimacy. These dynamics foster heightened self-imposed pressures for continuous availability and high performance, as well as differentiated treatment in career progression and everyday workplace interactions. By foregrounding an understudied group in organizational research, this study advances debates on gender, work, and diversity by theorizing how moralized expectations of availability operate as invisible demands within organizations. The study raises critical implications for organizational policies and work–life cultures by showing how ostensibly neutral flexibility arrangements may reproduce exclusion and render autonomy conditional under gendered normative frameworks. It also points to avenues for research that explore intersections of gender, class, race, and conditions shaping experiences of childfree women at work.

Career Rhythms Across the Lifespan: An Age-Graded Perspective on Women’s Careers

DEI
Maren Wright Voss (Harvard University); Justin Marcus (Koç University); Barbara Fritzsche (University of Central Florida); Heike Schroder (Queen's University Belfast)
Despite women’s full integration into most Western labor markets, organizational career models remain anchored in linear, uninterrupted trajectories that poorly fit women’s embodied and social life courses. Our discussion suggests that predominant organizational and institutional designs exacerbate gender inequality and constrain societal sustainability among demographically aging, low-fertility environments. We argue that this is because menstruation, maternity, motherhood, menopause, and extended longevity introduce predictable rhythms into women’s careers that are systematically misinterpreted as disruptions rather than structural features. Using the geometric metaphor of Flatlands and drawing on life course and gendered organization literatures, we advance a rhythmic career perspective that reconceptualizes women’s careers as multidimensional arcs characterized by periodic contractions and expansions in labor market participation. This novel reframing of women’s careers challenges predominant ideal-worker norms and therefore shifts evaluation from short-term continuity to lifetime contribution. We conclude with a blueprint of macro-, meso-, and micro-level interventions that enable organizations and governments to better capture the full arc of women’s productivity and wellbeing—benefiting not only women, but all workers whose careers unfold rhythmically over time.

Stuck Between Care and Career: Gendered Moral Reasoning in Mid-Career Transitions

DEI
Payal Mukherjee (Tata Institute of Social Sciences)
Career transitions are typically theorised as individual, strategic responses to labour market changes or organizational changes. Often, contemporary career models overlook how inequality, care obligations and personal and professional identity shape career moves, especially at mid-career level. Drawing on 50 in-depth interviews (25 women and 25 men), this study examines how gendered norms of care, responsibility and respectability, structure the meaning and consequences of mid-career transitions. Using Career Construction Theory, feminist care ethics, Schlossberg’s Transition Theory , and Gioia methodology of analysis, this study shows that career transitions function not only as economic or developmental choices but also as moral acts which are embedded in the inequalities of care obligations. Women in this study, narrate transitions as morally coherent efforts to sustain care, dignity and relations. Men, on the other hand, experience downward or lateral transitions as threats to identity and status, requiring narrative and moral repair, even if they uphold caregiving responsibilities voluntarily. Spousal and familial support across both groups serve as social authorization. This study extends DEI scholarship beyond access and representation to examine how moral legitimacy, relational support and identity protection produce unequal career possibilities. Implications and limitations are discussed.
