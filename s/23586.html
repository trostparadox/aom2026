Bridging Bureaucracies: How Parachuted Officials Drive Environmental Innovation

TIM
Xu Jiang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Yaocheng Yang (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Jun Xia (The University of Texas at Dallas); Qadeer Abdul (Shenzhen University); Huinan Zhang (The University of Texas at Dallas)
While previous studies adopting institutional theory recognize the existence of institutional complexity and its possible impacts on firms, limited attention has been paid to how institutional brokers can shape organizational responses to institutional complexity. We theorize how the appointment of a unique type of institutional brokers, parachuted officials (i.e., officials with central government working experience before assuming office as leaders of local governments), may deal with institutional complexity by pushing firms in their jurisdictions to engage in environmental innovation which can achieve both social and economic benefits. Drawing on a sample of 2,800 publicly listed firms from 2009 to 2018 and employing a difference-in-difference approach, the findings reveal that jurisdictions led by parachuted officials see significantly higher rates of green patenting. Furthermore, our analysis reveals a dual pathway through which parachuted officials influence firm behavior, contingent on both official and firm-level characteristics. Specifically, the politicization of officials’ central government experience and firms’ operational diversification amplify their influence. In contrast, personal ties between parachuted officials and firm CEOs attenuate this effect. Our study contributes to institutional theory by emphasizing the important role of institutional brokers, who resolve institutional complexity through bridging the interactions between different institutional constituents within the state.

How Can Peripheral SMEs Overcome Thin Innovation Environments?

TIM
Jose-Luis Hervas-oliver (Universitat Politecnica de Valencia); Ann Hipp; Petrik Runst
We study how doing-using-interaction (DUI)-like (non-research and development; non-R&D) innovating small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in peripheral areas overcome their locational disadvantages. Cross-fertilizing the literature on innovation modes and SME innovation, we argue that collaborating with partners outside the region can compensate for the lack of intra-regional knowledge. Using Community Innovation Survey (CIS) data from German SMEs (2011–2022), our results indicate that international collaboration drives the product innovation of peripheral SMEs. The positive effect of these collaborations is stronger if the firm possesses internal absorptive capacities. In contrast, urban SMEs located in a knowledge-rich environment do not benefit from far-reaching collaborations but instead from regional ones. This study contributes to the under-researched geography of innovation in peripheral settings by extending the discussion of non-R&D SMEs and the DUI innovation mode

Multiple Pathways to Regional Innovation: Configurations of Intermediaries and Institutions

TIM
Yiyi Jing (University of Pittsburgh); Susan K. Cohen (University of Pittsburgh)
Literature attributes variation in regional innovation performance to differences in regionally bound institutional infrastructure (e.g., laws, policies, cultural norms, universities, government agencies) that shape an environment conducive to knowledge creation or to the presence of diverse intermediaries that mobilize resources, broker relationships, and select among innovation opportunities. Despite recognition that innovation reflects their joint influence, we know little about which configurations of institutions and intermediaries lead to higher regional innovative performance. This study employs fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) to examine how different configurations (presence and characteristics) of actors that play institutional and intermediary roles affect regional innovation outcomes. We focus on assistive technologies (ATs) because, while they commonly seek to restore or augment human abilities, they encompass a broad spectrum of technology types ranging from market-driven adaptive products to science-based biomedical innovations, providing a comprehensive empirical context not limited to any single technological domain. Analyzing 559 U.S. counties with Assistive Technology patents, we identify which combinations of intermediaries produce high innovation performance. Our findings reveal multiple equifinal pathways to high innovation performance, demonstrating that effective configurations share certain foundational elements while differing in how they achieve diversity. Rather than requiring all conditions to be included simultaneously, regional innovation systems can succeed through alternative structural arrangements where different types of diversity serve as functional substitutes. This study contributes to the regional innovation literature by demonstrating that configurations of conditions, rather than individual factors, shape innovation outcomes.

One Size Does Not Fit All: An Analysis of Agglomeration-Firm Fit on New Manufacturing Performance

TIM
Lauren Lanahan (University of Oregon); Nikhil Kalathil (Carnegie Mellon University)
Firms benefit from their local agglomerations when there is a distinct fit between accessible agglomeration assets and firm needs. Operationally, we sample and identify new manufacturing firms in identical industries that are born in seemingly similar but marginally different agglomerated counties. We define horizontal agglomeration as geographies with greater co-location of own-industry establishments, and vertical agglomeration as geographies with greater co-location of industry suppliers. On average, new manufacturing firms report performance gains in horizontal agglomeration relative to vertical agglomeration. Regions with anchors amplify this effect. Separately, firms operating with downstream production report increased performance when in vertically agglomerated regions. Lastly, new manufacturing firms located away from the industry-dominant agglomeration structure report higher performance. One type of agglomeration does not fit all new manufacturing firms.

The Shifting Levers of Innovation: Capability Stages and National Business Systems

TIM
Amir Amini Sedeh (Saint Edward's University); Mohammad Mahdi Moeini Gharagozloo (Morgan State University)
National innovation is not driven by a single best institution. It is shaped by how National Business System pillars jointly discipline learning, coordination, and credible commitment as countries move from basic to advanced capability. Building on Whitley’s framework, we theorize a conditional hierarchy of institutional levers whose binding constraints, and complements, shift with capability stage. Evidence from 87 countries from 2006 to 2025 supports this logic. Skill formation, proxied by formal employee training, functions as the most robust lever because it upgrades routines that make other institutions productive. Integrity conditions and state coordination follow as central drivers by stabilizing expectations and enabling collective action. Finance operates less as a primary engine and more as a complement that becomes consequential once routines and credibility deepen. The broader implication is a stage sensitive theory of institutional complementarity that specifies which levers matter when, and why, rather than treating institutions as universally substitutable inputs.
