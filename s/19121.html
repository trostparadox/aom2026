When Incumbents Enter: Power Redistribution and Collective Political Action in an Emerging Industry

Sunday, Aug 2 2026 · 4:00PM–5:00PM
Sheraton Philadelphia Downtown Hotel — Poster Wall 1
ONE

People

Author: Claire-Lise Speisser CNAM

Session overview

Emerging renewable energy industries increasingly attract incumbent multinational firms from adjacent sectors, particularly in response to major geopolitical and socio-political shocks. While prior research suggests that such incumbents can accelerate industry development by mobilizing resources, legitimacy, and political influence, less is known about how their entry reshapes collective political action at the field level. This study examines how the entry of incumbent multinationals redistributes power and resources within an emerging industry, and how this redistribution alters collective framing and political performance. Drawing on a longitudinal ethnographic study of the European biomethane industry, we analyze the entry of oil and gas multinationals following the European Union’s Net-Zero pledge and the geopolitical shock induced by the Ukrainian war. These exogenous events opened political opportunity windows that enabled incumbents to become visible and politically active actors within a historically fragmented political field. Our findings show that incumbent entry increased the industry’s political visibility and agenda-setting capacity by introducing resource-based political authority, professionalized political practices, and privileged access to high-level policy arenas. However, this redistribution of power also reconfigured roles within industry associations, displaced historical actors, and intensified competition across political arenas, fragmenting collective framing efforts. As a result, despite heightened political attention and short-term policy advances, the industry struggled to sustain unified frames and stable political support over time. We contribute to research on industry emergence and institutional dynamics by showing that incumbent entry can generate ambivalent political outcomes, enhancing collective political visibility while constraining collective political performance, with implications for sustainability transitions.
