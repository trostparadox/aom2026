Social Network Ties and Work-Life Balance

CAR
Thomas Joseph Zagenczyk (North Carolina State University); Sara (Krivacek) Bianco (James Madison University); Scott Soltis (University of Vermont); Russ Marion; Jane Layton (Clemson University); Gail DePreist (Clemson University)
Work-life balance (WLB) is often framed as a personal pursuit – based on an employee’s perception, attitude, and decisions about their work and non-work roles – yet it unfolds within a social context. Drawing from social learning theory, we propose that employees’ WLB is shaped through direct interaction with coworkers – via friendship, advice, and multiplex ties – as well as through observation of coworkers in similar network positions (structural equivalents). Results from a study of a police department show that employees have similar WLB to their multiplex ties and structural equivalents. Multiplex ties were more strongly associated with similarity in WLB perceptions compared to friendship or advice ties, which suggests that the advice/friendship combination is more influential than instrumental ties or those based only on affect. Our results are bolstered by our use of “pure ties”, assigning each tie to only one category (i.e., friendship tie, advice tie, or multiplex tie) to reduce contamination. We compare this method to a typical approach in which ties are counted multiple times and highlight key differences. In addition, we find that employees tend to have WLB that is more similar to advice, friendship and multiplex ties of others with high (versus low) WLB, indicating that employees may learn from coworkers who are perceived as successful in their pursuit of WLB instead of those who are not. Theoretical and practical implications, as well as future research directions, are discussed.

Breaking Functional Inertia: How Informal Networks Enable Cross-Functional Exploration

CAR
Xu Han (The College of New Jersey)
Cross-functional exploration, defined as employees' lateral moves across organizational functions, can expand knowledge and skill breadth, yet most employees remain anchored in functionally bounded career paths. We argue that informal networks forged through prior co-working help employees overcome functional inertia by providing access to cross-functional knowledge, support, and opportunity information while strengthening the legitimacy of moving into unfamiliar work. We distinguish network type (cross-functional ties) from network position (cross-functional brokerage) and theorize that both predict cross-functional exploration, particularly for highly specialized employees. Using longitudinal personnel records from a large multinational firm, we construct an employee-month panel spanning over a decade with more than 26,000 employees. Rare events logit estimates show that functional specialization is associated with lower likelihood of cross-functional exploration, whereas cross-functional ties and brokerage are associated with higher likelihood. Both network advantages are most consequential among highly specialized employees, which attenuates the negative association between specialization and exploration. This study advances strategic human capital and internal mobility research by identifying co-working based informal networks as a driver of exploratory career moves, and contributes to network and organization design perspectives by demonstrating how informal structure can offset constraints imposed by functional differentiation.

Work Connectivity as a Career Signal: Implications for Thriving at Work

CAR
Xin Chen (Renmin University of China); Chu-Ding Ling (Renmin University of China)
Work connectivity behavior—responding to work demands after hours—is common but understudied in relation to thriving at work, defined as employees’ vitality and learning. While prior research has uniformly emphasized its resource-draining effects, we propose two opposing forms of work-related rumination as the explanatory mechanisms, based on the Conservation of Resources theory. Specifically, work connectivity behavior can promote thriving by encouraging problem-solving pondering as a form of cognitive resource gain. Conversely, it can undermine thriving by triggering affective rumination as a form of resource loss. We further examine career ambition as a key moderator that amplifies resource gain and mitigates resource loss. We conducted a three-wave survey of 299 Chinese employees to test the hypothesized model. The results supported the double-edged sword effect of work connectivity behavior: it indirectly enhances thriving via problem-solving pondering and reduces it via affective rumination. Furthermore, the results supported the buffering role of career ambition in the resource-loss path. This study thus contributes to the literature by offering a more balanced understanding of the double-edged effects of work connectivity behavior on thriving at work through a resource-based perspective. In addition, this study highlights the career-related signals conveyed by work connectivity behavior by examining career ambition as the moderator. We advocate that future studies explore this crucial, yet often overlooked, aspect.

Unpacking the Trajectories of Helping Behaviours in Early Career Employees

CAR
Lucy Xing (The University of Auckland); Shuai Yuan (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
The early career stage is a formative developmental period during which individuals establish behavioural patterns that shape long-term career success. Helping behaviours, including both help-seeking and help-giving, are central to this adjustment, yet have received limited attention in early career research. Reconceptualising helping behaviour as dynamic, co-evolving, and heterogeneous processes, we adopt a longitudinal, person-centred approach to examine joint developmental patterns of help-seeking and help-giving during the transition from study to work. Tracking 239 new graduates across their first four months of employment, we assessed helping behaviours monthly and applied latent class growth modelling using a rigorous three-step estimation procedure. Five distinct classes emerged: High Stablers, Moderate Stable-Declining, Low Stablers, High Increasers, and Moderate-Low Decliners. Class membership was predicted by proactive personality, performance goal orientation, and political skill, underscoring the role of individual differences in shaping helping trajectories. These classes, in turn, differentially predicted end-of-period outcomes, including task performance, job satisfaction, and turnover intentions. This study advances a temporal and differentiated understanding of early-career employees’ adjustment, offering insight into why some employees thrive while others struggle during this critical transition period.

Internal Mobility and Performance: Social Undermining as a Cost of Moving Within Organizations

CAR
Annie Eunhee Kim (University of Nevada, Reno); Tae-Youn Park (Sungkyunkwan University); Andrea Kim (Sungkyunkwan University); Youngsang Kim (Sungkyunkwan University); Kyongji Han (The University of Texas at Tyler); Yong Geun Kim (Hallym University)
Internal mobility is widely viewed as a beneficial tool for employee development and career advancement, yet its immediate performance consequences remain underexplored. Drawing on social identity theory, we examine how internal moves generate social friction that undermines employee performance. We argue that internal movers are more likely to experience social undermining from incumbent unit members, leading to short-term performance declines, particularly when movers are high performers or when they enter high-performing units where competitive threat is heightened. We test our model using two studies conducted in a large engineering and construction firm. Study 1 analyzes personnel records from 3,288 employees and shows that internal mobility is negatively related to subsequent performance, with stronger effects for high-performing movers and those joining high-performing units. Study 2 combines survey and personnel data from 240 employees and demonstrates that social undermining mediates this relationship under the same boundary conditions. Together, these findings highlight the proximal social costs of internal mobility and clarify when internal moves may hinder, rather than enhance, employee performance.
