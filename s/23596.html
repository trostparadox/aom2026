Race, Migration, and Unequal Access to Opportunity

Session overview

This session investigates access to opportunity across contexts shaped by migration, ethnicity, and institutional inequality. The papers explore doctoral education, migrant managers progression, refugee women's downgrading, skilled migrants employment outcomes, and applicant attraction to organizational structures. Together, they show how opportunity is unevenly distributed across national and organizational settings, with important implications for global inclusion and mobility.

Reimagining Access to Doctoral Education: An Empirical Study of the ASPIRE Programme

DEI
IFEDAPO FRANCIS AWOLOWO (Sheffield Hallam University); Femi Stephen Olawoyin (Sheffield Hallam University); Egena Ode (Manchester Metropolitan University); Oluwatoyin Dosumu; Adenike Abidoye (Sheffield Hallam University); Oluwaseun Ajao (Manchester Metropolitan University)
Persistent racial inequities in doctoral education constitute a critical yet under-examined challenge within Equity, Diversity and Inclusion (EDI) scholarship. Doctoral pathways serve as high-impact pipelines that shape future academics, policy experts, and organisational leaders, yet Black students remain systematically underrepresented within them. This paper presents an empirical qualitative study of the Accomplished Study Programme in Research Excellence (ASPIRE), a race-conscious mentoring and development initiative designed to widen access to doctoral education for Black students in the UK. Drawing on semi-structured interviews, Listening Rooms, reflective diaries, and outcome data from three cohorts, the study adopts an interpretivist case-study approach informed by Critical Race Theory (CRT). The analysis identifies three interrelated processes through which ASPIRE reshapes doctoral access: structural demystification, relational belonging and identity affirmation, and ethical–aspirational formation. These processes are theorised as constitutive elements of Relationally Situated Critical Pedagogy (RSCP). This framework foregrounds the interaction between structural conditions, relational pedagogy, and ethical development in shaping inclusion within doctoral pathways. The paper contributes empirically to EDI scholarship by evidencing how race-conscious mentorship and culturally sustaining pedagogies foster belonging, legitimacy, and aspiration in doctoral education, with implications for the redesign of doctoral pipelines within and beyond higher education.

Unconscious Bias or Attributes? Career Progression Signals for Managers

DEI
Nasreen Sultana (The University of Queensland); Andre Pekerti (The University of Queensland)
Very little attention has been paid on career progression of migrants in management for male and female, white and non–white (minority) migrants, particularly those from non-English speaking backgrounds. Migrants do not have considerable presence in upper management positions commensurate with their numbers in the general workforce. This paper identifies factors that act as barriers and facilitators influencing migrants’ career progression in management positions. Twenty migrant managers were interviewed to examine their personal experience in career progression. Findings indicate that career progression of migrants varies depending on their categorization as immigrants or members of the dominant in society, influenced by perceptions of warmth and competence in the migrants. Findings highlight of the importance of addressing unconscious biases and potential discriminatory practices in in human resources decision affecting migrants’ employment and career progression.

Configurational Pathways into Occupational Downgrading Among Ukrainian Refugee Women in Poland

DEI
Sabina Kubiciel - Lodzinska (Opole University of Technology); Aleksandra Rudawska (University of Szczecin); Anna Slawik (Uniwersytet Jagiellonski); Jolanta Maj (Wroclaw University of Technology)
Migrants frequently experience occupational downgrading, a problem that disproportionately affects refugees and undermines labour-market inclusion and long-term skill use. For highly educated refugee women, such mismatch often reflects conditional inclusion into host labour markets, where access to employment comes at the cost of downward mobility. While the drivers of occupational downgrading are partly known, the complex configurations of constraints shaping these trajectories remain underexplored. Addressing this gap, we apply a configurational approach using fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) on data from 54 highly educated Ukrainian refugee women in Poland to identify combinations of demographic characteristics, competencies, and social embeddedness associated with work below or commensurate with qualifications. The findings show that occupational downgrading is driven by age-dependent configurations rather than single disadvantages. Among younger women, downgrading stems from competence-related barriers or care responsibilities combined with temporary migration intentions, whereas for women aged 45 and above, social isolation emerges as the dominant pathway. Qualification-commensurate employment, in turn, depends on access to social networks, language competencies, and future-oriented settlement strategies. By demonstrating how intersecting constraints structure unequal labour-market inclusion, this study advances diversity and inclusion research through a configurational account of refugee women’s occupational trajectories.

Less Structure, More Appeal: Why Job Applicants Prefer Flatter Organizational Hierarchies

DEI
Tamera Shaw (Emory University)
Many large organizations have flattened their hierarchies by reducing managerial layers. Yet research on organizational structure and popular press accounts have largely emphasized the coordination problems and performance risks of flat hierarchies. In this paper, I argue that this focus overlooks an important benefit of flat organizational hierarchies for job seekers. Drawing on Self-Determination Theory, I propose that flatter organizational hierarchies increase organizational attractiveness by signaling autonomy and relatedness, and that these signals operate differently across racial groups. White applicants, who more often expect identity safety in organizational contexts, are especially likely to be responsive to autonomy cues. In contrast, racial minority applicants, who are more attuned to the possibility of identity-based evaluation, are more likely to be responsive to cues that suggest increased relatedness. Across four studies using archival Glassdoor data, surveys, and experiments, flatter hierarchies were generally more attractive, but for different psychological reasons across racial groups. These findings show that organizational hierarchy is a powerful and socially differentiated signal for job seekers.
