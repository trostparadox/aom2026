Less Is More? How Platform-Initiated Governance Reconfigurations Impact Complementor Performance

STR
ANGELA KARR (Seoul National University); Namgyoo Park (Seoul National University)
Rating systems are a central governance mechanism through which platform owners facilitate value-enhancing exchanges between complementors and users by reducing information asymmetry, improving market efficiency, and informally regulating complement quality. However, what happens when an established rating system is abruptly removed by a platform owner? This study investigates how platform owner-initiated de-governance, or the removal of a rating system, impacts complementor performance. Leveraging the Webtoon platform’s abrupt removal of its user-determined rating system, we employ a difference-in-differences design using a novel weekly panel dataset of 2,083 complementors across two web comic platforms (Webtoon and Tapas) from January to November 2025. We find that the removal of ratings is associated with an overall increase in complementor performance, consistent with the idea that de-governance increases strategic uncertainty and reallocates user attention, prompting users to shift from passive filtering based on numerical ratings to the active exploration of content. However, these performance gains are more pronounced for complementors with early lifecycle stage complements, while those offering niche complements benefit significantly less. These findings contribute to platform strategy and the attention-based view by challenging the assumption that more information can drive platform transactions, highlighting instead that uncertainty can reallocate user attention and reshape complementor performance outcomes.

Governing Social Bias in Performance Evaluations on Platforms: Evidence from Ballroom Dancing

STR
Benedikt David Christian Seigner (IE University)
We study how governance shapes social bias in platform evaluations. Using 105,287 scoring decisions from ballroom dance competitions, we exploit governance-imposed differences in evaluation format (solo vs. group dances) and judge assignment (organizer-selected vs. centrally assigned judges). We find a social proximity reward. A one-standard-deviation increase in prior judge-couple co-occurrence corresponds to finishing 0.64 rounds closer to the final. Solo dances reduce this reward by 29%, while central judge assignment eliminates it. By contrast, higher judge expertise amplifies the reward. These results contribute to the platform governance literature by showing that evaluation conditions, rather than evaluator traits, reduce social bias.

Dying for Attention: How Platform Submarket Characteristics Shape Complementor Contributions

STR
Elisabet Garrido Martinez (University of Zaragoza); Eduardo Lope (University of Zaragoza); Juan Maicas-Lopez (CUNEF University)
Platform literature has examined how platform-level characteristics influence complementor product releases. However, platforms are rarely homogeneous, as they consist of distinct submarkets with unique structural conditions that shape competitive dynamics. This study investigates how submarket heterogeneity affects complementor contributions, moving beyond the assumption that platform-level attributes alone drive behavior. Focusing on the Twitch live-streaming platform, where submarkets are delineated by language, we analyze a dataset of 19,601 channels across 30 languages between January 2016 and March 2025. We propose a structural framework centered on three attention-based dimensions: concentration, volatility, and institutionalization. These variables capture the distribution, dynamics, and formalization of attention within specific language segments. Our analysis highlights how local market structures determine the attractiveness and predictability of the environment for complementors. This research advances platform strategy by demonstrating that generativity is contingent upon submarket-level attention structures, offering a more granular understanding of digital ecosystem competition.

Hiring in a Cancel Culture: How Client Organizations Respond to Job Cancellations on Gig Platforms

STR
Erik Kommol (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Joey Van Angeren (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Mark Boons (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Organizations increasingly rely on gig platforms to address their fluctuating labor needs. But, due to the distinct characteristics of gig work, workers generally perceive a relatively low barrier to cancel jobs they had previously agreed to perform. Correspondingly, the rate of job cancellations on gig platforms is notoriously high. In this paper, we study how client organizations adapt their hiring strategies in response to job cancellations. We theorize that client organizations will simultaneously aim to (1) minimize the occurrence of job cancellations and (2) limit the impact of job cancellations. Specifically, client organizations will seek to hire more workers with whom they have had positive experiences in the past (“bridging”), and they will seek to allocate more workers to the same job (“buffering”). Our empirical analyses of 486,346 job postings by 1,896 client organizations on a Dutch gig platform show that client organizations indeed increase their use of both bridging and buffering strategies in response to job cancellations by gig workers. Our results further indicate that especially larger client organizations increase the number of workers they allocate to jobs when confronted with job cancellations. Moreover, when clients experience cancellations on jobs that require more compared to less specialized labor, they are more prone to hire workers with whom they have had positive experiences in the past. Finally, we observe that the timing of job cancellations matters, in that the increase in the use of bridging strategies is stronger in response to cancellations that happen after the cancellation deadline set by the client organization.

Firm Responses to Rivals’ Negative Rating Momentum

STR
Marlon F. R. Alves (SKEMA Business School); Jorge M. T. Carneiro (FGV EAESP); Fernando A R Serra (Ninth of July University)
This study investigates how firms interpret and respond to rivals’ negative rating momentum, sustained downward trajectories in competitors’ online ratings, on review platforms. We focus on whether such digital performance signals lead firms to reinforce their existing product-market positions (exploitation) and whether this response is stronger among high-status firms. We employ a multi-method design in the brewing industry. Study 1 analyzes a comprehensive dataset of over 3 million time-stamped reviews from a leading beer review platform and leverages a staggered event design around firms’ first exposure to rivals’ negative momentum to estimate its effect on existing-market investment, operationalized as intensified product introductions within markets the firm already serves. Study 2 is a tightly controlled scenario-based experiment with 209 senior executives, manipulating rivals’ rating momentum (declining vs. stable) and focal firm status to establish causality and probe the decision mechanism. Across both studies, exposure to rivals’ negative rating momentum increases firms’ exploitation-oriented responses, with the effect amplified by firm status. The experimental evidence indicates that this response is primarily driven by heightened perceptions of relative competitive position, even when focal firms’ absolute performance is held constant. This study advances research on competitive dynamics and status by showing how evaluation trajectories operate as competitive intelligence shaping core-market reinforcement, with high-status firms especially likely to double down on existing markets when rivals decline.
