Guided or Guarded? The Double-Edged Effects of Electronic Performance Monitoring on Well-Being

HR
KENI SONG (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Fabiola Heike Gerpott (WHU - Otto Beisheim School of Management); Ming Guo (Beijing Jiaotong University); Wladislaw Rivkin (Trinity College Dublin, The University of Dublin)
Electronic performance monitoring (EPM) is increasingly a standard human resource management practice in organizations. Anecdotal and empirical evidence reflects the fundamentally ambivalent nature of EPM for employee outcomes. Our study contributes to this field of research by clarifying when and how EPM supports versus undermines employee well-being. Integrating sociotechnical systems theory with the limited strength model of self-control, we conceptualize EPM as a technical subsystem that shapes well-being through two opposing self-control pathways. Specifically, EPM can increase effortful self-control to overcome inner resistance, while simultaneously reducing self-control requirements by decreasing role ambiguity. These processes have distinct implications for workplace anxiety and subjective vitality. Furthermore, we propose that team empowering leadership functions as a critical social subsystem that amplifies the beneficial effects of EPM and mitigates its detrimental consequences. We test our theoretical framework using a multilevel, three-wave survey study of 235 employees nested within 34 teams. Our findings advance a more nuanced understanding of EPM and offer theoretical and practical implications for the sustainable use of EPM in organizations.

Menstruation at Work: Exploring the Role of Workplace Conditions

HR
Anjana Ajith (The University of Queensland and Indian Institute of Technology Delhi Research Academy); Stefan Jooss (The University of Queensland); Daisung Jang (University of Melbourne); Biswajita Parida
Women represent almost half of the global workforce, yet menstruation remains a taboo topic in most organizations. In this qualitative study, we draw on 31 in-depth interviews with menstruators1 in Australia and India to examine how individuals’ menstrual experiences are shaped at work. Utilizing the affect-behavior-cognition framework, we depict how menstruators experience physical and emotional symptoms; how they manage menstruation at work through period disclosures and engagement with colleagues; and what they think about the impact of menstruation on their work. Individuals highlighted the workplace environment, flexible work arrangements, workplace climate, and the availability of healthcare facilities as important working conditions that matter for their menstrual experiences. Our study introduces the concept of decent work to the discourse on menstruation. We go beyond revealing how menstruation impacts work, illustrating how perceptions of workplace conditions influence individuals’ menstrual experiences. By uncovering stigma as a core mechanism shaping this dynamic, we enrich theory and understanding of menstruation at work and in doing so offer an impetus for more inclusive workplaces.

Caregivers and Providers: The Impact of Parent Role Disclosure in Employment Interviews

HR
Elizabeth Eley (Concordia University)
Gender bias in employment interviews is often attributed to discrimination against mothers, rooted in gendered assumptions that women are caregivers and men are breadwinners. However, these assumptions may not reflect the actual roles women and men occupy at home and at work. In this study, we disentangle gender role dynamics by examining how interviewers respond to cues applicants provide about their parental and work roles. We conceptualize gendered home norms (e.g., caregiving vs. providing) and gendered work norms (e.g., communal vs. analytical work) as evaluative schemas that shape how interviewers interpret applicants. Drawing on role congruity theory, we predicted that applicants who disclosed a parental role would receive lower hiring recommendations. We further hypothesized a “double congruence” advantage for applicants aligned with both norms, as well as gender-contingent penalties such that home-norm violations would be more costly for women and work-norm violations more costly for men. We tested these predictions in an experiment manipulating applicants’ disclosures of parental roles. Suprisingly, parental status alone did not disadvantage applicants. Instead, penalties emerged when applicants violated work norms. Although women received higher hiring recommendations overall, these advantages did not translate into higher salary. Together, these findings demonstrate gender bias in interviews while extending RCT to highlight the central role of work-norm violations in shaping evaluations.
