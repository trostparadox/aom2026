Innovation for Biodiversity Through Enskilment: A Practice Approach

ONE
Herman Stål (Umeå University); Pasi Heikkurinen (LUT University); Jarkko Pyysiäinen (University of Helsinki); Jenny Rinkinen (LUT University)
This article offers a practice approach to biodiversity protection, restoration, and regeneration. The approach is illustrated and developed through a comparative case study of two Swedish efforts to reconfigure urban planning so that it promotes restrictions on the use and ownership of cars. Our findings reveal the importance of enskilment as a practice dynamic that ties together practices in need of reconfiguration, capitalizing on the anomalies that arise in practice innovation. Through our approach, we contribute to conversations on biodiversity innovation by stressing that biodiversity is intrinsic to everyday practices.

Corporate Green Investment, Biodiversity Conservation, and Sectoral Carbon Intensity

ONE
Nurlan Orazalin (KIMEP University)
The objective of this study is to empirically examine the relationship between corporate green investment and biodiversity conservation initiatives. Using a sample of U.S. listed firms from 2002 to 2023, the results reveal that green investment is positively associated with biodiversity conservation actions. This positive effect is stronger for firms operating in carbon-intensive sectors. The findings are robust to alternative measures and various endogeneity tests. Heterogeneity analyses further reveal that the positive impact of green investment on biodiversity conservation is more pronounced for firms that have sustainability committees and for firms that have adopted the GRI guidelines. Additional analyses provide evidence that ESG performance positively moderates the relationship between green investment and biodiversity conservation. This study is among the first to uncover the association between green investment and biodiversity conservation in the context of U.S. firms. It extends the environmental strategy, biodiversity risk, and sustainability literature by emphasizing the role of green investments in conserving biodiversity and ecosystems, particularly in carbon-intensive sectors. Overall, the findings of this study contribute to the extant literature and provide valuable implications for corporate leaders, national governments, and regulators aiming to protect ecosystems, conserve biodiversity, and foster sustainable development.

Strategic Leadership of Biodiversity in Companies – A Grounded Maturity Model

ONE
Marja Turunen (University of Turku); Satu Päivi Teerikangas (University of Turku)
While business is a key driver of biodiversity loss, management research and corporate practice have been slow in recognising this crisis. Extant research has focused on incremental corporate means of addressing biodiversity, whether via reporting or measurement. In this paper, we engaged in an explorative, grounded theory research design to appreciate how companies address biodiversity in their business. We focused our analysis on the food sector, which ranks among the sectors contributing most to biodiversity loss worldwide. Our qualitative study consisted in 46 interviews with business leaders from 37 companies representing the Finnish food sector. Our analysis led us to distinguish four archetypal maturity categories regarding the studied companies’ approaches to addressing biodiversity in their business: forerunners, awakened ones, followers and laggards. Companies across these categories differed in their approaches to addressing biodiversity via their leadership and strategic management styles, which we summarize via a grounded maturity model.

Think Outside the Plot: An Alternative Approach to Biodiversity Management

ONE
Frank Figge (ESCP Business School); Andrea Stevenson Thorpe (Universidade Católica Portuguesa, Católica Porto Business School); Jason Good (Aquinas College)
Business management has begun to engage with biodiversity loss as a critical environmental challenge. Currently, an ‘isolated firm-centric’ approach largely dominates, where firms employ activities – and measure the impact of such activities – without taking into account the activities of other firms and their impact on the same. In this conceptual paper, we argue that this is at odds with biodiversity itself, as a phenomenon that cannot be neatly located and attributed to the confines of a singular firm. In response, we develop a ‘landscape-centric’ perspective on biodiversity management. To aid our theory building, we use agent-based modelling where we manipulate the extent to which agents (firms) consider neighbouring plots belonging to other co-located firms. Simulation outputs show: (1) Complete inaction rapidly leads to total biodiversity collapse; (2) an isolated firm-centric approach maintains relatively high local richness of biodiversity but substantially reduces regional richness and spatial differentiation and; (3) when agents (firms) take into account the biodiversity of neighbouring plots, dramatically higher regional richness and spatial differentiation is produced with almost identical levels of local richness – compared to when an isolated firm-centric is employed. These outcomes challenge the utility of an isolated firm-centric approach towards biodiversity management and our novel landscape approach advances theory on how such management may be optimised.
