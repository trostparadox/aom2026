The "Double-Edged Sword" Effect of Algorithmic HRM on Employee Well-Being and Time Theft Behavior

CTO
Yang Liu (Northwestern Polytechnical University); Wang Juan (Northwestern Polytechnical University)
Algorithmic human resource management (algorithmic HRM) has become increasingly prevalent across organizations. Despite its efficiency advantages, concerns have emerged regarding its potential impact on employees’ well-being and performance-related outcomes. Drawing on the stereotype content model, this study examines how algorithmic HRM influences employees’ well-being and time theft behavior. To test this theoretical model, we adopted a mixed-method approach combining an experimental study (Study 1) and a multi-wave field survey (Study 2). The results indicate that algorithmic HRM has an indirect negative effect on employees’ well-being through reduced perceived warmth, while it has an indirect negative effect on their time theft through enhanced perceived competence. However, responsible leadership weakens the negative effect of algorithmic HRM on employees’ perceived warmth, as well as the indirect effect of algorithmic HRM on employees’ well-being via perceived warmth. We also discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings and proposing directions for future research.

Mediated Platform Governance: The Transformative Role of Labor Mediators in the Platform Economy

CTO
Shuang Frost (Aarhus University); Adam K. Frost (University of Southern Denmark); Jack Linzhou Xing (The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology)
Management scholarship often portrays digital platforms as efficient market coordinators that govern labor through direct algorithmic directives, ostensibly rendering traditional intermediaries obsolete. We challenge this dyadic view by arguing that in high-friction institutional environments, the universalizing abstraction of algorithms is structurally insufficient to manage the particularities of situated economic activities. Drawing on a longitudinal ethnography of China’s ride-hailing industry (2014–2024), we show that this limitation necessitated a reliance on labor contract companies (LCCs), local third-party organizations that function not merely as resource aggregators but as constitutive governance agents. To theorize this function, we leverage the sociological distinction between intermediaries, which transport force without modification, and mediators, which actively translate and transform the elements they carry. We posit that third-party mediators repair the structural gap between algorithms and local contexts through three distinct mechanisms: resource mediation (metabolizing socially bound capital into platform-ready supply), signal mediation (refracting abstract digital metrics into affective social discipline), and status mediation (transmuting the institutionally illegitimate labor and capital into compliant corporate fleets). These mechanisms constitute what we call mediated platform governance, a dual-structure configuration in which a platform retains architectural control over digital infrastructures (e.g., pricing algorithms, dispatch logic) while systematically enrolling third-party organizations to exercise situated authority over the workforce. Crucially, we demonstrate that this configuration generates a Janus-faced societal impact: it expands access for marginalized workers, but only by subjecting them to rent extraction, coercive labor intensification, and structural stratification. Ultimately, our findings reveal that global platform expansion depends not on disembedding economic exchange from social structure, but on capturing and repurposing traditional labor infrastructures to sustain the “future of work.”

Algorithmic Management in Gig Versus Non-Gig Settings and the Role of Manager–Worker Dynamics

CTO
Larissa Pomrehn (TU Dortmund University)
Algorithmic management (AM) has transformed how work is organized. While research over the last decade has largely focused on gig work, less is known about AM in non-gig organizations, where managers and established processes may shape worker reactions. Drawing on self-determination theory (SDT), we conduct two studies. Study 1 (n = 418) compares gig and non-gig workers confronted with AM decisions, examining the relationships between AM and autonomy, justice, workplace isolation, and algoactivism. Study 2 (n = 446) focuses on non-gig settings, comparing managers and workers and extending the analysis to role ambiguity, job identity change, and leader–member exchange (LMX). Using multigroup SEM, Study 1 showed that AM increased autonomy for gig workers but reduced it for non-gig workers. For both groups, AM increased perceptions of justice. AM decreased workplace isolation among gig workers but not non-gig workers and increased algoactivism in the latter group. In Study 2, managers reported reduced autonomy and increased role ambiguity and identity change, whereas workers reported no such effects. Instead, workers perceived AM to increase justice, enhance LMX, and reduce workplace isolation. To understand the mechanisms underlying these findings, Study 2 included qualitative open-ended responses, which further strengthen the differences between managers and workers. We contribute to AM research by extending it to non-gig contexts and demonstrating the value of including SDT and manager–worker dynamics to understand AM reactions.

App as Boss: Algorithmic Control and Unequal Capabilities in Bangladesh’s Food-Delivery Sector

CTO
Sharmin Nahar (Lancaster University); Iqbal Hossain Moral; Rebecca Yu Li
Algorithmic management is rapidly expanding across platform work, yet its inequality effects remain under-specified in Global South contexts. Drawing on the capability approach, this paper theorises platform algorithms as a capability-conversion infrastructure that shapes whether riders can translate resources (devices, vehicles, time, digital skills, and local knowledge) into valued functionings such as predictable earnings, safety, skill development, and voice. We conducted comparative, multi-source qualitative fieldwork across two major food-delivery platforms in Bangladesh (Dhaka and Chattogram; January– August 2025), combining interviews with 42 participants (30 riders, 8 platform operations staff, and 4 intermediaries such as restaurant managers), seven ride-alongs, and analysis of platform artefacts (e.g., app interfaces, performance dashboards, training and policy materials). Findings show diverging trajectories within the rider population: some move toward capability-building as evaluation becomes learnable and setbacks recoverable; many remain locked in a metric-survival treadmill where “staying visible” is the central achievement; and others slide into exclusionary dynamics driven by opaque evaluation, rigid thresholds, and weak contestability. The paper contributes a mechanism-based explanation of when algorithmic governance becomes capability-building versus exclusionary and identifies actionable levers—legibility, recoverability, contestability, and training transferability—that shape these outcomes.
