Who Controls the Model? AI Control Rights and Underinvestment

TIM
Shaohui Wang (Georgia State University)
This paper develops a property-rights--theoretic model to study how AI governance structures shape firms’ investment incentives and aggregate welfare. We conceptualize AI governance not as a binary make-or-buy decision, but as an allocation of disaggregated control rights over access, improvement, exclusivity, and forking. In a setting with incomplete contracts, an AI platform and a user firm make relationship-specific investments in AI quality, human capital, and proprietary data, and subsequently bargain over surplus. The model reveals a systematic underinvestment problem under the dominant platform-centric provision mode---standard SaaS or API-based access (Regime~C). When platforms retain residual control over improvement, data-derived value, and technological duplication, user firms anticipate ex post appropriation and weaken their incentives to invest in AI-complementary human capital and data assets. As a result, equilibrium investment levels fall below the first-best benchmark, and total social welfare is not maximized, even when AI technologies are highly augmentation-intensive. We characterize alternative governance regimes---user-controlled contracts, distributed (forkable) deployment, and full integration---and show how reallocating specific control rights mitigates different hold-up channels. In particular, granting users stronger exclusivity or technological independence can partially restore investment incentives, though each regime involves distinct trade-offs between user-side and platform-side underinvestment. To assess the empirical relevance of the theory, we analyze AI-related disclosures from 3482 publicly listed firms’ 10-K filings. Consistent with the model’s predictions, firms describing augmentation-intensive AI applications are significantly more likely to adopt user-centered governance structures and to increase investments in proprietary data assets. Taken together, the findings suggest that the prevailing platform-dominated AI governance model induces socially inefficient underinvestment. The results highlight an important role for public policy and regulation: strengthening enforceable user rights over data, human capital, and AI-generated improvements can alleviate investment distortions and improve welfare in AI-driven economies.

A Systematic Review and Future Agenda of Responsible AI: Based on the CIMO Framework

TIM
Xiaodong Marcus Li (South China University of Technology); Ying Hu (South China University of Technology); Jingxuan Yang (Sun Yat-Sen University); Zhenghao Michael Xia
Responsible artificial intelligence (RAI) has emerged as a central concern for aligning AI-driven innovation with ethical principles, stakeholder trust, and societal well-being. Despite growing scholarly attention, existing RAI research, particularly within the fields of technology and innovation management (TIM) and information systems (IS), remains conceptually fragmented, with limited integration across contexts, practices, and outcomes. To address this gap, this study conducts a comprehensive systematic review using a two-stage methodology. First, a bibliometric analysis provides a quantitative overview of the field’s intellectual landscape, publication dynamics, and thematic patterns. Second, we apply the Context–Intervention–Mechanism–Outcome (CIMO) framework to synthesize qualitative insights from 45 peer-reviewed studies, offering a structured lens to examine how RAI practices unfold across diverse socio-technical environments. The review identifies and categorizes the dominant contextual conditions, intervention strategies, generative mechanisms, and outcomes that collectively shape the effectiveness of RAI implementations. The study contributes threefold: (1) constructing an integrative CIMO-based framework that explains how responsibility is operationalized and sustained in AI-enabled innovation; (2) theorizing six generative mechanisms that elucidate how and why specific RAI interventions yield responsible outcomes across individual, organizational, and societal contexts; and (3) proposing a future research agenda and practical guidance for developers, managers, and policymakers to tailor RAI strategies to contingent organizational and institutional contexts. This synthesis advances theoretical understanding of RAI in TIM and IS and offers actionable pathways to foster sustainable RAI adoption.

Female Executives and AI Washing: Divergence Between Talk and Action

TIM
Yu Gao; Miao Wen Tian (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
With the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) technology, ethical governance of AI has increasingly drawn attention. Meanwhile, managers’ characteristics play an important role in firms’ AI-related decision making. Relative to male executives, female executives differ in external recognition and risk preferences, which may affect the AI-related decision making. Using the data of listed companies in China from 2010 to 2023, this study empirically tests the influence of female executives on AI washing and its contingencies upon internal control and external supervision. Our findings show that female executives significantly promote AI washing, this result holds after a series of robust tests. Moreover, effective internal control and strict external supervision can effectively weaken this positive relationship. Heterogeneity tests reveal that female executives who are younger, highly educated, and without overseas experience are more likely to drive AI washing. At the firm level, this effect is more pronounced in non-state-owned enterprises, firms in growth and maturity stages, as well as non-AI companies. We reveal how the characteristics of female executives influence corporate AI-related decision making, contributing to a broader understanding of corporate decision-making in emerging technology settings.

Innovation as Camouflage: The Impact of Corporate Fraud on AI Innovation

TIM
Zhexiong Tao (University of International Business and Economics); Mingjun Xie (University of International Business and Economics); Qianzhi Wei (Tsinghua University)
This study examines how corporate fraud influences artificial intelligence (AI) innovation. While prior research has examined ethical and neutral factors that promote AI innovation, the impact of corporate unethical factors remains underexplored. Drawing on a perspective of hypocrisy, we argue that fraudulent firms are more motivated than their non-fraudulent counterparts to release deceptive signals through AI innovation during the fraud-committing period. We further propose that such signaling diminishes in contexts where religiosity is high, or when firms have greater prominence and better financial performance, as these conditions reduce their incentives to engage in deceptive signaling. Using a sample of U.S. Russell 3000 firms from 2004 to 2022, we find support for our arguments. Our study contributes to the literature on AI innovation and the false signaling theory by theorizing corporate fraud as an unethical antecedent of firms’ AI innovation and by introducing strategic deceptive innovation signaling as a mechanism that links fraudulent behavior to investment-seeking motives.
