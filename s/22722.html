Due Diligence Policies in Supply Networks: Social Sustainability Incidents and Shareholder Value

OSCM
Christian Wagner (Swiss Federal Institute of Technology Zürich); Christoph G. Schmidt (University of Bath); Stephan M. Wagner; Jens Roehrich (University of Bath)
There is a rise in mandatory due diligence policies (DDPs) that aim to shift the responsibility for social sustainability incidents (SSIs) from suppliers to the focal firm. However, SSIs still occur in global supply networks, raising questions about the effectiveness of DDPS . To examine the mechanisms embedded in DDPs, we adopt a multi-method research design. Study 1 combines a qualitative content analysis of the DDPs of OECD member countries with semi-structured interviews with managers of focal firms subject to DDPs. Findings highlight that DDPs pose a dual legal obligation on focal firms: an obligation of results, making focal firms legally accountable for SSIs in their supply network, and an obligation of means to implement extended governance processes. Access to and influence over the supply networks emerge as key enablers of compliance with the latter. Study 2 conducts an international event study to estimate the financial consequences of SSIs occurring in focal firms’ supply networks. Results show that the adverse impact on shareholder value increases by 272% under DDPs. Without DDPs, accessibility mitigated the financial consequences, while influence amplified them. Under DDPs, these moderating effects disappear, indicating a shift in shareholders' focus from preventive efforts to actual harm. Our findings highlight how DDPs reshape supply network governance and financial accountability, offering critical insights for scholars, managers, and policymakers.

How Contractors’ Resource Dependence Shapes Delivery Reliability in Procurement

OSCM
Alireza Sheikh-Zadeh (Texas Tech University)
Buyers depend on external contractors to deliver critical goods and services on schedule, yet delivery reliability varies widely even under comparable contract terms. Drawing on resource dependence theory, we study how contractors’ upstream financing and buffering choices affect late deliveries, and how these relationships are moderated by contractors’ competitive complexity. We examine two dependence-management levers: reliance on supplier trade credit, which pushes liquidity pressure onto upstream partners, and inventory buffers, which create absorbed slack against upstream uncertainty. We conceptualize competitive complexity as a capability for dynamically orchestrating resources in turbulent environments. Using a unique dataset linking U.S. Defense Logistics Agency contract orders with financial indicators and news-based competitive-action measures for publicly traded contractors, we find that greater use of trade credit is associated with more late deliveries, while larger inventory buffers are associated with fewer; competitive complexity weakens both relationships. The results suggest that efficiency-oriented policies (e.g., stretching payables, running lean inventories) may ease cash constraints but can impair operational performance. Notably, competitive complexity can partially stand in for physical and financial slack via dynamic resource management. These insights inform theory and practice on evaluating and improving contractor reliability in public and other high-stakes procurement settings.

Developing Capabilities for Sustainable Operations: Collaborations for Sharing Similar Resources

OSCM
Lisa Govik (Chalmers University of Technology); Ala Arvidsson (Chalmers University of Technology); Henry Lopez-Vega (Umeå University)
Organizations are transitioning to more sustainable operations, where they embrace new emission-free technologies, such as electromobility. A barrier for the expansion of electric transportation with heavy vehicles is the lack of available and competitive charging infrastructure away from the home base. This research addresses this operational challenge, by investigating how organizations build capabilities to share charging infrastructure to support sustainable transportation. So far, most ecosystem research represents platforms with dedicated orchestrators, we study an emerging leaderless ecosystem for electrical charging. We conducted explorative research, collecting data from multiple organizations, including transport operators, municipalities, OEMs, and energy providers, who are attempting to share charging infrastructure for heavy vehicles. Our main sources of data are from 99 semi-structured interviews with different organizations. This study reveals two sets of activities that form the ecosystem-level innovation capability: (1) intra-organization collaborations and (2) inter-organization collaborations. Also, we found mechanisms of that enables organizations to share in emergent ecosystems.

Blocked Loops: Building Stakeholder Collaboration in Construction Sector Circular Procurement

OSCM
Udathara Damsari Angulugaha Gamage (Monash University); Amrik Sohal (Monash University); Glen Croy (Monash University)
Circular procurement is widely promoted to embed circular economy principles in the built environment, yet progress remains uneven because collaboration across project-based supply chains is difficult to sustain. To explain why stakeholder collaboration breaks down and how it can be strengthened, we use Wood and Gray’s (1991) collaboration theory to bring together barriers that circular procurement studies often treat separately. We adopt an inductive qualitative design, drawing on 21 semi-structured interviews with procurement professionals and circular economy specialists across Australian built-environment supply chains. We found five mutually reinforcing challenges that hinder effective collaboration: governance and coordination breakdowns, knowledge and information uncertainty, capability and operational readiness constraints, economic and commercial feasibility pressures, and behavioural and cultural resistance. Interpreted through collaboration theory, these challenges reflect persistent misalignments across five interdependent collaboration conditions of governance, shared goals, trust, communication, and capacity. Collaboration fails not because actors are unwilling, but because these conditions are difficult to maintain under project temporariness, liability exposure, first-cost procurement logics, and asymmetric risk allocation. Based on participants’ proposals, we synthesise a seven-step strategic process to establish collaboration and integrate the findings into a collaboration-enabled circular procurement framework. The framework makes a theoretical contribution by specifying how bundled interventions reconfigure collaboration conditions and link interventions to collaboration dynamics and circular procurement outcomes.

Relational Paths to Circular Procurement: The Role of Trust and Environmental Uncertainty

OSCM
Domenico Mecca (EADA Business School); Francesco Testa (Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna); Edoardo Quattromini (Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna); Fabio Iraldo (Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna)
Circular economy (CE) transition relies heavily on buyer–supplier collaboration. In uncertain environments, however, firms may place different levels of effort on relational mechanisms to maximize sensing and adaptation to external changes, while limiting dependence. Drawing on relational view theory, this study hypothesizes linear and quadratic effects of relational enablers on companies’ circular procurement routines and examines how these effects are further moderated by environmental uncertainty and goodwill trust. We conducted a questionnaire-based survey of 261 Italian manufacturing firms and empirically tested the hypothesized relationships using hierarchical regression analysis. The results show that knowledge sharing routines with the supplier and effective relational governance significantly enhance firms’ circular procurement, whereas complementary resources and capabilities do not exert a robust effect. Relation-specific asset investments support circular procurement up to a turning point, beyond which additional specificity is associated with lowering effect. Higher trust amplifies this effect, shifting the turning point and delaying the onset of negative returns. This study offers nuanced insights into the effectiveness of relational enablers for firms transitioning toward CE in uncertain times. The study provides both fresh theoretical insight and practical guidance for managers leading circular transition through supply chain collaboration.
