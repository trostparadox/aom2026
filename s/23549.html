Session overview

These papers examine how racial inequality is produced and reproduced through organizational structures, legitimacy judgments, and political processes. The session brings together work on categorization threat, governance responses to racialized bias, organizational politics, and broader theories of marginalization. Together, the papers advance understanding of how race shapes power, opportunity, and institutional outcomes in organizations.

Identity-Consciousness Induces Categorization Threat in Weakly Identified Racial Minorities

DEI
Charles Chu (Boston University); Naomi Fa-Kaji (University of Virginia)
The prevailing consensus is that, due to concerns about one’s racial group being devalued, racial minorities prefer and benefit from identity-conscious (versus identity-blind) organizational diversity ideologies because identity-conscious messaging signals that one’s racial group is respected and valued. However, this work emphasizes assuaging such a devaluation threat as racial minorities’ primary identity-based concern. Challenging this primacy of devaluation processes in the study of racial minorities’ experiences of organizational diversity ideologies, this article introduces the concept of categorization threat (i.e., the concern that one is being viewed in terms of their racial category membership rather than being seen as a unique individual). Specifically, we propose that for weakly, as opposed to strongly, identified racial minorities, for whom race is less central to their sense of self, organizations that espouse identity-conscious (versus identity-blind) diversity ideologies will evoke more categorization threat, which will in turn reduce their attraction to organizations that espouse such ideologies. We find evidence for this hypothesis in three pre-registered studies sampling racial minority participants (i.e., Asian, Black, and Hispanic/Latino Americans) using an ecologically valid set of real-world organizational diversity statements from top Fortune 25 firms (Study 1), and a controlled manipulation of identity-conscious (versus identity-blind) organizational diversity ideologies (Studies 2).

The Legitimacy Tax: How Racialized Bias Reshapes Corporate Governance

DEI
Anthony Kim (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Sellam Birhane (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
This paper examines how institutional anti-Black bias shapes corporate governance decisions. We develop the concept of compensatory signaling: firms’ use of governance mechanisms—specifically board composition and executive compensation—to manage legitimacy threats imposed by biased external audiences, rather than responding to actual performance deficits. Using hand-coded data on TMT racial composition for S&P 500 firms linked to board structure and CEO compensation (2007–2019), we find that before the Black Lives Matter (BLM) movement, firms with Black executives were significantly more likely to appoint additional independent directors and to award above-market CEO compensation. These patterns reflect boards’ attempts to counter unfounded stakeholder skepticism about Black executives by signaling heightened oversight and organizational confidence. Critically, these relationships attenuate substantially after BLM, indicating that as institutional norms shifted to affirm racial inclusion, the perceived need for such compensatory governance responses diminished. Our findings demonstrate that Black executive representation operates as a contingent source of legitimacy, not because of inherent deficits in Black leaders, but because anti-Black bias distorts how external evaluators interpret diverse leadership. By documenting how systemic racism in corporate evaluation drives governance choices, this study brings racialized bias to the center of corporate governance theory and reveals the burden placed on Black executives through seemingly neutral governance mechanisms.

Undervalued But Useful: Gendered Gatekeeping and the Hidden Value of Rejected Female-Led Patents

DEI
Zitai Hu (Renmin University of China); Wenlong He (Renmin University of China)
Despite enhancing firm innovation, female inventors face examination biases that hinder their acquisition of patent rights. This study explores how firms can capture the value of female-led inventions regardless of the undervaluation by institutional gatekeeping. Using a sample of 292,359 invention patent applications from 1,086 Chinese firms (2009–2019) and leveraging the Long and Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network for gender identification, we find that female-led inventions are systematically undervalued by the patent system in Chinese context, as evidenced by their lower grant rates, yet their rejected invention applications contribute more to subsequent innovation—both internally and externally—than those led by male inventors. Moreover, firms with higher female representation more effectively internalize knowledge from these rejected inventions, thereby mitigating external spillovers. We advance the literature by identifying value realization pathways independent of formal protection, exposing the dissonance between institutional evaluation and intrinsic value, and reconceptualizing gender diversity as a governance mechanism for knowledge appropriation.

Intersectionality and Work for Marginalized Populations

DEI
SM Musa (Singapore Management University)
Work is often suggested to be a refuge for marginalized people: a source of dignity, stability, and belonging in the face of exclusion. Yet it can also be a site where vulnerability is reproduced and intensified. To explain this tension, we develop a multi-level, processual theory that explains how intersectional vulnerability, organizational recognition, and agency constitute one another in shaping marginalized individuals’ experiences of work. Conceptualizing intersectional vulnerability as a relational and experienced condition, we present organizations as recognition systems that mediate how marginalized individuals experience work. We show that, because marginalized individuals enter organizations with differing levels of vulnerability and encounter recognition systems that vary in robustness, work-as-refuge does not take a single form. Instead, distinct configurations of vulnerability and recognition give rise to four qualitatively different forms of work-as-refuge: protected, conditional, ambivalent, and precarious. Building on this typology, we reconceptualize agency as constrained and configurational, theorizing four patterns of job crafting through which marginalized individuals navigate vulnerability and negotiate recognition. By linking macro-level structures of marginalization with meso-level organizational processes and micro-level adaptive action, we offer a new foundation for theorizing marginalization in organizational life. Key Words: Marginalization, Work-as-Refuge, Intersectional Vulnerability, Organization as Recognition Systems, Job Crafting.

Who Gets to Play the Game? A Theory of Racialized Organizational Politics

DEI
Anthony D. Meyers (Baruch College, CUNY); Ryan Scott Teschner (Baruch College, CUNY); Tsedale M. Melaku (Baruch College, CUNY)
Organizational politics is typically theorized as race-neutral, despite growing evidence that race shapes access to influence, legitimacy, and reward. We develop a theory of racialized organizational politics that explains how racialized institutional logics shape motivation, ability, and opportunity, how individuals translate these conditions into political behavior, and how racialized legitimacy judgments filter the returns to that behavior. By integrating organizational politics, the motivation–ability–opportunity framework, and the theory of racialized organizations, we reconceptualize politics as a central mechanism through which racial inequality is produced and contested in organizational life.
