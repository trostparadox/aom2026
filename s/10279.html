When Work Autonomy Attracts Applicants—and When It Doesn’t: Evidence From a Job-Posting Experiment

Friday, Jul 31 2026 · 1:00PM–2:00PM
Pennsylvania Convention Center — Poster Wall 2
HR

People

Author: Jiaqi Li UC Berkeley, Haas School of Business

Session overview

Work autonomy is widely valued, yet little research examines how its different dimensions shape job choice. Using a survey experiment with 1,021 Prolific participants, this study separates work autonomy into scheduling autonomy (temporal/locational flexibility) and substantive autonomy (freedom over projects/workflow) and tests their effects on job interest. Participants viewed job postings emphasizing scheduling autonomy, substantive autonomy, or neither, then rated their interest. Results show a clear divergence: scheduling autonomy increases job interest by 6.7%, while substantive autonomy has no effect. Lower-income participants value scheduling autonomy more, with each $10k income decrease linked to 1.7% greater interest; income does not moderate substantive autonomy preferences. These findings contribute to labor market sorting scholarship by showing that work autonomy’s attractiveness depends on the dimension emphasized and the socioeconomic status of applicants. Practically, these findings suggest that organizations should rethink how they signal autonomy and tailor those messages to target applicants.
