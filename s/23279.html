Cuckoo in the Nest: A Performance Feedback Explanation of Mission Drift in Social Ventures

ENT
William C Zhou (Sacred Heart University)
This paper provides a novel perspective for understanding mission drift in social ventures. We propose that social ventures initiate problemistic search based on performance feedback on two conflicting goals: the economic goal and the social goal. We find that social ventures increase interest rates when economic performance is below aspirations, but decrease interest rates when social performance is below aspirations. We also find that the negative relationship between social performance below aspirations and the interest rate becomes less salient when economic performance is also below aspirations, leading to mission drift.

Mission Drift Across the Investment Lifecycle: A Systematic Review

ENT
Elizaveta Petrovskaia (University of Iceland); Magnus Thor Torfason (University of Iceland); Kai N. Hockerts (Copenhagen Business School)
Mission drift, the gradual reorientation of a social enterprise away from its original social purpose, remains a critical challenge for hybrid organizations balancing social and commercial logics. This systematic literature review synthesizes N = 92 peer-reviewed articles published through December 2025 to clarify how mission drift and impact fidelity are conceptualized, what drives misalignment, and which mechanisms safeguard mission integrity over time. Following PRISMA 2020 procedures, the synthesis identifies three interlocking themes: identity-based foundations of mission fidelity, stakeholder and institutional pressures shaping prioritization, and governance arrangements that mitigate or intensify drift. Integrating these streams, the review develops a multilevel framework explaining how identity, stakeholder relations, and governance jointly condition mission adherence across growth and investment stages. The review also highlights methodological constraints in current evidence and outlines a future research agenda focused on longitudinal dynamics, measurement development, and governance innovations relevant to scaling and impact investing.

Mission Drift, Moral Legitimacy and Adaptation in For-Profit Social Ventures

ENT
Adam Clark (Chapman University)
For-profit social ventures must adapt their strategies, products, and revenue models to survive, yet such adaptations frequently trigger accusations of mission drift. Prior research explains how hybrids establish and protect their mission, but offers limited theory of how adaptation itself becomes morally contested. We address this gap by conceptualizing mission drift as a breach of a prosocial contract—the shared understanding that binds a venture’s profit-seeking activities to specific moral obligations. Because stakeholders cannot directly observe intentions or continuously monitor tradeoffs between social and commercial goals, adaptations are interpreted through the lens of perceived contract violation. We develop a theory of prosocial contract renegotiation that explains when and how ventures can revise commitments without forfeiting legitimacy. Successful renegotiation hinges on three mechanisms: morally justifying the revision of the ventures obligations, authorizing change through stakeholder engagement, and restoring credibility that revised commitments will bind through governance and verification structures. This framework shifts mission drift from an internal “balancing” problem to an audience-evaluation process, explaining when adaptation in for-profit social ventures is condemned as breach or accepted as legitimate renegotiation.

Moral Value Trade-Offs and Decision Difficulty in Social Enterprises

ENT
Theodor Lucian Vladasel; Mircea Epure (Pompeu Fabra University)
How do people cognitively process trade-offs between competing social and economic values in social enterprise contexts? Building on protected value literature, we argue that individuals with stronger commitment to social values engage in longer deliberation when making such trade-offs. This is especially true when decisions involve larger transgressions of social values, regardless of the final choice. We test these predictions in an experiment with varying monetary rewards in fictional social enterprises and measure the time subjects take to make an effort allocation choice between commercial and social tasks. Individuals with stronger commitment to social values take longer to decide, but we only find weak evidence of longer decision times for higher-powered incentives. These responses are concentrated among those most socially motivated and women. Social values in social enterprises need not be strictly protected, with implications for these hybrids' strategic choices.

Staying True by Changing Course: Mission Evolution in Social Enterprises

ENT
Aswathi Narikundil Jayan (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore); Srivardhini K. Jha (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore)
Mission is the raison d’être of social enterprises. Despite its central importance, research on social enterprises has predominantly conceptualized mission in static and dichotomous terms, focusing on mission drift versus mission alignment. In doing so, it has neglected the subjective, negotiated, and evolving processes through which mission is constructed and enacted, thereby undertheorizing the concept. This study addresses this gap by exploring mission evolution, defined as a process through which social enterprises adjust, reinterpret, or rebalance their missions while maintaining mission integrity. Drawing on an inductive multiple-case study of Indian social enterprises, the study illuminates five distinct pathways through which social enterprises enact mission evolution: mission reorientation, mission expansion, mission slicing, mission sidestep, and mission recalibration. This nuanced and malleable view of mission and its evolution offers a vocabulary that better reflects how social enterprises navigate change while staying rooted in their core purpose. Specifically, the findings demonstrate how mission evolution broadens the range of ways in which actors negotiate tensions between competing institutional logics in hybrid ventures.
