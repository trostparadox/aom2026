Watching Oneself Too Closely: Why Workplace Impostor Thoughts Lead to Work Procrastination Behavior

OB
Yue Zhao (Nankai University); Qinghong Yuan
Workplace impostor thoughts are pervasive in contemporary organizations but little is known about how these thoughts may undermine employees’ self-regulation in their daily work. Drawing on ego depletion theory, this study examines how workplace impostor thoughts influence work procrastination behavior through self-monitoring. We propose that, driven by fear of being exposed as incompetent, impostor thoughts heighten employees’ self-monitoring, which depletes self-control resources and subsequently increases work procrastination. Moreover, we identify the level of workplace anxiety as a critical psychological context that shapes the extent to which impostor thoughts translate into self-monitoring and downstream procrastination. Using a three-wave survey design, we find that workplace impostor thoughts are positively related to work procrastination behavior. Self-monitoring mediates this relationship and exhibits a suppressing effect. In addition, workplace anxiety strengthens the effect of workplace impostor thoughts on self-monitoring and the indirect effect on work procrastination via self-monitoring. This study contributes to research on workplace impostor thoughts by highlighting the conditions under which self-monitoring consumes self-control resources and by extending understanding of how impostor thoughts shape maladaptive work behaviors, with implications for theory and practice.
