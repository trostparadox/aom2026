When Expertise Becomes Hybrid: Reorganizing Professional Knowledge and Authority in the Age of AI

OMT
Jennifer Ling Bagdasarian (Johns Hopkins University); Elizabeth Goodrick (Florida Atlantic University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly embedded in professional work, yet we know little about how it reshapes professional knowledge once adoption moves beyond implementation. Drawing on a qualitative study of clinicians using an AI-based dermatology tool, this paper examines how AI reconfigures professional knowledge control at the field level. We show that AI does not displace professional judgment or erode professional authority. Instead, it reorganizes how knowledge is exercised, who can legitimately act with expertise, and how professional roles and relationships are structured. Our findings identify three interrelated mechanisms through which AI reshapes professional knowledge control: (1) reshaping professional expertise through hybrid judgment, as professionals increasingly exercise judgment in interaction with AI-supported assessments; (2) expanding access to professional expertise by loosening the tight coupling between expertise and credentialed roles; and (3) reorganizing professional boundaries and relationships by altering patterns of coordination, referral, and oversight across professional roles. Together, these mechanisms show how AI contributes to gradual institutional change through everyday professional practice. By highlighting how AI transforms professional institutions from within, this study extends research on professions and institutional change beyond accounts of task automation or technological disruption.

When Supplemental Matters: How Automation of Supplemental Work Disrupts Professional Core Expertise

OMT
Farnam Mohebi (UC Berkeley, Haas School of Business)
Organizations are increasingly deploying artificial intelligence (AI) to automate supplemental (non-core) professional tasks, promising to free experts to focus on high-value core work. This optimization logic assumes that eliminating non-core tasks enhances professional effectiveness and efficiency by allowing experts to concentrate on activities where their expertise matters most. Yet we know remarkably little about what supplemental work actually contributes to professional expertise. Through an 18-month ethnographic study of radiation oncologists integrating AI-assisted contouring technology, I reveal two mechanisms through which supplemental work enables core professional expertise. In radiation oncology, clinicians must delineate (contour) tumors for treatment on patient imaging while also contouring nearby normal organs to protect them from radiation in the planning. Physicians describe this non-tumor organ contouring as "boring," repetitive, and time-consuming– supplemental work that distracts from their core task of tumor identification and treatment planning and they are willing to automate it. However, I observed that engagement with low-stakes supplemental work creates cognitive space for diagnostic insight: the low intensity of contouring normal organs paradoxically enables clinicians to notice tumor anomalies and diagnostic patterns impossible to detect under the tunnel vision of high-stakes tumor delineation. Second, manual supplemental work expands therapeutic and action repertoires: extended engagement with patient anatomy deepens understanding of treatment options, while normal organs become sites of creative action– the perceived "boring" nature of this work paradoxically enables clinicians to experiment and refine techniques without high-stakes pressure. When automation removed manual organ contouring, both mechanisms disappeared–not because the technology failed, but because it succeeded in eliminating supplemental work that had quietly enabled professional expertise enactment. These findings challenge prevailing assumptions about professional work hierarchies and automation benefits, revealing that what appears supplemental from a task-output perspective may be essential from an expertise-enactment perspective. Rather than simply freeing experts for higher-value work, automating supplemental tasks can inadvertently sever connections between technical practice and professional capability enactments.

Mind the Drift: How Domain Experts Articulate Model Fluctuations Through Model Work

OMT
Pernille Berg Lassen (Aalborg University); Kasper Elmholdt (Aalborg University)
Organizations that use machine learning (ML) models in their decision-making need to continuously monitor these models to prevent unwanted model drift and ensure satisfactory performance. Yet doing so is challenging because fluctuations in model performance are embedded in domain experts' everyday activities, making it difficult for ML experts to detect them independently. This raises the question: How do organizations keep ML models on track? We investigate this question through a two-year ethnographic study of financial fraud detection and prevention in a Danish public authority and observe how domain experts perform what we call “model work.” We understand model work as articulations of model fluctuations that reveal model drift and identify salient technological blind spots. In this paper, we examine the interaction between ML and domain experts to theorize the work they perform in detecting model deviations, calibrating model performance, and formulating model potentialities. Specifically, we show the central role of a stratified group of domain experts engaged in model work and how they articulate problems arising from both within the domain and from the technology. Building on this, the paper offers two contributions. First, by showing how a stratified group of domain experts articulates model flaws, we deepen the understanding of domain expertise in the context of ML technologies. Second, by focusing on ongoing model work, we explain how ML technologies become moving targets. Indeed, ML models evolve and expand as human experts work to keep them on track.

Getting Comfortable with Technology: Maintaining, Reconstituting and Reconfiguring Professional Work

OMT
Ian Rodgers (University of Oxford)
Digitalization in professional service firms (PSFs) is expected to shift value from human expertise to technological capital. Yet within the same firm, some technologies are excluded, others are integrated into existing workflows, and a few transform professional work. How do professionals sort digital technologies – including AI ​– into these divergent trajectories, and with what implications for PSF digitalization? Drawing on 53 interviews in an elite UK law firm, I analyze technology use at the level of specific applications and use cases. I identify an iterative process of “getting comfortable” through which professionals establish (or refuse) digital technology’s warrantability for client work. This process comprises four practices: assessing practical benefits for a use case, evaluating fit with professional identity and expertise, validating through trial use under work pressures, and reflecting on continuing appropriateness as work and technologies evolve. These practices sort technologies into three trajectories of professional work: maintaining work practices by excluding or minimizing technology; reconstituting work practices by using technology in existing workflows while preserving identity and expertise; and reconfiguring work practices by integrating technology into substantively reorganized workflows. The study contributes to research on digitalization and professional work by specifying a work-level mechanism – “getting comfortable” – that explains heterogeneity in technology integration and what this means for PSF digitalization.

Agentic Boundary Work in Human-AI Assemblages: A Study in Journalistic Media

OMT
Jarno Pasonen (LUT University, LUT Business School); Paavo Ritala (LUT University); Päivi Maijanen; Mika Ruokonen
The introduction of novel agentic technologies into organizational settings with strong professional norms and ethics, such as journalism, challenges the perceived boundaries of agency. Drawing on empirical data from four Finnish media organizations, we show how the introduction of generative AI into professional work settings initially created ambiguity about the agentic boundaries within emerging human-AI assemblages. Then, we demonstrate how this ambiguity has triggered a sense of urgency to resolve the boundaries between humans and AI, leading to two types of agentic boundary perceptions: human-agentic superiority and machine-agentic incapacity, which together clarify the reconfigured roles between humans and technology. We also show how these perceptions led to agentic boundary work practices, in which human-AI boundaries are either guarded and novel reconfigurations are restricted within a disempowering assemblage, or aligned, leading to an empowering assemblage with new configurations and relations. Based on these findings, we theorize a grounded model of agentic boundary work that explains the conditions under which agentic-boundary ambiguity gets resolved and how human-AI assemblages are reconfigured to accommodate the introduction of an agentic technology into organizational settings. We also contribute to the literature and practice of journalism by unpacking the core agency struggles, attributions, and resolutions that journalistic knowledge workers face in the context of generative AI.
