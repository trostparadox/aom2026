From Diversity to De-Nationalism: Impact of TMT Gender Diversity on Corporate Rhetorical Nationalism

SIM
Tailun Chen (Zhejiang University); Jingwen Li (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Nationalism is making a resurgence in a more bottom-up and radical manner, with corporations emerging as central actors in this process. Corporate rhetorical nationalism, as the most prevalent form of organizational nationalism, has been shown to substantially impact corporate legitimacy. To address the current research gap in the micro-level determinants of organizational nationalism, this study examines the impact of gender diversity in top management teams on corporate rhetorical nationalism from the perspective of corporate managers. Drawing on gender role congruity theory and signaling theory, we utilize data from Chinese listed companies between 2013 and 2022 to demonstrate that TMT gender diversity exerts a suppressive effect on corporate rhetorical nationalism. The reason lies in the conflict between the agentic characteristics inherent in corporate rhetoric nationalism and the communal tendencies of TMTs with higher gender diversity. Signaling such masculinity is more likely to be perceived as a deviation from gender norms. However, we also found that the presence of negative media coverage tends to weaken this negative influence. This study advances the current understanding of the nature of organizational nationalism by examining the interaction between gender and nationalism.

The Dual-Interest (Particular and Primary) of the Purposeful Real Entity

SIM
Emiliano Di Carlo
This article advances the corporate purpose debate by proposing a dual-interest theoretical framework grounded in the conception of the firm as a purposeful real entity. While contemporary accounts argue that purposeful firms should “profitably solve the problems of people and the planet, rather than profit from creating problems,” (British Academy, 2019; Mayer, 2023) they lack operational guidance on how to resolve governance dilemmas when profitability conflicts with social problem-solving. This article argues that this tension stems from an incomplete account of the structure of corporate interests. Rather than multiplying objectives or simply shifting priority from shareholders to stakeholders, it is necessary to clarify in what sense the firm itself can be said to have interests. The article develops a dual-interest framework that distinguishes analytically between the Particular Interest of the Firm (PARTIF) and the Primary Interest of the Firm (PIF). The PARTIF refers to the objective and non-discretionary conditions of continuity of the firm, namely the maintenance over time of profitability, solidity, and liquidity. The PIF concerns the legitimate pursuit of corporate purpose and PARTIF within the boundaries established by legal and ethical norms. This dual-interest architecture provides a normative filter for evaluating decisions that involve trade-offs among stakeholder claims, limiting managerial discretion and reducing the risk of purpose-based agency problems. By offering a structured decision criterion and clarifying directors’ fiduciary priorities, the framework may contribute to a more resilient and accountable form of capitalism capable of integrating corporate continuity with responsible value creation.

Shadow Institutional Work and Moral Residues Under Conditions of Institutional Unreliability

SIM
Divya Tyagi (Institute of Management Technology, Ghaziabad, India); Mudit Shukla (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore); Sreelekha Mishra (Indian Institute of Management Bodh Gaya); Sushanta Kumar Mishra; Mit Vachhrajani (Indian Institute of Management Indore); Himanshu Rai (Indian Institute of Management Indore)
Institutional research has emphasized how institutions stabilize social life by coordinating action and allocating responsibility through formal authority and established roles. Less attention has been paid to situations in which institutions remain formally authoritative yet are experienced as difficult to rely upon by those who depend on them. This study examines how dependent actors navigate such institutional arrangements and how responsibility is lived under conditions of institutional unreliability. Drawing on qualitative interviews with 60 family members who lost loved ones during the COVID-19 pandemic in India, we analyze engagement with healthcare, administrative, transport, and ritual institutions that remained formally intact but variably reliable in practice. Our analysis highlights two interrelated dynamics. First, families engaged in shadow institutional work, i.e., necessity-driven efforts through which dependent actors temporarily substitute for institutional functions in order to make institutional arrangements workable without altering institutional authority. Second, responsibility assumed through these efforts generated moral residues, as obligations taken on under constraint continued to shape moral evaluation and self-understanding over time. By foregrounding the standpoint of dependent actors, this study distinguishes institutional authority from institutional reliability and shows how institutions may remain stable while redistributing responsibility onto those least positioned to influence them. In doing so, it extends institutional theory and related research on work, inequality, and dignity by illuminating how institutional arrangements are lived and carried forward under conditions of dependence.

Responsible Leadership Under Identity Constraints: Public Trust and Investment Transformation

SIM
Steve Michael Loren
Organizations that occupy central societal roles—such as public pension funds—face increasing pressure to respond to grand challenges while preserving public trust and legitimacy. Such pressures often require substantial changes in organizational practices that risk undermining core identity commitments. This study examines how responsible leadership enables organizations to navigate this tension. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative case study of a large global pension fund confronting demographic and financial shocks, the study analyzes how a newly appointed Chief Investment Officer implemented mandated changes to investment practices without redefining the organization’s core identity as a prudent, long-term public steward. The findings identify three mechanisms through which responsible leadership preserved organizational identity under identity-threatening conditions: (1) constructing responsible markets, (2) embedding accountability through incentive alignment, and (3) engaging in collective legitimation through thought leadership. By translating societal responsibility into material practices, governance arrangements, and market interventions, responsible leadership stabilized legitimacy while enabling substantial practice change. The study contributes to research on responsible management by advancing a material and processual account of responsible leadership, theorizing organizational identity maintenance as the preservation of a societal role, and illuminating how business actors can respond to grand challenges while sustaining public trust.

Income Inequality, Social Capital, and Organizational Misconduct in the U.S. Trucking Industry

SIM
Horacio Enrique Rousseau (Florida State University); Sebastian Garcia-Dastugue (Florida International University); Cuneyt Eroglu (Northeastern University)
Strain theory explains organizational misconduct as a response to strains—such as unmet goals, resource loss, or adverse conditions—that generate negative emotions and prompt rule-breaking when legitimate coping options are lacking. We extend strain theory to a community-embedded perspective linking local socioeconomic conditions to misconduct. We argue that community income inequality functions as a contextual amplifier of strain by heightening feelings of relative deprivation, unfairness, and competitive intensity, motivating deviant behavior as a coping mechanism. Conversely, community social capital, i.e., trust-based networks that enable reciprocity and collective action, curbs misconduct directly and attenuates the effects of inequality by reinforcing shared norms and providing coping resources. We expect this attenuation to be weaker for publicly traded firms, given their lower local embeddedness and higher external scrutiny. We test these expectations using a panel of 10,556 U.S. motor carriers (2013–2019) and link inspection violations to county-level data. Linear mixed models show that a one-standard-deviation increase in inequality raises violations by 3.5 percent; this effect is substantially weaker in high-social-capital counties. Attenuation is weaker for publicly traded and geographically dispersed carriers. Our findings position strain theory within community contexts, showing how inequality, social capital, and ownership jointly shape organizational misconduct.
