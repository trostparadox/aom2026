Building Informal Authority in Standard-Setting Organizations: The Rapporteur Selection in 3GPP

TIM
Jing-Ming Shiu (National Cheng Kung University); Masanori Yasumoto (Yokohama National University); Mark P. Dallas (Union College); Jing-Zhi Chen (National Cheng Kung University)
When firms serve as rapporteurs in a standard-setting organization, they exercise informal authority by coordinating and leading other firms in developing technical specifications. In 3GPP(Third Generation Partnership Project), becoming a rapporteur requires receiving support from at least four other firms. This study examines how interfirm support relationships within 3GPP shape the selection of rapporteurs. Our findings indicate that the more a firm supports other firms, the more it can generate a legitimacy effect, thereby increasing its subsequent opportunities to serve as a rapporteur. Likewise, the more a firm is supported by other firms, the more it can generate an indispensability effect, which also increases its subsequent opportunities to serve as a rapporteur. However, when a firm becomes highly supported and thus attains stronger indispensability, the legitimacy benefit of supporting others exhibits diminishing returns and may even reverse. The primary contributions of this study are to clarify how interfirm support in standard-setting organizations affect rapporteur appointments and to address the relative paucity of research on informal authority in the literature on meta-organizations.

How Public Disclosure of Patent Information Promotes The Re-Coupling of Innovation

TIM
Yuan Liu (Peking University)
This paper examines innovation decoupling, defined as the gap between publicly disclosed innovation information and firms’ actual research progress. It argues that the gap arises through two distinct routes. The first is protective decoupling, in which firms disclose only what is necessary for compliance while withholding substantive technical content to safeguard core knowledge. The second is symbolic decoupling, in which firms amplify innovation signals to satisfy short term capital market expectations while real research inputs and outputs lag behind. The paper proposes that a two way supervision regime can narrow this gap by strengthening mandatory disclosure and by improving enforceable protection against pre grant infringement. It uses the 2000 American Inventors Protection Act as a quasi natural experiment and combines analyst report based measures of perceived innovation with firm level research spending and patent outcomes. The results show a decline in both forms of decoupling after the reform and a clearer alignment between market perceived innovation and realized innovation performance. The study connects institutional decoupling research with the innovation disclosure literature and offers a governance framework and causal evidence on the consequences of innovation decoupling.

Patent Information Disclosure and Market Reactions: No Elty vs Potential Impact

TIM
Jianwei Dang; Kazuyuki Motohashi; Qiuhan Zhao (Tongji University)
We examine how financial markets value patented innovation across disclosure stages in the pharmaceutical sector. Using U.S. patents from publicly listed firms, we construct text-based measures of technological novelty and early diffusion impact. Event-study regressions based on KPSS returns reveal that market reactions are already sizable at the stage of scientific publication for patent-paper pairs (PPPs), and are nearly as large at pre-grant publication as at patent grant—challenging the grant-centric view of patent valuation. Stage-specific regressions show a marked shift in valuation logic: novelty is priced early, especially when peer-reviewed science certifies the invention, while impact becomes salient only once technical content and downstream reuse become observable at the patent publication or grant stage. In PPPs, novelty is strongly rewarded in early stages, but impact is not, suggesting that science-linked inventions follow a distinct valuation channel. These patterns are robust to science linkage definitions and alternative measures. Our findings highlight that both when and what gets disclosed jointly shape the financial value of innovation.

Mitigating the Novelty Penalty: The Role of Certification in Knowledge Diffusion

TIM
Shashi Kant Kumawat; Mukund Chari (University of Colorado-Boulder)
Technological novelty drives breakthrough value but creates systematic barriers to diffusion. While existing research documents this "novelty penalty" as a consequence of evaluator uncertainty and cognitive friction, theory remains underdeveloped regarding the institutional mechanisms that might serve as antidotes. We develop a contingent theory where certification confers technological legitimacy, specifically addressing the evaluative frictions impeding novel knowledge flows. We theorize that this legitimating effect is proportional to the uncertainty it resolves, predicting that certification is most valuable for the most novel inventions. Testing this framework using 40,108 patent dyads from U.S. Department of Defense SBIR awardees and a rigorous within-citer matched-pair design, we find robust evidence for a "certification premium." More importantly, we find a significant positive interaction: the acceleration effect of certification amplifies as technological novelty increases. This study demonstrates that the novelty penalty is not an immutable market failure but a conditional outcome manageable through strategic institutional signaling.

Secrecy, Spectrum, and Certification: Evidence from Wireless Electronic Devices

TIM
Ruby Zhang (Harvard University); Roberto Fontana (University of Pavia); Shane Greenstein (Harvard University); Do Yoon Kim (Boston College)
This paper examines the strategic decisions of firms to withhold information in innovation-intensive markets, highlighting that secrecy contains distinct temporal dimensions. The study illustrates the thesis using wireless products, the majority of which innovate by utilizing unlicensed spectrum. Using all FCC equipment filings from 2001 to 2021, we analyze firms’ choices between long-term confidentiality (LTC), which permanently conceals technical information such as block diagrams, and short-term confidentiality (STC), which temporarily delays the public release of marketing information, including user manuals. We argue that the former acts to preserve “trade secrets,” the traditional role of IPR. In contrast, the latter acts as “commercialization insurance,” a previously unrecognized role for secrecy to ensure against launch and coordination risks in the commercialization process. We examine evidence consistent with this view. We find that the determinants for LTC and STC differ sharply. LTC use reflects appropriability concerns and institutional complexities— technically complex products and competitive markets are 12 to 35% more likely to adopt it. Traditional forms of IPR, such as patenting, are a substitute for LTC, especially in the case of WiFi. By contrast, STC is driven by the commercialization timing of branded products by large firms. Large, domestic, patent-owning firms with regulatorily complex products are more likely to use STC. Technical aspects of the product do not predict STC usage. Usage is consistently higher among incumbents for STC, while it quickly converges for LTC, even after controlling for all other variables. Together, these results show that permanent and temporary confidentiality serve distinct strategic purposes.
