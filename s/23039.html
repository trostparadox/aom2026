DARK SIDE CASE - Flo Health: Deceptive Data Practices in Digital Reproductive Health

CMS
Nicole M Perrone (Wilfrid Laurier University); Prescott C. Ensign (Wilfrid Laurier University)
Flo Health, Inc., developer of the widely used Flo Period & Ovulation Tracker, positioned itself as a trusted resource for individuals seeking to manage menstrual and reproductive health. With over 100 million downloads and millions of daily active users, Flo promoted itself as a confidential, secure, and medically informed platform that enabled users to log intimate details such as period dates, mood symptoms, sexual activity, fertility windows, and pregnancy progression. Its marketing emphasized privacy, stating that users’ health data would remain confidential and would be used only to enhance the app’s functionality.However, an investigation by the United States Federal Trade Commission (FTC) found that Flo shared sensitive reproductive health information with third-party analytics and marketing companies despite explicit assurances to the contrary. These disclosures were transmitted through embedded software development kits (SDKs) from partners including Facebook, Google, AppsFlyer, Flurry, and Fabric. The FTC claimed that Flo misrepresented its data practices, deceived consumers about the confidentiality of their health information, and violated Section 5 of the FTC Act (15 U.S.C. § 45), which prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in commerce. This case study examines Flo Heath, Inc.’s conduct during this period, the findings of the FTC investigation, and the ethical issues that arise when companies handling sensitive health data fail to meet stated commitments. The broader FemTech industry context is noted to illustrate the environment in which the misconduct occurs.

DARK SIDE CASE: They Just Wanna Have Fun?

CMS
Jussara Pereira (UNIVERSIDADE FEDERAL DE MINAS GERAIS - UFMG); Alice Oleto (Fundação Dom Cabral); Carlos Eduardo de Lima; Erica Souza Siqueira (FGV EAESP)
Switzerland, North Macedonia and Brazil what those countries have in common? Despite their differences in culture, economy, and geography, all three nations have witnessed tragedies in their recent history: fires breaking out in spaces of leisure, music, and youth celebration. The 2026 Le Constellation fire in Switzerland, the 2025 Pulse nightclub fire in Kocani, North Macedonia, and the 2013 Kiss Nightclub fire in Brazil all expose a chilling commonality: young lives lost during what should have been moments of joy and connection. These incidents raise urgent and unsettling questions: Are our young people truly safe when they seek leisure? What responsibilities do states, organizations, and managers hold in ensuring that moments of fun do not turn into fatal tragedies? This teaching case focuses on the Kiss Nightclub fire, a tragic event that resulted in the death of 242 people and leaved 636 survivors. The case offers students an opportunity to critically analyze issues related to managerial responsibility, corporate social irresponsibility, public policy, and human rights. By applying a critical management framework as theory, students are encouraged to reflect on how managerial decisions, driven primarily by economic imperatives, can lead to disastrous consequences when ethical considerations and safety standards are neglected. This case is suited for courses in corporate social responsibility, ethical leadership, and disaster management. Students are challenged to consider proactive measures for creating organizational cultures centered on ethics and safety, ensuring that business decisions prioritize the well-being of people and communities.

DARK SIDE CASE: Grengine: Scaling Justice in an Unequal System

CMS
Jennifer Cherneski (NAIT)
This case examines the experiences of Connie Stacey, founder and CEO of Grengine, a Canadian clean energy technology company developing advanced battery systems for off-grid and industrial use. While Grengine’s technology aligns with global climate and sustainability goals, the company struggles to access growth capital. The case exposes how venture capital, impact investing, and public funding ecosystems, ostensibly meritocratic, reproduce gendered and structural inequities, forcing a justice-oriented founder to navigate power negotiations as she grapples with scaling growth while participating in systems that reproduced exclusion. Written from a critical organizational perspective, this story invites students to interrogate how capital markets discipline founders, normalize exclusion, and frame discrimination as “risk.”
