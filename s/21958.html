Beyond Gender: How Caregiving and Ideal Worker Norms Shape Work–Family Conflict and Well-Being

Authors: Miguel Mejicano Quinta; Linda Duxbury Carleton University

Session overview

Work–family conflict remains a central concern in organizational behavior, particularly in demanding and hierarchical environments where expectations of constant availability intensify strain. This study examines how caregiving responsibilities, gender, and perceptions of organizational culture jointly shape work–family dynamics and well-being within the Canadian Armed Forces, an extreme-case context characterized by high operational demands and rigid structures. Using data from a national survey of 1,627 CAF members and employing Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling with Multi-Group Analysis, we assess how role overload predicts work–family interference and perceived stress across gender–caregiving subgroups. Results show that work and family role overload consistently contribute to both work-to-family and family-to-work interference, but the consequences for well-being differ markedly by subgroup. Female caregivers reported the highest stress levels, with both directions of interference emerging as significant predictors. Mediation patterns also varied: work-to-family interference partially explained the link between work role overload and stress for caregivers, while family-to-work interference mediated the effects of role overload only for female caregivers. Perceptions of ideal worker norms further moderated several relationships in subgroup-specific and sometimes counterintuitive ways, buffering strain for female caregivers in some cases while amplifying it for male caregivers and male non-caregivers in others. These findings underscore the importance of considering the intersection of gender, caregiving status, and organizational culture when studying work–family conflict and employee well-being. The study highlights the need for more nuanced theorizing and for policies that address how ideal worker expectations disproportionately burden caregivers in high-demand institutional settings.
