The Curious Case of the Nepo Baby: Legacy Premium, Humility, and Legitimacy Judgements Across Gender

Authors: Sonia Ghumman University of Hawaii at Mänoa; Marjan Houshmand University of Hawaii at Mänoa; Mahboubeh Shirmohammadlou University of Hawaii at Mänoa

Session overview

How do third-party observers evaluate beneficiaries of inherited advantage often labeled “nepo babies,” and when can humility messaging mitigate perceived nepotism and bolster legitimacy? Although organizations with inherited leadership structures are very common (e.g., family businesses, political dynasties, legacy careers), such forms of nepotism have rarely been examined from the perspective of third-party observers. Drawing on research on privilege, organizational justice, and socioeconomic inequality, we examine how inherited advantage shapes judgments of legitimacy, fairness, and merit. We also investigate how nepo babies communicate their advantage through humility messaging and how observers interpret such signals, including whether these evaluations differ by target gender. Using Hollywood as a cultural backdrop, we conducted a between-subject vignette-based experiment in which participants evaluated actors who were either nepo babies or self-made, with target gender and humility messaging being experimentally manipulated. Our results show that nepo babies were evaluated less favorably than their self-made counterparts. Humility messaging among nepo babies yielded inconsistent effects, with no clear advantage to acknowledging or downplaying privilege. Moreover, female self-made targets were evaluated more positively on several key outcomes than other conditions, and male nepo babies who acknowledged their privilege were often among the most negatively evaluated targets, revealing pronounced gender differences in evaluations of inherited advantage. These findings show how third-party observers evaluate nepo babies and their signaling of inherited advantage, extending critiques of meritocratic narratives surrounding nepotism, social mobility, and unequal opportunity.
