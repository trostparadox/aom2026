The Politics of Ownership: How Institutional Investors Shape Corporate Political Activity

STR
Kartik Rao (Trent University); Tianjiao Xu (Tilburg University); Nahyun Kim (Tilburg University)
We study how institutional investors shape corporate political activity (CPA) by examining how differences in investors’ political engagement affect firms. We argue that variation in institutional investors’ political activity reflects their disposition towards CPA and serves as a cue to managers about investors’ preferences. We predict that politically active investors will increase firms’ CPA by legitimizing the strategic value of political action, improving oversight, and sharing expertise, thereby lowering the risks of political involvement. We further propose two moderating conditions that strengthen this influence: i) investors’ concentration of holdings in the focal industry, which enhances information, monitoring and coordination capacity; and ii) the intensity of regulation in the industry, which increases the salience and payoffs of political engagement. Using panel data on U.S. firms that were constituents of the S&P 500 index between 2000 and 2020, we find strong support for our predictions. Our study contributes new insights by reframing investors as political actors whose heterogeneous engagement shapes firms’ political actions.

Hidden Governance? Overlapping Institutional Ownership and Supplier CSI

STR
Wenxin Wu (Tongji University)
Addressing corporate social irresponsibility (CSI) in supply chains remains a critical and unresolved sustainability challenge. Existing research has primarily focused on direct and buyer-driven governance mechanisms, overlooking indirect forms of governance embedded within supply chain networks. This study examines how overlapping institutional ownership between suppliers and their buyers serves as an indirect and invisible form of network-based governance that reduces supplier CSI. The governance effect is amplified under conditions that heighten the need for external governance, such as misaligned sustainability orientations or heightened buyer dependence. It is more effective for financially material CSI, which attracts greater investor attention, and is stronger when overlapping investors have long-term horizons. This study advances understanding of CSI governance by revealing how ownership-based interconnections foster social accountability across supply chains.

Too Costly to Govern? Executive Risk Perceptions and Avoidance of Politically Active Partners

STR
Mirzokhidjon Abdurakhmonov (University of Nebraska-Lincoln); Elle Hyunjung Yoon (Iowa State University)
Firms often form alliances to reduce uncertainty, yet partnerships with politically active firms can create new risks. Politically active partners can provide policy access and influence, but their value can disappear quickly after elections, investigations, or scandals. Their political activity is also difficult to evaluate through standard performance metrics and requires ongoing monitoring and interpretation. We argue that these governance demands matter most when executives perceive elevated firm risk. Integrating resource dependence theory with the attention based view of the firm, we theorize that perceived risk narrows executive attention toward immediate operational threats, making politically active partners less attractive because the cognitive costs of governing them exceed available attention. We further argue that this avoidance depends on conditions shaping attention capacity. The negative relationship strengthens when focal firms already lobby intensively, because internal political engagement draws from the same limited pool of nonmarket attention needed to govern a politically active partner. In contrast, the relationship weakens when firms are innovation intensive, since routines for learning and regulatory engagement help maintain broader attentional scope under risk. It also weakens when firms are market dominant and when industries are concentrated, because specialized resources and information spillovers reduce monitoring burdens. We test these arguments using alliance decisions by S&P 1500 firms from 2002 to 2020. Our study identifies a cognitive boundary condition for resource dependence logic, clarifies when politically active partners become strategic liabilities, and extends attention based theory to interorganizational partner selection.

Foreign Institutional Investors and Workplace Safety: A Stakeholder Stewardship Perspective

STR
Dennis Wajda (Coastal Carolina University); Dhvani Badwaik (University of Rhode Island); Mohammad Jasim Uddin
Workplace accidents remain a pervasive yet unevenly governed ethical problem, especially where labor protections are weak and enforcement is inconsistent. We examine whether, and under what conditions, foreign institutional investors (FIIs) act as stakeholder stewards who help protect workers’ safety. Building on instrumental stakeholder theory, we argue that FIIs face distinctive monitoring incentives arising from global reputational spillovers, home-country ESG mandates, and structural independence from local business networks, which together can strengthen board oversight and operational risk management. Using a longitudinal dataset of 1,020 listed firms across 49 countries from 2004 to 2016, we find that higher FII ownership is associated with fewer reported workplace accidents. This association is stronger when foreign ownership is more concentrated—enabling FIIs to overcome collective-action constraints—and when host-country employee protections are weaker, consistent with a substitution logic in which private monitoring partially fills institutional voids. The results are robust across alternative specifications, reweighting, timing and falsification tests, and an instrumental-variables specification. Our findings contribute to business-ethics, governance, and comparative-institutional research by reframing FIIs as potential co-governors of workplace ethics.

When it Counts, Do Directors Back CSR? Career Concerns, Multi-Directorship, and Value-Relevant CSR

STR
Yongtae Kim (Santa Clara University); Erik Wang (Lingnan University); Adele Xing (Santa Clara University)
Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) has mixed performance implications, in part because it aggregates activities that differ in their relevance to shareholder value. We distinguish between value relevant and value irrelevant CSR and examine how directors shape these choices. We argue that directors’ career concerns drive a shift toward value-relevant CSR and away from value-irrelevant CSR. To identify this causal effect, we exploit merger shocks that exogenously reduce directors’ outside board appointments, thereby heightening their career concerns tied to the focal firm. Following these shocks, focal firms increase value-relevant CSR and reduce value-irrelevant CSR, with stronger effects when affected directors serve on CSR committees and when they are insiders. As a result, directors associated with these shifts subsequently receive greater shareholder support in director elections.
