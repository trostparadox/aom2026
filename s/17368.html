Session overview

Online communities (e.g., Q&A forums, open-source software ecosystems, social platforms) have become a durable, high-impact organizational form through which firms, markets, and civil society solve problems, develop expertise, mobilize collective effort, and negotiate norms at scale (e.g., Faraj, Jarvenpaa, & Majchrzak, 2011; Safadi, Skousen, & Karahanna, 2025). Online communities offer alternative solutions to core problems of organizing – communication (how does information flow, and who sees what, when), collaboration (how work is decomposed and matched to expertise), and control (how authority, status, and sanctions are distributed) – via sociotechnical structures rather than formal hierarchy (Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Massa & O’Mahony, 2021). Online community researchers have robustly demonstrated that communities’ impact (e.g., learning, coordination, influence, and the diffusion of beneficial vs. harmful knowledge) is mediated through communities’ architectures that classify, connect, provide feedback, and render content visible (Aaltonen & Lanzara, 2015). However, contemporary research suggests that the “rules of the game” inside online communities are shifting in ways that open (and close) new theoretical and empirical opportunities. For example, generative artificial intelligence is increasingly embedded in online interactions and is reshaping who participates and how discourse travels (Boussioux, Lane, Zhang, Jacimovic, & Lakhani, 2024; Safadi, Lalor, & Berente, 2024). Moreover, platform governance is trending toward tighter boundary management. Governance and regulation debates in platform ecosystems increasingly turn on data access and platform power, making “who can see and reuse what data” a practical constraint on research design and a core determinant of dynamics and welfare within online communities (Krämer & Shekhar, 2025). At the same time, many impactful communities are migrating to, or simply building atop, semi-private and decentralized spaces (e.g., IRC, Signal, Telegram, Discord) where interaction is encrypted, invite-gated, and organized through servers/channels rather than fully public feeds (Massa & O’Mahony, 2021). This rapidly changing empirical terrain raises a pressing agenda for theory: research on sociotechnical structures (classification and boundary architecture, network mixing, feedback exposure, visibility signals, and temporal coordination) needs tighter integration to explain how online communities accomplish communication, collaboration, and control under shifting conditions. We propose this symposium as a catalyst for this integration, organizing the conversation around classification and connection, feedback and visibility regimes, and temporal coordination mechanisms as interlocking levers that jointly shape learning, influence, and the distribution of beneficial and harmful knowledge online.
