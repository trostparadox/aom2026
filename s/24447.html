Bearing Consumer Responsibility: CSR Implementation and Disclosure Under Asymmetric Information

OSCM
Ying Zhou (Central South University); Hai Li (Central South University)
As global corporate social responsibility (CSR) standards tighten and stakeholder expectations rise, implementing and disclosing CSR across supply chains has become a central concern. Within a delegation framework, we examine how CSR policies differ depending on whether the manufacturer or the retailer internalizes consumer surplus in its objective. Our main findings are as follows: (1) CSR information is disclosed to consumers only when the CSR level exceeds a certain threshold. (2) The optimal CSR level under centralized decision-making coincides with that under decentralization when the retailer assumes CSR responsibilities. Interestingly, if the retailer commits to and discloses a high CSR level, decentralized decision-making generates higher profits than centralized decision-making by enabling higher per-unit margins. (3) Manufacturers consistently choose higher CSR levels than retailers, even when consumer social awareness is low or market transparency is limited. This finding underscores the stronger intrinsic CSR incentives of upstream firms, which are notably weaker for retailers. (4) Retailers achieve higher profits when manufacturers assume CSR responsibilities, reflecting a preference for responsibility shifting. Furthermore, assigning CSR to manufacturers can yield Pareto improvements for both parties under conditions of moderate consumer social awareness and limited information transparency. Importantly, assigning CSR responsibilities to manufacturers is optimal from both consumer surplus and social welfare perspectives.
