How Family Involvement Shapes Entrepreneurial Language Expression

ENT
Shane Reid (Texas State University); Nathan Hayes (Louisiana State University); Tyge Payne (Oklahoma State University)
Publicly traded family firms face persistent scrutiny from external audiences, making strategic communication essential for managing legitimacy and stakeholder impression. Blending research on strategic communication and impression management with insights on socioemotional wealth preservation, this study examines how family firms use entrepreneurial language in shareholder letters. Using computer-aided text analysis of S&P 500 firms, we compare entrepreneurial expression in family and nonfamily firms and examine how variation in family ownership, governance involvement, and generational stage shapes entrepreneurial language among family firms. The findings show that, on average, family firms express lower levels of entrepreneurial language than nonfamily firms, but that entrepreneurial expression increases as families become more directly involved, particularly through governance rather than ownership alone. In addition, the association between family involvement and entrepreneurial language weakens in later-generation firms. Taken together, the results highlight entrepreneurial language as a distinct communicative mechanism through which family firms navigate capital market expectations while protecting family-centered priorities.

State-Owned Equity and Innovation Efficiency in Family Firms: An Institutional Logics Perspectives

ENT
Yingxian Zeng (Jinan University); Wenping Ye (Jinan University); Xiaotong Li (Sun Yat-Sen University); Xinchun Li (Sun Yat-Sen University)
As a key indicator measuring firms’ ability to allocate and transform innovation resources, innovation efficiency is particularly crucial for family firms with constrained resource endowments. This study focuses on the impact of state-owned equity on the innovation efficiency of family firms. It analyzes three mechanisms: management experience and monitoring constraints, technical talent attraction, and information resource advantages. Its potential contributions lie in revealing the "black box" of how state capital affects family firms’ innovation efficiency, deepening the understanding of the contextual dependence of family firms’ innovation behavior from the heterogeneous factor of state ownership, and providing theoretical and practical references for family firms to enhance innovation transformation capabilities by leveraging external institutional resources.

Structure Without Voice: Board Independence in Chinese Family Firms (WITHDRAWN)

ENT
Zhihao Ren (IE Business School); Anabel Mendoza López (Carlos III University of Madrid); Cristina Cruz
This study examines how controlling ownership shapes structural and behavioural board independence in Chinese listed firms. We theorize and find evidence of structural–behavioural decoupling: While family-owned firms appoint a higher proportion of independent directors than state-owned enterprises (SOEs), independent directors in family firms are less likely to express dissent. We explain this paradox through three mechanisms: legitimacy signalling, which motivates family firms to structurally over-comply with governance norms, and relational sanctioning and reputational containment, which jointly constrain behavioural independence. Drawing on regulatory data on board proposals and independent director dissent, our findings advance a behavioural theory of corporate governance by showing how institutional and relational dynamics jointly shape directors’ oversight behaviour and the realization of board independence.

The Impact of Nonfamily Structural Power Concentration on Innovation Radicalness in Family Firms

ENT
Qiuyue Lyu (Zhejiang University); Junsheng Dou
This study draws on upper echelons theory to examine how power distribution within the top management team (TMT), along with internal relational dynamics, influences innovation outcomes in family firms. Specifically, it theorizes and empirically investigates how the structural power concentration of nonfamily managers within the TMT affects innovation radicalness, and how different types of TMT faultlines—relationship-related faultline strength, task-related faultline strength, and family-nonfamily faultline size—moderate this relationship. The results show that nonfamily structural power concentration significantly enhances innovation radicalness. Task-related faultline strength enhances this relationship by facilitating information processing and resource integration, while the family-nonfamily faultline size weakens it by reinforcing identity boundaries. These findings are based on a sample of 2,218 Chinese listed family firms from 2009 to 2023.

The Role of Ownership Team Structure in Driving Innovation in Small Firms

ENT
Jordi Geeraerts (KU Leuven); Elisabeth Mueller (IESEG School of Management)
Innovation is vital for the ongoing competitiveness of small firms. This study examines how ownership team structure, specifically, the presence and ownership share of owner-managers, affects the probability of firms to innovate. Drawing on the judgment-based approach, we argue that owner-managers, who combine ownership and managerial roles, are particularly effective at supporting innovation in young firms due to the combination of decision-making power and deep knowledge about the firm. Using longitudinal data from 43,206 small, privately owned German firms (2007–2021), we find a positive relationship between the presence of owner-managers and the innovation propensity in young firms, but their impact diminishes as the firms mature. Having an owner-manager with majority ownership is especially beneficial for innovation due to unconstrained decision making. Our findings underscore the importance of choosing the ownership structure of small firms in accordance with their ambition to innovate.
