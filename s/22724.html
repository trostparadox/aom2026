To Escalate or Not to Escalate: Formal Processes, Tacit Knowledge, and Ambiguous Supplier Misconduct

OSCM
Emily C. Dickey (The University of Tennessee-Knoxville); Prisca Brosi; Jan C. Fransoo (Tilburg University)
Organizations rely heavily on formal compliance systems to govern supplier behavior, yet procurement managers frequently confront ambiguous signals of potential misconduct that fall outside clearly defined violations. In such cases, escalation requires interpretive judgment rather than rule-based verification. Drawing on qualitative interviews with senior procurement professionals, we theorize escalation under ambiguity as a weak-signal decision problem shaped by both governance structures and experiential expertise. The interviews highlight tacit knowledge—developed through firsthand supplier exposure, pattern recognition, and intuition—as central to recognizing and acting on subtle anomalies that are not captured in audit documentation. We test these insights in a vignette-based experiment that manipulates formal processes (strict vs. loose) and tacit knowledge (low vs. high) within an ambiguous supplier audit scenario. Results show that tacit knowledge significantly increases managers’ likelihood of escalating concerns under ambiguity, while formal process strictness does not exert a direct effect on escalation and does not condition the influence of tacit knowledge. These findings advance behavioral supply chain management by demonstrating that escalation under ambiguity is driven primarily by experiential sensemaking rather than procedural framing, highlighting the limits of formal compliance systems and the critical role of tacit expertise in ethical supplier oversight.

Configurations of Buyer–Supplier AI Orientations and Firm Resilience: A Matching Perspective

OSCM
Yan Liu (Xi'an Jiaotong University); Qiang Wang (Xi'an Jiaotong University)
While Artificial Intelligence (AI) is recognized as a key enabler of firm resilience, its effectiveness often depends on the alignment between supply chain partners rather than individual firm efforts. Drawing on matching theory, this study examines how configurations of AI capability orientations—automation and smartness—within buyer–supplier dyads influence buyer firm resilience. We employ an large language model (LLM)-based approach to quantify AI orientations using a sample of 3,181 Chinese listed dyads from 2018 to 2022. The results demonstrate that resilience is bolstered by complementary configurations (buyer automation/supplier smartness and vice versa) or by compatibility in automation. Crucially, we find that a dual emphasis on smartness can be counterproductive, weakening buyer resilience. By shifting the focus from firm-centric AI adoption to dyadic alignment, this study provides a strategic roadmap for firms to navigate technological synergies and frictions in volatile markets.

Understanding and Addressing Constraints in Supply Chains

OSCM
Geoffrey Featherstone (The University of Melbourne); Mehrdokht Pournader (University of Melbourne); Russell George Thompson (The University of Melbourne)
Supply chains have historically faced, and are increasingly facing, various operational challenges that constrain their operations. Constraints in supply chain interfaces (i.e., across modes, actors and nodes) have rarely been the subject of in-depth empirical investigation, particularly in understanding their emergence and propagation within supply chains. Our study employs an abductive qualitative methodology, based on 33 in-depth interviews (972 coded quotes) with consultants, managers, and executives of an intermodal logistics supply chain in Australia, along with relevant archival data, to develop a typology of constraints at supply chain interfaces. We identify constraints in supply chains belonging to three primary categories: structural constraint, behavioural misalignment, and temporal misalignment. We also identify constraints that can cascade and/or fracture within supply chains as they flow across interfaces. We present the proposed Constraints Interfaces Typology, based on all emerging categories of constraints within the intermodal supply chain, and outline directions for future research and practice.

Crisis-Driven Regulatory Interventions and Buyer–Supplier Conflict in Global Supply Chains

OSCM
Xiaoyang Deng (Wuhan University); Tao Wang (Wuhan University); Yongqing Ye (Wuhan University)
This study investigates how crisis-driven regulatory interventions—specifically COVID-19 lockdowns—impact conflict between international buyers and suppliers. Drawing from institutional theory and supply chain crisis management frameworks, we argue that provisional but stringent regulations can act as stabilizing forces, reducing uncertainty and enhancing coordination in global supply chains. Using longitudinal survey data from 196 Chinese manufacturer-foreign buyer relationships across 36 countries, supplemented by archival data, the findings show that stricter lockdowns reduce buyer-supplier conflict. This effect is more pronounced under two specific conditions. First, it intensifies in host countries characterized by high governmental efficiency or limited dependence on foreign trade. Second, it becomes stronger when firms engage in frequent transactional activities. The study contributes to institutional theory by highlighting the role of provisional regulations in crisis contexts and offers insights into conflict mitigation in international business.
