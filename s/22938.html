Algorithmic Hollowing of Responsibility

OMT
Aybike Mergen (Ozyegin University); Nergiz Çetin-Kiliç (Özyegin University); Mustafa Ozbilgin
Algorithmic Hollowing of Responsibility: How Deterritorialization, Reterritorialization, And Machinic Capture Transform Organizational Accountability How does algorithmic organizing transform accountability? Through comparative case analysis of the UK Post Office Horizon scandal and the AI-driven hiring discrimination (Mobley v. Workday, Inc.), we develop a process model theorizing accountability transformation through Deleuzian assemblage theory. We identify three mechanisms that operate recursively to reconfigure accountability from a moral-relational practice grounded in identifiable human judgment to a technocratic regime embedded within opaque socio-technical assemblages: (i) deterritorialization of responsibility; (ii) reterritorialization through algorithmic legitimation, and (iii) machinic capture. We extend Deleuzian assemblage theory by theorizing machinic capture as a third mechanism necessary for the stabilization of technocratic accountability regimes. Our analysis reveals that these mechanisms create self-reinforcing cycles: each enables and strengthens the others, explaining both the remarkable stability of technocratic accountability and the extraordinary institutional mobilization required to disrupt it. We contribute by extending assemblage theory to organizational accountability, revealing recursive dynamics that create path dependencies, and reconceptualizing accountability as qualitatively transformed rather than merely reduced.

From Technologies of Rationality to Technologies of Meaning

OMT
Irene Vega-Ramon (LUT University, LUT Business School); Letizia Mortara (University of Cambridge); Paavo Ritala (LUT University)
What are we really talking – and theorizing – about when we view Artificial Intelligence (AI) as a technology which emulates, replicates, or even surpasses human intelligence? In this study, we theorize AI using the cognitive categorization literature as a theoretical resource to explain how the introduction of emerging AI technologies at work shift the embedded ways through which human work is perceived and organized around. We identify a gradual categorical shift in the correspondence between humans and artificial intelligence. Two grounding features characterize how the organization and management field thinks and theorizes the unique feature of humans (and AI) at work: rationality – at the core of the faculty of reason – and, more recently, language – at the core of the faculty of meaning. Accordingly, we document how AI’s theorizing at work was initially driven by focusing on rationality traits, resulting in a categorization of what March names “technology of rationality”. However, more recently, the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) at work has increased the focus on language and the resulting category of meaning associated with AI, which we distinguish as “technologies of meaning” in contrast to technologies of rationality. These emerging technological categories enact different organizational realities and present opportunities or threats to aspects of work previously associated only with humans.

Coordinating Knowledge Work in the Era of AI: The Effect of Epistemic Hybrids on Project Performance

OMT
Jeffrey Hans Peo (University of Oxford)
Using data from 1,331 consulting engagement from a global consultancy, this study addresses the question of whether epistemic hybrids – those with formal training in both professional and data science domains – generate superior performance in project work that applies artificial intelligence to complex professional problems. I find that while such epistemic hybrids are associated with superior performance, the influence is partially mediated by fostering ensuring collaborative relationships. Exploring this mechanism in more depth, I show that epistemic hybrids may act as both attractors and generators of familiarity amongst project team members. Further, I differentiate between productive and relational familiarity and show that only epistemic hybrids generate a productive familiarity associated with superior project performance.

When Artificial Intelligence Meets Organizational Intelligence

OMT
Mayur Joshi (Telfer School of Management, University of Ottawa); Mark J. Zbaracki (Western University)
Why does the promise of predictive artificial intelligence (AI) so often diverge from its organizational reality? Prior research alternately treats AI as a tool that augments decision making or as an agentic technology capable of autonomous discovery, yet struggles to explain how these views play out in practice. Drawing on a longitudinal ethnography of predictive AI initiatives in two large Indian banks, we examine how predictive AI is organized amid competing expectations for what AI is and should do. We reconceptualize AI integration as a problem of assemblage formation rather than technology adoption. We show how organizational actors assemble configurations of humans (and their expectations), data, algorithms, and models that enact distinct orientations toward AI, and how these assemblages collide and are reworked over time. Rather than converging on a single configuration, AI initiatives stabilize through partial and uneven reassemblies in which selected data-driven capabilities are absorbed into durable procedural and monitoring infrastructures. By unpacking the internal composition and biographic trajectories of AI assemblages, our study explains why predictive AI can be simultaneously transformative and disappointing, and advances an assemblage-based account of AI integration centered on organizational intelligence and organizing processes.

When AI Supervises and is Supervised: The Algorithmic Sandwich Model of Work

OMT
Kazuki Kozuka (Kyoto University)
This study theoretically and empirically examines how performance and well-being diverge in workplaces where the same employee is simultaneously subject to algorithmic evaluation and supervision (AI superior) and verification and accountability for generative AI output (AI subordinate). Existing algorithmic management research has primarily assumed unidirectional governance (people as the evaluated party), while human-AI collaboration research has treated AI as a supporting actor. However, the intersecting situations within the same individual have not been adequately theorized. This study defines the simultaneous existence of upward algorithmic evaluation pressure (AM_SUP) and downward generative AI responsibility (AM_SUB) as verification burden, and presents the Algorithmic Sandwich Model (ASM), in which outcomes diverge constructively through role conflict and cognitive exhaustion. Based on self-determination theory and configuration theory, we applied fsQCA to data from a simulated work experiment involving 40 participants. Results showed that high performance and low well-being are not symmetrical but arise from different configurations of conditions. In particular, a configuration in which AM_SUP and AM_SUB are simultaneously high and autonomy, fairness, role order, and cognitive resources are disrupted is linked to a consistent decline in both outcomes. Algorithmic literacy is not a resource that relieves pressure, but rather functions as a branching condition that reorganizes the pathway by which the burden of verification spreads to psychological breakdown. This research redefines the success or failure of AI adoption as a matter of the configuration design of evaluation and verification responsibility rather than technological selection, and provides a theoretical foundation for expanding algorithmic governance to a two-way model.
