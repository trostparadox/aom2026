Emerging Together: How Brand Collaborations Give Rise to Proto-Categories

OMT
Lok Ting Chan (National Taiwan University); Jade Lo (Drexel University); Robert Jung (Drexel University); Hsueh-Liang Wu
How do inter-organizational collaborations shape the emergence of new market categories? While prior research has shed light on how individual organizations influence categorical meanings, we know less about how inter-organizational collaborations, especially across distinct industries, can generate new categories. Focusing on brand collaborations as a distinctive form of inter-organizational collaboration, we theorize how firms may bundle previously unconnected identities, thereby offering audiences new interpretive combinations that may serve as proto-categories, or the earliest stage of categorization grounded in exemplars. Conceptualizing brand collaborations as relational infrastructures for meaning-making, we argue that identity distance between collaborators has an inverted-U-shaped relationship with proto-category emergence, and that this relationship is moderated by the consistency and diversity of collaborative efforts over time. Using data on 515 co-branded collaborations in and around the luxury fashion industry covering approximately three decades, the analysis supports our theoretical predictions. Our study contributes to research on category dynamics by foregrounding producer– producer relationships as generative sites of categorical innovation. It also bridges the literatures on strategic alliances and market categorization, showing that brand collaborations not only facilitate resource exchange but can also shape the evolution of market categories.

Under the Same Roof, Yet Standing Out: Performance Outcomes in the Hollywood Industry

OMT
Manimozhi RAMACHANDIRAN (NEOMA Business School); Fabio Fonti (NEOMA Business School); Margherita Corina (NEOMA Business School)
Categorization theory posits that products spanning multiple categories suffer performance disadvantages due to ambiguity or audience confusion. Hence, hybrid products’ persistent emergence and occasional success remain a theoretical puzzle. This study addresses it by examining how the distinctiveness of hybrid products, measured relative to similar category-bundled peers—a conceptually coherent set of category combinations—influences market performance. Drawing on optimal distinctiveness theory, we posit a U-shaped relationship between a hybrid product's distinctiveness and market performance. We further theorize that this U-shaped effect becomes more pronounced in low-density markets (i.e., markets with fewer hybrid products within the same category combinations), where audience expectations are less rigid and hybrid products are more salient. Conversely, in high-density markets, the effect is likely to weaken due to greater audience exposure to diverse content, which reduces penalties for moderate distinctiveness but also tempers the benefits of novelty. Analyses using data from 4,826 Hollywood films released between 2000 and 2019 support these hypotheses. This research advances categorization theory by showing that hybrid product success hinges not only on category membership, but also on achieving optimal levels of distinctiveness—either through conformity or meaningful differentiation—within coherent bundles. It also highlights how market structure shapes the performance outcomes of hybrid distinctiveness in cultural industries.

Governing by Thresholds: Audit Cutoffs and Boundary Maintenance in Charities

OMT
Felix Irresberger (Durham University Business School); Ruomei Yang (University of Liverpool)
Organizations are increasingly governed through categorical accountability states triggered by numerical cutoffs. We examine this phenomenon in the context of charities in England and Wales, where statutory audit requirements apply once reported gross income exceeds £1,000,000, while charities below the threshold may rely on independent examination. We show that charities' income distribution exhibits distinct bunching below this threshold, emerging only after a reform raised the income-only audit requirement from £500,000 to £1,000,000. Charities audited regardless of income, under an asset-based rule, display no bunching behavior at the threshold, indicating that reactivity is concentrated where crossing the threshold changes governance state. This form of 'boundary maintenance' produces observable accounting patterns: unrestricted reserves jump discontinuously at £1,000,000, reflecting timing and recognition choices within the reporting infrastructure. By locating boundary maintenance in the production of a consequential accounting number, the study extends reactivity and categorization research to statutory governance thresholds and clarifies when such boundaries induce strategic, rule-conforming adaptation. From our empirical results, we develop a theory of reflexive governance gates: statutory thresholds linked to self-produced accounting metrics that switch organizations between distinct oversight regimes (e.g., audit versus independent examination) and, in doing so, feed back into how the triggering metric is assembled.

When Compartmentalization Fails: Legitimacy Polarity Traps and Categorical Imperatives

OMT
Alexandru Roman (University of Maine)
Institutional fields increasingly confront organizations with demands that are not merely conflicting but mutually exclusive. In some settings, organizations experience rapid and symmetric condemnation from opposing stakeholders despite extensive efforts to separate practices across units, time, or audiences. We explain this pattern by theorizing legitimacy polarity traps—field conditions under which compartmentalization becomes structurally unavailable, independent of organizational capability. Legitimacy polarity traps emerge when stakeholder categorical cognition is activated by the interaction of three field-level features: mutually exclusive demands, bilateral enforcement balance, and high practice visibility. When these features intersect with salient practice identities, stakeholders abandon unit-level evaluation and instead categorize organizations holistically across practices and fields. This categorical logic imposes an identity-based imperative for consistency, rendering organizational variation illegitimate and converting differentiation into evidence of moral incoherence. As a result, organizational agency collapses even among highly capable actors. Empirically relevant domains include reproductive healthcare, ESG investing, LGBTQ+ positioning, DEI programs, geopolitical conflicts, and content moderation. The theory yields three counterintuitive predictions. First, organizations that engage in contested practices before categorical imperatives solidify become more constrained once polarity emerges due to imprinting effects (P1). Second, organizations with greater visibility encounter faster legitimacy constraint because informational thresholds generate discontinuous shifts in stakeholder evaluation (P2). Third, exiting legitimacy polarity traps requires greater convergence than entry, as path-dependent feedback produces hysteresis in categorical judgments (P3). We advance institutional theory by identifying when field structure transforms evaluative processes in ways that eliminate the viability of compartmentalization as a response to institutional complexity.
