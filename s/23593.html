Collaborative Readiness and the Relational Foundations of Serendipity in Modern Workplaces

TIM
Nele Terveen (The University of Auckland); Christian Busch (University of Southern California); Cheryl Wakslak (University of Southern California)
Serendipity, unexpected interactions that generate novel insights and value, is widely viewed as a key driver of innovation and collaboration. Prior research has largely assumed that serendipity depends on physical proximity, leading many organizations to introduce return-to-office mandates in an effort to foster spontaneous collaboration. Drawing on a 20-month longitudinal qualitative study of four multinational technology firms, we challenge this proximity-centric view and show that serendipity is far more fragile and relational than commonly assumed. Our findings reveal that forced proximity often suppresses what we conceptualize as ‘collaborative readiness for serendipity’: the relational disposition to be open, available, and willing to engage with unplanned encounters. While mandated proximity triggers resentment toward leadership, active avoidance of interaction, and passive disengagement, it caused serendipity to collapse. At the same time, we identify three relational mechanisms through which collaborative readiness can be restored: individual connection that reactivates agency, managerial mediation that neutralizes relational barriers, and organizational care practices that remove resentment. We develop a two-sided conceptual model of relational serendipity that explains how serendipity is suppressed and relationally restored under forced proximity. Theoretically, we advance a serendipity engineering paradox, reconceptualize serendipity as a relational state rather than a cognitive or structural by-product, and introduce collaborative readiness as a critical theoretical condition for creative collaboration and innovation.

Conscious Capitalism in Entrepreneurship: How Hybrid Motives Shape Innovation in New Ventures

TIM
Hui Xie (Kunming University of Science and Technology); Keith Hmieleski (Texas Christian University); Bin Ma (IE Business School)
Conscious capitalism suggests that enduring value creation depends on balancing profit motives with prosocial purpose. We extend this perspective to entrepreneurship by theorizing how such hybrid motives influence the innovation performance of new ventures. Drawing on the dialectic perspective of innovation, we argue these motives jointly shape two key processes central to navigating tensions in innovation. The first is integrative complexity, which enables entrepreneurs to reconcile competing perspectives, and the second is persistence, which sustains commitment in the face of uncertainty. We theorize that innovation is enhanced when profit and prosocial motives operate together, with profit motives providing feasibility and resource discipline while prosocial motives broaden cognitive frames and reinforce meaning. Evidence from two multi-wave field studies of entrepreneurs, supplemented with a qualitative study, supports this hybrid motive model by showing that profit and prosocial motives enhance innovation performance through integrative complexity and persistence. By positioning conscious capitalism as a motive-based lens for entrepreneurship, our study responds to critiques that question whether conscious capitalism infringes on shareholder rights, downplays trade-offs among stakeholders, or is limited to established corporations rather than new ventures. We highlight instead that the hybrid balance of motives is consequential in early-stage ventures, where path dependencies are formed and where innovation is critical both for ensuring survival and for determining the scope and scale of the social good that is created.

Reinvention In Use: Adaptation Trajectories in the Implementation of Management Innovation

TIM
Ermal Hetemi (Linnaeus University); Evelina Ericsson (KTH Royal Institute of Technology); Joakim Lilliesköld
Management innovation practices related to product development, for example Lean Product Development (LPD) and Design for Six Sigma (DFSS), have been increasingly widespread and implemented. To explain the adoption of these practices, existing research has focused on factors and conditions at the individual or (inter-) organizational level that promote product development work yet captured primarily through survey questionnaires. Few studies have empirically investigated the ongoing adaptation process or management innovation practice implementation within the organizational context, and how these practices influence the flexibly and innovative oriented context of the PD work. We present a unique perspective on management innovation practices implementation by studying the LPD and DFSS adaptation processes with the organizational context foregrounded. Drawing on comparative case study research in the automotive industry, our qualitative analysis is based on semi-structured interviews and observations of the organizational level staff work of two prominent manufacturing firms in Sweden (Volvo and Scania). We provide insights and theoretical arguments that (1) management innovation implementation involves emergent adaptation by local actors, elaborating diffusion theory with process and agency insights, (2) organizational context (culture, structure, strategy) critically shapes, the what we term adaptation trajectory, offering a processual understanding on management innovation implementation, and (3) that the adaptation of these practices is often intertwined with other changes, suggesting a co-evolutionary or systems perspective on management innovation. These contributions fill gaps in existing literature and have practical implications.

When Do Negative Events Trigger Team Learning in Innovation Teams? An Event-Dimension Perspective

TIM
CHUNXIA WANG (University College Dublin); Dorota Piaskowska (University College Dublin); Mary Parkinson
Innovation teams frequently encounter negative events, yet prior research offers inconsistent findings regarding whether and how such events stimulate team learning. We argue that a key reason is that negative events are often treated as broad labels or examined in isolation, obscuring meaningful variation in how teams perceive and respond to them. We conduct a systematic literature review of research on negative events encountered by teams engaged in innovation tasks. Synthesizing the reviewed studies, we identify five dominant types of negative events and extract the key event dimensions articulated across prior work. Taking Event System Theory as a scaffold, we develop an event-dimension frame to characterize negative events in innovation context, and identify two important additional dimensions—event recurrence and resolution speed— that remain underdeveloped. Building on this event dimension frame, we advance a proximal event-processing account—centered on attention allocation and affective reactions—to theorize when negative events trigger team learning and why they evoke different learning behaviors. This study put forward a dimension-based perspective on team learning from negative events and provides a conceptual foundation for future empirical study of event-driving team learning.

When Innovations Meet Employees: A Scoping Review of Resistance During Organizational Implementation

TIM
Roniwan Abbas (Tilburg University); Zi-Lin He (Tilburg University); Joris Knoben (Tilburg University); Maria Jacobs (Tilburg University)
Innovation implementation in organizations is challenging, not only because of contextual barriers but also because employees may resist change. Across disciplines, research evidence remains fragmented and the conceptual distinction between barriers and individual resistance is blurred. Based on 84 studies published between 2005 and 2025, this scoping review maps empirical research on innovation implementation in organizational contexts and clarifies how barriers and resistance are conceptualized, operationalized, and related. Findings show that barriers are widely discussed but rarely defined, and are typically presented as an array of context-specific factors that make implementation difficult at individual and organizational levels. Resistance is less often labeled directly but can be meaningfully organized into cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions. Most attention focuses on cognitive and behavioral resistance, whereas affective resistance is much less examined. The relationship between barriers and resistance is usually left unspecified. The review provides conceptual clarity and outlines directions for future research on how barriers translate into resistance.
