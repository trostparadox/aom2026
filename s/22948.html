Changing the Frame: Rethinking the Reflexivity-Responsibility Nexus

ODC
Julie Wolfram Cox (Monash Business School); Andreas Pekarek (University of Melbourne); John Stewart Hassard (Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester)
The principle and practice of reflexivity remains important within management and organization studies (MOS) as a means for interrogating research assumptions. This importance is indicated by an accumulation of reflexivity types and by the extension of associated responsibilities for researchers. In response, we rethink the nature of the reflexivity-responsibility nexus. We first contribute a typology to delineate how different social science positions on reflexivity can inform and advance its associations with researcher responsibility. In comparison with romantic, humanist perspectives - which privilege individual and social-relational knowing and responsibility for improvements, and anti-humanism - which rejects the concept of researcher reflexivity as a basis for responsibility, we direct attention to a post-humanist perspective known as new-humanism. New-humanism reconstitutes reflexivity away from research conduct toward larger choices about research topics, also recognising without privileging human responsibility for the future. Second, we show how new-humanism can be applied to take up post-humanist assumptions without extremity or abstraction. We illustrate how new-humanism can inform a research agenda within MOS to promote more hopeful and equitable futures in three reflexive ways: leveraging present sociomaterial insecurities, applying experimentation, and practising prefiguration. Third, we contribute a series of principles to assist responsible choices within this agenda.

Once Upon a Legend: Purpose as a Gate for Serendipity amid Unpredictability

ODC
Edoardo Trincanato (Not Associated); Marco Balzano (University of Trieste); Emidia Vagnoni (University of Ferrara)
In turbulent environments, a core strategic challenge is making sense of what truly deserves attention. While corporate purpose is increasingly recognized as a source of direction and coherence, we know little about how a reinterpreted (“reborn”) purpose shapes strategic action over time. Building on the Attention-Based View (ABV) and sensemaking theory, we examine how strategic leaders mobilize a reborn corporate purpose to navigate turbulence and channel ambiguous and emergent cues into serendipitous strategic opportunities. Based on a longitudinal, extreme-case study of MV Agusta (2018–2025), the legendary Italian motorcycle manufacturer, we abductively derive a three-stage process model from how leaders (1) reframed the firm’s purpose by reconnecting a revered heritage claim (“Motorcycle Art”) with renewed strategic intent, (2) implemented this purpose as a cognitive and relational filter that structures attention and stakeholder engagement, and (3) realized it through patterns of action that selectively amplify some cues while discarding others, underpinning strategic renewal. Our findings reveal how purpose functions as a gate of salience: a meaning-making mechanism that shapes which signals become thinkable, legitimate, and strategically actionable. We contribute to research on corporate purpose, the ABV, and serendipity by theorizing purpose as a dynamic attentional logic that gates the activation of ambiguous cues under turbulence.

Resilience as a Value Judgement

ODC
Bruna Brito (University of Victoria)
Resilience has become a pervasive yet conceptually fragmented construct in management and organization studies. Existing accounts define resilience as either a property that enables actors to withstand disruption or a process through which they adapt over time. Despite definitional variation, these perspectives share an underlying realist assumption: resilience exists independently of interpretation and can be inferred from observable outcomes such as survival, recovery, or performance under adversity. This view obscures the interpretive work through which actors construct disruption, continuity, and change. I advance a social constructivist perspective that reframes resilience as a value judgment enacted through narrative. I argue that resilience is produced through stories that render adversity meaningful and articulate what should be preserved, adapted, or transformed. I develop a typology showing that resilience narratives vary along two dimensions: source of resilience and evidence of resilience. The combination of these dimensions yields four ideal types: robustness, endurance, adaptation and transformation narratives. Conceptualizing resilience as the performative effect of narrative reframes fragmentation in the literature not as contradiction but as plurality in how actors make adversity intelligible, opening new avenues for research.

Organizational Identity and Strategic Responses of Oil and Gas Firms in the Energy Transition

ODC
Thao Vo (BI Norwegian Business School); Helene Loe Colman (BI Norwegian Business School); Birgitte Grogaard (BI Norwegian Business School)
The energy transition exposes oil and gas multinational enterprises (MNEs) to multiple and often competing pressures: maintaining fossil-fuel-based activities for energy security while meeting environmental and climate goals. These pressures might challenge their core organizational perceptions of “who we are” – their organizational identity. Yet, little is known about how oil and gas MNEs respond to these pressures from an organizational identity lens. We address this gap through a 12-year longitudinal case study of Equinor, a firm pursuing both oil and gas and renewable energy businesses. Using firm-level publicly available documents, we examine how organizational identity interacts with strategic actions over time. We find that organizational identity shapes action, while organizational actions feed back organizational identity. Moreover, we identify three types of organizational identity claims – legacy, functional, and aspirational – and their interactions that together constitute the firm’s dynamic projected identity. We also identify two types of strategic actions – substantive and persuasive. Together, the recursive and dynamic interactions between organizational identity and action enable the firm to change while maintaining continuity and securing legitimacy. Our study contributes to research on the interplay between organizational identity and strategy and provides insights into how oil and gas MNEs respond to the energy transition.
