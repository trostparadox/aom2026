Ethical Constraint: Gender Differences in Misconduct and Startup Survival

OMT
Salem Alsanousi (London Business School); Aleksandra Joanna Kacperczyk (London Business School)
Female-led new ventures experience higher failure rates than male-led ventures, yet the mechanisms underlying this disparity remain incompletely understood. We examine whether employment violations play a role in startup survival and whether their association with failure differs by founder gender. Building on research documenting that employees provide less labor to female-led ventures for equivalent compensation, we theorize that employment violations may be particularly consequential for female-led ventures because they address systematic disadvantages in labor extraction. Using data on 18 million U.S. firms and 103 million firm-year observations from 2000-2014, we show four findings. First replicating prior studies, female-led ventures experience 10.2% higher failure rates than male-led ventures. Second, employment violations are associated with a 30.4% reduction in failure probability. Third, this association is stronger for female-led ventures (38.8% reduction) than for male-led ventures (22.5% reduction). Fourth, despite this stronger association, female-led ventures engage in 21% fewer violations. The gender gap operates entirely at the extensive margin: among firms that violate, we find no gender differences in violation intensity. This finding is consistent with gender differences in ethical thresholds rather than differential risk preferences. Our findings document a competitive dynamic in which those who stand to benefit most from certain behaviors are least likely to engage in them, suggesting that differences in regulatory compliance may contribute to persistent gender disparities in entrepreneurial outcomes.

Audience Favor–Gain Framework and Status Payoff: Evidence from Media Coverage of Misconduct

OMT
Lifei Li (Nanjing University); cheng dejun (Nanjing University); Songyang Qi (Nanjing University Business School); Jun Chu (Wuhan University)
Research on status from the status-as-asset and status-as-liability perspectives has produced contradictory findings regarding whether high status benefits actors, particularly in the context of misconduct. The asset view suggests that audiences’ lenient attitudes and favorable interpretations buffer high-status actors from the consequences of misconduct, whereas the liability view argues that heightened expectations and stricter scrutiny amplify negative outcomes. This tension suggests a loose coupling between status and status advantage. The study addresses this puzzle by developing a favor–gain framework that conceptualizes audience responses to high-status actors involved in misconduct as judgment-based. Audiences assess the anticipated gains and costs of favoring high-status actors, and positive net returns motivate continued favorable engagement, while unfavorable tradeoffs lead to withdrawal. The examination used a sample of 5,437 media reports covering 1,877 financial misconduct events involving Chinese listed firms, produced by 152 media outlets. The results indicate that higher-status misconduct firms receive more favorable coverage, an effect strengthened when favorable coverage yields greater gains through prior media attitudes and weakened when it entails higher costs under heightened misconduct visibility. These findings highlight the central role of audience-side considerations in shaping status advantages and offer a dynamic account of how status benefits are constructed and contested.

That’s a Scandal... For You! When and How Organizational Scandals Foster Polarization

OMT
Gastone Gualtieri (ZHAW School of Management and Law); Rasmus Pichler (Rotterdam School of Management); Marco Clemente (ZHAW School of Management and Law)
We investigate how organizational scandals can foster societal polarization, a link that has not yet been theorized in the literature. Organizational scandals – defined as publicized transgressions that attract significant media attention and public disapproval – are pivotal moments of public moral debate that affect audience’s understandings of norms. Prior studies have typically treated the disapproval around scandals as relatively homogeneous, resulting in the alignment of audience’s normative understandings. Yet, in some cases scandals may create divergence in the judgement of different audience groups and foster normative polarization. To explain when and how this happens, we leverage the concept of the Overton Window – a framework from political studies that describes the range of behaviors considered socially acceptable. We develop a model that explains when and how scandals decouple the Overton Window and trigger the formation of opposing normative frameworks across the audience, fostering polarization. Overall, this paper contributes to polarization research by uncovering the mechanisms of scandal-induced polarization and to scandal research by conceptualizing organizational scandals as potential catalysts for polarization, revealing their perspectival nature in contemporary society.

Scandal, Silence… or Fizzle? Journalists’ Agency in the Scandalization of Organizational Misconduct

OMT
Gastone Gualtieri (ZHAW School of Management and Law); Marco Clemente (ZHAW School of Management and Law); Elena Dalpiaz (USI Lugano, Switzerland)
We investigate how journalists’ agency shapes whether organizational misconduct becomes a full-blown scandal, remains silent, or fizzles after exposure. Organizational scandals – defined as publicized transgressions that attract significant media attention and public disapproval – are quintessentially mediated events, yet prior studies largely explain scandalization through transgression, transgressor, and audience characteristics while treating media production as a black box. Drawing on 40 in-depth interviews with investigative journalists and editors in the UK, the US, and Italy, we develop a process model of media scandalization across three phases: Activation, Exposure, and Sustained Engagement. We show that journalists and editors evaluate and pursue transgression stories through three scandalization logics: macro (societal significance and industry practices), meso (editorial strategy and organizational constraints), and micro (role conceptions, values, expertise). Misalignments among these logics generate tensions that can suppress stories, reshape exposure through framing and collaboration, and condition whether audience feedback loops sustain attention or lead to scandal fizzle. Overall, this paper contributes to scandal research by theorizing the micro-foundations of scandalization and to social evaluation and institutional theory by showing how mediated evaluations and disruptive events hinge on journalists’ situated sensemaking and organizational constraints.

The Cost of Values: The Moral Legitimacy and Performance Challenges of Hybrid Organizations

OMT
Cédric Legrand (emlyon); Bernard Forgues (emlyon business school)
Organizations combining a market logic with a social welfare logic – hybrid organizations – attract growing scholarly attention. While they pursue intrinsically moral goals, their moral legitimacy is rarely questioned – provided they avoid mission drift. Yet, the moral market literature suggests that the market logic is often seen as incompatible with goods or goals imbued with moral meanings. This suggests that hybrids may face more moral concerns than previously thought. In this paper, we examine when and why hybrids face moral legitimacy challenges from consumers, and how this affects their financial performance. We argue the combination of market and social welfare logics is in and by itself a source of moral concerns, which intensifies when consumers encounter cues suggesting that social welfare values are compromised by market considerations. As exposure to such cues increases, consumers are more likely to downgrade their moral legitimacy judgments, resulting in lower demand and weaker financial performance for hybrid organizations. We also examine three factors influencing audiences’ reactions to such cues: the dilution of their attention by more positive cues, their propensity to generalize across similar organizations, and their moral assessment criteria determined by their own logics. Our findings shed light on how external audiences perceive hybrid organizations and the implications for their financial viability – a key condition for fulfilling their social or environmental missions.
