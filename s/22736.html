A Study on the Impact of Human-AI Collaboration on Team Resilience

OB
Yuanyuan Liu (Taiyuan University of Technology); Yiwen Shi (Beijing Institute of Technology); Wentao Bu (Taiyuan University of Technology); Jizu Li (Taiyuan University of Technology)
With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence technology, human-AI collaboration has become a critical focus in organizational management. However, current research on human-AI collaboration presents a dual-track approach, focusing on both “technological attributes” and “human-machine relationships”, while it overlooks the impact of human-AI collaboration on interpersonal dynamics. This research gap prevents a comprehensive understanding of employees’ responses within the human-AI collaborative context. Grounded in social interaction theory, this study reveals the mechanism through which human-AI collaboration influences team resilience from the dual perspectives of “human-machine” and “interpersonal” relationships. Employing a mixed-method research approach that combines experimental methods and survey techniques, the findings indicate that, from the “human-machine” relationship perspective, human-AI collaboration enhances team resilience through perceived usefulness of AI technology. From the “interpersonal” relationship perspective, human-AI strengthens team resilience through AI teammate acceptance. Furthermore, teammates’ skill levels positively moderate the intensity of the effects along both pathways, with high-skilled teammates being more effective in leveraging technological advantages. This article provides a theoretical framework for organizations to enhance team resilience through optimized human-AI collaboration models while also offering empirical evidence for talent selection and development, particularly during periods of technological transformation, underscoring its significant practical value for maintaining team stability.

Anthropomorphic AI Performance Fluctuations Harm Collaboration and Learning: Performance Credit Lens

OB
Kunjing Li; Liyun Wei (Wuhan University); ?? ? (Wuhan University); Shuoli Wang; Xiao Tan (Shanghai Jianqiao University); Junzhe Jiang (Wuhan University); Yuebo Luo (Wuhan University)
Drawing on attribution theory, this study investigates the phenomenon of AI performance fluctuations in the workplace and explains its impact on employee behaviors. We propose that fluctuating AI performance significantly diminishes its performance credit. As a result, employees are less likely to attribute AI successes to its stable capability, thereby undermining internal attributions toward the AI. This shift in attribution reduces employees’ willingness to collaborate with and learn from the AI. Furthermore, we posit that AI anthropomorphism acts as a critical moderator, such that when the AI exhibits high anthropomorphic features, the negative impact of performance fluctuations on performance credit is strengthened, leading to a greater reduction in employee collaboration and learning behaviors. Based on four-wave longitudinal data, our analysis supports the mediating role of performance credit in the relationship between AI performance fluctuations and employee behaviors, as well as the moderating role of AI anthropomorphism. This study not only highlights the unique impact of AI performance fluctuations and identifies performance credit as a key explanatory mechanism, but also uncovers the potential risks associated with anthropomorphic design, therefore offering novel insights for AI design and human-AI collaboration research.

How Does Employee-Generative AI Collaboration Impact Workplace Helping Behavior

OB
Robin Mengxi Yang; Mingjun Yang (RMIT University); Yifei Zhang (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences)
Generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI), a core digital - transformation driver, has effectively integrated into workplace collaboration. As human - machine collaboration becomes normal, its potential risks to workplace social ecology are emerging. Existing research focuses on AI's productivity value, with few discussions on its negative impacts on interpersonal relationships. To fill this gap, we develop and test a novel model based on interdependence and attachment theories to explain how and when employee - Gen AI collaboration may reduce workplace helping behavior. Through two studies, we found Gen AI collaboration weakens workplace helping via perceived interpersonal tie. Emotional attachment to Gen AI moderates its direct negative effect on this tie and indirect effect on helping, with both effects stronger at higher attachment. Our study shows Gen AI collaboration negatively affects employees' interpersonal relationships and workplace helping, providing theoretical basis for social substitution effects in human - machine collaboration and practical guidance for organizations to use Gen AI's advantages while reducing harm to interpersonal ties.

The Knowledge Dilemma vis-à-vis AI: Sharing Versus Hiding from an Approach-Avoidance Perspective

OB
Shengmin Liu (University of Shanghai for Science and Technology); Yuxuan Chen (University of Nottingham Ningbo China); Jie Wang (University of Nottingham Ningbo China)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping work, yet it still requires human knowledge to perform. Although the literature shows AI can both enhance and threaten employees’ roles, how employees interact with AI systems in sharing and hiding knowledge is largely ignored. Drawing on the approach-avoidance framework, we theorized dual motivational pathways by which AI use shapes employees’ knowledge behaviors toward AI systems. We conducted a three-wave field study of full-time employees in AI-transforming organizations (N = 548) and a 2×2 experiment with engineers (N = 400) that manipulates technology types (AI vs. traditional) and human-AI relationship (cooperation vs. competition). Across these studies, we consistently found that AI use was positively related to problem-solving efficacy and job insecurity simultaneously. The results also revealed positive indirect relationships: AI use was positively related to knowledge sharing with AI via problem-solving efficacy, and to knowledge hiding from AI via job insecurity. Further, we demonstrated that the knowledge sharing path was more pronounced for employees with a more cooperative human-AI relationship, while the knowledge hiding path was more prominent for employees with a more competitive human-AI relationship. We discussed theoretical and practical implications.

How Employees Respond to AI Performance Shortfalls: A Within-Person Investigation

OB
Wenqing Yu (Zhejiang University); Yiduo Shao (University of Iowa); Shenjiang Mo (Zhejiang University)
As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly embedded in daily work, prior research has focused primarily on AI adoption and use, offering limited insight into how employees respond when AI fails to meet expectations. Drawing on expectancy violations theory, we examine how employees react when AI performance falls below their expectations. Across three studies, we found that such performance shortfalls elicited both adaptive and maladaptive responses, which in turn exerted beneficial effects on employee performance and detrimental effects on well-being, respectively. We further found that these responses were contingent on employees’ beliefs about their future opportunities. By shifting attention from AI adoption to performance variability, this study advances understanding of human-AI collaboration and offers new insights into employee experiences in AI-integrated workplaces.
