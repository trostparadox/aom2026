Self–Other Agreement on Leader Agreeableness: Implications for LMX and Citizenship Behavior

OB
Youngduk Lee (The University of Hong Kong); Chou-Yu (Joey) Tsai (State University of New York at Binghamton); Jeeyoon Park; Christopher M. Berry (Indiana University Bloomington); Seongsu Kim (Seoul National University); Joseph Kim (University of Illinois at Chicago); Kyoung Yong Kim (Villanova University)
In today’s work environment, where diversity and teamwork are emphasized, leaders who are kind and considerate to diverse employees are needed more than ever. The leader personality trait that most closely represents this interpersonal tendency is a leader’s agreeableness. However, previous studies on leader agreeableness have reported mixed results. We propose that one of the reasons for this may be that prior research has mainly relied on a leader’s self-rating for measurement and has not considered other stakeholders. As such, we draw from the self-other agreement framework to test the joint effect of the leader’s self- and subordinate ratings of leader agreeableness on leader-member exchange (LMX) and interpersonal citizenship behavior (OCB-I). We collected 225 leader-subordinate dyads and examined our hypotheses with multilevel polynomial regression and response surface methodology. We found that compared to using self-ratings alone, using self- and subordinate ratings together and examining joint effects of their combinations can provide a more nuanced understanding of the impact of leader agreeableness on LMX and OCB-I. We discuss theoretical implications for personality and leadership research (e.g., trait approach to leadership, follower-centric view, LMX). We also provide practical implications on how a multisource personality assessment can be used in leadership development.

Social Balance and LMX in a Dual-Leadership Context: Where One Ends and the Other Begins

OB
Damian Claudio Zivic (Indiana University)
As organizations seek to remain competitive by organizing work around projects and assigning employees to multiple simultaneous teams, members are likely to find themselves reporting to more than one direct leader. This paper studies the structure and outcomes of a unique and underexplored triad, which I call the member-dual-leader triad. First, by drawing from Social Balance Theory (SBT), I predict the structural balance of the triad. Second, I present a set of hypotheses including contradictory and competing predictions based on LMX theory and SBT. With a survey study of 915 members of such triads, I find support for my hypotheses predicting the structural balance of the triad; thus, supporting SBT, as well as finding support for when member outcomes are expected to be most desirable. However, testing the competing hypotheses, LMX theory better explains the member outcomes providing evidence that the quantity, and not the balancing, of relationships in a member-dual-leader triad is associated with more desirable member outcomes. Therefore, my study highlights the dual-leadership context as a boundary condition for SBT attitudinal and behavioral predictions.

When My Boss’s Boss Acts Like My Boss: Skip-Level Assignments and Employee Appraisals

OB
Xinchen Lou (Peking University)
Skip-level assignments—where leaders two levels upward assign tasks directly to non-immediate subordinates—are increasingly common yet underexplored in leadership literature. Drawing on stress appraisal theory, this research investigates how employees interpret and respond to these vertical bypassing task assignments. We propose that the relational context, specifically Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) and perceived Leader-Leader Exchange (LLX), shapes whether employees appraise skip-level assignments as motivating challenges or stressful hindrances. We argue that high LMX or high perceived LLX fosters challenge appraisals, leading to proactive task engagement. Conversely, low LMX or low perceived LLX promotes hindrance appraisals, resulting in interaction avoidance toward the skip-level leader. Results from a scenario-based experiment (Study 1) and a multi-wave survey (Study 2) support our moderated mediation model. This research contributes to multi-level leadership theory by clarifying the psychological mechanisms linking skip-level practices to frontline behaviors and highlighting the critical role of vertical relational alignment.

When Agreement Becomes Pressure: Leader–Team LMX Alignment and Team Citizenship Pressure

OB
Ji Hoon Lee (Ji Hoon Lee); Kyoungmin Cho (Pennsylvania State U); Ye Dai (Southern Illinois University, Carbondale)
This study examines how shared perceptions of the leader–team relationship shape team-level citizenship pressure and its behavioral consequences. Drawing on optimal distinctiveness theory, we argue that alignment between leaders’ and teams’ perceptions of leader–member exchange (LMX)—whether at high or low levels of relationship quality—can generate normative pressure that constrains discretion, whereas moderate levels of alignment afford greater latitude for discretion. Using multisource, time-lagged data from 285 officers nested within 104 South Korean police teams, polynomial regression and response-surface analyses reveal a U-shaped relationship between leader–team LMX alignment and team citizenship pressure: both high–high and low–low alignment are associated with heightened collective pressure to engage in extra-role contributions. Team citizenship pressure, in turn, is positively related to unethical pro-organizational behavior (UPB) but was not significantly related to organizational citizenship behavior (OCB). These findings suggest that relational alignment may function as a form of normative control, transforming discretionary effort into compelled contribution and increasing ethical risk. By reframing leader–team LMX alignment as a team-level reference point shaping pressure climates, this study highlights when relational harmony carries hidden moral costs.

Delegation in Need is not Effective Indeed: A Contingent Model of Leader Ego Depletion and LMX

OB
Qin Su (Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University); Erzhuo Wang; Wuyang Chen (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
Effective delegation behavior is believed to benefit both leaders and followers, as it frees leaders from active supervisory roles and develops followers with more opportunities. However, empirical studies have produced mixed findings regarding when and how to delegate. To develop a more nuanced understanding of how followers interpret and react to leader delegation, first, we investigate delegation at the within-person level to account for its dynamic nature. Second, we integrate the job-demands and resources (JD-R) theory with social information processing theory to propose that followers often leverage leaders’ daily ego-depletion states and their static relationship quality (leader-member exchange, LMX) as cognitive anchors to calibrate their responses to daily leader delegation. Results from an experience sampling survey and an online experiment support the buffering rather than exaggerating effect of leader ego depletion on the delegation effect on follower work engagement. Furthermore, this buffering effect is more salient among high LMX followers. This study identifies both dynamic and static boundary conditions for leader daily delegation and contributes to JD-R theory by proposing and testing the moderation effect of LMX on the interaction effects of job demands and resources.
