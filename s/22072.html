Session overview

Despite decades of research on diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI), organizations continue to struggle with backlash, disengagement, and polarization when addressing racial inequity. Much of DEI scholarship and practice relies on framing racial inequality through concepts such as White privilege, systemic bias, and historical disadvantage, assuming these frames promote empathy, responsibility, and equity-oriented behavior. However, emerging evidence suggests that these assumptions overlook how individuals emotionally and cognitively process such messages. This PDW draws on experimental and correlational evidence from a study of White employees’ responses to racial inequity framing to demonstrate that emotional and attributional reactions— rather than framing alone—drive DEI-relevant outcomes. Contrary to prevailing theory, White guilt and external attributions for racial inequality were associated with higher levels of covert racism (e.g., colorblind ideology), whereas anger—typically viewed as resistance—was associated with lower racism, particularly among individuals with greater intergroup contact. These findings challenge dominant models of how individuals make sense of inequality, responsibility, and group-based advantage. This workshop trains participants to identify, diagnose, and strategically respond to distinct emotional and cognitive pathways that are activated when people encounter DEI messaging. Participants will engage in hands-on exercises that apply this framework to real-world organizational communications, including diversity statements, equity reports, and training materials. By integrating social psychology, cognition and emotion research, and applied design tools, this PDW equips scholars, educators, and practitioners to better understand how inequality is interpreted and how DEI interventions can be designed to increase engagement and produce more effective equity outcomes in organizational settings.
