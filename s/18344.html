Protean Career Orientation in a Collectivist Culture: A Longitudinal Study of Transition to Work

Pennsylvania Convention Center — Poster Wall 3

Authors: Thanh-Hang Pham RMIT University; Seng Kok RMIT University; Jung Woo Han RMIT University; Quyen Dang RMIT University

Session overview

The transition from university to work has become increasingly uncertain for fresh graduates, shaped by technological disruption, global crises, and a highly volatile labor market. Drawing on the concept of protean career orientation (PCO), this study explores how Vietnamese graduates enact and adapt their career orientations during the university-to-work transition within a collectivistic cultural context. Adopting a longitudinal qualitative design, the study followed 38 Vietnamese participants across a 18-month-period of their transition to work through three waves of in-depth, semi-structured interviews. The findings reveal three key insights. First, Vietnamese graduates express both core dimensions of PCO, i.e., self-direction and value-driven orientation, yet these are shaped by cultural forces such as Confucianism, Buddhism, and Vietnam’s agrarian historical roots. In particular, self-direction is enacted through relational norms, often by internalizing family clan expectations rooted in filial piety teachings, while universal PCO values such as freedom are also reinterpreted through a collectivist lens, emphasizing time spent caring for parents or fulfilling family duties. Second, PCO is reported to be dynamic rather than static: all participants reported changes in their PCO over time. Third, PCO’s adaptations are driven not only by external triggers but by internal mechanisms of career courage and self-reflection. Theoretically, the study advances the understanding of PCO by framing it as a culturally embedded, dynamic construct rather than a fixed trait. Practically, the findings offer actionable insights for graduates, career practitioners, organizations, universities, and policymakers seeking to support sustainable early career development in collectivistic and volatile contexts.
