Mechanism of Incoherence in Business Model Innovation During Digital Transformation

TIM
Chen Ye (University of Cambridge)
Digital transformation requires firms not only to adopt new technologies but also to innovate their business models. However, many firms struggle with business model incoherence, which undermines their ability to capture digital opportunities. This study investigates the mechanisms of business model incoherence using qualitative analyses and system dynamics modeling of firms from diverse industries in the US, Europe, and China. I combine multi-case evidence with system dynamics to translate causal loop diagrams into quantitative stock-and-flow models that represent feedback, delays, and non-linear interactions. Calibrated using interview, workshop, and archival evidence from a subset of firms and validated through structural and behavioral validation, including a comparison of simulation outputs with longitudinal case trajectories, and an independent test using the remaining firms that were reserved, together with a comprehensive sensitivity analysis to test robustness, the models reproduce field patterns. I provide mechanism-consistent explanations and simulation experiments and scenario analyses that align with case trajectories. I reveal a distinct mechanism of disciplined incoherence where customer cooperation follows a U-shaped recovery trajectory: initial incoherence degrades operational stability, but subsequently triggers problemistic search and radical innovation that restore competitive advantage. By enhancing strategic contradiction theory and extending the Business Model Cohesiveness Scorecard (BMCS), the research provides actionable insights for managers to proactively manage incoherence during digital transformation.

Open Innovation Strategies for Business Model Innovation: The Moderating Role of Absorptive Capacity

TIM
Wolfgang Hora (Johannes Kepler University Linz); Djordje Zivkovic (University of Liechtenstein); Simon Liegl (University of St. Gallen)
Business model innovation (BMI) is a crucial mechanism that allows firms to adapt to changing market conditions. However, the strategic factors that facilitate such renewal — particularly the distinct roles of different open innovation strategies — require further investigation. This study examines how various forms of open innovation—outside-in, inside-out, and coupled strategies—contribute to BMI and the conditions under which they are most effective. Using survey data from senior executives of established companies, we demonstrate that all three strategies are positively associated with BMI. Furthermore, our findings reveal that absorptive capacity plays a fundamental role. It significantly strengthens the positive impact of outside-in strategies and mitigates the effect of inside-out strategies. However, absorptive capacity appears to have no impact on coupled strategies. These results clarify the mixed effects of open innovation on BMI and establish absorptive capacity as a key boundary condition that shapes the pathway to BMI.

When Value Becomes Constraint: Blockchain as a Catalyst for Business Model Viability and Failure

TIM
Aleksandra Baron (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Andreas Alexiou (University of Southampton Business School); Ard-Pieter De Man (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam); Nina Telg (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam)
Blockchain is often described as a technology with substantial strategic potential and has recently regained attention as a pragmatic infrastructure for organizational coordination, yet many blockchain business models still fail to achieve viability. The literature offers little agreement on why this promise translates into viable business models in only a limited number of cases. This study addresses this gap through a systematic literature review. The findings show that the same features that underpin blockchain’s value (i.e., decentralization, transparency, and immutability) also pose challenges that impede its realization. By integrating research on blockchain business model logics with studies on adoption challenges, the review demonstrates that value dimensions and adoption barriers are closely connected. Building on this insight, the study conceptualizes blockchain as a catalyst for ongoing business model evolution, where constraints from early design choices resurface over time and require continual reassessment and adaptation. The study also derives a set of propositions that specify the conditions under which blockchain features are more likely to support the development of viable business models. Together, these findings explain why many initiatives stall and clarify when blockchain can contribute to business model viability.

R&D in Experiential Leisure: Reconstructing Research and Development for the Experience Economy

TIM
Euclides Major
R&D is typically defined and measured through manufacturing-centric inputs such as formal R&D personnel, laboratories, and technology development, making experience industries (tourism, hospitality, events, immersive leisure) appear “R&D-poor.” This inference is misleading. In experience settings, systematic knowledge creation is embedded in live delivery, co-created with customers, and materializes as scripts, service architectures, measurement systems, and replication packages rather than as patentable artifacts. Drawing on service research and tourism innovation scholarship, and leveraging prior work that separates R&D from innovation and distinguishes product R&D from process R&D, we develop a theory of Experiential Leisure R&D. We conceptualize experiential R&D as purposeful experimentation and codification aimed at designing, delivering, and reproducing experiential value co-creation systems across contexts. The theory specifies three R&D domains: experience-concept, delivery-system, and institutionalization R&D linking them through an iterative process and proposes an experience-centric measurement stack that captures experimentation intensity, codification artifacts, partner interfaces, and well-being outcomes.
