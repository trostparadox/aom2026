The Broken Ladder: Dynamics of AI-Induced Collapse in Labor Markets

OMT
Mahdi M Najafabadi (California State University Northridge); Mohammad Hossein Jarrahi (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill); Pouyan Eslami (California State University-Northridge)
The rapid adoption of Generative AI in professional careers offers immediate efficiency gains by automating entry-level tasks. This structural shift reconfigures the organizational pathways for learning, capability development, and socialization. Because entry-level work is the primary mechanism for a new employee to acquire the tacit knowledge required for competent performance and beyond, adapt to organizational culture, and learn its core processes and routines, this phenomenon puts at risk the continuity of the internal labor market’s developmental pipeline. Using System Dynamics as a simulation-based theory development approach, we depict the long-term impact of automating novice roles on organizational viability. The results reveal a competency trap: while AI adoption initially reduces costs and leads to short-term efficiency, it triggers a consequential collapse in senior staff at higher levels in the long run, due to the mentorship pipeline erosion. This leads to a temporal decoupling where technology adoption rises sharply while organizational capabilities degrade silently, creating a latent risk that is invisible to short-term metrics. We argue that the widespread automation of entry-level tasks constitutes an unprecedented erosion of Absorptive Capacity driven by a collapse in the Regenerative Capacity of the professional talent pipeline, i.e., its ability to replenish firm-specific expertise over time. We show that prioritizing task-level efficiency can turn firms into hollow structures, unable to build talent internally. Because this effect is systemic, at the large scale, it leads to a “Tragedy of the Commons” (i.e., collective action problem) situation where firms are unable to hire experienced labor from a depleted market.

The Adaptation of Artificially Intelligent Routines

OMT
Murad A. Mithani (Rutgers University-Camden); Ipek Kocoglu (Kean University)
Research on international business recognizes imitation of host-country practices as a primary mechanism through which foreign firms mitigate the liability of foreignness. However, firms with Artificial Intelligence (AI) mediated products have been argued to shun imitation because consumers either do not recognize or view them differently. In this study, we investigate how foreign and domestic AI firms navigate the formation and evolution of organizational routines. Using longitudinal data on AI-based financial advisors in the U.S., we find that foreign entrants differ from domestic entrants not only in initial routine configuration, but also in the structure of routine change over time. At entry, foreign firms exhibit greater routine ambiguity and a broader mix of routines than domestic firms. However, rather than converging through imitation, foreign firms operate within a regime that constrains routine adjustment: many foreign firms remain weakly routinized, and a smaller subset selectively commits to routines that are uncommon among domestic firms. Domestic firms, by contrast, adjust routines through linear adjustments that support incremental consolidation. Our study contributes by elaborating on not just what routines firms use, but how those routines evolve. It demonstrates that exposure to the host environment does not uniformly facilitate imitation. Instead, foreignness constrains the paths of routine adjustment, transforming learning from incremental refinement into selective commitment.

Temporal Meta-Uncertainty in Algorithmically Mediated Organizations

OMT
Natasha Overmeyer (University of Oregon)
Organizations increasingly rely on algorithmic forecasting systems to inform judgment under uncertainty. While such systems are often assumed to reduce uncertainty through improved prediction, we theorize that they fundamentally transform uncertainty’s epistemic structure. Drawing on research on temporal organizing, epistemic infrastructures, and performativity, we introduce temporal meta-uncertainty—a second-order uncertainty that arises when organizational actors must adjudicate among multiple, internally coherent but epistemically incompatible temporal projections generated by architecturally diverse algorithmic systems. We develop a theoretical framework identifying four sources through which temporal meta-uncertainty emerges and persists: epistemic model plurality creates divergence in temporal predictions, algorithmic opacity sustains this divergence, performative forecasting reinforces it, and irreversible temporal commitments convert it into a strategic hazard. Together, these mechanisms ex-plain how algorithmic prediction infrastructures multiply modeled futures rather than converging on singular authoritative projections, fundamentally shifting organizational judgment from evaluating outcomes to selecting among competing temporal epistemologies. We illustrate the framework through simulation comparing forecasting architectures under varied temporal and environmental conditions. Results reveal a paradox of AI-mediated prediction: models achieving superior first-order predictive accuracy simultaneously increase cross-model disagreement, particularly as historical data accumulates. This AI paradox, that better individual models generate worse collective consensus, demonstrates that temporal meta-uncertainty is not a transient artifact of immature tools, but rather is a structural feature of algorithmically-mediated organizational environments. This study contributes to organization and management theory by reconceptualizing uncertainty as structurally transformed rather than reduced in algorithmic environ-ments, extending temporal organization theory to account for plural epistemic infrastructures, theorizing performativity in algorithmic prediction contexts, and developing implications for organizational judgment, coordination, and decision-making under epistemic infrastructure plurality.

From Analysts to Algorithms: Effects of Machine-Based Market Intermediaries on Firm Valuation

OMT
Kim Claes (Cornell University); Heeyon Kim (Cornell University); Raunaq Pungaliya (Sungkyunkwan University); Yanbo Wang (Sungkyunkwan University)
Organizations are increasingly evaluated not only by human intermediaries—such as analysts, critics, and experts—but also by algorithmic systems that systematically retrieve and process information about firms. We argue that these algorithmic market intermediaries improve the informational environment of the firms they cover by increasing the availability of firm-level information used in market valuation. Using detailed access logs from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (21.9 billion retrieval events from 2003–2017), we proxy algorithmic attention to firms through quantifiable measures of machine-led downloads of firms’ corporate disclosures. We find that firms receiving greater machine coverage are evaluated more favorably in the market, consistent with the view that machine intermediaries reduce uncertainty by improving the informational inputs on which evaluators rely. To bound this mechanism, we show that the effect is stronger when uncertainty is higher—such as for diversified firms, whose complexity poses challenges for human evaluators—and when human analyst coverage is more limited. Leveraging a regulatory change that increased the machine-readability of disclosures, we further implement a difference-in-differences analysis demonstrating that firms experiencing larger increases in machine coverage exhibit correspondingly larger improvements in market valuation. Taken together, these findings suggest that machine intermediaries constitute a consequential new audience for organizations, operating alongside human intermediaries and reshaping how firms are attended to, interpreted, and valued in financial markets.

Talking AI: What Firms Say and How Markets React

OMT
Lily Morse (University of Denver); Arkadiy Lenchak (University of Denver); Olga Bruyaka (West Virginia University)
Artificial intelligence (AI) is becoming a prominent topic in corporate communication, yet investors face substantial uncertainty about its economic implications. We investigate whether and to what extent financial markets respond to firms’ managerial communication about AI during quarterly earnings calls. Drawing on signaling theory, we conceptualize AI talk as a rhetorical signal conveying forward-looking information about firms’ strategic orientation toward AI under conditions of high uncertainty. Using S&P 1500 earnings call transcripts from 2010 to 2024, we develop an AI dictionary to measure the incidence and intensity of AI-related communication and document its evolution, thematic content, and market impact. Across multiple empirical approaches, we find evidence that AI talk is associated with higher cumulative abnormal returns, controlling for firm characteristics, earnings surprises, industry effects, and linguistic features. Portfolio strategies formed on AI talk yield positive and significant risk-adjusted excess returns over the subsequent quarter. We further show that market responses to AI talk are moderated by firms’ R&D intensity and operational efficiency, with stronger reactions when these attributes provide clearer cues about firms’ innovation and efficiency profiles. Notably, firms with high R&D intensity and high operational efficiency elicit the strongest positive reactions, whereas firms with low R&D intensity or operational efficiency exhibit smaller but positive responses. In contrast, firms with moderate R&D intensity or operational efficiency generate muted reactions. Overall, these findings show that AI talk functions as a consequential rhetorical signal with economically meaningful stock price effects.
