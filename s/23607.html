Session overview

This theory-driven session advances new ways of understanding inclusion and exclusion in organizational life. The papers examine exclusion climates, actors' motives for exclusion, inclusion as responsive co-creation, and the distinctions between allyship actions and allyship actors. Together, they challenge conventional models of inclusion as a one-way process and offer richer conceptual tools for understanding power, reciprocity, and inequality.

The Illusion of Inclusion: A Theory-Driven Framework for the Formation of Exclusion Climates

DEI
Michàlle Mor Barak (University of Southern California); Kim Brimhall (Boston University); Gil Luria (University of Haifa)
Although inclusion has been studied extensively, exclusion, with a few notable exceptions, has been studied primarily as it relates to inclusion, situated at the opposite end of the same continuum. Furthermore, climate for exclusion remains unexplored. This paper addresses this gap by: (1) defining climate for exclusion and drawing on theories of social identity, symbolic interaction, and similarity attraction to explain how it develops and what sustains it; (2) proposing a conceptual model that describes antecedents, outcomes, and moderators of climate for exclusion; and (3) suggesting that understanding how climate for exclusion is created and the dynamics that maintain it could guide the development of more effective interventions. We argue that climate for exclusion – i.e., shared perceptions among in-group members of an organization that devaluing out-group members is acceptable or even encouraged and rewarded – is not simply the absence of inclusion, but rather a distinct facet of organizational climate with its own unique drivers. Importantly, this paper highlights that organizations can simultaneously exhibit a climate for inclusion within an in-group while maintaining systemic exclusion of others, creating an illusion of inclusion. We conclude with actionable recommendations for research, policy, and practice, to address exclusion at its roots.

Exploring the Relational Dynamics of Allyship: Understanding Marginalized Individuals' Perspectives

DEI
Guler Kizilenis Ulusman (York University)
Research on workplace allyship has largely emphasized allies’ identities, motivations, and behaviors, often assuming that allyship is inherently beneficial once enacted. Drawing on an inductive, constructivist grounded theory study of 50 marginalized professionals in North America, this study reconceptualizes allyship from the perspective of its intended beneficiaries. Based on in-depth interviews with women, Black professionals, and LGBTQ+ employees, this study develops the Dynamic Allyship Framework, a model explaining how marginalized employees interpret, evaluate, and adapt to allyship. The findings show that allyship is not experienced as a stable identity or a discrete set of supportive actions. Instead, allyship unfolds as a relational and evaluative process through four interrelated phases: Awareness, Action, Reflection, and Adaptation. Marginalized employees assess allyship by tracking consistency between words and actions, sensitivity to context, and willingness to act under risk. Authenticity emerges not as an inherent property of ally behavior but as an evaluative outcome constructed through repeated interaction and pattern recognition. Emotional–relational signals such as trust, validation, fatigue, and fear operate as feedback mechanisms that shape movement across phases and inform adaptive responses, including engagement escalation, boundary regulation, and protective withdrawal. Contextual conditions, including leadership signaling, inclusion climate, organizational practices, and connectivity, authorize or constrain allyship across all stages. By centering marginalized employees as active sensemakers rather than passive recipients, this study advances a processual theory of allyship. The Dynamic Allyship Framework integrates agency, emotion, and adaptation into a unified model, extending allyship scholarship and clarifying when and why allyship is sustained, recalibrated, or withdrawn in organizational life. Keywords: Workplace allyship; marginalized employees; process theory; authenticity; emotional labor; inclusion; relational dynamics; qualitative research; grounded theory

Understanding Allyship: Distinguishing Actors and Actions in the Pursuit of Social Change

DEI
Juliane Schittek (Imperial College London); Celia Moore
We develop and test theory distinguishing two distinct targets of evaluation in the allyship process: allyship actions and the actors who engage in them. Drawing on signaling and categorization theory, we propose that evaluations of actions and actors follow different cognitive processes, with important implications for our understanding of when and how allyship is effective and creates meaningful change for members of marginalized groups. Across three studies, we first develop a comprehensive understanding of the set of actions that allyship entails. We then examine how observers evaluate allyship actions in isolation, and how they evaluate actors as a function of their patterns of engagement with those actions. Our findings reveal a critical distinction: the behaviors perceived as most effective for creating structural change are not necessarily the ones that make individuals appear to be effective allies or the ones that help to foster inclusion. This distinction reframes debates about ‘effective allyship’ as debates about the evaluative targets: actions are judged by anticipated impact, whereas actors are judged by inferred category membership.

Why Actors Exclude: A Social Exchange Perspective on Workplace Exclusion Motives

DEI
Rowan Moelijker (Rotterdam School of Management); Lynn Shore (Colorado State University); Beth G. Chung (San Diego State University)
Workplace exclusion is pervasive and harmful, with past theorizing largely focused on targets’ experiences, often assuming malevolent intent. By adopting a target-focused and harm-centered lens, existing research risks overlooking important antecedents that help explain why workplace exclusion persists. To broaden understanding of workplace exclusion as a relational phenomenon, we develop an actor-focused model of workplace exclusion motives grounded in social exchange theory. We conceptualize workplace exclusion as actors’ decisions to withhold opportunities for targets to participate in potential exchanges of instrumental or socioemotional resources. From this perspective, exclusion emerges from actors’ evaluations of their social environment and the suitability of engaging in exchange with a potential exchange partner. We identify three potential evaluations that render an exchange unsuitable: anticipated harmful exchanges that threaten the actor, anticipated unproductive exchanges that offer limited value, and anticipated adverse exchanges that may burden the target or harm the collective. These evaluations give rise to a taxonomy of three different exclusion motives—antisocial, pro-self, and prosocial—which can result in socioemotional or instrumental exclusion depending on the situation at hand. By shifting attention from targets to actors, this manuscript challenges prevailing assumptions about why workplace exclusion occurs and offers foundation for future theorizing and intervention.

Rebalancing Power: A Theory of Inclusion as Responsive Co-Creation

DEI
Ruba Ezzeddine (University of Cologne); Anne Burmeister (University of Cologne); Stephanie J. Creary (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania)
Inclusion has become a foundational construct in organizational research on inequality and marginalization. Yet dominant conceptualizations often depict inclusion as enacted by majority group members and received by marginalized members. This unidirectional framing limits theory by obscuring the relational dynamics through which inclusion is negotiated in interaction and inadvertently reproduces power asymmetries. In this paper, we develop a process theory that reconceptualizes inclusion as responsive co-creation—a relational mechanism through which organizational members jointly construct inclusion and renegotiate power through everyday interactions. We classify inclusion scholarship into two streams: Majority Members as Helpers and Minoritized Members as Agents, and show how each perspective, despite advancing inclusion goals, can generate unintended consequences that sustain dependency and unequal inclusion labor. Drawing on relational and power theories, we theorize how responsive co-creation shifts inclusion from fixed includer–included roles toward reciprocal role oscillation and mutual influence. Ultimately, we reposition inclusion as a relational process of power negotiation through which workplace marginalization is contested and power asymmetries are rebalanced.
