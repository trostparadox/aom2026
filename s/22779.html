Markets as Moral Adjudicators: A Meta-Analysis of Corporate Misconduct Penalties

SIM
Noushi Rahman (Pace University); Vijay Sampath (Fairleigh Dickinson University); Naomi A. Gardberg (Baruch College, CUNY); Nafis Rahman (The University of Hong Kong)
Corporate misconduct research spans multiple literatures and reports heterogeneous market punishment effects. We conduct a meta-analysis of 83 effect sizes, covering 17,796 firm-events studied between 1981 and 2022, to test competing explanations for variation in market reactions to corporate wrongdoing. Using random-effects meta-regression with a theory-competition design, we compare stakeholder salience theory (proximity effects) and retributive justice theory (blameability effects) in explaining punishment severity. Both stakeholder proximity and moral blameability significantly predict negative market reactions; however, blameability retains explanatory power in joint models while proximity does not. Regulatory involvement moderates these relationships in a manner consistent with optimal penalty substitution. Higher levels of formal enforcement are associated with weaker incremental market penalties for high-proximity violations and highly blameworthy offenses. Our theory-competition approach advances understanding of when and why markets punish corporate misconduct and provides guidance for firms assessing reputational risk exposure and for regulators designing enforcement strategies that account for market discipline.

Tough Call for Directors to Remain in or Exit Fraudulent Boards: Conflict Among Three Identities

SIM
Asuka Takaoka (Tokyo Woman's Christian University); Toru Yoshikawa (Waseda University)
Knowledge regarding why directors exit their company boards following corporate fraud remains limited. Specifically, previous researchers have not empirically tested this relationship using identity theory, despite its significance to broader stakeholders. In this study, we proposed three identities relevant to outside directors: two personal identities, one derived from their full-time professions and the other from being directors, and a social or group-based identity as a member of a specific board. Using panel data of 20,672 outside directors of Japanese listed firms between 2010 and 2020, we then investigated the effect of outside directors’ identities on their exit-or-remain decisions following fraud, with finer granularity by fraud types and directors’ resource-dependent roles. The results revealed that fraud, particularly severe fraud, is associated with high levels of director exit. Particularly, outside directors at prestigious firms are more likely to leave compared to those at less prestigious firms. However, outside directors who are business experts tend to remain and help the firm out of the crisis, unlike directors with non-business backgrounds such as specialists and community influentials. This study extends identity theory in the organizational crisis context by demonstrating how personal and social identities determine director exit from fraudulent firms.

Firm’s CSR as CEO’s Conditional Capital: CSR, Ethical Misconduct, and CEO Dismissal

SIM
Warren Boeker (University of Washington); Uisung David Park (Syracuse University); David M. Gomulya (Singapore Management University)
This study examines how corporate social responsibility (CSR) shapes CEO accountability in the aftermath of ethical misconduct. Drawing on expectancy violation theory, we argue that firm’s CSR functions as CEO’s conditional capital, it can buffer leaders in stable contexts but heightens scrutiny when firms violate core ethical standards. Using a matched sample of 1,130 cases of financial misrepresentation by S&P 1500 firms, we find that greater CSR commitment increases the likelihood of CEO dismissal following misconduct. This effect is strongest when misconduct is severe and when it occurs in industries where misconduct is pervasive, underscoring how CSR amplifies stakeholder expectations and intensifies leadership consequences when moral leadership expectations are violated. We further show that governance structures shape this relationship: lone-insider boards heighten CEO accountability, whereas CEO-appointed boards reduce it. These findings advance understanding of CSR as both a reputational asset and liability, highlighting its role in conditioning responses to organizational wrongdoing. More broadly, this study integrates CSR, misconduct, and governance research to explain how moral expectations, industry norms, and board power dynamics jointly shape executive accountability.

Admiring the Villains: Positive Emotion-Driven Silence of Unethical Behavior Observers

SIM
Vivek Mishra (Indian Institute of Technology, Delhi); Varun Sharma
Scholars have studied the individuals’ emotions as the antecedent of their silence regarding the observed unethical behavior. However, they have remained primarily focused on negative emotions such as fear. We argue that individuals may also experience positive emotions when observing unethical behavior, especially when it has a positive context, such as unethical pro-organizational behavior (UPB). Here, we suggest that UPB observers are likely to feel admiration, a positive emotion, for UPB due to its pro-organizational element. Using the moral foundation theory (MFT), we suggest that UPB observers’ relative inclination to loyalty over fairness would determine their positive emotion of admiration, eventually affecting their prosocial silence (peer and organization-focused). Using a vignette-based field study design, we collected time-lagged data from 285 working individuals from the USA. We find that observers felt significantly greater positive emotion of admiration in the case of UPB than unethical behavior for self-interest. Further, this positive emotion of admiration also successfully predicted the observers’ prosocial silence organizational-focused, in case of UPB exclusively. Our study contributes to the literature by underlining the potential dark side of a positive emotion i.e. how admiration may also lead to the silence. Accordingly, it advises that organizations should educate their employees so that they do not fall for the pro-organizational façade of UPB.
