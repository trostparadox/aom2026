Session overview

Remote and hybrid work arrangements have received extensive scholarly and practitioner attention in recent years (Bloom et al., 2024; Choudhury et al., 2024). Existing research has primarily examined individual-level outcomes such as productivity, wellbeing, and work-life balance—often treating hybrid work as a static policy choice. Yet organizations continue to struggle to consistently implement hybrid work practices despite years of experimentation, suggesting that the challenges extend far beyond questions of optimal policy design (Elliott et al., 2025). The shift to remote and hybrid work represents more than a change in location; it constitutes a fundamental reorganization of the spatial and temporal structures that have historically anchored organizational life (Mazmanian et al., 2013). When work is no longer organized around a default place or time, workers, managers, and organizations must actively construct what was previously taken for granted. This symposium brings together scholars examining how this necessary reconstruction unfolds across multiple levels of analysis. The first paper investigates how workers design daily routines through affordance-based reasoning when flexible arrangements remove traditional anchors. The second paper reveals how performance incentives—one of the most traditional organizational practices—may inadvertently discourage employees from using the flexibility organizations offer. The third paper explores how broader socioeconomic forces, including technological change and labor market instability, reshape workplace friendships and social connections. The fourth paper offers a cultural explanation for persistent implementation challenges, introducing the concept of value-enactment ambiguity to explain why symbolic alignment around shared values can mask divergent interpretations of legitimate work practices. Together, these papers illuminate why remote and hybrid work disrupts organizational life at individual, organizational, and societal levels in interconnected ways—and why structural solutions, not just policy adjustments, may be required for genuine reconfiguration of contemporary work.
