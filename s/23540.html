Artificial Intelligence Capability and Firm Value: A Transaction Cost Perspective

TIM
CHENGMING HU (The University of Newcastle); Xuefeng Shao; Dian Sun (Not Associated); Yujie Zhu (The University of Newcastle); Yan Wang; Hongqian Liu (East China Normal University)
Although artificial intelligence (AI) capability has become a central lever for value creation, its net effect on firm outcomes remains unclear. Building on transaction cost theory, this study examines how corporate AI capability shapes financial performance by first lowering search, contracting, monitoring, and coordination costs, and then raising safeguard and complexity costs as its scope expands. Using a matched panel of United States listed firms from 2000 to 2024 that combines USPTO patents, Compustat accounts, and BoardEx governance data, the study finds an inverted U-shaped effect of AI capability on financial performance. Furthermore, the findings demonstrate that the board’s national diversity and size flatten this curve by pacing early expansion and containing later exposure. By revealing the nonlinear aspects and governance conditions that produce this inverted U-shaped effect, this study clarifies when AI investments translate into durable financial gains.

Corporate Knowledge and Investments in AI Startups

TIM
Benedetta Montanaro (Politecnico di Milano - School of Management); Mattia Pedota
This paper examines how corporates’ knowledge bases shape their investment decisions in AI startups through Corporate Venture Capital programs. Drawing on the innovation management and absorptive capacity literature, we analyze how different dimensions of AI-related knowledge, including intensity, specialization and technological diversity, are associated with firms’ investments in AI startups. Using a dataset combining global CVC investment data with patent-based measures of corporate knowledge, we find that the relationship between corporate knowledge and investments in AI startups is non-linear. While the accumulation of AI-related knowledge and moderate levels of AI specialization are associated with greater investment activity, excessive specialization in AI and broad technological diversification reduce firms’ engagement with AI startups. On the contrary, diversity within the AI knowledge domain is positively associated with AI investments.

Overperformance Rhythm: and Strategic AI Investment

TIM
Jiuyu Dong (Dalian University of Technology); Yutong He (Dalian University of Technology); Jifeng Yang (Shanghai University)
How does the temporal pattern of firms’ overperformance shape their artificial intelligence investment decisions? Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm, this study examines overperformance rhythm, defined as the temporal regularity with which firms exceed performance aspirations, and its implications for heterogeneous AI investments. We find that volatile overperformance rhythms heighten managerial risk perception and fragment attention, thereby discouraging AI hardware investment while encouraging AI software investment. Moreover, these effects are contingent on overperformance duration and intensity, which jointly shape the credibility and cognitive pressure of overperformance signals. Using panel data from Chinese listed manufacturing firms between 2018 and 2024, our findings highlight the importance of temporal performance patterns in explaining firms’ strategic responses to overperformance feedback and offer a nuanced explanation of how overperformance shapes AI investment strategies.

Unleashing Productivity: The Role of AI and Data-Driven Investments in Firm Performance

TIM
Alexander Ehrlich (ZEW - Leibniz Centre for European Economic Research); Bettina Peters (ZEW - Leibniz Centre for European Economic Research)
Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to drastically change production, R&D processes, business models, and the organizational structure of firms across a wide range of industries. In order to reap the benefits of artificial intelligence, data is generally considered to be essential. However, little is known about its quantitative impact. This study provides novel empirical evidence on the role of data investment for unleashing productivity gains from adopting AI technologies. Using firm-level panel data for German firms from 2018 to 2023, we find that AI adoption is associated with an average productivity increase of approximately 6%. These effects are robust across model specifications and stable over time. Beyond the standalone effect of AI, our analysis shows that data investments independently contribute to productivity, with gains of 3.5% to 3.9%. Moreover, we find strong evidence of complementarity: firms that combine AI adoption with data investment achieve additional productivity gains of 1.7 percentage points. Finally, we show that internally sourced data significantly increases the productivity impact of AI, whereas external data does not yield similar gains. These findings emphasize that realizing the full productivity potential of AI depends not only on adopting the technology itself but also on building high-quality, firm-specific data capabilities.
