Geopolitical Tension and Digital Entrepreneurs’ Content Creation on International Platforms

IM
Jingyuan Li (Kelley School of Business, Indiana University); Jiali Zhou (American University); Jose Uribe (Kelley School of Business, Indiana University)
Recent research has begun to examine how rising geopolitical tensions affected traditional firms pursuing international market opportunities. Much less is known about how digital entrepreneurs navigate geopolitical risk when operating on international platforms. We address this gap by examining digital creators’ content strategies in response to geopolitical tension through an authenticity-based lens. Leveraging the tension between China and Western countries over alleged forced labor in the Xinjiang Cotton supply chain as a quasi-natural experiment, we examine how Chinese fashion creators on YouTube adjusted the brands featured in their content in response to the controversy. We propose that Chinese creators reduce featuring Western brands that criticize Xinjiang Cotton as a way to signal their authenticity. This effect is strengthened when audience or creator characteristics weaken creators’ baseline authenticity and is attenuated when creators are more secure in audience perception of their authenticity. We further uncover “spillover effects” in the form of creators’ broader pro-nationalist signaling, whereby creators increased their incorporation of Chinese brands while avoiding (unimplicated) Japanese brands that could appear sensitive due to historical inter-country tensions. We find support for our hypotheses with difference-in-differences analyses on fashion creators on YouTube. Finally, our results suggest that authenticity-preserving content adaptations carry commercial costs for creators. Together, we highlight how rising geopolitical tensions shape the strategic choices and business outcomes of digital entrepreneurs.

Regulatory Interventions and Corporate Political Reconfiguration in Sin Industries

IM
Christiaan Alexander Roell (UNSW Sydney); Felix Arndt (University of Waterloo); Wilson Ng (IDRAC Business School); Stephanie Villers (University of Waterloo)
When politico-religious audiences perceive the behavior of a multinational enterprise (MNE) as harmful to society, they may press for regulatory interventions that threaten the MNE’s legitimacy and market access. Extant literature suggests that MNEs’ ability to understand and influence government policies contributes to long-term success in host countries. Not only is it difficult for MNEs operating in sin industries – whose products are widely viewed as harmful to society – to develop effective political strategies that build legitimacy, it may be even more challenging to restore their legitimacy following destabilizing changes in government policy that restrict the MNE’s market. In 2015, the Indonesian government shocked the beer industry by closing a primary product distribution channel of the MNE (the minimart ban), mainly to appease Islamic political parties and religious pressure groups. We investigate how the market leader in the beer category, a century-old subsidiary of a European beer-brewer, sought to restore its legitimacy following sudden, restrictive policy and increasing opposition from politico-religious audiences in the world’s largest Muslim-majority country. Our longitudinal case study reveals that the political strategy on which it relied on had become insufficient after the minimart ban; this prompted the MNE to reconfigure its political strategies to restore legitimacy through six mechanisms – shifting the narrative, co-opting opponents, diversification, mobilizing allies, bridging institutional logics, and product legitimation – and, in doing so, we offer insights into the dynamics and dependencies of political strategies in a young democracy with increasing religious conservatism.

Trade Shocks And The Expectations Wedge: How Managers Reassess Competitiveness Under Uncertainty

IM
Siyana Gurova (ZHAW School of Management and Law); Benedikt Zoller-Rydzek; Florian Keller
Abrupt trade-policy shifts increasingly shape competitiveness in small, highly open economies. We argue that tariff escalations are a dual shock: they raise expected trade costs and trade-policy uncertainty, triggering multidimensional managerial updating. Because trade frictions bite most directly at the level of specific offerings, export exposure should reduce product-level perceived competitiveness. At the same time, heightened uncertainty can increase the salience of organizational adaptability, yielding more resilient (or even improved) firm-level assessments. In turn, international embeddedness creates operational real options to shift sourcing, production, and market servicing across jurisdictions, widening adjustment margins under trade shocks. We test these claims with the Swiss Managers Survey (2022–2025), pooling 2022–2024 and comparing them to the May 2025 wave after the U.S. “reciprocal” tariff announcement. We estimate average treatment effects on the treated with a doubly robust difference-in-differences estimator combining propensity-score weighting and outcome regression. The findings reveal dimensional divergence: relative to less-exposed firms, export-exposed firms update significantly more negatively on product-level competitiveness but more favorably on firm-level competitiveness; a wedge test confirms the product-level change is significantly more negative, in line with the theorized product–firm wedge. EU-affiliate structures are associated with more resilient post-shock updating, with larger effects among Swiss-headquartered firms.

Geopolitical Tensions and Firm Exit Decisions

IM
Omid Aliasghar (The University of Auckland); Grigorij Ljubownikow (The University of Auckland)
Geopolitical tension is a crucial consideration in the strategy of multinational enterprises (MNEs), yet firms rarely observe interstate relations directly. Instead, managers, regulators, and stakeholders interpret geopolitical developments through media coverage, which amplifies and frames signals of political strain. Despite its importance, little is known about how media-reported geopolitical tension translates into firm-level strategic responses or why MNEs facing the same political environment often behave differently. We develop a resource-oriented account of foreign subsidiary exit that integrates a dyadic, media-based measure of geopolitical tension with resource-based logic. We argue that media attention transforms geopolitical developments into perceived strategic threats and that resulting exit pressures depend critically on firms’ strategic asset structures. We test our hypotheses using an unbalanced panel of 15,472 firm-year observations for 1,637 publicly listed U.S. firms and 6,682 exits from Chinese subsidiaries between 1996 and 2024. Our results show that media-reported geopolitical tension increases the likelihood of subsidiary exit, but this effect is concentrated among asset-intensive MNEs holding fixed, immobile, and innovation-related assets. In particular, innovation-intensive MNEs are more sensitive to media-reported geopolitical tension, which can intensify regulatory scrutiny and strategic uncertainty surrounding knowledge-based activities. These findings advance research on political risk and international strategy by clarifying when and why media-driven geopolitical tensions trigger foreign subsidiary exits.
