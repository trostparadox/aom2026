The Algorithm Will See You Now: A Theoretical Framework for AI-Enhanced Organizational Learning

MOC
Gayle O'Keefe (University of North Carolina at Greensboro); Nir Kshetri (University of North Carolina at Greensboro)
Organizational learning has been a central theme in management research yet debates over its definition and frameworks remain largely unresolved. With the rapid evolution of artificial intelligence (AI), there is a pressing need to revisit how organizations create, distribute, and apply knowledge. This paper advances a reconceptualization of learning processes by integrating AI's transformative capacity to accelerate, personalize, and scale knowledge alongside its inherent risks of bias, misinformation, and eroded source credibility. Building on classic organizational learning theory, including Argyris's single and double-loop learning and March's exploration-exploitation framework, this study proposes a theoretical model that captures how AI reshapes knowledge creation, dissemination, and validation at individual and collective levels. The paper develops six theoretical propositions examining the relationships between AI integration, learning velocity, knowledge democratization, quality control mechanisms, and organizational adaptation. By reframing learning as a socio-technical capability where human judgment and AI analytics operate in tandem, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature and provides a foundation for empirical research into how organizations can evolve their learning processes in the digital era. This framework offers researchers testable propositions for examining AI's impact on organizational learning while providing practitioners with strategic guidance on implementing validation mechanisms.

Project Failure, Innovator Resilience and Product Innovation

MOC
Xiangming Tao (University of Sussex)
While prior research has long suggested that failure can serve as a valuable source of learning and innovation, how failure experiences are translated into innovative outcomes remains insufficiently understood. Drawing on sensemaking and resilience perspectives, this study examines how failure experiences in new product development (NPD) projects shape subsequent learning processes and product innovation outcomes. Focusing on project managers as central innovation actors, the study theorizes that learning from project failure constitutes a critical mechanism linking failure experiences to product innovativeness, while individual resilience as an innovator condition the effectiveness of this learning process. Using multi-wave survey evidence from matched respondents involved in NPD projects, the findings reveal a nuanced relationship between failure experiences and product innovativeness that depends on both learning dynamics and innovator resilience. Specifically, the benefits of failure for innovation are contingent on the extent to which project managers engage in meaningful learning and possess the capacity to adapt, recover, and reframe adversity in innovative contexts. By clarifying when and how project failure contributes to product innovation, this study advances research on learning from failure and the microfoundations of innovation outcomes. It highlights innovator resilience as a critical yet underexplored boundary condition in the failure–innovation relationship and offers actionable insights for managing failure in innovation-intensive project environments.

The Informant Accuracy Paradox: Why Knowledge of Social Network Structure Survives Memory Errors

MOC
Hui Sun (Frankfurt School of Finance and Management)
Researchers have long debated how accurately individuals perceive their own social networks. While sociologists often view individuals as unreliable informants of their social ties, neuroscientists find that the brain reliably encodes network structures. We resolve this “informant accuracy paradox” by distinguishing between dyadic accuracy (individual ties) and structural accuracy (global patterns). Drawing on contemporary theories of the brain as a predictive machine, we argue that human memory functions as a compression device that is adaptive for social navigation and inference. Using simulations, we demonstrate that while recall is lossy, frequently forgetting existing ties (omission errors) or falsely inferring non-existing ties through triadic closure (commission errors), these commission errors actually preserve and sometimes even improve the accuracy of inferred network centrality (i.e., who is more central in the network). These results reconcile pessimistic sociological assessments of informant inaccuracy with optimistic neuroscientific evidence on neural encoding of social networks. More broadly, they suggest that apparent inaccuracy in dyadic recall reflects an efficient cognitive strategy: compressing complex social information while preserving what matters for social navigation.

Identity Implications for the AI Futures Racing Toward Us: Identity Elasticity, Threat, and Theft

MOC
Mathew Laurence Sheep (Florida Gulf Coast University)
What are ways in which human and AI identities will interact and intertwine as AI technological evolution is racing toward the present at unprecedented speed of innovation and organizational/societal disruption? The purpose of this paper is to introduce frameworks of abductive logic and analytic perspectives to engage in exigent, time-sensitive research that will pre-emptively prepare individuals, organizations, and societies to address the massive reconfigurations that rapid GenAI-AGI evolution will bring in near and distant futures—especially to understand how those reconfigurations might plausibly impact individual identities in terms of human responses in identity work—whether to expand identity to incorporate AI collaboration as part of that identity (identity elasticity), to fear or resist AI due to lingering mistrust or power issues (identity threat), or to resent and challenge AI as an illegitimate replacement of formerly “owned” role identities and positions held (identity theft). I review a nascent and disciplinarily diffused literature on these topics and propose abductive logic and research approaches as most appropriate to engage plausible futures of AI development vis-à-vis human identity as concepts evolve of “what it means to be human” in the age of AI.
