A Value-Logic Perspective on Organisational Scaling

ENT
Amanda Calderwood (University of Ulster); Ben Fitzgerald Kiely (University of Limerick); Pamela Dawn Patayon (University of Galway); Conor Carroll (University of Limerick); Kristel Miller
Despite extensive research, organisational scaling continues to be treated as a singular, economically oriented process, offering limited insight into how scaling is related to other types of value orientation and creation. This systematic review addresses this gap by examining how scaling is related to economic, social, and environmental value by analysing the antecedents, process and outputs. Using a PRISMA approach, this review synthesises 60 peer-reviewed papers to map how scaling is conceptualised across these value domains. The findings reveal that scaling can produce significant social value, yet research to date is fragmented, with a dominant focus on economic value. There is evidence that scaling organisations also can contribute to environmental value however, limited empirical research exists, opening up opportunities for future research across each of the 3 value types. This review provides new insights into how common antecedents and processes can exist across different value domains; however, the scaling outputs vary depending on the underlying value logics. A process-based framework is presented which conceptualising scaling pathway capable which produce different value outputs. This review advances scaling theory by reconceptualising organisational scaling as a value-logic–contingent process in which similar antecedents and coordination mechanisms generate distinct economic, social, and environmental outcomes.

External Enablers and Scaling Pathways: An Awareness–Motivation–Capability Perspective

ENT
Daniel Aldave (Auburn University)
Firms often face exogenous changes that make expansion attractive—or even necessary—yet they differ markedly in whether they scale and in how they do so. Existing research has emphasized internal growth dynamics or treated scaling as a path-dependent consequence of favorable conditions, offering limited explanation for when scaling becomes salient and why firms prioritize different scale-up modes. This paper develops a configurational theory of scaling that explains how structured variation in external change shapes both the activation of scaling and the allocation of scaling effort. Integrating the External Enablers framework with the Awareness–Motivation–Capability perspective, the theory shows how differences in the scope and onset of external enablers structure managerial attention, urgency, and evaluative focus. Narrowly scoped enablers are more likely to activate scaling, while onset conditions reweight firms’ prioritization across internally oriented and externally oriented scaling pathways. These relationships are conditioned by managerial capabilities, resource slack, and business model scalability, which shape interpretive clarity and feasibility under time pressure. The framework explains why firms exposed to similar external developments scale at different moments and through different pathways, advancing a view of scaling as a contingent strategic response to environmental change.

Human Resource Management Propensity for Scaling-Up Performance: The Role of Ambidextrous Contexts

ENT
Euclides Major; Aristides Isidoro Ferreira; Sandra Pereira Costa (ISCTE - University Institute of Lisbon); Sofia Oliveira
Why do some firms grow while others successfully scale? This article examines how human resource management (HRM) enables high-growth firms to move beyond episodic expansion and achieve sustained scaling. Drawing on dynamic capabilities and organizational ambidexterity, we adopt a three-study mixed-method design. Study 1 uses interviews with founders and HR leaders to trace how HR practices adapt across scaling episodes. Study 2 develops and validates a new measure of HRM agility and strategic alignment in scaling contexts. Study 3 employs Necessary Condition Analysis to show that HRM agility, strategic alignment, and ambidextrous organizational contexts constitute necessary prerequisites for sustained scale-up performance. Overall, the findings position HRM not as a downstream support function but as a strategic mechanism underpinning firms’ ability to meet short-term operational pressures while preserving long-term adaptability. We contribute to management theory by theorizing HRM as a dynamic enabler of scaling, extending both ambidexterity and capability perspectives. For practitioners, we offer actionable guidance and a validated diagnostic tool to assess whether HRM systems are equipped for the challenges of scaling.

Limits to the Value of Experimentation? Startup Scaling Under Varying Demand Conditions

ENT
Jingya You (University of Wisconsin-Madison); Deepak Somaya (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign)
The benefits of experimentation for firm success and scaling are widely studied in prior research, yet its role under varying demand conditions remains underexplored. This paper examines whether startups change how much they experiment when facing greater demand-side opportunities and how experimentation in turn affects the scaling of these startups. We theorize that demand-side opportunities increase the opportunity costs of experimentation and that strategic commitments inherent in experimentation constrain scaling under favorable demand conditions. Leveraging the 2016 Indian demonetization as a quasi-experiment, we find that experimentation decreased among Indian software startups experiencing stronger demand and startups with greater (recent) experimentation scaled less under these conditions. Additional tests provide evidence consistent with the proposed theory. These findings contribute to the literatures on startup experimentation and scaling.

Timing the Ascent: Open-Source Software and the Decision to Start Scaling

ENT
Jabar Torkaman (ESADE Business School - Ramon Llull University); Dimo Ringov (ESADE Business School)
The transition from startup to scaleup is a crucial but challenging decision. Much of the literature has focused on how the timing of this transition impacts subsequent firm performance outcomes. Yet, we know considerably less about the determinants of the timing of scaling and how these determinants may condition the effects of the scaling timing on subsequent firm performance. A key issue is the trade-off between accelerating resource development to scale sooner and the increased risks of early scaling. This study investigates whether and how firms can effectively reduce the time to start scaling while managing the challenges that stem from early scaling. Looking at US digital firms and leveraging the launch of an open-source machine learning platform (TensorFlow by Google), this study argues and finds evidence on how access to open-source software can enable firms to shorten resource development time and, thus, start scaling sooner without compromising performance.
