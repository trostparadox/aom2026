Capital in the Capitol: The Myth of Congressional Insider Trading

SIM
Barney Chen (UCLA Anderson School of Management); Bruce Sacerdote (Dartmouth College)
Do elected officials exploit informational advantages for personal financial gain? This question has attracted heightened attention amid increased scrutiny of congressional stock trading, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior research finds little evidence that legislators outperform the market, but existing studies rely on limited time periods and offer limited insight into the mechanisms underlying trade timing. We revisit this question by constructing a novel dataset covering the stock trading activity of U.S. members of Congress and their immediate families from 2012 to 2023. We find that, on average, legislators' portfolios underperform market benchmarks following the STOCK Act and after 2020. We further introduce a new timing-based test and show that legislators' trades largely reflect prevailing market sentiment rather than anticipating future price changes. Together, these results suggest that legislators' financial behavior more closely resembles that of uninformed investors than strategic insiders. Our findings contribute to research on political elites, information asymmetries, and the behavioral consequences of regulatory oversight.

Investor Heterogeneity and Corporate Lobbying: Evidence from Public and Private Firms

SIM
Emma Kyoungseo Hong (Rutgers Business School); Tony Lizhang He (Rutgers Business School); Markus David Taussig (Rutgers Business School)
Empirical research on corporate political lobbying has focused on public (listed) firms, largely overlooking private (unlisted) firms and how different stakeholders shape corporate political voice. In this paper, we examine these understudied distinctions by bringing private firms into view, tracing the descriptive contours of public vs. private lobbying and examining how ownership structures and investor heterogeneity influence which public policy issues firms engage with and on whose behalf corporate political activity (CPA) is undertaken. To do so, we construct a dataset that links U.S. federal lobbying records to ownership information for both public firms and the largest 20,000 private firms in the U.S. We find that, while public firms lobby more, private firms are active and meaningful participants in lobbying, exhibiting political activity comparable to that of public firms at both the extensive and intensive margins. Furthermore, we reveal patterns consistent with certain investors acting as “political principals”, in that their presence in portfolio firms is associated with particular lobbying priorities or capabilities. We document this phenomenon in several ways: (1) firms increase their lobbying following the entry of certain new investor types (angels, corporate investors, hedge funds, impact investors, private equity, venture capital); (2) firms broaden the issue scope of their lobbying portfolios as their investor bases become more heterogeneous; (3) firms’ lobbying behaviors align more strongly with those of “portfolio peers” that share common investors than with those of industry rivals; and (4) different investor types, particular when leveraging within-firm variation in private firms, are associated with distinct issue priorities—for example, angels with environmental lobbying, hedge funds with tax-related lobbying, and venture capital with innovation-related lobbying. Together, these findings advance research on social issues in management by clarifying how corporate political influence is structured, which stakeholders amplify particular policy concerns, and how ownership structures and investor logics may shape corporate political voice.

Is the Political Market a Useful Metaphor in Corporate Political Activity Scholarship?

SIM
Nicolas Dahan (Seton Hall University)
In 2023, Katic and Hillman published a literature review about corporate political activity (CPA), stressing the “ubiquity” of the political market (PM) metaphor in structuring the CPA field, and suggesting insightful updates to refresh it, which they coined “political marketplace 2.0”. This came on the heels of a bibliometric analysis of sixty years of CPA research by Lenway and colleagues (2022), who also confirmed the “central” role of the PM metaphor so far. While I do not reject the evidence, I diverge from the mainstream conclusions that the extensive use of the PM metaphor has been fully warranted in the past (Lenway et al., 2022), and that we should merely update the PM metaphor in order to continue using it extensively going forward (Katic & Hillman, 2023). I contend that the PM metaphor has become more theoretical blinders than a fruitful concept. I propose that instead we qualify more precisely when the PM metaphor may still be relevant, by articulating four boundary conditions for its use: a pluralist institutional setting; instrumentally-motivated actors; a mutually beneficial exchange; and direct CPA. Ultimately, I conclude that in most cases, we should embrace alternative theoretical perspectives of CPA that already exist or remain to be articulated.

How Does Investor Pressure Compel Multinational Enterprises to Maintain Environmental Commitments

SIM
Salman Kimiagari (Thompson Rivers University); Omowumi Darlington Agboola (Thompson Rivers University)
The persistence of environmental commitments by multinational enterprises operating in institutionally fragile and geopolitically volatile contexts remains a critical but insufficiently theorized issue in international business research. This paper develops a conceptual explanation for why some firms sustain environmental commitments despite weak domestic enforcement. Grounded in governance-fragile host countries, we argue that institutional voids do not simply permit environmental backsliding but reconfigure governance by shifting effective enforcement authority toward global financial actors. Drawing on the Institutional Void-Based View, Neo-Institutional Theory, Resource Dependence Theory, and Signalling Theory, we theorize investor-enforced environmental continuity as a transnational governance mechanism through which firms navigate institutional fragility. As environmental, social, and governance expectations become embedded in capital allocation, investor scrutiny functions as a portable substitute for host-state regulation, disciplining firms through engagement, divestment threats, risk pricing, and index inclusion. This substitution generates legitimacy and resource-access incentives while constraining managerial discretion under conditions of low regulatory verification. The study advances a conceptual framework and four propositions that link institutional fragility to governance substitution, transnational legitimacy dependence, capital market dependence, and a compliance spectrum ranging from substantive performance to symbolic signalling. In doing so, the paper reconceptualizes institutional voids as triggers of alternative governance logics in multinational environmental governance.

When Transparency Fails: Political Disclosure, Lobbying, and Accounting Controversies

SIM
Abdullah Al-Mamun (University of Wollongong); Mehdi Samimi (City University of New York); Swadip Bhattacharjee; Searat Ali (University of Wollongong)
Corporate political spending disclosure is widely promoted as a governance mechanism that enhances transparency and reduces corporate misconduct. Yet firms vary substantially in how they engage in politics, raising the question of whether disclosure is equally effective across political contexts. Using data on S&P 500 firms from 2011 to 2023, we examine the relationship between corporate political spending disclosure and accounting controversies, and how this relationship is shaped by lobbying intensity. We find that higher levels of political spending disclosure are associated with fewer accounting controversies on average, consistent with transparency-based governance arguments. However, this effect is significantly weakened in firms that engage heavily in lobbying. Additional analyses show that the protective effect of disclosure is further eroded in firms with high free cash flow, while it is strongest in firms with more independent and gender-diverse boards. These findings suggest that political transparency can reduce accounting misconduct, but only when it reflects a substantive commitment to accountability rather than a symbolic response to external pressure. The study contributes to research on corporate political activity, disclosure, and financial misconduct by showing when and why political transparency succeeds or fails as a governance tool.
