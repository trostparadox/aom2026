Foreign Patent Litigation and the Geographic Retrenchment of MNCs’ Patent Portfolios

IM
Yutong Liu (Zhejiang University); Wen Helena Li (University of Technology, Sydney); Jian Du (Zhejiang University)
As multinational corporations (MNCs) expand globally, they increasingly face the risk of patent litigation in foreign jurisdictions, an underexplored threat with implications for global innovation management. Drawing on threat-rigidity theory, we examine how defendant MNCs adjust the geographic diversity of their patent portfolios following foreign patent litigation. We find that MNCs respond by retrenching their geographic diversity of their patent portfolios, narrowing their global innovation footprint to reduce risk exposure. However, this contraction is moderated by MNC’s financial slack, which provides resources for continued exploratory innovation despite external threats. In contrast, heightened media prominence in the host country act as a threat amplifier, intensifying the degree of strategic retrenchment. Overall, our study contributes to the global innovation literature by highlighting the underexplored role of foreign patent litigation in the global innovation cycle and by showing how legal shocks in host countries reshape the geographic footprint of MNCs’ innovation activities.

Subsidiary Knowledge and Competence-Creating Mandates

IM
Xinyi Wang (The Ohio State University); Deepak Nayak (The Ohio State University); Minyoung Kim (The Ohio State University)
When do multinational subsidiaries gain competence-creating mandates? We argue that the characteristics of subsidiary knowledge – not merely its volume – play a critical yet underexplored role in mandate allocation. Drawing on attention-based view of the firm, the knowledge-based view of the firm, and literature on strategic relatedness and diversification, we theorize that headquarters grant mandates when subsidiary knowledge occupies a “sweet spot”: proximate enough to the parent’s existing competence base to enable strategic complementarity yet distinct enough to avoid redundancy, and complex enough to demonstrate strategic distinctiveness yet comprehensible enough to permit organizational integration. Accordingly, we predict inverted-U relationships between both knowledge proximity and knowledge complexity and the likelihood of obtaining a competence-creating mandate, measured by intra-firm citation network centrality. Analyzing a multi-industry sample containing 49,181 parent-subsidiary-year observations across 1,709 U.S. multinationals and 68 subsidiary countries over 1994–2015, we find strong empirical support for our predictions. Our theoretical framework and empirical findings advance subsidiary evolution theory by highlighting the dyadic nature of mandate allocation and demonstrating that competence-creating mandates depend not only on how much subsidiaries produce, but also on whether headquarters can perceive the strategic value of subsidiary knowledge and organizationally integrate it into the MNE’s broader competence base.

Relative and Absolute Technological Capabilities as Determinants of Foreign R&D Investments

IM
Qingwei Li (The Hang Seng University of Hong Kong); Siddharth Natarajan (Nanyang Technological University); Seung Ho Park (The Hang Seng University of Hong Kong)
Foreign R&D has been predominantly theorized as a catch-up means for technological lagging firms to reduce capability gaps with advanced market leaders, implying that incentives should decline as gaps close. However, we argue that increasing technological proximity to global competitors can instead raise incentives for further overseas R&D investments. In contrast, technological proximity to local rivals provides lower marginal incentives for establishing new overseas R&D units relative to proximity to global competitors. Beyond relative technological capabilities, we propose that absolute scientific capabilities constitute an important boundary condition, such that strong scientific capabilities weaken the global-proximity effect. We test these arguments using patent data from Chinese listed pharmaceutical firms (2013–2023) and matched patent data from US-listed firms, complemented by PubMed-based measures of firms’ scientific capabilities derived from publications in leading journals (2000–2022). The results support our theoretical predictions. Our study contributes to global strategy research by showing that overseas R&D is a contingent strategic choice under global technological convergence, shaped jointly by firms’ relative positioning in the global technology space and their internal scientific capabilities.

How Technological Capabilities and Cluster Embeddedness Shape Cross-Border Acquisition Strategies?

IM
Surender Munjal (Aston University); Sumati Varma (University of Delhi); Amit Soni (University of Delhi)
Focusing on firms in the Indian information technology industry, this study examines how cross-border acquisitions (CBAs) undertaken by firms are influenced by their technological capability and presence in industrial cluster. Using a panel of 1,555 firms from 2008 to 2018, our analysis reveals an inverted U-shaped relationship between technological capability and the number of CBAs undertaken by a firm. Furthermore, this relationship is moderated by the firm’s presence in an industrial cluster. While clusters serve as repositories of resources and capabilities, they initially reduce a firm’s need for CBAs but later drive firms toward accelerated international acquisitions. These findings offer novel theoretical insights and practical implications for managers navigating CBAs in technology-intensive sectors.
