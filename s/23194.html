Common Crisis, Diverse Impacts: Disruption of Global Seafood Production Networks

ONE
Christopher R Whynacht (University of Massachusetts Boston)
Global Production Networks (GPNs) help conceptualize the political, cultural, and socioeconomic dimensions of the global economy, yet have often overlooked their ecological foundations. This study applies a GPN lens to study impacts and responses to a tragedy of the commons scenario where the Atlantic cod fishery collapsed due to over-harvesting, destabilizing local communities and the wider seafood production system. This crisis had immediate consequences for harvesters and local communities but also provoked actors to reassess relationships and the sustainability of business practices across the GPN. While lead-firms largely seek to patch over disruptions by finding alternate sources of supply, environmental degradation prompts local actors toward regenerative stewardship of the resource base and diversification of economic outputs. This research provides insights into the impacts ecological disruption has on the global economy, improves our understanding of responses to other forms of environmental degradation, and can support management of common pool natural resources.

The Power of Negative Materiality: How Market Absence Sustains Regenerative Organizing

ONE
Ryoji Miyawaki (Kyoto University); Masaru Karube (Hitotsubashi University); Jin-ichiro Yamada (Kyoto University); Yuki Miyazawa (Hitotsubashi University)
A substantial body of circular economy research posits market formation as a prerequisite for sustaining circular initiatives. In the absence of markets to absorb recovered by-products or valorize environmental benefits, such initiatives are often expected to stall at the pilot stage. However, this assumption cannot fully explain cases in which organizing persists despite prolonged market absence. This study examines the emergence and persistence of a circular ecosystem in Saga City, Japan, originating from a municipal waste incineration plant—a typical NIMBY facility—without the development of a mature CO2 utilization market. Drawing on a qualitative process case study, we analyze ongoing experimentation, dialogue, and engagement among diverse actors around the future use and meaning of CO2 and the incineration plant. Our analysis identifies two structural conditions that sustained these dynamics: market absence and negative materiality. Under these conditions, orchestration emerged not as design or optimization but as a practice that absorbed frictions and prevented relationships and experimentation from halting. We conceptualize this process as regenerative organizing: a spiral dynamic in which localized experiments, justificatory work, and interactions accumulate through trickle-up processes, regenerating not only material resources such as CO2 but also the knowledge, relationships, emotions, and place meanings that sustain continued organizing. Keywords: Regenerative Organizing; Market Absence; Negative Materiality; Circular Ecosystems

Regenerative Organizing Beyond Dualism: A Multispecies Ethnography of Finnish Forestry

ONE
Martta Aune Inkeri Nieminen (Aalto University)
A long-standing economic–ecological dualism characterizes business sustainability scholarship, where economic and ecological goals are typically treated as inherently tension-laden and in need of balancing. This dualistic framing also permeates public and policy debates on Finnish forestry, where forest management is commonly positioned as a trade-off between profitability and ecological values. Drawing on three years of multispecies ethnographic research within a continuous cover forestry community in Finland, this study examines how regenerative organizing reconfigures human–nature relations and how institutionally produced economic and ecological logics materialize, or fail to materialize, in practice.

Creating Value Beyond the Firm: A Stakeholder Theory Perspective on Regenerative Ecosysistems

ONE
Angel Sevil (Universidad del Desarrollo); Tomas Santa-Maria (Universidad del Desarrollo)
Despite growing interest in regenerative business practices, limited research explains how firms organize stakeholder relationships to move from harm reduction toward the active renewal of socio-ecological systems. This paper addresses this gap by integrating stakeholder theory with a regenerative ecosystem perspective to examine how value is jointly created and captured beyond the firm's boundaries. Based on an in-depth empirical single-case study, we show that regenerative ecosystems emerge when firms reposition stakeholders as co-creators of system-level outcomes rather than as recipients of firm-generated value. We identify three interrelated processes underpinning this shift: aligning innovation with a shared regenerative purpose, cultivating trust and legitimacy through sustained community embeddedness, and enabling reciprocal flows of knowledge and resources across organizational boundaries. These processes allow firms to capture economic value through reputational advantages, relational lock-in, and efficiency gains, while simultaneously enhancing community resilience and ecological vitality. We conceptualize regenerative ecosystems as dynamic stakeholder configurations that sustain, restore, and evolve the socio-ecological systems in which economic activity is embedded. By articulating the micro-processes and value logics of regeneration, this study extends stakeholder theory, advances regenerative business scholarship, and offers a grounded framework for understanding business ecosystems oriented towards positive impact.

Orchestrating Regeneration: How Temporality & Spatiality Shape Corporate Strategies For Biodiversity

ONE
Sara Tessitore (Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna); Niccolò Maria Todaro; Duccio Tosi (Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna); Vinicio Di Iorio (Ivey Business School); Francesco Testa (Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna)
Companies are increasingly called to move beyond mitigating their environmental impacts and adopt regenerative approaches that enhance the health of socio-ecological systems. However, little is known about how such ambitions are translated into corporate strategy. This study investigates how companies interpret and combine spatial and temporal dimensions in developing regenerative business strategies for biodiversity. The study adopts a multiple case study design on seven multinational companies embracing a regenerative business model in biodiversity-salient sectors. Data were gathered through semi-structured interviews with key managers, complemented with corporate and archival documents, and analyzed abductively using the Gioia methodology. Our analysis reveals three distinct yet coexisting logics through which companies develop regenerative strategies for biodiversity: Dependency Management, Stewardship & Advocacy, and Trust-Building. Each logic reflects different temporal and spatial orientations ranging from short-term and localized practices to long-term and multi-scale ones. Companies combine these logics, highlighting that the formation of regenerative strategies is an adaptive configuration of interrelated practices enacted across heterogeneous temporal and spatial scales. The study advances regenerative strategy research by demonstrating that strategy formation is a plural and dynamic process. It shows that regenerative strategizing entails the coexistence and interplay of multiple logics, each underpinned by different spatio-temporal orientations.
